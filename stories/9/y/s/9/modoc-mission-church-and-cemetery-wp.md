# Modoc Mission Church and Cemetery

In a corner of northeastern Oklahoma, a Quaker mission church and a cemetery hold the story of the Modoc people, exiled 2,000 miles from their homeland after the Modoc War and determined to endure.

**Location:** 36.8647, -94.6619

**Categories:** Churches on the National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Cemeteries on the National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Buildings and structures in Ottawa County, Oklahoma, Modoc, Native American history, Quakerism

## Introduction

The earliest dated grave in the Modoc Cemetery belongs to Rosie Jack. She died in April 1874, about six months after her father, the Modoc leader Keintpoos, commonly called Captain Jack, was hanged at Fort Klamath, Oregon, and less than six months after her people stepped off a train in Baxter Springs, Kansas, some 2,000 miles from home. Beside the cemetery stands the mission church where the exiled Modoc worshipped, studied and rebuilt. Listed together on the National Register of Historic Places in 1980, the church and cemetery near Miami, Oklahoma, preserve a history of loss, and of stubborn survival.

## Gallows Built Before the Verdict

The exile began with the Modoc War of 1872–1873. Afterward, four Modoc warriors, Keintpoos, Schonchin John, Black Jim and Boston Charley, were tried by a military commission for war crimes. Their gallows went up before the trial began, foreshadowing its outcome. On October 3, 1873, the four men were hanged at Fort Klamath, and the remaining Modoc people were forced to watch. So were two other warriors, Barncho and Slolux, who at the last minute were sent to Alcatraz instead of dying beside the others.

## Two Thousand Miles in Cattle Cars

Nine days later, on October 12, the survivors were loaded onto wagons at Fort Klamath and taken toward a destination no one disclosed to them. After a stop to send Barncho and Slolux to Alcatraz, 153 Modoc people were moved from the wagons into cattle cars, with soldiers riding in two more cars. The men and boys, seen as potential risks, were chained down inside. The train traveled east for about 2,000 miles. On November 16, 1873, it reached Baxter Springs, Kansas, where the Modoc were handed over to an Indian agent named Hiram Jones and sent on to the Quapaw Agency in what is now northeastern Oklahoma.

## The First Hard Winter

They arrived in the middle of November. On the very first day, led by their own Scar-faced Charley and helped by three outsiders, the Modoc built temporary wooden barracks, while some families sheltered in tents; those shelters had to last until the following June. The federal government sent no funding for food, clothing or medicine until March 1874, four months after they arrived. Deaths rose through the first decade in Oklahoma, and by the time of allotment in 1891, 68 Modoc people were able to claim land, many of them born after removal. Corruption made things worse. Jones and his supervisor, Superintendent Enoch Hoag, both Quakers, had staffed the agency with family: 11 of its 12 employees were relatives of one or the other. Complaints from Modoc people and local residents dated to 1874 and 1875 and were dismissed, until an 1878 finding confirmed them. Both men lost their posts in 1879.

## Schoolrooms and a New Faith

Under those conditions, the Modoc adopted Euro-American ways of life as a means of survival. Men hired on with neighboring farmers or hauled materials to local towns, women sold beadwork and intricate handwoven baskets, and families raised their own crops and livestock. They also committed to education. Within six weeks of arrival, 25 Modoc children were attending the Quapaw Boarding School, and six years after removal the federal government scheduled a building on the 4,000-acre Modoc reservation to serve as both school and church. Some students went as far as the Carlisle boarding school, though after the death of Adam McCarty, stepson of the executed Schonchin John, families grew more hesitant to send children away. By 1881 most Modoc people had embraced the Quaker faith. Steamboat Frank, also called Modoc Frank, became one of the first recorded Native ministers of the Society of Friends. He fell ill while at a seminary in Vassalboro, Maine, tried to make it home to his family, and died in Portland, Maine, in the summer of 1886.

## Where Rosie Jack Rests

In the early 1890s the Society of Friends bought the church and school building and moved it a short distance, placing it right beside the Modoc Cemetery, then renovated it to add living quarters. Rosie Jack's grave was already there. Many other Modoc people who fought in the Modoc War are buried in the same ground, some in unmarked graves. When the Modoc Nation won federal recognition in 1978, one of its first acts was to apply to the Department of Housing and Urban Development to buy the church and its land. After the purchase, the nation worked to restore the building to its original appearance. The National Register listing followed in 1980, covering four contributing buildings, and in 1988 the Major William McBride Chapter of the National Society United States Daughters of 1812 sponsored a historical marker at the church.

## Flight Context

The Modoc Mission Church and Cemetery lie at 36.8647°N, 94.6619°W in Ottawa County, northeastern Oklahoma, close to the Missouri line and east of the city of Miami. Baxter Springs, Kansas, where the Modoc exiles arrived by train in 1873, is roughly a dozen miles to the north-northwest. From 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, use Miami to the west and the state line to the east as references; the small church and cemetery are best spotted at low altitude in clear weather. Miami Regional Airport (KMIO) is about 11 nm west-northwest, Neosho Hugh Robinson Airport (KEOS) about 13 nm east-southeast, and Joplin Regional Airport (KJLN) about 19 nm north-northeast.
