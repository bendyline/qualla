# Coleman Theatre

A mining magnate's 1929 gift to Miami, Oklahoma, the Coleman Theatre pairs a Spanish Colonial Revival exterior on Route 66 with a lavish Louis XV interior, and was once billed as the most elaborate theater between Dallas and Kansas City.

## Introduction

Nothing between Dallas and Kansas City, the boast went, could match it. When the Coleman Theatre opened on April 18, 1929, on the stretch of U.S. Route 66 that runs through Miami, Oklahoma, it was billed as the most elaborate theater in all that distance. Twin bell towers with red tile roofs rose above the street, carved terra cotta framed its arched windows, and inside waited gilded statues, winding staircases and a Mighty Wurlitzer organ. The man behind it was George Coleman, a local mining magnate determined to give his town first-class entertainment. Nearly a century later, his palace is still standing on Main Street.

## A Stucco Palace on Main Street

From the sidewalk, the Coleman looks like a building borrowed from Spain. Its Spanish Colonial Revival exterior is a stucco palace of compound arched windows trimmed in hand-carved terra cotta, a central spire-like bell tower, and twin towers with small balconies, wrought iron railings and red tile hip roofs. The building was designed to do more than show films. Plans called for commercial shops on the first floor along its east and south sides, where the theater entrances were placed, and for the Masonic Lodge Hall to occupy the eastern half of the second floor. It was a working piece of downtown, a place where business, fraternal life and entertainment shared one roof along the highway that carried travelers across the country.

## Louis XV in Oklahoma

Step inside and the architecture changes its accent entirely. The interior was done in an elaborate Louis XV style, with carved winding staircases flanked by gilded statues holding candelabra and a grand chandelier overhead. On opening night, audiences saw Ruth Chatterton in the film The Dummy, followed by five acts of vaudeville on the stage. In its early years, the Coleman hosted vaudeville performers, musical groups and movies, with live music from a ten-person orchestra. Presiding over it all was the Wurlitzer, a theater pipe organ with three keyboards and 13 ranks of pipes, ready to accompany whatever the evening brought. For a town in the far northeastern corner of Oklahoma, it was a remarkable place to spend an evening.

## The Family's Gift

The Coleman stayed in the family for six decades. In 1989, the Coleman family donated the building to the City of Miami, on the condition that it be restored and used as a performing arts center. The work that followed drew on private and public money, including a matching grant from the federal Economic Development Administration, and on the labor of hundreds of community volunteers. The original chandelier was restored, and the Louis XV interior was returned to its 1920s appearance. Even the old Mighty Wurlitzer, long thought lost, came home, refurbished and reopened in 1996. By then the theater had already gained national recognition, having been listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1983. George Coleman's own residence, the George L. Coleman Sr. House, is also on the National Register, a reminder of the man whose fortune built the town a palace.

## Flight Context

The Coleman Theatre stands at about 36.876°N, 94.878°W on Main Street in downtown Miami, Oklahoma, along historic U.S. Route 66 in Ottawa County. From 2,500-3,500 feet MSL, the downtown grid of Miami is easy to pick out, with the Neosho River curving past the town. Miami Regional Airport (KMIO) lies just north of town, and Joplin Regional Airport (KJLN) lies about 28 miles to the northeast in Missouri. Grand Lake o' the Cherokees spreads to the south. The area sits where Oklahoma, Kansas and Missouri come together, and visibility is generally good outside of spring and summer storm season.
