# Richards Spur

In a working limestone quarry north of Lawton, cave fissures filled nearly 290 million years ago hold one of the most diverse communities of early land animals ever found.

**Location:** 34.8354, -98.3974

**Categories:** Permian paleontological sites, Paleontology in Oklahoma, Fossil sites, Geology of Oklahoma, Comanche County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

The Dolese Brothers Limestone Quarry north of Lawton, Oklahoma, is still being mined, and the work cuts both ways for science. Each new face of rock destroys part of an extraordinary fossil site and exposes fresh ground at the same time. The site is called Richards Spur, and older papers often call it Fort Sill, after the nearby Army post. Inside the quarry walls, narrow fissures packed with clay and mudstone hold the bones of animals that lived around 289 to 286 million years ago. Together they make up one of the most diverse communities of Paleozoic land-dwelling vertebrates known anywhere.

## Caves in Upended Stone

The rock at Richards Spur is Arbuckle Limestone, which dates to the Ordovician period. During the Pennsylvanian and Permian, the limestone was pushed up, exposed and tilted until its layers stood vertical. Then water went to work on it. In the early Permian a karst system of caves opened inside the stone, decorated with calcite formations such as stalactites, stalagmites and the knobby growths known as cave popcorn. Most of the openings are narrow vertical slots about 40 to 60 centimeters wide. The clay and mudstone that later filled them date to the Artinskian stage of the early Permian, and that fill is where the bones are found. The fossils only go so far down, because the lower parts of the system, about 25 meters below the surface, contain none.

## Found by Quarrymen

Quarry workers first reported fossils here in 1932. The news set off a wave of collecting by local and international geologists. Most of the bones, though not all, are stained dark by hydrocarbons that seeped into the deposits. The same work that uncovers the fossils also makes the site hard to study. Active mining keeps destroying some fissures and opening others, so no one has ever mapped the full layout of the cave system. Most of the Permian fill ends up in the quarry's waste dumps without any record of its layering or position, and that loss has made it difficult to reconstruct how the deposits formed. Researchers think the rocks may match the upper Garber or lower Hennessey Formation found elsewhere in Oklahoma.

## A Crowded Permian World

One of the most abundant animals at the site is the early reptile Captorhinus. It shared the landscape with microsaurs such as Cardiocephalus and Euryodus. Scientists have usually classed the recumbirostran microsaurs as amphibians, although some studies since 2017 suggest they may belong with the reptiles instead. Richards Spur also has the most diverse assortment of parareptiles known from the Early Permian, including Delorhynchus, and it has produced the rare early diapsid Orovenator. Varanopids such as Varanops and Mycterosaurus are here too. They are usually counted as synapsids, the lineage that led to mammals, though some studies since 2018 suggest they may have been reptiles. The amphibians include dissorophids such as Cacops and Aspidosaurus and small amphibamiforms called Tersomius and Pasawioops. Even Dimetrodon turns up, though only barely, as a single fragment of a neural spine.

## Bones That Remember

Some of the bones at Richards Spur record how their owners lived and suffered. Tail vertebrae from several juvenile captorhinids have fracture planes, built-in weak points where the tail could break off. Some modern lizards can do the same thing, which is known as caudal autotomy. One varanopid specimen has fused tail vertebrae, a sign of a metabolic bone disease similar to Paget's disease. Bones like these offer a glimpse of injury, illness and survival nearly 290 million years ago. Collecting them is also a race, because the quarrying that opens new fissures destroys others, and much of the fossil-bearing fill goes to the waste dumps before anyone can study it.

## Flight Context

Richards Spur lies at about 34.8354°N, 98.3974°W, roughly 13 nm north of downtown Lawton. The active limestone quarry stands out as bare, excavated ground against the surrounding countryside. The Wichita Mountains rise to the west and southwest, and Fort Sill's cantonment and ranges lie to the south. Fly at about 2,500–3,500 ft AGL for a good view of the quarry benches. Do not descend low over an active quarry. Henry Post Army Airfield (KFSI) at Fort Sill is about 11 nm due south and is military. Lawton–Fort Sill Regional Airport (KLAW) is about 16 nm south. Fort Sill's restricted ranges border the area, so check current charts for active military airspace. Visibility is usually good, but spring and summer can bring fast-building storms.
