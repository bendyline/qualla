# Spanish Fort, Texas

Named for a Spanish fort that never existed, this near-ghost town on the Red River was really a fortified Taovaya town that routed a Spanish army - and later a rowdy cattle-trail stop where Justin Boots began.

**Location:** 33.9520, -97.6267

**Categories:** Ghost towns in North Texas, Native American history of Texas, National Register of Historic Places in Montague County, Texas, Unincorporated communities in Montague County, Texas, Cattle drives

## Introduction

Spanish Fort was never a Spanish fort. The name is a mistake, and an ironic one. The earthworks and stockades that once stood near this stretch of the Red River belonged to the Taovaya, and in 1759 they turned back a Spanish army that had come to punish them. More than a century later, Anglo settlers found Spanish artifacts and old ruins nearby and drew the wrong conclusion. The name stuck. Today the community in north-central Montague County is nearly empty, with a population of about 50 in 2000 according to the Handbook of Texas, but its story runs from a Taovaya stronghold through a roaring cattle-trail town to the birth of one of Texas's best-known boot companies.

## The Town Under a French Flag

Spanish records say the Taovaya built two permanent settlements here between 1750 and 1757, one on each side of the Red River. In 1759, Colonel Diego Ortiz Parrilla led a campaign of revenge against the Taovaya and Comanche after the 1758 destruction of Mission Santa Cruz de San Sabá, which stood near his Presidio San Luis de las Amarillas. When several hundred Spanish soldiers arrived, they found a town with an estimated population of 6,000, flying the French flag and defended by entrenchments, wooden stockades and a moat. After four hours of fighting, the Spanish retreated, abandoning their two cannons and their baggage train. Peace had been established by 1771, but continued raiding, especially for horses, brought Athanase de Mézières, lieutenant governor of the Natchitoches province, to the town in 1778. He persuaded the Taovaya to give up the two cannons and named the place San Teodoro.

## An Emptied Homeland

Disease did what the Spanish army could not. Smallpox epidemics that began in 1778, followed by American expansion after the Louisiana Purchase of 1803, devastated the Taovaya population. Around 1841 the people who remained abandoned the fortification at San Teodoro, and it slowly crumbled. By 1859, when an early white settler visited the ruins, the Taovaya had been gone for years, and he knew nothing of the people who had built and defended the place. He imagined it had been a Spanish fort. More than a century of farming later erased what was left of the old fortifications. A state historical monument erected in 1936 marks the site of San Teodoro, and markers placed in 1936 and 1976 recognize the Taovaya culture and the 1759 battle. The site joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1975.

## Boots Made on the Trail

By the early 1870s, a town called Burlington had grown near the site. Cattle drivers bound for the Chisholm Trail bedded their herds at Red River Station and rode into town for supplies and entertainment. When residents applied for a post office in 1876, postal officials turned them down because another Texas post office already had that name. Two local men suggested "Spanish Fort" in honor of the nearby ruins, and the new post office opened in 1877. At its busiest, the town had several shops and churches, a Masonic lodge, five doctors, four hotels and a string of saloons, the best known being J. W. Schrock's Cowboy Saloon, where cattlemen gathered to drink and swap stories. On the town plaza, Herman Joseph Justin started a boot business with a clever system: he took orders from drivers heading north and had their custom boots ready when they came back south. That business eventually became Justin Industries.

## A Hard Town Goes Quiet

Spanish Fort had about 300 residents by 1885 and at least two newspapers, the Burlington Times and the Spanish Fort New Era. It also had a reputation as a nasty town. Justin's wife later claimed there had been more than forty killings during the height of the cattle era, including three men murdered before breakfast one Christmas morning. Outlaws hiding in Indian Territory crossed the Red River for supplies and often started fights. The excitement faded when the cattle trails shifted west and the Missouri, Kansas and Texas Railway passed the town by. Justin moved his boot business to nearby Nocona in 1889, soon after the railroad reached it, and the business kept growing there until his sons moved it to Fort Worth in 1925. Spanish Fort held at about 250 people through the first four decades of the twentieth century, but by 1952 only 40 remained. Around 1970 the post office and all but one business closed, and by 2000 most of the buildings on the square stood vacant. On February 10, 2009, an EF4 tornado touched down nearby and began its path by snapping pecan trees; before it ended, the same tornado had killed eight people in Oklahoma.

## Flight Context

Spanish Fort lies at 33.952 N, 97.6267 W at the end of Farm to Market Road 103, about one mile south of the Red River in north-central Montague County, Texas, 17 miles north of Nocona. From 2,500-4,000 feet AGL, follow the meandering Red River, which marks the Texas-Oklahoma line, and look for the small, sparse grid of the old town just south of it among farm fields. Nearest airports: Bowie Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 0F2, no ICAO code), just northeast of Bowie and roughly 26 miles to the south-southwest, and Gainesville Municipal Airport (KGLE) in Cooke County, roughly 32 miles to the southeast. Spring brings severe thunderstorms and tornadoes to the Red River valley.
