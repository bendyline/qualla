# Slaughterville, Oklahoma

A wooded, rural town south of Norman that took its name from a grocer, incorporated to keep its neighbors from swallowing it, and in 2004 turned down an offer to become Veggieville.

**Location:** 35.0836, -97.2869

**Categories:** Towns in Cleveland County, Oklahoma, Oklahoma City metropolitan area, Towns in Oklahoma, Land Run of 1889, Unusual place names

## Introduction

In 2004 the town council of Slaughterville, Oklahoma, received an unusual offer. People for the Ethical Treatment of Animals asked the town to change its name to Veggieville and offered a donation of $20,000 worth of veggie burgers to sweeten the deal. The council heard presentations from PETA members and from local residents, and the town then said no. The name stayed. Its origin is gentler than it sounds, because it has nothing to do with stockyards or butchery. The town was named for a grocery store.

## Named for a Grocer

In the early twentieth century a man named James Slaughter ran a grocery here, and the community came to be known by his store's name. The land had been settled well before then. It lay in the Unassigned Lands of Indian Territory, which opened in the Land Run of 1889. At high noon on April 22 of that year, an estimated 50,000 people were lined up to claim part of about two million acres. A gunshot signaled the start, and the waiting lines of riders and wagons broke apart as people raced to claim land. The first building at what became Slaughterville went up that same year. Yet for eight decades after the run, the community never incorporated.

## Incorporated in Self-Defense

Slaughterville did not become a town until 1970. The reason was its neighbors. Incorporating let the community avoid being annexed by Norman, Noble or Lexington. The new town took in about 27 square miles. In the 1980s it gave up roughly 40 percent of that area, but it later annexed more land, and by 2000 it covered about 38 square miles, more than when it started. Almost all of it is land, and less than one percent of the town's area is water. That is a great deal of room for a small population. In 2020 the town's 4,163 residents and 1,646 housing units worked out to about 108 people per square mile, a density in keeping with its rural character.

## Room to Breathe

Slaughterville does not look much like a suburb, even though it sits in the Oklahoma City metropolitan area. Most residents live in homes on acreages, so the land has stayed rural, and much of the town is wooded. Part of the western boundary runs along the Canadian River, with McClain County on the far bank. U.S. Route 77 runs through town, reaching the center of Oklahoma City 33 miles to the north and Purcell 6 miles to the south. The 2020 census counted 4,163 residents, only 0.6 percent more than in 2010. It is a town of families. In 2020 about 58 percent of households were married couples, about 36 percent included children under 18, and the median age was 39. Trees and open space still define the place, and the town has kept the name that came with it.

## Flight Context

Slaughterville's town center is at about 35.0836°N, 97.2869°W in southern Cleveland County, just south of Norman. U.S. Route 77 is a good line feature running north and south through the town, and the Canadian River marks part of its western edge. Look for a patchwork of wooded acreages rather than a compact town grid. Fly at about 2,500–3,500 ft AGL. McCaslin Airport, just west of town, has no ICAO code (FAA identifier O44). Purcell Municipal Airport (FAA 3O3, no ICAO code) is about 8 nm southwest. University of Oklahoma Westheimer Airport (KOUN) in Norman is about 13 nm northwest. Tinker Air Force Base (KTIK) and Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC) lie 20–25 nm to the north and northwest, so check charts for their airspace before you head toward Oklahoma City. Watch for spring and summer thunderstorms, which are common in central Oklahoma.
