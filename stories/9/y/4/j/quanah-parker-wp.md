# Quanah Parker

Son of a Comanche chief and a captive Texas girl, Quanah Parker fought the U.S. Army at Adobe Walls, then led his people onto the reservation and became a rancher, a friend of Theodore Roosevelt, and a founding voice of the Native American Church.

**Location:** 34.6695, -98.3953

**Categories:** Chiefs of the Comanche, Comanche campaign, Native American Church, People from Comanche County, Oklahoma, Native American history of Texas, Fort Sill

## Introduction

Comanche warriors often took on bolder, more active names as they grew into manhood. Quanah never did. In the Comanche language kwana means "an odor" or "a smell," and it was the name his mother gave him. He kept it at first as a tribute to her after she was taken from the Comanche and returned to the Texans she had been born among. Quanah Parker would become a war leader of the Kwahadi, or Antelope, band, the man who eventually led his people onto the reservation, and then something few could have predicted: a wealthy rancher, a hunting companion of a president, and a spiritual leader. He lies today on Chief's Knoll at Fort Sill, Oklahoma.

## A Mother Taken Twice

Quanah was the son of the Comanche chief Peta Nocona and Cynthia Ann Parker, who had been captured as a child during the 1836 raid on Fort Parker in Texas and raised among the Nokoni band. He was likely born into the Nokoni and grew up among the Kwahadi. In December 1860, Texas forces captured his mother and his little sister, Topsana, in what became known as the Pease River Massacre. What happened to Quanah and his father that day is disputed. One account had Quanah and his brother Pecos escaping on horseback; Quanah himself said that he, Pecos and their father were at a larger camp miles away. After Peta Nocona's death, around 1864, the Nokoni chief Horseback took the two brothers under his wing and taught them the ways of the Comanche warrior. In 1867, as a young man, Quanah came to the treaty talks at Medicine Lodge, Kansas, as an observer, and refused to sign.

## Dawn at Adobe Walls

By the early 1870s the Plains peoples were losing their fight for their land, and hide hunters were slaughtering the buffalo the Comanche lived on. When a Comanche named Isatai'i, claiming to be a medicine man, called the bands together, he and Quanah recruited warriors for raids to avenge slain relatives. Other chiefs pointed to the buffalo hide merchants as the real threat. Between 250 and more than 700 warriors (estimates vary), mostly Comanche, Cheyenne and Kiowa, trusting Isatai'i's promise that his medicine would stop bullets, rode toward the Adobe Walls trading post in the Texas Panhandle. The attack came at dawn on June 27, 1874. But a cracking ridgepole in the saloon had left most of the hunters awake, and the saloonkeeper had reportedly heard the raid was coming, and the hunters' two-foot-thick sod walls and long-range .50-caliber Sharps rifles held. Quanah's horse was shot from under him at five hundred yards. Sheltering behind a buffalo carcass, he was hit by a bullet that ricocheted off his powder horn and lodged between his shoulder blade and neck. The wound was not serious, and his comrades carried him to safety.

## The Road to Fort Sill

Adobe Walls reversed policy in Washington and set off the Red River War. On September 28, 1874, Colonel Ranald Mackenzie and his Tonkawa scouts destroyed the Comanche, Kiowa and Cheyenne camps at Palo Duro Canyon and, the next day, had more than a thousand captured horses shot, the heart of those peoples' wealth and power. With the buffalo nearly exterminated and the army pressing constantly, the Kwahadi surrendered. In late May and early June 1875, Quanah was among the leaders who brought them in to Fort Sill, ending their free life on the southern plains. He never won election as chief. Instead the federal government appointed him principal chief of the entire Comanche Nation, and he helped Mackenzie and the Indian agent settle his people on the Kiowa-Comanche-Apache Reservation.

## Rancher, Deputy and Diplomat

Quanah adapted with remarkable speed. He at first opposed leasing tribal land to Texas ranchers, then changed course, forging close ties with cattlemen such as Charles Goodnight and the Burnett family and building his own herds as early as 1880. He grew wealthy, settled near Cache, and built the Star House, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1970. He became a leading emissary to Congress, was elected deputy sheriff of Lawton in 1902, and acted in silent films, including The Bank Robbery in 1908. In April 1905 Theodore Roosevelt visited him at the Star House, and the two went wolf hunting near Frederick. Quanah pressed the president to let the tribe keep 400,000 acres slated for sale to homesteaders, an argument he eventually lost. That hunt is believed to be one reason Roosevelt proclaimed the Wichita Forest and Game Preserve in June 1905, the forerunner of the Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge.

## Talking to Jesus in the Tipi

Quanah rejected both monogamy and conventional Protestant Christianity. He had eight wives and twenty-five children, some of them adopted, and he always wore his hair long and braided. After a bull gored him in South Texas, a Mexican curandera treated him with peyote tea, and he became one of the first important leaders of the Native American Church, fighting for the legal use of peyote in its ceremonies. "The White Man goes into his church house and talks about Jesus," he taught, "but the Indian goes into his tipi and talks to Jesus." He died at the Star House on February 23, 1911. After his death the leadership title of chief was replaced with chairman, which is why he is remembered as the "Last Chief of the Comanche." In 1957 his remains were moved to Fort Sill, along with his mother's. Towns, highways, a lake in the Wichita Mountains and even an asteroid now carry his name.

## Flight Context

Quanah Parker's grave on Chief's Knoll lies at 34.6695 N, 98.3953 W in the Fort Sill Post Cemetery, just north of Lawton, Oklahoma. From 2,500-4,000 feet AGL, use Lawton to the south and the Wichita Mountains to the northwest as references; Cache, where Quanah ranched and built the Star House, lies to the west. Fort Sill's artillery ranges sit beneath restricted airspace, so check charts and NOTAMs before overflying. Nearest airports: Henry Post Army Airfield (KFSI) on Fort Sill (military use) and Lawton-Fort Sill Regional Airport (KLAW), about two miles south of Lawton. Spring thunderstorms can be severe.
