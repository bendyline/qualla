# Geronimo

The Bedonkohe Apache medicine man whose grief turned him into the most feared fighter of the Southwest spent his final two decades as a prisoner of war, and lies buried at Fort Sill, Oklahoma.

**Location:** 34.6968, -98.3704

**Categories:** Geronimo, People of the Apache Wars, Chiricahua people, 19th-century Native American leaders, Fort Sill, Prisoners of war

## Introduction

Paratroopers shout his name as they leap from aircraft. The tradition began with U.S. Army jumpers inspired by the 1939 film Geronimo, who yelled it to show they had no fear of the drop. Yet the man behind the battle cry spent more than two decades at the end of his life as a prisoner of war, and he died in 1909 at the Fort Sill hospital in Oklahoma, still in the Army's custody. He is buried there, in the Beef Creek Apache Cemetery, among the graves of relatives and other Apache prisoners of war. Between the camp where he was born and the grave where he lies runs one of the hardest stories in the history of the American West.

## A Medicine Man, Not a Chief

Geronimo was born on June 16, 1829, into the Bedonkohe band of the Apache, near Turkey Creek, a tributary of the Gila River in what is now New Mexico, then claimed by Mexico, although the Apache disputed that claim. His grandfather, Mahko, had been chief of the Bedonkohe. After his father died, his mother took him to live with the Tchihende, and he grew up among them. At seventeen he married Alope, of the Nedni-Chiricahua band, and they had three children. Despite his fame, Geronimo was never a chief. He was a medicine man, and other Apache described seeing him sense distant events as they happened, anticipate the future and heal the sick. He was also a superb leader in raiding and war, often at the head of parties of thirty to fifty warriors.

## The Camp Outside Janos

The Apache and Mexico had been locked in a cycle of raids and reprisals for generations; in 1835 Mexico had even placed a bounty on Apache scalps. On March 5, 1851, while the men of Geronimo's band were in the town of Janos, in Chihuahua, trading, 400 Mexican soldiers from Sonora under Colonel José María Carrasco attacked their camp. Geronimo later remembered gathering with the survivors after dark in a thicket by the river: "when all were counted, I found that my aged mother, my young wife, and my three small children were among the slain." The loss shaped the rest of his life. He hated Mexicans from that day on, and for decades he and his followers attacked and killed Mexicans wherever they found them, amassing a record of brutality that few of his contemporaries matched. Raids and retaliation on every side rarely spared women, children or bystanders.

## Breakouts and the Sierra Madre

After the United States took over the region, the Apache were confined to reservations where malaria and other diseases took a heavy toll and corrupt agents sometimes let rations run perilously short. In 1878, 1881 and 1885 Geronimo led his followers off the reservation and into the rugged Sierra Madre Occidental of Mexico, raiding ranches, travelers and villages on both sides of the border. U.S. columns backed by Apache Scouts hunted them relentlessly. In March 1886, during surrender talks at the Cañón de los Embudos, photographer C. S. Fly made the only photographs that exist of Geronimo's surrender, one of them at Geronimo's own request. That night, warned that his people would be murdered once they crossed the border, Geronimo slipped away again. By the end his band numbered just 38 men, women and children, yet over the final campaign they eluded thousands of Mexican and American troops for more than a year. Later in 1886, after Lieutenant Charles Gatewood persuaded him to talk, he surrendered for the last time, to General Nelson Miles at Skeleton Canyon, Arizona.

## A Prisoner on Display

Geronimo repeatedly insisted his surrender had been conditional, while General Oliver O. Howard said it was accepted without condition. He and his people, including the Apache Scouts who had helped track him, were sent first to Fort Sam Houston in San Antonio and then to Fort Pickens in Pensacola, Florida. In that alien climate, The Washington Post later reported, the Apache died "like flies at frost time," while visitors were let into the fort to gawk at him. More than a hundred Chiricahua children were sent to the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania, where many caught tuberculosis and died. Later, held at Fort Sill, Geronimo became a showpiece. Under Army guard he appeared at the 1898 exposition in Omaha, the 1901 Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo and the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair, posing for photographs and selling his crafts. In 1905 the Indian Office brought him to appear in the parade for Theodore Roosevelt's second inauguration. That same year he published his autobiography, and late in life he embraced Christianity.

## The Grave at Beef Creek

In February 1909, riding home, Geronimo was thrown from his horse and lay in the cold all night before a friend found him, gravely ill. He died on February 17. On his deathbed, he told his nephew that he regretted his decision to surrender. His grave has not always rested easily. In 1986 a former San Carlos Apache chairman received an anonymous claim that Yale's Skull and Bones society held Geronimo's skull; the society's attorney denied it, and a 1918 letter that surfaced in 2006 has implications that remain debated. A Fort Sill spokesman has said there is no evidence the bones are anywhere but in the grave site. His name lives on in three towns, a Liberty ship and a 1994 postage stamp, and its use as a code name in the 2011 raid on Osama bin Laden outraged some Native Americans.

## Flight Context

Geronimo's grave in the Beef Creek Apache Cemetery lies at 34.6968 N, 98.3704 W on Fort Sill, just north of Lawton, Oklahoma. From 2,500-4,000 feet AGL, look for the post's buildings and parade grounds, with Lawton to the south and the Wichita Mountains rising to the northwest. Fort Sill's artillery ranges lie beneath restricted airspace; check charts and NOTAMs before overflying. Nearest airports: Henry Post Army Airfield (KFSI) on Fort Sill (military use) and Lawton-Fort Sill Regional Airport (KLAW), about two miles south of Lawton. Spring thunderstorms can be severe.
