# Heck Thomas

Heck Thomas carried a dead Union general's horse to Robert E. Lee as a boy, hunted outlaws across Indian Territory as one of the Three Guardsmen, and ended his career as Lawton's first police chief.

**Location:** 34.6322, -98.4003

**Categories:** Lawmen of the American Old West, United States Marshals, Law enforcement officials from Oklahoma, Confederate States Army soldiers, Lawton, Oklahoma

## Introduction

In 1908 a bystander in southwestern Oklahoma saw what looked like a bank robbery and did not wait to see how it ended. He jumped out a window and ran for the police. The robbery was fake. It was being filmed for a one-reel silent movie called The Bank Robbery, and the posse that chased down the bandits was led by Heck Thomas, who had spent decades catching real outlaws. By then he was the police chief of Lawton. He is buried in the town's Highland Cemetery, and his story reaches back to the Civil War.

## A Courier at Twelve

Henry Andrew Thomas was born in January 1850 in Oxford, Georgia, the youngest child of Martha Ann Bedell (née Fullwood) and Lovick Pierce Thomas. When the Civil War began, he followed his father and his uncle, Edward Lloyd Thomas, into service as a courier while he was still about twelve. Both men were officers in the 35th Georgia Infantry and fought in Virginia. On September 1, 1862, Union General Philip Kearny was killed at the Battle of Chantilly. His black horse, still saddled and bridled, was brought to the rear along with a sword, and young Heck took charge of them. Robert E. Lee ordered the horse and equipment returned through the lines to Kearny's widow. Heck brought them forward himself, and he later called it "one of the proudest minutes of my life." He added, "I had ridden the horse and cared for him up to that time, and I hated to part with him."

## From Atlanta Streets to Judge Parker's Court

In 1863 typhoid fever sent him home to his family in Athens, Georgia. As a young man he clerked at his brother Lovick's store in Atlanta and later joined the city's police. He married Isabel Gray in 1871. Four years later the family moved to Texas, where his cousin Jim Thomas helped him get work as a railroad guard. He was promoted to railroad detective and then joined the Fort Worth Detective Association. His next post took him much farther. He became a deputy U.S. marshal based in Fort Smith, Arkansas, under U.S. District Judge Isaac C. Parker, whose district reached into Indian Territory. In 1889 Thomas went after Ned Christie, a Cherokee man wanted as a suspect in the killing of a U.S. marshal.

## The Three Guardsmen

By 1889 Thomas had teamed up with two other deputy marshals, Chris Madsen and Bill Tilghman. People called them the Three Guardsmen. Over the following decade they were credited with capturing more than three hundred outlaws and killing several, and with bringing law and order to Indian Territory, which became part of the state of Oklahoma in 1907. Thomas became known for pursuit that never let up. Years after his release from prison, Emmett Dalton said that pressure from Thomas was why the Dalton Gang tried to rob two banks at once in Coffeyville, Kansas, on October 5, 1892. They meant to make one big score and leave the territory for a while. Instead four gang members died in the shootout, and Emmett was the only survivor. The Guardsmen were also credited with breaking up the Wild Bunch. Its founder, Bill Doolin, escaped from custody on July 5, 1896, and in August a posse led by Thomas tracked him down and killed him.

## Lawton's First Chief

By 1902 settlers had built many towns across what would become Oklahoma, and Thomas was sent to Lawton. When the town elected him its first police chief, he resigned as a deputy marshal. He held the job for seven years, until his health began to fail. The 1908 film brought his old world and his new one together. Bill Tilghman, his fellow Guardsman, directed it. James Bennie Kent worked the camera for the Oklahoma Natural Mutoscene Company, and scenes were shot in Cache and in the Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge. The Comanche leader Quanah Parker had a small part. Heck Thomas died of Bright's disease in Lawton on August 14 or 15, 1912, at 62. Hollywood kept telling his story afterward. Actor Robert Anderson played him in the 1964 Death Valley Days episode There Was Another Dalton Brother, and he appeared again, played by David Thomas Newman, in the series Wild West Chronicles.

## Flight Context

Heck Thomas's grave in Lawton's Highland Cemetery is at about 34.6322°N, 98.4003°W. Lawton itself is easy to spot, and the Wichita Mountains rise to the northwest, where parts of his 1908 film were shot. Cache, the other filming location, lies about 12 nm west of the city. A pass at 2,500–3,500 ft AGL takes in the city and the mountains together. The cemetery sits only about 1 nm south of Henry Post Army Airfield (KFSI), the military field at Fort Sill. Lawton–Fort Sill Regional Airport (KLAW) is about 4 nm south-southwest. With two airfields this close, and Fort Sill's ranges nearby, check current charts for military and terminal airspace before overflying the area. Visibility over the southern plains is usually good, but thunderstorms can build quickly in spring and summer.
