# Cynthia Ann Parker

Taken by Comanche raiders as a girl in 1836, Cynthia Ann Parker became Naduah, wife of Peta Nocona and mother of Quanah - and was taken again, back into a world she no longer belonged to.

**Location:** 34.6695, -98.3953

**Categories:** Comanche history, Captives of Native Americans, Native American history of Texas, Fort Sill, Women of the American frontier

## Introduction

Twenty-four years separated the two raids that defined Cynthia Ann Parker's life. The first, in 1836, carried her away from her family on the Texas frontier and into the Comanche nation. The second, in 1860, carried her back. In between, the girl from Illinois became Naduah, a Comanche wife and mother, and the story of how she was lost twice has echoed through Texas history, fiction and film ever since. She now rests at the Fort Sill Post Cemetery in Oklahoma, beside her son Quanah, the last free Comanche chief.

## The Raid on Fort Parker

On May 19, 1836, a force of Comanche warriors, with Kiowa and Kichai allies, attacked the Parker family's settlement. Estimates of their number range from 100 to 600. John Parker and his men had not understood the military strength of the Comanche and were unprepared for the speed and ferocity of the assault. They fought a rearguard action to cover some of the fleeing women and children, then fell back into the fort, where the outnumbered defenders were quickly overwhelmed. Five men were killed. The raiders took Cynthia Ann, then a young girl estimated at somewhere between eight and twelve years old, and four other captives back to Comanche country. Over the years, the other four captives were released when ransoms were paid. Cynthia Ann was not among them.

## Naduah

A Tenowish Comanche couple adopted her and raised her as their own daughter, and she became Comanche in every sense. Her Comanche name, rendered Naduah or Nadua, has been translated as "Someone Found." She married Peta Nocona, a chief, and by all appearances it was a happy marriage. They had three children: two sons, Quanah and Pecos, also called Pecan, and a daughter, Topsannah, or Prairie Flower. Quanah would grow up to become the last free Comanche chief. For Naduah, the life she had been taken into had become simply her life, with a husband, children and a people of her own.

## Taken a Second Time

On December 18 or 19, 1860, Texas Rangers led by Lawrence Sullivan "Sul" Ross struck a Comanche hunting camp near the Pease River, a raid that some historians have called the Pease River Massacre. Among those seized were a woman and her infant daughter. The men did not shoot but surrounded and stopped her, and Ross ordered a lieutenant to stay with the woman and child. She was Cynthia Ann Parker, and the girl was Topsannah. Her return to her birth family captured the country's imagination. In 1861 the Texas legislature granted her a square league of land, about 4,400 acres, and a pension of $100 a year for five years, and named two of her cousins as her guardians. None of it made her whole. She was uncomfortable with the attention and never adjusted to her new surroundings, and some said she missed her sons and worried about them.

## A Grave That Moved North

In 1862 her brother Silas Jr. became her guardian and took her to his home in Van Zandt County. When he joined the Confederate Army, she went to live with her sister, Orlena Parker O'Quinn. Topsannah died young and was buried at Edom, in Van Zandt County. The records of Cynthia Ann's own final years are uncertain. Sources place her birth anywhere from 1825 to 1827 and her death from 1864 to 1871. The only record of her death, in the unpublished notebook of Susan Parker St. John, gives March 1871, while her tombstone reads 1870. In 1910 Quanah had his mother's remains moved to Post Oak Mission Cemetery near Cache, Oklahoma. When he died in February 1911, he was buried beside her. In 1957 both were moved to the Fort Sill Post Cemetery, and in 1965 the state of Texas moved Topsannah's body to lie near her mother and brother, reuniting in death a family that life had torn apart.

## A Story Retold

Parker's life has never stopped being told. The rebuilt Fort Parker stands on its original site near Groesbeck, which holds a Christmas festival there every December, and the city of Crowell has held a Cynthia Ann Parker Festival in her memory. Author Michael Blake said that Stands with a Fist, the character in the 1990 film Dances With Wolves, was based on her. Her history is also counted among the stories behind The Searchers, the 1956 John Ford film in which John Wayne plays a frontiersman who spends years hunting for his kidnapped niece. Julia Smith composed a one-act opera, Cynthia Parker, and writers from Dorothy M. Johnson to Lucia St. Clair Robson have imagined her two lives in fiction.

## Flight Context

Cynthia Ann Parker's grave lies at 34.6695 N, 98.3953 W in the Fort Sill Post Cemetery on Fort Sill, just north of Lawton, Oklahoma. From 2,500-4,000 feet AGL, the post's historic core and the city of Lawton to the south are easy references, with the Wichita Mountains rising to the northwest. Much of Fort Sill lies beneath restricted airspace used for artillery; check charts and NOTAMs before overflying. Nearest airports: Henry Post Army Airfield (KFSI) on Fort Sill (military use) and Lawton-Fort Sill Regional Airport (KLAW), about two miles south of Lawton. Spring thunderstorms can be severe.
