# Turner Falls

Honey Creek drops 77 feet into a natural swimming pool in the Arbuckle Mountains, at a waterfall named for a farmer of Scottish descent and his Chickasaw wife's homestead and kept for more than a century as a park by the town of Davis, Oklahoma.

**Location:** 34.4253, -97.1486

**Categories:** Protected areas of Murray County, Oklahoma, Waterfalls of Oklahoma, Parks in Oklahoma, Landforms of Murray County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

Around here, it is considered the tallest waterfall in Oklahoma. The measuring tape calls it a tie. Turner Falls drops 77 feet down the face of the Arbuckle Mountains, a height matched by a waterfall in Natural Falls State Park, but local pride is not much interested in sharing the title. Whatever the record books say, the scene speaks for itself: Honey Creek spilling over the edge into a natural swimming pool, six miles south of the town of Davis, in the rugged hills of south-central Oklahoma.

## Seventy-Seven Feet of Honey Creek

The water comes from Honey Creek, which runs through the Arbuckle Mountains and then tumbles 77 feet in a cascade before landing in a pool at the base. The pool is natural, not built, and it is one of two natural swimming pools within the surrounding park. On a hot Oklahoma summer day, the combination of falling water, cool pool and rock walls makes an easy argument for itself. The same qualities that draw people to the water also demand respect for it, though, and the falls have a darker side that every visitor should know about before wading in.

## Turner's Cabin

The falls carry the name of Mazeppa Thomas Turner, a Virginia-born son of Scottish parents who married Laura J. Johnson, a Chickasaw woman, and became a farmer in what is now Murray County. In 1878 Turner moved to a cabin on the edge of Honey Creek, and he is credited with discovering the waterfall. That word deserves a little care. In 1878 the land around Honey Creek lay within the Chickasaw Nation, decades before Oklahoma statehood, and Turner was living there as the husband of a Chickasaw woman. He may have been the first to put his name to the falls, but it is unlikely he was the first person to stand beside them. Still, the name stuck, and a farmer's surname has been attached to the falls ever since.

## A Century in Town Hands

The town of Davis acquired the falls and the land around them in 1919, making Turner Falls Park a city park. The Oklahoma Historical Society calls it the oldest park within the state, a title the park itself repeats, though Sulphur's Platt National Park, set aside in 1902, would dispute it. Davis ran the park itself from 1919 until 1950, then leased it to outside operators for nearly three decades before resuming control in 1978. The city has run it ever since. The park now spreads across 1,500 acres of Arbuckle Mountain terrain, well beyond the falls themselves. Nature trails wind through the hills, and there are caves to explore along with other striking geological features. Visitors come for swimming, cave exploration, walking trails and picnicking, and overnight camping is available for those who want to linger.

## A Castle and a Caution

One of the park's oddest sights has nothing to do with water. In the 1930s, Dr. Ellsworth Collings, a professor at the University of Oklahoma who later became dean of its School of Education, built himself a summer home here in the form of a castle. The stone structure still stands, and visitors can walk right into it, an academic's medieval fantasy perched in the Oklahoma hills. The park's other lesson is more sobering. Turner Falls has claimed lives, and the pools that make it so inviting can be dangerous. Only experienced swimmers should swim there. Honey Creek has been falling over this edge far longer than anyone has been watching, and it asks only that the people who come to enjoy it take the water seriously.

## Flight Context

Turner Falls lies at approximately 34.4253° N, 97.1486° W on Honey Creek in the Arbuckle Mountains of Murray County, about six miles south of Davis, Oklahoma. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet above ground level, look for the wooded, rocky ridges of the Arbuckles, which stand out from the surrounding farmland; Interstate 35 and U.S. 77 cut south through the mountains beside the park and make an easy line to follow. The falls themselves are small from altitude, so look for the creek valley and the swimming area at its base. Ardmore Municipal Airport (KADM) is roughly 11 miles to the southeast. Spring and early summer bring frequent thunderstorms to south-central Oklahoma, and summer haze can soften views of the ridges.
