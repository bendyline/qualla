# Gene Autry, Oklahoma

A tiny Carter County town that had already been Lou, Dresden and Berwyn renamed itself for the singing cowboy in 1941 before 35,000 people and a live radio audience, only to see its hopes for a famous resident undone by Pearl Harbor three weeks later.

**Location:** 34.2819, -97.0369

**Categories:** Towns in Carter County, Oklahoma, Towns in Oklahoma, Ardmore, Oklahoma micropolitan area

## Introduction

On November 16, 1941, a town of barely more than two hundred people played host to some 35,000. They came to Berwyn, in Carter County, Oklahoma, to watch it give up its name. The crowd, which included Governor Leon Phillips, was there to see the town rechristened in honor of the singer and movie star Gene Autry, and the ceremony went out live on Autry's Melody Ranch radio show. For one afternoon, a small stop on the Santa Fe railroad line had a radio audience listening in. It would not stay that way for long.

## Lou, Dresden, Berwyn

Renaming was nothing new here. The community began as Lou, named by C.C. Henderson for his wife, and its post office opened on July 11, 1883. At the time, the settlement lay in Pickens County of the Chickasaw Nation, decades before Oklahoma statehood. Lou lasted a little over four months. On November 22, 1883, the town became Dresden, and on September 1, 1887, it changed again, to Berwyn, after Berwyn, Pennsylvania. That last choice was part of a pattern. Several towns along the Santa Fe railroad line through the Territory were named, or renamed, for stations on the Main Line of the Pennsylvania Railroad, a bit of eastern railroad geography transplanted onto the prairie. Berwyn held on to its borrowed name for more than half a century, until a famous former telegraph operator gave the town a reason to trade it in.

## The Telegrapher's Return

Gene Autry was not quite a native son, but close. He was born in Tioga, Texas, and his family moved to Oklahoma while he was still an infant. He grew up in the southern Oklahoma towns of Achille and Ravia, and before fame found him he worked as a railroad telegraph operator, by some accounts at a post near Berwyn. By the time he came back, he was a singing star of radio and motion pictures, with his own radio show and a traveling rodeo. Autry bought the 1,200-acre Flying A Ranch on the west edge of Berwyn to serve as headquarters for that rodeo. To the people of Berwyn, it looked like the start of something permanent, and they decided to honor their new neighbor by taking his name.

## Twenty-One Days

The timing seemed almost too good. Eight days before the renaming ceremony, Autry's house in California burned down, and hopes rose that the star would now make his permanent home on the Flying A. Then, just 21 days after the ceremony, Japan attacked Pearl Harbor. The United States went to war, and Autry enlisted in the Army Air Corps. He later sold the Flying A Ranch. The dream of a movie star living on the edge of town ended almost as soon as it began. The name, however, stayed. Eighty-five years later, the town still carries it, a reminder of an autumn when a small Oklahoma community bet its identity on a cowboy singer, only to have history intervene within three weeks.

## Runways and a Schoolhouse

The war brought the town something else. In 1942, a military air base was built just north of Gene Autry, the installation later known as Ardmore Air Force Base, and it provided jobs. Even so, the population slipped, from 227 in the 1940 census to 110 by 1960. Today about 150 people live here. The town's footprint has grown considerably, though, expanding well to the north of its original location since 2000, and the Washita River now cuts across its northeast corner on its way southeast toward Lake Texoma on the Texas border. The past is not hard to find. The old Berwyn school, which once served the town under its previous name, is now the volunteer-run Gene Autry Oklahoma Museum, keeping the memory of the singing cowboy and his brief Oklahoma homecoming alive.

## Flight Context

Gene Autry lies at approximately 34.2819° N, 97.0369° W in Carter County, southern Oklahoma, about 9 miles northeast of downtown Ardmore. The town sits immediately beside Ardmore Municipal Airport (KADM), the former military air base built in 1942, whose runways make the best visual landmark from 2,000 to 3,000 feet above ground level. The Washita River winds across the northeast corner of the town and continues southeast toward Lake Texoma on the Texas border, which is visible as a large body of water on a clear day. The Arbuckle Mountains rise to the north. Southern Oklahoma is prone to spring and early-summer thunderstorms, and summer haze can limit long-distance visibility.
