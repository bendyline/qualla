# Paragould meteorite

Before dawn on February 17, 1930, a fireball seen across five states dropped a roughly 800-pound stone into a Greene County farm field, the second largest witnessed meteorite fall ever recovered in the United States.

**Location:** 36.0000, -90.6583

## Introduction

At about 4:08 on the morning of February 17, 1930, the sky over northeastern Arkansas lit up. People as far away as Illinois, Indiana, Missouri and Kansas saw the fireball streak overhead, and some of those who watched it assumed they were seeing an airplane going down in flames. It was something far older. A stone from space was breaking apart as it plunged toward the farmland around Paragould, and when the largest piece was finally found, early reports put it at roughly 800 pounds (the Field Museum later recorded 745). Counting all its pieces, it remains the second largest witnessed meteorite fall ever recovered in the United States, surpassed only by the Norton County meteorite.

## A Hole in Fletcher's Field

The fireball did not arrive in one piece. The meteorite split as it fell, scattering fragments across the countryside, and finding them took time. Four weeks after the fall, W. H. Hodges came upon the largest one on the farm of Joe H. Fletcher, south of Bethel Church off Highway 358, south of Paragould. It lay at the bottom of an eight-foot hole it had punched into the ground. Freeing it was hard work: five men and a team of horses needed three hours to dig the stone out. The mass they hauled up measured 41 inches long, 24 inches wide and 16 inches thick. Elsewhere, farmer Raymond Parkinson found a smaller stone near Finch, Arkansas, within hours of the fall; it weighs about 33 kilograms, and a final fragment of just 3.75 kilograms eventually turned up.

## The Professor's Gamble

Word of the find reached Harvey H. Nininger, a professor at McPherson College in Kansas. He wanted the Paragould stone badly enough to bid $3,600 for it, a huge sum in 1930. The bet paid off. Nininger sold the meteorite for $6,200 to Stanley Field, the Field Museum's president, who presented it to Chicago's Field Museum of Natural History, which still owns it. The smaller fragments went their own ways, one to Washington, D.C., and another to New York. For more than half a century afterward, the main mass, a chondrite, one of the stony types of meteorite, belonged to a museum hundreds of miles from the Arkansas field where it had landed.

## The Long Way Home

In 1988 the meteorite came back to Arkansas, on loan to the University of Arkansas in Fayetteville. It went first to the University Museum and then, after November 2003, under the care of the Arkansas Center for Space and Planetary Sciences. For a stretch it was displayed in the university's Mullins Library, until April 11, 2008, when it moved to the Arkansas Center for Space and Planetary Sciences building. Paragould itself had to wait longer. In 2023, after some 35 years in Fayetteville, the stone returned to the town that gave it its name, arranged by the Paragould Chamber of Commerce with the Field Museum. It went on display at the Greene County Museum, timed so that the meteorite would be home for the total solar eclipse that crossed the area on April 8, 2024. Nearly a century after it fell, the sky had brought people back to look at it again.

## Flight Context

The fall area is placed at about 36.000°N, 90.658°W, near Finch southwest of Paragould in Greene County, Arkansas, an area-level point because published accounts differ on exactly where the stones landed. From 2,500-3,500 feet MSL, the town of Paragould lies about 10 miles to the east-northeast on the wooded, north-south line of Crowley's Ridge, which stands out against the flat Delta farmland. Highway 358 runs through the countryside southwest of town where the largest piece came down. Kirk Field (KPGR) serves Paragould, and Jonesboro Municipal Airport (KJBR) lies about 12 miles to the south. Visibility is usually good over the open fields, with haze and summer thunderstorms the main concerns.
