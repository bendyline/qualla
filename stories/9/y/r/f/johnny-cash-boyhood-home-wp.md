# Johnny Cash Boyhood Home

Farm No. 266 in the New Deal's Dyess Colony was where young J.R. Cash picked cotton, weathered floods and lost his brother, and the house now stands as a museum to the life that shaped his songs.

**Location:** 35.5974, -90.2450

**Categories:** Johnny Cash, Historic house museums in Arkansas, National Register of Historic Places in Mississippi County, Arkansas, New Deal, Arkansas State University

## Introduction

Before it was the Johnny Cash Boyhood Home, it was Farm No. 266. The number came from the federal government, which built the house in 1934 as part of a project to give poor families land they might one day own. In March 1935, Ray and Carrie Cash arrived to claim it with their children, including a three-year-old boy the family called J.R. He would live there until he finished high school in 1950. The cotton, the floods and the grief of those years would all find their way into the songs of the man the world came to know as Johnny Cash.

## Colonization Project No. 1

The house stood in Dyess Colony, a New Deal settlement of the Franklin Roosevelt administration founded in Mississippi County, Arkansas, in 1934. The colony took its name from William Reynolds Dyess, a Mississippi native and Arkansas's first WPA administrator, who brought the idea of supplying farms to families in poverty to Harry Hopkins. It was called "Colonization Project No. 1." Some 16,000 acres were set aside for 500 families, and the first of them began arriving in October 1934.

Every family in Dyess depended on cotton. None were sharecroppers, which made the prospect of one day buying their farms from the government a real one. The Cash house is one of the few from the colony still standing.

## Singing in the Rows

The Cash family settled on 20 acres of the colony. Johnny Cash, born on February 26, 1932, was the fourth of Ray and Carrie Cash's seven children, and by the age of five J.R. was already working in his father's cotton fields, singing along with his family as they worked. The Great Depression was grinding on, and the family went through many hardships. The government eventually stopped funding the colony scheme, but the Cashes kept farming their land. Years later, Cash drew on those days of picking cotton for songs of his own, among them "Pickin' Time."

## High Water and Heartbreak

The land that promised a future could also turn on the family. In 1937 the Mississippi and Ohio rivers flooded, and the water came so high that the Cashes had to evacuate. Everyone except Ray left for Kingsland and stayed away until it was safe to return. The farm flooded on at least two occasions, and those experiences inspired Cash's song "Five Feet High and Rising."

The hardest loss came in 1944. In May, Johnny's older brother Jack, just 14, fell into a table saw at work and was mortally wounded. He died of his wounds about a week later. It was a horrific personal tragedy for a family that had already endured the Depression and the floods, and it came while J.R. was still a boy living in the farmhouse.

## The House Kept

Arkansas State University acquired the home in 2011, and its Heritage Sites Office now runs it as a small museum, Historic Dyess Colony: Johnny Cash Boyhood Home. In 2018 the house was listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

Visitors come to see a modest farmhouse, but what it preserves is larger than one family. It is a surviving piece of a government experiment that tried to lift 500 poor families out of poverty by giving them land to work and a chance to own it. For the Cash family, that chance came with cotton rows, rising rivers and a son lost too young, and a boy who carried all of it into his music.
