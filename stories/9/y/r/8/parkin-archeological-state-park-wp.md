# Parkin Archeological State Park

Where the Tyronza River meets the St. Francis in eastern Arkansas, a moated, palisaded Mississippian town once thrived for three centuries, and many archaeologists believe it is Casqui, the town Hernando de Soto's expedition reached in 1541.

**Location:** 35.2771, -90.5574

## Introduction

"A very good one, thoroughly well stockaded," Rodrigo Ranjel, one of Hernando de Soto's chroniclers, wrote of Pacaha, the neighboring town the Spaniards reached after leaving Casqui. Its walls, he noted, "were furnished with towers and a ditch round about, for the most part full of water which flows in by a canal from the river; and this ditch was full of excellent fish of divers kinds." Fortified towns like it lined this river country, and many archaeologists believe Casqui itself stood here, at the confluence of the St. Francis and Tyronza rivers in eastern Arkansas. The great platform mound still rises beside the St. Francis, and a single glass bead pulled from the soil in 1966 helps tie this quiet spot in Cross County to one of the earliest European journeys into the American interior.

## Capital of the Rivers

Between roughly 1350 and 1650, the Parkin site was home to a palisaded village covering about 17 acres. It was the leading town of what archaeologists call the Parkin phase, a Late Mississippian culture of around 21 villages strung along the St. Francis and Tyronza rivers, and it gives that culture its name. Smaller towns ranked below it in a hierarchy that suggests paramount chiefs governing allied villages. The soil here was not especially rich, so the location was likely chosen for control of river traffic and trade. At the heart of the town stood a platform mound about 21 feet tall, with a projecting terrace, facing a central plaza where people gathered for ceremonies and games such as chunkey. Six smaller mounds and numerous wattle-and-daub houses with thatched roofs, aligned to the mound and plaza, filled out the plan.

## Walls, Moat and Harvest

Life at Parkin was shaped by the threat of war. A palisade with bastions and archer slots guarded the village, and a large moat connected to the St. Francis River wrapped around it. Each day people left the walls to farm, hunt and gather, then returned to the safety of the town at night. Their fields grew maize, beans, squash and sunflowers, and much of the planting and processing was done by women, who also gathered wild pecans and persimmons. Maize went into large above-ground cribs to last the year. Men hunted deer, turkey and rabbit, while the rivers and moat yielded catfish, drum, alligator gar, turtles and mussels. The Spanish chroniclers were struck by what they saw, describing the region as intensely cultivated and the most populous they had encountered in La Florida, and recording repeated gifts of fish from Casqui's people.

## Faces in Clay

Most of the pottery made at Parkin was everyday ware, and an early archaeologist, Clarence Bloomfield Moore, dismissed St. Francis River pottery as "rude and scanty." He missed a great deal. Parkin potters also produced striking effigy vessels: underwater panthers, fish and dogs, red-and-white spiral bottles, and five human head effigy pots. Built by hand from strips of clay and colored with slips of galena for white and hematite for red, the head pots offer something rare, an artist's view of what the people of this town may have looked like. Burials often held a bowl and a bottle, offerings placed alongside the dead.

## A Bead, a Bell and a Cross

In 1966, excavators at Parkin recovered a Spanish glass bead matching the seven-layer chevron beads the de Soto expedition carried, along with a brass object known as a Clarksdale bell. The bell lay in a child's burial with four pottery vessels of known Parkin phase types, placing Spanish goods in a datable context at one of the few southeastern sites where that is possible. The chronicles say that in 1541 de Soto had a great cross raised atop the chief's mound at Casqui. Archaeologists found a large charred posthole at the summit of the main mound in 1977, and while its connection to the cross cannot be proven beyond doubt, it remains under study. By the 1670s, the Quapaw people were living in this part of the country.

## Guarding the Mound

The Parkin site was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1964 and added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1966. Arkansas began developing the state park in 1991, and its visitor center complex was dedicated on October 29, 1994. Inside, a museum and exhibit hall present the story of the town, and a research station run with the Arkansas Archeological Survey keeps the work of discovery going on site. The park also holds a newer layer of history: the restored Northern Ohio School House, which served the children of workers at a sawmill that operated here in the early 1900s.

## Flight Context

Parkin Archeological State Park lies at about 35.277°N, 90.557°W on the northern edge of the town of Parkin in Cross County, Arkansas. From 2,500-3,500 feet MSL, look for the point where the Tyronza River joins the St. Francis River; the main platform mound stands beside the St. Francis. The surrounding Mississippi Delta is flat farmland, with the wooded line of Crowley's Ridge rising to the west. Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM, formerly Memphis International) is roughly 37 miles to the east-southeast, and West Memphis Municipal Airport (KAWM) is about 21 miles to the east-southeast. Summer haze is common over the Delta, but the flat terrain makes navigation straightforward.
