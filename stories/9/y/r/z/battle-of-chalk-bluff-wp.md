# Battle of Chalk Bluff

In May 1863 a Confederate rear guard held a ridge above the St. Francis River long enough for Marmaduke's cavalry to bridge the stream and escape, winning a fight that still ended his Missouri expedition.

**Location:** 36.4773, -90.1588

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Arkansas, Battles of the American Civil War in Missouri, Trans-Mississippi theater of the American Civil War, 1863 in Arkansas, Chalk Bluff, Arkansas, Marmaduke's Second Expedition into Missouri

## Introduction

The St. Francis River runs between steep banks of chalky white clay here, and in the spring of 1863 those banks were a serious problem. Confederate General John S. Marmaduke needed to get an entire cavalry division across the river, and the pale, sheer bluffs made fording difficult for horsemen. Behind him, Union troops were closing in. What followed on May 1 and 2, 1863, was the Battle of Chalk Bluff, sometimes called the Skirmish at Chalk Bluff: a fight Marmaduke won on the field, yet one that brought his expedition to an end.

## A Retreat Along the Ridge

Marmaduke had left his camps in Arkansas that spring with about 5,000 men, a good share of them unarmed or dismounted, bound for southeastern Missouri. The raid, his second expedition into the state, went badly. After sharp fighting at the Battle of Cape Girardeau, he was defeated, and on April 27 he began withdrawing toward Helena, Arkansas. His column followed a road along Crowley's Ridge, a long rise that protected his flanks, because the country on either side was mainly marsh. Close behind came U.S. Brigadier General William Vandever, commanding the Second Division of the Army of the Frontier, whose men pursued the Confederates through Missouri all the way to Chalk Bluff. There, just northwest of St. Francis in what is now Clay County, Arkansas, Marmaduke planned to cross the river.

## Buying Time on Gravel Hill

Crossing would take time, so Marmaduke built a shield. His engineers and pioneers set to work on a bridge strong enough to carry the whole division, while a rear guard spread along the ridge to protect them. The first defensive line formed at the hamlet of Four Mile. About a mile and a half back, at Gravel Hill on the crest of the ridge above the river, Marmaduke posted his reserves as a second line. His men began digging entrenchments to hold off any Union attack. The plan depended on those soldiers standing their ground long enough for the builders to finish.

## Cannon Over the Shoulder

The fighting began on May 1 and carried into the next day. Vandever's men could not drive the Confederates from the heights. The rear guard held, delaying the Union pursuers long enough for the bridge to be completed, and Marmaduke's main body crossed the St. Francis. Confederate sources, including Marmaduke himself and a few letters from enlisted soldiers, describe the fighting differently, stating that the Union side also suffered significant casualties trying to take the entrenched ridges. By their account, General Thompson moved artillery across the river by raft on the first night, so that Union troops attacking the ridges took cannon fire coming over their right shoulder and raking down the long line of their formation.

## A Win That Ended the Campaign

The raid had already failed at Cape Girardeau, and the escape across the river closed it: Marmaduke returned to camp. In some respects he had won the battle tactically, but the Union claimed a strategic victory, since his spring offensive was over. The crossing remained worth fighting for. Later in the war, both armies sent raiding parties to control it, and a few minor skirmishes broke out on the same ground. Today the small Chalk Bluff Battlefield Park, listed on the National Register of Historic Places, preserves a portion of the field. The Chalk Bluff Hiking Trail crosses parts of the battlefield, with interpretive wayside markers and panels along the way. The communities whose names mark the battle, Chalk Bluff, Four Mile and Gravel Hill, no longer exist.

## Flight Context

Chalk Bluff Battlefield Park lies at 36.4773°N, 90.1588°W, where Crowley's Ridge meets the St. Francis River on the Arkansas–Missouri line, just northwest of the town of St. Francis in Clay County, Arkansas. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, follow the St. Francis River and look for the long, narrow rise of Crowley's Ridge standing above the flat lowlands; the small park itself is hard to pick out without those references. Nearby airports include Malden Regional (KMAW) about 11 nm northeast, Kennett Memorial (KTKX) about 16 nm south-southeast, and Poplar Bluff Regional Business Airport (KPOF) about 20 nm north-northwest. Terrain is low throughout the region; clear conditions make the ridge line easiest to trace.
