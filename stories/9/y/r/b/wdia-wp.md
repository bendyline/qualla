# WDIA

A struggling Memphis station found its voice when it turned to the Black listeners no one else served, becoming the first U.S. station programmed entirely for African Americans and a launchpad for B.B. King.

**Location:** 35.2681, -90.0175

**Categories:** African-American radio, Radio stations in Memphis, Tennessee, 1947 establishments in Tennessee, Music of Memphis, Tennessee, Civil rights history

## Introduction

On October 25, 1948, a Memphis high-school teacher and syndicated columnist named Nat D. Williams went on the air with a program called Tan Town Jubilee. It was one of the first radio shows in the United States pitched to Black listeners, and it came from a station that had been going nowhere. WDIA soon became the first radio station in the country programmed entirely for African Americans. Before long its signal would carry Memphis voices and Memphis music through the Mississippi Delta, all the way to the Gulf Coast.

## An Audience Hiding in Plain Sight

WDIA signed on June 7, 1947, from studios on Union Avenue, broadcasting at 730 on the dial. Its owners, John Pepper and Bert Ferguson, were both white, and their first format was a grab bag of country and western, light pop, "homemaker shows," network programs, soap operas and even classical music. The station did not do well. The turn came when Ferguson learned about "targeted programming" and recognized one audience in Memphis that no other station served: half of the listeners who could hear WDIA's signal were African American. The station hired Williams, who took to WDIA's airwaves in 1948 and is described in various accounts as the first publicly identified Black disc jockey in Memphis or in the South, and he showed what that audience had been missing. Advertisers, suddenly aware of a "new" market among Black listeners, followed.

## Fifty Thousand Watts

In June 1954, WDIA was licensed to raise its power from 250 watts to 50,000, a jump that moved it to 1070 kHz. The stronger signal reached the Mississippi Delta's dense African American population and could be heard from the Missouri Bootheel to the Gulf Coast. By one estimate, WDIA reached 10 percent of the African American population of the United States. Its lineup mixed the practical with the flamboyant. Ernest Brazzell gave crop advice, while Maurice "Hot Rod" Hulbert and Theo "Bless My Bones" Wade brought personality to the microphone. Martha Jean Steinberg, later a WJLB star as Martha Jean "The Queen," was known here as "Princess Premium Stuff." Robert Thomas earned his DJ job, and the name "Honeyboy," by winning a citywide amateur competition. Ford Nelson was still working as an active gospel DJ on WDIA as of 2013.

## Where B.B. King Found His Crowd

WDIA hired musicians early in their careers and played their music to listeners across the Delta. B.B. King joined the station in early 1949 with a daily 15-minute show, promoting first a patent medicine called Pep-Ti-Kon and later Lucky Strike cigarettes, WDIA's first major advertiser. The following year he took over an afternoon DJ slot previously hosted by Hot Rod Hulbert. King credited those days with building his audience and launching his career, and he described the station as giving him a sense of freedom. Rufus Thomas also got his start working at WDIA, and Elvis Presley was greatly influenced by what the station played.

## More Than a Signal

The station turned its reach back toward its own community through the WDIA Goodwill Fund, created to help and empower Black communities. Its first job was practical: providing transportation to school for disabled Black children. Over time the fund expanded to college scholarships, boys clubs, 125 Little League teams for Memphis and neighboring communities, and help with low-cost supplemental housing. A.C. Williams put the total raised at more than $900,000 over the years. In the 1970s and 1980s, WDIA's owners also ran KDIA, a similarly formatted station in the San Francisco Bay Area, and in 1996 Clear Channel Communications, now iHeartMedia, bought WDIA. Its studios today are in Southeast Memphis, while its transmitter site is in North Memphis. In 2020 the station and the Beale Street Historic District were added to the United States Civil Rights Trail.

## Flight Context

These coordinates, 35.2681°N, 90.0175°W, mark WDIA's transmitter site in North Memphis; the station's studios are in Southeast Memphis. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, the Mississippi River to the west and downtown Memphis roughly 8 miles to the south are the easiest references. Millington-Memphis Airport (KNQA) lies about 9 nm northeast, West Memphis Municipal (KAWM) about 13 nm southwest across the river, and Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM, renamed from Memphis International in 2026) about 14 nm south; the city sits beneath Memphis Class B airspace, so coordinate with approach control. Clear days give the best view of the river bends north of the city.
