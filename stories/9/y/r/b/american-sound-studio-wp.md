# American Sound Studio

From a North Memphis studio now marked only by a sign outside a discount store, Chips Moman and a house band called the Memphis Boys cut roughly 120 chart hits, including Elvis Presley's last Hot 100 number one.

**Location:** 35.1638, -90.0367

**Categories:** Music of Memphis, Tennessee, Recording studios in Tennessee, Former recording studios, Elvis Presley, History of Memphis, Tennessee

## Introduction

The historical marker stands outside a Family Dollar store. Little else on this stretch of Thomas Street in North Memphis hints that the building once here produced a run of hits few studios have matched. Between 1967 and 1971, roughly 120 songs recorded at American Sound Studio made Billboard's top 100. During one week, a quarter of that chart came from this single studio and featured the same band backing a spread of very different singers. Elvis Presley recorded "Suspicious Minds" here, and Dusty Springfield, Neil Diamond and Wilson Pickett all cut records with the same crew.

## Two Rooms, Running Day and Night

Producer Chips Moman and his partner Don Crews opened American Sound Studio at 827 Thomas Street in 1964. Within a few years demand outgrew the room, and Moman's musicians spilled into other studios around Memphis, among them Royal, Ardent and Sounds of Memphis, and even crossed into Alabama to work at Fame Studios in Muscle Shoals. A second studio would keep most productions in house. Moman and Crews found one across town at 2272 Deadrick Avenue: Onyx, built in 1967 with construction commissioned by Steve Sholes of RCA Records, and described as the first purpose-built recording studio in Memphis. They bought it in December 1968 and renamed it American Recording Studio East, also called the Annex. Its stereo echo chambers and large tracking floor suited horn and orchestra sessions. Matching equipment let tapes move easily between the two buildings, and both could run all day and all night.

## The Band With a Street Address

The house band went by two names: formally the 827 Thomas Street Band, informally the Memphis Boys. Reggie Young played guitar, Tommy Cogbill and Mike Leech played bass, Gene Chrisman sat at the drums, Bobby Wood took piano and Bobby Emmons played organ. Their range was remarkable. The same players who laid down Joe Tex's "Skinny Legs and All" could turn around and cut the Box Tops' "Cry Like a Baby," Merrilee Rush's "Angel of the Morning," Neil Diamond's "Sweet Caroline" or Danny O'Keefe's "Good Time Charlie's Got the Blues." The bass that rides out Dusty Springfield's "Son of a Preacher Man" is Cogbill's; the album it came from, Dusty in Memphis, later received a Grammy Hall of Fame Award. The band also backed flutist Herbie Mann on his 1969 jazz-rock album Memphis Underground, and in 2007 its members entered the Musicians Hall of Fame and Museum in Nashville.

## A Memphis Winter for the King

In January and February 1969, Elvis Presley recorded an extensive run of tracks at American during the period known as his comeback. The first to be released was "In the Ghetto," unusual in his repertoire for its social commentary on the cycle of crime and poverty. Then came "Suspicious Minds," cut in January with Moman producing and engineering. It became Presley's last number one on the Billboard Hot 100 and a centerpiece of the live performances he began that year. Four charting singles came out of the sessions, along with two critically acclaimed 1969 albums, From Elvis in Memphis and the studio portion of From Memphis to Vegas/From Vegas to Memphis. Strings and horns were scored at the Annex, where Mike Leech and Glen Spreen did most of the arranging, "sweetening" the tracks.

## Hits That Came in Sideways

Some of American's most successful records came with stories attached. In 1967 Dan Penn produced the Box Tops' "The Letter" here with a 16-year-old lead singer named Alex Chilton, dubbing in the sound of an airplane taking off from a special-effects record. Under two minutes long, it held Billboard's number-one spot for four weeks and sold more than a million copies. Joe Tex's "I Gotcha" had an even stranger path. Tex re-recorded the song at American in late 1971 and released it as the B-side of "A Mother's Prayer," but radio DJs flipped the single over. "I Gotcha" climbed to number one on the R&B chart and number two on the pop chart, sold around three million copies and gave Tex his first major hit in five years.

## What the Wrecking Ball Spared

Moman and Crews dissolved their partnership in 1972. The Thomas Street studio closed that year and was torn down in 1989; Moman later opened American studios in Atlanta and then Nashville, but neither remains. The Deadrick Avenue building, restored to its old name of Onyx, kept working. From 1978 to 1988 it was home to former Stax engineer and producer Allen Jones and the Bar-Kays, and from 1990 to 2005, as Easley McCain Recording, it hosted the White Stripes, Wilco, Loretta Lynn, Jeff Buckley and, closing a circle, Alex Chilton. A major fire struck in 2005. Producer Brad Dunn, backed by his father Robert and his uncle Donald "Duck" Dunn, campaigned to save it and sought input from Moman and from Don and Erick Crews. In 2011 the restored studio reopened as American Recording Studio, the only physical location of the American legacy still standing.

## Flight Context

The former American Sound Studio site at 827 Thomas Street sits at 35.1638°N, 90.0367°W in North Memphis, a little over a mile north-northeast of downtown and east of the Mississippi River. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, the river and the downtown Memphis skyline just to the south-southwest are the easiest references; the site itself is now a Family Dollar store with a county historical marker and is not distinctive from the air. The surviving American East building on Deadrick Avenue lies across town at 2272 Deadrick Avenue. Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM, renamed from Memphis International in 2026) is about 8 nm south-southeast; its Class B airspace covers the city, so coordinate with approach control. Millington-Memphis Airport (KNQA) lies about 14 nm northeast and West Memphis Municipal (KAWM) about 10 nm west across the river. Summer haze can reduce visibility over the Mississippi Valley.
