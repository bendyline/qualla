# WHBQ (AM)

Long before it became Memphis's sports-talk station, WHBQ was where disc jockey Dewey Phillips played an Elvis Presley record on the radio for the very first time.

**Location:** 35.2533, -90.0475

**Categories:** Radio stations in Memphis, Tennessee, RKO General, Sports radio stations in the United States, Radio stations established in 1925, Music of Memphis, Tennessee

## Introduction

Tune to 560 AM in Memphis today and you will hear sports talk, Memphis Redbirds baseball and Ole Miss football. It takes some imagining to hear the station's earlier life, when a nighttime disc jockey built its reputation on rhythm and blues, and when, in 1954, WHBQ became the station that put an Elvis Presley record on the radio for the first time. WHBQ has been on the air since 1925, and across more than a century it has reinvented itself again and again.

## Climbing the Dial

WHBQ first signed on March 25, 1925, making it one of the earliest stations in Memphis, with studios in the historic Hotel Claridge. Through the 1930s it broadcast on 1370 kilocycles at a modest 100 watts. When the North American Regional Broadcasting Agreement took effect in 1941, the station shifted to 1400 kHz at 250 watts and moved its studios to the Hotel Gayoso. During the 1940s it joined the Mutual Broadcasting System as a network affiliate, and at the end of that decade it settled at 560 kHz, the spot it still holds. With the move came a boost in power to 5,000 watts by day and 1,000 watts at night.

## Red, Hot and Blue

In March 1954, General Teleradio, the broadcasting arm of the General Tire and Rubber Company and later known as RKO General, bought the WHBQ stations. The new owners eventually turned the AM station into a leading Top 40 outlet, but its reputation was built by one disc jockey. Dewey Phillips played rhythm and blues on his nighttime show, "Red, Hot and Blue," and in 1954 he cued up a recording no radio audience had heard before: "That's All Right" by Elvis Presley, then a young truck driver and budding musician. It marked the first time an Elvis recording was broadcast on the radio.

## The Farm Team

For many years WHBQ was considered a "farm team" for RKO's bigger stations. Young, ambitious disc jockeys, among them Rick Dees and future game-show host Wink Martindale, worked the Memphis microphones hoping to be called up to RKO's larger markets in Boston, New York City and San Francisco, or to KHJ in Los Angeles, which introduced the Boss Radio Top 40 format in 1965. In the 1960s, under programmer Bill Drake, WHBQ became Boss Radio, known for the hottest hits and the most popular DJs. The competition for listeners had a darker chapter. In 1977 George Klein, a disc jockey and former program director, was found guilty of conspiracy in a scheme involving stolen Arbitron ratings diaries. He admitted filling out diaries to inflate WHBQ's ratings and was sentenced to 60 days in federal prison.

## Losing the Music, Finding the Game

By the early 1980s the once-mighty Top 40 station could no longer keep up with the growing popularity of FM contemporary music stations. WHBQ played oldies from 1981 to 1983, then switched to full-service talk with local hosts and syndicated shows from NBC Talknet and ABC TalkRadio. In 1988 RKO sold the station to Flinn Broadcasting, a local company, which tried oldies again, then country music, and even heavy metal late at night. In 1992 WHBQ went all sports. It carried CBS Sports Radio and then NBC Sports Radio, switching to Fox Sports Radio when NBC dropped full-time sports programming at the end of 2018. Since October 2020 it has also been simulcast on 98.5 FM, and since September 2026 on 107.5 FM, and it remains the Memphis home of Ole Miss Rebels football and men's basketball and the main outlet for the Memphis Redbirds.

## Flight Context

WHBQ's listed coordinates, 35.2533°N, 90.0475°W, fall in the northern part of Memphis, about seven miles north of downtown; the station's studios and transmitter are both within the city. The station itself is not distinctive from the air. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, use the Mississippi River to the west and the downtown Memphis skyline to the south as references. Millington-Memphis Airport (KNQA) is about 11 nm northeast, West Memphis Municipal (KAWM) about 12 nm southwest across the river, and Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM, renamed from Memphis International in 2026) about 13 nm south-southeast, beneath Memphis Class B airspace. Haze over the Mississippi Valley can limit visibility on summer afternoons.
