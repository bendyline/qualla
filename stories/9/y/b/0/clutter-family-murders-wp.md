# Clutter family murders

In November 1959, two paroled ex-convicts chasing a nonexistent safe killed Herb, Bonnie, Nancy and Kenyon Clutter in their farmhouse near Holcomb, Kansas, a crime Truman Capote made famous in In Cold Blood.

**Location:** 37.9851, -100.9996

**Categories:** 1959 murders in the United States, Finney County, Kansas, Murder in Kansas, In Cold Blood, True crime

## Introduction

Herb Clutter did not own a safe. A prosperous farmer outside the small western Kansas community of Holcomb, he transacted his business by check, and it was generally known in the area that he seldom kept much cash at home. But a former farmhand told a prison cellmate otherwise, and on that false story two paroled ex-convicts built a plan. In the early morning of November 15, 1959, they murdered Herb Clutter, his wife Bonnie, and their teenage children, Nancy and Kenyon, in the family's farmhouse. Truman Capote's book In Cold Blood would make the case famous. Before that, it was the loss of four people a small town knew well.

## The Clutters of Holcomb

Herb Clutter was 48, his wife Bonnie 45. Their two oldest daughters, Eveanna and Beverly, had already moved out to start their adult lives. Accounts that described Bonnie as long incapacitated by depression and physical ailments were later disputed. At home were the two youngest. Kenyon, 15, attended Holcomb High School. His sister Nancy, 16, was a junior there, a straight-A student who played clarinet in the school band, went to church regularly and was active in 4-H. She loved horseback riding, baking, needlepoint, music and sewing, and younger girls often sought her out for help with their cooking, their baking or their music lessons. Well liked and outgoing, she was the kind of teenager a small farming town notices and remembers.

## A Rumor of Cash

Richard "Dick" Hickock and Perry Edward Smith had recently been paroled from the Kansas State Penitentiary. Floyd Wells, a former cellmate of Hickock's, had once worked as a farmhand for Herb Clutter, and he told Hickock that Clutter kept large amounts of cash in a safe. It was not true. Hickock nonetheless hatched a plan to steal the safe and start a new life in Mexico, and he recruited Smith, another former cellmate, to join him. According to Capote, Hickock called it "a cinch, the perfect score." On the evening of November 14, 1959, the two men drove more than 400 miles across Kansas. In the early hours of the next morning they reached Holcomb, found the Clutter home and walked in through an unlocked door while the family slept.

## Less Than Fifty Dollars

There was no safe to find. After searching the house and binding the family, the two men killed all four Clutters and fled. They took with them Kenyon's Zenith portable radio, a pair of Herb's binoculars and less than $50 in cash, presumed to be what was left of a $60 check Herb had cashed. Who killed whom was never fully settled. In his oral confession, Smith said Hickock had killed Nancy and Bonnie, but he refused to sign it. According to Capote, Smith later wanted to take responsibility for all four killings because he felt "sorry for Dick's mother." Hickock always maintained that Smith had killed all four. By the killers' own account, Kenyon had been killed by Smith.

## Six Years and a Book

Hickock and Smith were arrested six weeks after the murders, found guilty, sentenced to death and executed by the state of Kansas on April 14, 1965. Even before the arrests, Truman Capote had learned of the crime and traveled to Kansas with his childhood friend and fellow author Harper Lee. Together they interviewed residents and investigators and filled thousands of pages with notes. Capote spent six years on the book. Published in 1966, In Cold Blood was an instant success and became one of the best-selling true crime books in publishing history, commonly ranked second behind Vincent Bugliosi's Helter Skelter. Films followed, from the 1967 adaptation to Capote in 2005, which won Philip Seymour Hoffman the Academy Award for Best Actor. Yet the heart of the story remains the family: a farmer who paid by check, a mother, a boy of fifteen, and a girl who helped younger neighbors learn to bake.
