# El Cuartelejo

In a Kansas creek canyon, Pueblo refugees fleeing Spanish rule built a seven-room stone house among Apache hosts, leaving behind the northernmost pueblo known.

**Location:** 38.6781, -100.9142

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in Kansas, National Historic Landmarks in Kansas, Dwellings of the Pueblo peoples, Apache, Tourist attractions in Scott County, Kansas

## Introduction

The Spanish named it El Cuartelejo, from a word meaning an old building or barracks, and the name hints at how out of place it must have seemed: walls of stone and adobe standing beside a creek on the open plains, far from the Pueblo towns of New Mexico where such buildings belonged. The ruin sits in Ladder Creek Canyon, inside Lake Scott State Park, about 13 miles north of Scott City, Kansas. It is the northernmost pueblo known and the only one ever found in Kansas, recorded by archaeologists as the Scott County Pueblo site, 14SC1. Its builders were refugees, and the story of why they came this far is the story of the place.

## Apache Country First

Before anyone laid those stones, the canyon had residents. Apache people of the Dismal River culture, an archaeological tradition generally dated to about 1650 through 1750, lived at the Kansas site, and the bands known as the Cuartelejo Apache ranged well into what is now eastern Colorado, along the Arkansas and Purgatoire Rivers near where Bent's Fort would rise in the 19th century, and north along the Smoky Hill and Republican Rivers. They lived in rancherías, clusters of huts or houses arranged around a central plaza, leaving them when it was time to hunt buffalo in northeastern Colorado. When University of Kansas researchers S.W. Williston and H.T. Martin excavated here between 1898 and 1900, they concluded that these Apache occupied the site both before and after the years they shared it with Pueblo people. The newcomers, in other words, came to live among hosts who were already at home.

## Fleeing the Encomienda

What drove them north was the Spanish colonial order in Nuevo México. Under the encomienda system, which amounted to a form of slavery, Spanish landholders were given people from the pueblos to live with them and work, cultivating crops or producing other goods. In exchange the landholder was responsible for their welfare, a duty that included suppressing their spiritual practices. Throughout the 17th century, Pueblo people moved north onto the Great Plains of western Kansas and eastern Colorado to live with the Cuartelejo Apache, perhaps as early as 1620. Tewa people came, mostly from Santa Clara Pueblo, and so did people from Taos. Around 1640 the Taos mission priest, Fray Pedro de Miranda, was killed, and Taos people joined the Apache after the revolt then and again in 1664. Spain wanted its laborers back. Later, Juan de Archuleta led an expedition to capture the Pueblo people and return them to Nuevo México.

## Seven Rooms on the Plains

The great rupture came with the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. In its aftermath, refugees, possibly from Taos and Picuris, fled north into Kansas by 1696, and here beside Ladder Creek they built a seven-room stone pueblo. It was a piece of home carried onto the grasslands. Like the pueblos of the southwest, it had a grinding trough, ovens and slab-lined hearths, the everyday fixtures of a Pueblo household. Pottery from related sites tells the same story of two peoples living side by side: wares of the Padouca Apache and other Apache bands mixed with Pueblo pottery. The Spanish came looking again. In 1706, Juan de Ulibarrí travelled to the Arkansas River area of Colorado to capture and return the Picuris and other Pueblo people who had fled north.

## Riders from the West

The shelter did not last. By 1710 Comanche and Ute raiders were attacking Apache rancherías, and the Comanche assaults went on until 1735. The Palomas Apache joined the Cuartelejo Apache at El Cuarto, in the Las Animas district along the Arkansas River, but the pressure from the west kept coming, and the Comanche drove Apache bands including the Carlanas and Chilpaines out of the region. The El Cuartelejo settlements were abandoned. Their people established rancherías near Pecos Pueblo in Nuevo México, while some Apache moved to the plains east of it. Europeans moved into the gap. In 1726, Padoucas from Kansas led French traders west to El Cuartelejo, hoping to open trade with the Spanish, and in 1748 Frenchmen occupied the old rancherías and set up trading posts to deal with the Comanche. By 1760 the Comanche dominated the region, controlling the trade networks of the Spanish to the southwest and of the Pawnee and Wichita on the eastern plains.

## A Homestead Guards the Ruin

In 1888, Herbert and Eliza Steele homesteaded on the land and began turning up Native artifacts. The excavations by Williston and Martin followed a decade later, and in 1922 two acres of the Steeles' land, including the pueblo ruins, were given to the Kansas Society of the Daughters of the American Revolution so the public could appreciate them. The DAR raised a monument in 1925. On July 19, 1964, the El Cuartelejo Archaeological District was designated a National Historic Landmark. Partial walls have since been reconstructed to show the pueblo's layout as it appeared in 1898, and the Steeles' house serves as a museum for Lake Scott State Park. Standing in the canyon, you can trace the outline of seven rooms: a floor plan drawn by people who walked away from an empire and found, for a while, somewhere else to live.

## Flight Context

El Cuartelejo lies at 38.678°N, 100.914°W in Ladder Creek Canyon within Lake Scott State Park, about 13 miles north of Scott City, Kansas. From roughly 3,000-5,000 feet above ground, look for the canyon and the small reservoir of Lake Scott breaking up the flat, open farmland of the High Plains; the reconstructed pueblo outline sits near the canyon floor. Scott City Municipal Airport (KTQK) lies just southeast of Scott City, and Garden City Regional Airport (KGCK) is farther south. Western Kansas usually offers long visibility, but summer afternoons can bring thunderstorms and gusty winds.
