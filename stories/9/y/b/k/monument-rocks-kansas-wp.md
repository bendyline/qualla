# Monument Rocks (Kansas)

Pale chalk buttes and arches rising from the Gove County prairie, Monument Rocks formed on the floor of an 80-million-year-old inland sea, guided travelers on the Smoky Hill Trail, and became Kansas's first National Natural Landmark.

**Location:** 38.7906, -100.7620

**Categories:** Natural arches of Kansas, Landforms of Gove County, Kansas, National Natural Landmarks in Kansas, Landmarks in Kansas, Tourist attractions in Gove County, Kansas

## Introduction

In the 1860s, travelers on the Smoky Hill Trail across western Kansas watched for pale shapes rising out of the grass. The chalk towers of Monument Rocks served the Butterfield Overland Despatch as a landmark, a fixed point to steer by on the open plains. They still stop people in their tracks. Rising as high as 70 feet above the prairie of Gove County, carved into buttes and arches, these Chalk Pyramids were built from the floor of a vanished sea. In 1968 they became the first landmark in Kansas chosen by the U.S. Department of the Interior as a National Natural Landmark.

## Floor of a Vanished Sea

Stand beside the rocks and you are standing on what was once the bottom of an ocean. During the Cretaceous Period, the Western Interior Seaway flooded the middle of the continent, splitting North America into two separate landmasses. On its floor, carbonate sediments settled layer upon layer, and over time they hardened into the chalk that forms these towers. Geologists place the formations within the Smoky Hill Chalk Member of the Niobrara Chalk, and they date the deposits to about 80 million years ago, late in the age of dinosaurs. The sea is long gone, drained away as the continent changed, but its sediment remains in these monoliths. Their soft, pale faces are a cross-section of time, each band recording another stretch of that ancient seabed.

## Buttes, Arches and Buried Life

Erosion has since done the sculpting. Where softer material wore away, the chalk that remained stands as a cluster of freestanding buttes and arches with pale, weathered walls. Among them is an arch that frames a slice of Kansas sky, and the whole group reads from a distance like the ruins of some forgotten city. The chalk is also rich in fossils, the remains of life from that long-vanished sea preserved in the rock itself. Visitors are asked to leave those fossils where they lie, and the list of rules that protects the site is short and firm: no climbing, no fossil hunting, no camping, no horses, no campfires and no littering.

## Landmark on the Smoky Hill Trail

Long before tourists arrived, the rocks had a practical job. In the 1860s, the Butterfield Overland Despatch trail, also known as the Smoky Hill Trail, used these formations as a landmark to guide travelers through the region. The route was important enough to need protection. From 1865 to 1868, a military post called Fort Monument stood about a mile southwest of the rocks, guarding the trail. The fort did not last, but the rocks outlived it, and they were drawing photographers early: one surviving image of the southernmost rock dates to the early 1890s. For more than a century and a half, these pale towers have marked this stretch of prairie for anyone passing through.

## A Wonder on Private Land

Official recognition came in stages. In 1968, Monument Rocks became the first National Natural Landmark in Kansas, and on January 29, 2008, the formations and Castle Rock, another chalk landmark 31 miles to the east, were jointly named one of the 8 Wonders of Kansas. Remarkably, the site sits on private property. The owners allow anyone to come and see the formations, which lie along unpaved roads south of Oakley. That openness is part of the experience: an unpaved road, the wind, and the rocks rising out of the grass. After dark the formations take on another life, and photographers have captured the Milky Way arching over the chalk.

## Flight Context

Monument Rocks sit at 38.7906°N, 100.762°W in Gove County, western Kansas, roughly 20–25 miles south of Oakley and a few miles east of U.S. Highway 83. The bright white chalk stands out sharply against the surrounding tan and green prairie, making the formations easy to spot from 1,500–2,500 feet AGL on a clear day; the arches are best appreciated on a slow, wide orbit. Castle Rock, another chalk landmark, lies about 31 miles to the east. The nearest airports are small municipal fields at Oakley and Scott City; for larger fields, Garden City Regional (KGCK) is about 60 miles to the south and Hays Regional (KHYS) roughly 80 miles to the east. Expect strong, gusty High Plains winds and fast-building summer thunderstorms.
