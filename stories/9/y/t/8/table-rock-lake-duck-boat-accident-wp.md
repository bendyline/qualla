# Table Rock Lake duck boat accident

On a July evening in 2018, a tourist duck boat sank in a sudden storm on Table Rock Lake near Branson, killing 17 of the 31 people aboard and exposing safety warnings that had gone unheeded for nearly two decades.

**Location:** 36.6072, -93.3194

**Categories:** 2018 disasters in the United States, 2018 in Missouri, Maritime incidents in 2018, Boating accident deaths, Ozarks

## Introduction

Thirty-one people were aboard the duck boat when it drove into Table Rock Lake on the evening of July 19, 2018. Seventeen of them died. The youngest was a year old and the oldest was 70. Nine belonged to a single family visiting from Indianapolis, and among the dead was a couple from Higginsville, Missouri, who were celebrating their 45th wedding anniversary. They had boarded a Ride the Ducks amphibious vehicle for a sightseeing tour near Branson, and out on the open water they met a fast-moving storm.

## A War Machine Turned Tour Bus

The DUKW, nicknamed the "Duck," was a wheeled amphibious vehicle used by the United States military and its allies in the later years of World War II and in the Korean War. After Korea the vehicles became available as surplus, and a veteran in Minnesota began giving tourists rides on the Wisconsin River. Over the next 50 years the idea spread to Austin, Boston, Pittsburgh, Philadelphia, Washington, D.C., and Branson. The dangers were already documented. On May 1, 1999, a duck boat sank near Hot Springs, Arkansas, and 13 people died. The National Transportation Safety Board blamed inadequate maintenance above all, but also cited inadequate reserve buoyancy and a continuous canopy roof that kept passengers from escaping. It recommended that such vehicles either gain enough buoyancy to stay afloat when flooded, or require life jackets and remove the canopies.

## Sixty-Mile-an-Hour Wind

About an hour before the accident, the National Weather Service issued a severe thunderstorm warning for areas around the lake. Whether the boat's two crew members knew of it, or what they tried to do, remains unknown. Shortly after 7 p.m. a line of storms reached the water, part of a significant derecho that also spawned several tornadoes. Gusts reached 73 miles per hour, and the fast-moving storm whipped up waves of three to five feet. The first 911 call came in at 7:09 p.m., when, according to the local sheriff, the boat was already going under. None of the passengers or crew was wearing a life jacket. Tia Coleman, who lost nine family members, including her husband and three children, said the crew had told passengers not to put on life jackets because they would not need them. By the next morning, officials had accounted for everyone aboard and confirmed 17 deaths.

## Raised From Eighty Feet

NTSB investigators arrived the following day. Ripley Entertainment, which had bought the Branson Ride the Ducks operation in 2017, closed it pending the investigation and out of respect for the victims' families, and said it would pay funeral and medical expenses for everyone who had been aboard; its president said the storm "came out of nowhere." The day after the sinking, Stone Mountain Park near Atlanta suspended its own duck boat rides. On July 23, after divers examined and photographed the wreck, the boat was raised from the bottom in an operation supervised by the U.S. Coast Guard, and divers recovered a recording device from about 80 feet below the surface.

## Warnings Written Twenty Years Before

In November 2019 the NTSB issued a safety recommendation report with blunt findings. The Coast Guard, it said, "has repeatedly ignored safety recommendations that could have made tourist duck boats safer and potentially prevented" the sinking. "These safety issues were identified almost 20 years prior to the sinking of the Stretch Duck 7 and remain relevant to this accident," the report said. The board had long warned about the vehicles' design and the danger their overhead canopies posed to passengers trapped inside.

## No Verdict for the Seventeen

The legal aftermath stretched on for years. The boat's captain was first charged in federal court in 2018, and a 2019 federal indictment named him along with the Branson operation's general manager and an operations supervisor. A federal judge dismissed those charges in December 2020, accepting a magistrate's reasoning that Table Rock Lake was not a navigable waterway under admiralty law. Stone County filed state charges in July 2021, including 17 counts of first-degree involuntary manslaughter against each man. A judge dismissed them in April 2022, finding no evidence the staff knew of the storm's "gust front," and the Missouri Attorney General's office refiled identical charges two days later. In December 2025 a Stone County judge dismissed those as well, ruling there was no probable cause. Families also pursued civil suits, at least two of which ended in confidential settlements.

## Flight Context

The accident site lies at about 36.6072°N, 93.3194°W on Table Rock Lake, a few miles southwest of Branson, Missouri, in the White River valley of the Ozarks. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, Table Rock Dam and the lake's branching arms are the dominant landmarks, with Branson and Lake Taneycomo to the east-northeast. Please treat this as a place of remembrance. M. Graham Clark Downtown Airport (KPLK) at Point Lookout is about 4.5 nm east-northeast and Branson Airport (KBBG) about 7 nm southeast. Summer thunderstorms here can build and move quickly, as this accident showed; check convective forecasts and SIGMETs before flying over the lakes in July heat.
