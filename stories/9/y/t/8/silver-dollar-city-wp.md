# Silver Dollar City

Prospectors came to this Ozark hilltop for lead and marble and found neither; a Chicago family built an 1880s village over the cave instead and discovered the real treasure was visitors.

**Location:** 36.6671, -93.3389

**Categories:** Amusement parks in Missouri, Amusement parks opened in 1960, Tourist attractions in Stone County, Missouri, Show caves in Missouri, Ozarks

## Introduction

The first men to explore the great cavern beneath this Ozark hilltop were looking for lead. In 1869, lead-mining magnate Henry T. Blow went down with six miners and found none, though they were convinced that the flat ceiling of one room was made of marble, and so they named it Marble Cave. Neither the lead nor the marble would ever pay off, and it would take another failed mining venture, a Canadian dairyman and a Chicago vacuum cleaner salesman before anyone worked out what this hilltop was really good for. The answer, today, is Silver Dollar City: a 61-acre, 1880s-themed park on the Indian Point peninsula of Table Rock Lake.

## Guano, Limestone and a Paper Town

The cave sat undisturbed until 1882, when a party led by T. Hodges Jones and Truman S. Powell entered it, again hoping for lead. Instead they found enormous deposits of guano and a flat wall that they, too, took for marble. Two years later Jones bought the property and formed the Marble Cave Mining and Manufacturing Company, which planned a town called Marble City on the rough hilltop and recorded its plat map at the courthouse in Galena, Missouri, in 1884. The dream did not last. By 1889 much of the guano had been mined out, the marble wall had proved to be limestone, and no lead ore had turned up. The company shut down.

## The Dairyman's Gamble

That same year, William Henry Lynch, a Canadian miner and dairyman, paid $10,000 for the cave and a square mile of land around its entrance. With his family's help he opened it to sightseers on October 18, 1894. The venture was not immediately profitable and closed until Lynch raised more money to reopen it sometime after 1900; it has stayed open ever since, making it one of the oldest continuously running tourist attractions in the Ozarks. When Harold Bell Wright's 1907 novel The Shepherd of the Hills stirred national interest in the region, Lynch cut brush and cleared a path from Branson for visitors arriving by car. That track became Missouri Route 76, and by the 1920s the cave was a major attraction. After Lynch died in 1927, his daughters took over and renamed it Marvel Cave.

## A Village on the Surface

In April 1950, Chicago vacuum cleaner salesman Hugo Herschend bought a 99-year lease on the cave. He died in 1955, and his wife, Mary, took over day-to-day operations with their sons, Jack and Peter. Together they made sweeping improvements, including a narrow-gauge funicular railway that hauled visitors 218 feet from the depths of the cave back to the surface. With the cave finished, the family looked upward. They began building an 1880s-style Ozark village around the entrance, and Mary insisted on authenticity and on preserving the area's natural beauty. Silver Dollar City opened on May 1, 1960, with five shops, a church, a log cabin and a street show reenacting the Hatfield–McCoy feud several times a day. Its name came from Ozark Jubilee writer and publicist Don Richardson, inspired by the idea of giving visitors silver dollars as change. More than 125,000 people came that first year, four times the cave's usual annual visitors. "We discovered we were in the theme park business," Pete Herschend said.

## From Feuds to Inversions

The park began charging admission in 1968. In 1972 Genevieve Lynch, the last of William Lynch's daughters, died and left the land beneath Silver Dollar City and Marvel Cave to the College of the Ozarks and Branson Presbyterian Church; the Herschends continue to operate it. That same year brought the park's first roller coaster, Fire in the Hole, a ride through the burning town of Marmaros, set ablaze in the story by the Baldknobbers, which ran until 2023. Later coasters pushed much harder. Outlaw Run, opened in 2013, was the first wooden roller coaster built by Rocky Mountain Construction and the first wooden coaster with multiple inversions. Time Traveler, opened in 2018, became the tallest and fastest spinning coaster when it debuted. Resident craftsmen still demonstrate their trades for visitors, and the Herschends carried the formula east in 1976, buying a Tennessee park that became Dollywood after a 1986 partnership with Dolly Parton.

## Six and a Half Million Lights

Hollywood noticed early. The Clampetts of The Beverly Hillbillies opened their 1969–1970 season with a visit to Silver Dollar City, treated on screen as a real town, and five episodes were eventually filmed in the park. The season now runs from mid-March to early January, and it ends with An Old Time Christmas, when the park is decorated with 6.5 million lights and a five-story special-effects Christmas tree. In 2007 Good Morning America named the festival one of the top five holiday events in the country. In October 2024 the park announced a 10-year plan to expand into the surrounding area, beginning with a 262-room resort hotel that, as of August 2026, is on track to open in 2027.

## Flight Context

Silver Dollar City sits at 36.6671°N, 93.3389°W on the Indian Point peninsula of Table Rock Lake, just off Missouri Route 76 west of Branson. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, look for the lake's branching arms around the peninsula and the park's coaster structures among the wooded hills; Branson and Lake Taneycomo lie a few miles to the east. The Ozark terrain here is hilly, so maintain safe clearance. M. Graham Clark Downtown Airport (KPLK) at Point Lookout is about 6 nm east-southeast and Branson Airport (KBBG) about 10 nm southeast. Morning fog can linger over the lakes and hollows.
