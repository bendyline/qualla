# Fantastic Caverns

First explored in 1867 by twelve women from a Springfield athletic club, the cave north of Springfield, Missouri, has been a speakeasy, a Klan meeting hall, a country music stage and a fallout shelter, and now offers a ride-through tour by Jeep-drawn tram.

**Location:** 37.2874, -93.3585

**Categories:** Caves of Missouri, Landforms of Greene County, Missouri, Show caves in the United States, Tourist attractions in Springfield, Missouri

## Introduction

On February 27, 1867, twelve women from the Springfield Women's Athletic Club gathered their ladders, ropes and lanterns and went underground. A farmer named John Knox had advertised in the Springfield paper for someone to explore a cave on his land, and these women answered the call. By lantern light, they found a pair of enormous stalagmites and named the chamber the Hall of Giants. Then they did what explorers have always done: they left their names on the rock. Their names can still be seen on a cave wall in the place now known as Fantastic Caverns, and the women are remembered as its first explorers.

## A Dog, a War and a Secret

The cave's modern story began in 1862, when Knox was out hunting and his dog ran into a small opening in the woods, likely chasing a rabbit or squirrel. Knox had found a cave, but the timing was dangerous. The Civil War was raging, and caves could be mined for saltpeter, a key ingredient in gunpowder. Not wanting either the Union or Confederate governments to exploit his discovery, Knox kept it quiet until 1867. Only then did he place his advertisement. The cave was first explored on February 14, 1867, and on February 27 the twelve women of the athletic club made their descent. Because they carved their names into the rock and were written up in the Springfield Tri-Weekly Patriot newspaper, they are considered the cave's first explorers, a claim they signed in stone.

## Secrets in the Ballroom

In the decades that followed, the cave's large chambers attracted people who wanted to gather out of sight, not all of them benign. In the 1920s, the Ku Klux Klan briefly held meetings in the chamber called the Grand Ballroom, an ugly chapter in the cave's long history. During Prohibition, the cave took on another underground life as a speakeasy of sorts, with drinking, gambling and carousing reached by a foot trail built to the auditorium. After several years, local law enforcement shut the club down. The cave's later gatherings were far more welcoming. In the late 1950s, the country music radio show Farm-A-Rama was taped in the auditorium room, featuring artists such as Tom T. Hall and Buck Owens, and concerts continued into the 1960s.

## Riding the Riverbed

The cave took the name Fantastic Caverns in the 1950s, and in the early 1960s the Trimble family, owners of the Shepherd of the Hills theater, bought it and began the ride-through tours that made it famous. Fantastic Caverns calls itself America's Ride-Thru Cave and is described as the only cave in the United States with a completely ride-through tour. Visitors settle into a tram pulled by a Jeep for a journey of roughly an hour, and no walking is required. The route follows the path left behind by an ancient underground river, carrying riders past the formations that water and time have built. Music returned in the 1970s, when shows from the cave were broadcast on KGBX radio.

## Shelter Below Springfield

The Cold War gave the cave yet another role. In 1970, the Greene County Office of Civil Defense designated Fantastic Caverns as a community fallout shelter, a place where local residents might have sought protection had the worst happened. That designation adds one more layer to a remarkably varied history. In a little over a century, the same stone rooms had hosted pioneering women with lanterns, Prohibition drinkers, a hate group's secret meetings, country musicians and, at least on paper, a whole community seeking safety. Through it all, the Hall of Giants has stood in the dark, the same pair of stalagmites those twelve women named in 1867.
