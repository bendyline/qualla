# Big Eddy Site

On a bank of the Sac River in southwest Missouri, layer upon layer of flood silt preserved some 14,000 years of human history, including Clovis tools and a broken stone anvil that may be older still, until the river itself began to wash it away.

**Location:** 37.7205, -93.7161

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in Missouri, Clovis sites, Archaic period in North America, Buildings and structures in Cedar County, Missouri, 1997 archaeological discoveries

## Introduction

The same river that saved the Big Eddy Site has been destroying it. For some 14,000 years, the Sac River in Cedar County, Missouri, flooded this spot gently and often, laying down thick bands of silt over whatever people left behind. The result is an unusually clear archaeological record: a nearly continuous story of human life running from recent prehistory back to the Clovis hunters, and possibly beyond them. Then, in the 1970s, a hydroelectric dam upstream began releasing water, and the river turned from archivist to eraser.

## Pages of Silt

The Sac has carved a cut bank some 5.2 meters high here, and in its face the layers are easy to read. Periodically the river cut deep enough to stop flooding the site, then later shifted course and began burying it again, leaving distinct bands separated by pauses. Gravel layers and paleosols, ancient soils hardened and sealed beneath later deposits, act as lids, keeping younger material from filtering down into older levels. Because of that sealing, the artifacts here stack neatly by age: Mississippian and Woodland remains near the top, Archaic tools and midden debris below them, then Paleoindian material deeper still. The site, formally 23CE426, may even preserve evidence of the transition from Clovis to the later Dalton culture.

## Treasure in the Cutbank

After the Stockton hydroelectric dam, whose lake began filling in 1969, went into service in the 1970s, its releases began eating into the bank, and artifacts started washing out. Local collectors picked some up, drawing professional attention, and in 1986 archaeologists surveying the valley spotted artifacts eroding from the bank. No formal dig followed until 1997, when the Kansas City District of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers realized the site was in imminent danger and hired Missouri State University's Center for Archaeological Research. The team, led by Jack Ray and Neal Lopinot, arrived with modest expectations. A road grader stripped the plow zone, two test trenches went down four meters, and eighteen deep sediment cores were drilled. The site proved far richer than expected, and the crew switched to painstaking hand excavation. Test probes found artifacts, manuports and charcoal down to four meters, dated between 12,700 and 13,000 years before present.

## Clovis and After

Around three meters down lies the hard band called Paleosol 1, holding points from the Dalton and San Patrice cultures and the debris of what appears to have been a stone-tool workshop, with many flakes and broken preforms. Beneath it, at 3.30 meters, the team found a fluted Clovis point of the Gainey type, with charcoal nearby radiocarbon-dated to about 10,710 and 10,940 years before present, and a Clovis blade just below. Big Eddy is perhaps the only well-stratified site bridging the Clovis–Dalton transition, when stone technology changed abruptly after the extinction of North America's megafauna. Fifteen charcoal dates from the Paleoindian levels give it the kind of secure dating that is rare for sites of that age.

## The Anvil Question

What made Big Eddy famous lay deeper. At 3.9 meters, a gravel lens seals everything below it as pre-Clovis in age, and beneath it the team found a large tabular stone broken into three pieces, possibly an anvil, along with a cobble that may have served as a hammer. The possible anvil weighs 4.5 kilograms, far larger than anything else at that level, and experiments showed that even dam releases could not budge it. One broken piece had been rotated 120 degrees, and a spall from its upper surface lay beneath it, strongly suggesting someone moved it after it broke. The hammer cobble bears seven cones of percussion on a single end. Experts split: Tom Dillehay of the Monte Verde site judged the anvil's wear manmade, while lithics specialists Marvin Kay and Stanley Ahler attributed the marks to nature. Ray and Lopinot, hopefully skeptical, argue the stones may have been used to smash the bones of mastodons and other large game, but no clearly human-modified object has yet been found below the Clovis layers. Trampling experiments that Lopinot and Ray published in 2007, using Asian elephants and American bison, found that many of the modified cobbles, pebbles and flakes from those pre-Clovis levels were probably produced by large mammals trampling them, not by people.

## A Race Against the Current

People kept returning to this bend for good reason. It sits where the Ozark forest meets the prairie, offering game from both and fish, mussels and waterfowl from the river, while chert for tools lay in the gravel bars and bluffs. Early hunters here could have found mastodon, tapir, ground sloth, giant beaver and horse. That abundance is now at the mercy of the dam. Reports from the 2000s put the eroding bank just 10 feet from the site and retreating about four feet a year. The Corps funded five seasons of excavation, and when the money ran out, Ray and Lopinot kept coming on weekends and sought private donations to save what they could. Fieldwork wound down in the mid-2000s.

## Flight Context

The Big Eddy Site lies at 37.7205°N, 93.7161°W on the Sac River in Cedar County, southwest Missouri, below Stockton Dam and the broad arms of Stockton Lake. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, follow the Sac River's winding course downstream from the dam; the site itself is an unmarked riverbank and cannot be distinguished from the air, and it should not be visited without permission. Stockton Municipal Airport (FAA identifier MO3) is about 6 nm southwest, Nevada Municipal Airport (KNVD) about 29 nm west-northwest, and Springfield–Branson National Airport (KSGF) about 32 nm south-southeast. The rolling Ozark border country is generally good VFR terrain.
