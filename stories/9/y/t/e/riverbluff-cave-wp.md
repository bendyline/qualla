# Riverbluff Cave

Blasted open by road builders south of Springfield, Missouri, on September 11, 2001, Riverbluff Cave turned out to be an Ice Age time capsule, with giant short-faced bear claw marks, peccary tracks and fossils reaching back hundreds of thousands of years.

**Location:** 37.1063, -93.3293

## Introduction

At two o'clock on the afternoon of September 11, 2001, a day the country would remember for very different reasons, road crews extending Cox Road south of Springfield, Missouri, set off a charge packed into a limestone outcrop. The explosion blew a hole in the rock and opened the roof of a cave that no one knew was there. Below the future roadbed lay an entrance crowded with stalactites, stalagmites and columns, and a floor that had been waiting a very long time for company. On the walls were scratch marks a dozen feet or more above the floor, left by one of the largest bears ever to walk the earth.

## Written in Limestone

Riverbluff Cave formed in the widespread karst landscape of the Springfield Plateau, on the western side of the Missouri Ozarks. Here, the bedrock is made of soluble Mississippian limestones, and over immense spans of time slightly acidic water dissolved channels through the stone, creating the passages and decorating them with formations. The cave runs roughly 2,000 feet. Its sediments hold a deep record: layers inside have been dated from about 600,000 years to as much as a million years old, and some estimates of its fossils run older still. That depth of time makes Riverbluff arguably the oldest known repository of Ice Age bones in any North American cave. For paleontologists, it is less a hole in the ground than a library, with each layer of mud a page from the Pleistocene.

## The Bear in the Dark

The most dramatic residents were giant short-faced bears, Arctodus simus, often described as one of the largest bears that ever lived, standing around five to six feet tall at the shoulder. They left plenty of evidence behind. The cave floor is dimpled with sleeping hollows the bears dug for themselves, and their claw marks rake the walls to heights of a dozen feet or more. Picture an animal large enough to leave scratches that high curling up to rest in these passages, and the cave suddenly feels crowded. The bears were not alone. Hundreds of fossilized peccary footprints trail across the sediments, and researchers have recovered the remains of ancient wolves and cats, camel bones, and the bones and teeth of mammoths, a whole vanished Ozark world preserved underground.

## Protected, Not Forgotten

A find this fragile could not become an ordinary tourist stop. Riverbluff Cave is not open to the public, which helps keep its footprints, bear beds and bone layers intact for study. Instead, the cave's discoveries come to visitors. The Missouri Institute of Natural Science operates the Riverbluff Cave Museum in Springfield, where fossils and artifacts recovered from the site are displayed, and virtual tours of the cave itself are available online. The arrangement lets schoolchildren and curious travelers see what lay hidden beneath the Ozarks without anyone disturbing the floor where giant bears once slept. It all began with a road project and a routine blast, on a day when most of the nation's attention was fixed elsewhere.

## Flight Context

Riverbluff Cave lies at about 37.106°N, 93.329°W on the southern edge of Springfield, Missouri, in Greene County. The cave is underground and closed to the public, so there is nothing to see from the air beyond the roads above it; from 2,500-3,500 feet MSL, use the southern outskirts of Springfield and the wooded Ozark country beyond as your reference. Springfield-Branson National Airport (KSGF) lies about 10 miles to the north-northwest, so expect to be inside or near Springfield's terminal airspace. Visibility is generally good, with spring and summer thunderstorms the main weather concern.
