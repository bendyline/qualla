# Young Brothers massacre

On a January afternoon in 1932, a posse armed only with handguns tried to arrest two fugitive brothers at a farmhouse near Brookline, Missouri, and six lawmen died in one of the deadliest single attacks on US police officers of the 20th century.

**Location:** 37.1854, -93.3925

**Categories:** Shootouts in the United States, Massacres in 1932, History of Greene County, Missouri, Law enforcement history, 1932 in Missouri

## Introduction

The officers in the front yard had shouted for the men inside the farmhouse to come out, and no one answered. But Ollie Crosswhite said he had heard someone walking around in there. It was the afternoon of January 2, 1932, outside the little village of Brookline, Missouri, now part of Republic, and that quiet house was about to become the scene of one of the deadliest single attacks on US police officers of the 20th century. Six lawmen died there. Crosswhite was one of them.

## The Young Triumvirate

Through the 1920s, Paul, Harry and Jennings Young were familiar faces to the lawmen of southwest Missouri. They were small-time thieves, and each served time for burglary and theft at the Missouri State Penitentiary, known as "Old Jeff"; Jennings and Paul also did terms at Leavenworth. Local officers knew them so well that they called the brothers the "Young Triumvirate." They were also considered non-violent. That changed on June 2, 1929, when Mark Noe, City Marshal of Republic, stopped a car driven by Harry Young and was found shot dead in a roadside ditch the next morning, with Harry as the chief suspect. Harry vanished with his brothers, and they allegedly lived in Texas under a false name for two and a half years, running an auto theft ring that the FBI later described as one of the largest of its kind.

## A Posse Carrying Pistols

The Youngs still valued family ties. By the end of 1931, Harry and Jennings, known as "Jinx," had decided to visit the family farm in Missouri. On January 2, Sheriff Marcell Hendrix of Greene County received reliable word that the two were at the farm near Brookline, not far from Springfield. Hendrix quickly gathered a posse, ten officers and one civilian, and set out. By today's standards they were badly unprepared. They carried nothing but handguns, and most had no spare ammunition.

## Through the Back Door

The officers gathered in the front yard and called for the brothers to come out. When there was no response, Hendrix ordered tear gas fired into the house, with no immediate effect. He and his deputy, Wiley Mashburn, then kicked in the back door. Two people inside opened fire, one with a 12-gauge shotgun and the other with a .32-20 rifle. Who exactly was in the house has never been completely settled, though all evidence points to Harry and Jennings Young. Hendrix and Mashburn fell, mortally wounded. The men outside fired into the windows while the gunmen kept shooting at officers standing in the open, and three more lawmen, Tony Oliver, Sid Meadows and Charles Houser, were quickly shot down. Out of ammunition and pinned down, the survivors had to leave their dead and dying colleagues behind and run for their lives.

## Behind the Storm Cellar

They did not know that Ollie Crosswhite was still alive and unhurt, crouched behind a storm cellar at the back of the house. Once the gunmen realized he was there, one pinned him down with rifle fire while the other crept up behind him and killed him with a shotgun. As a relief party was hurriedly organized in Springfield, the killers took money and weapons from the fallen officers and fled. Three days later, on January 5, Houston police entered a home where the brothers were hiding and found that they had retreated to a bathroom. After an exchange of gunfire came a silence, then several shots, and a voice calling out, "We're dead, come on in." The Houston coroner's office concluded that the brothers had shot each other in a suicide pact to avoid capture. The guns taken from the lawmen at Brookline were found on their bodies.

## Six Names in Stone

A monument carrying the names of the six slain officers stands in front of the police headquarters in Springfield and in front of the Greene County Courts building: Sheriff Marcell Hendrix, Deputy Wiley Mashburn, Tony Oliver, Sid Meadows, Charles Houser and Ollie Crosswhite. The farmhouse itself was intentionally burned down in December 2024 by a crew the property's owners hired; they are building a new house on the site. The story has been retold in books and in an independent docudrama directed by Jim Hancock, whose title borrows the last words heard in that Houston house: Come On In We're Dead.
