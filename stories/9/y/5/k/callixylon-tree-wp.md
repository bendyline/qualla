# Callixylon tree

A petrified Devonian tree stump found in an Oklahoma field in 1913, reputedly the largest Callixylon stump in the world, spent twenty-three years as the prize in a tug-of-war between the Smithsonian and East Central University before ending up at the college as a memorial to the man who wanted it for Washington.

**Location:** 34.4646, -96.3992

**Categories:** Individual trees in Oklahoma, East Central University

## Introduction

For twenty-three years, a tree stump was the object of a quiet tug-of-war between the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, D.C., and a college in Ada, Oklahoma. It was not an ordinary stump. It had turned to stone, and the tree it came from had grown in the Devonian period, some 375 million years ago. The college won in the end, though not in the way anyone planned. The stump went to East Central University because the man who wanted it for the Smithsonian died before he could bring it east, and today it stands at the entrance to the campus with a plaque dedicated to him.

## A Stone Tree in a Farm Field

The story begins with a man named John Fitts. In 1913 he came across the fossil on a farm near Goose Creek, south of the community of Jesse in Pontotoc County, not far from the Giant Fitts Oilfield. What he found was not a single tidy trunk but a series of pieces, fragments of a massive petrified stump. Over time those pieces were reassembled into a stump standing eight feet tall. The tree turned out to be Callixylon whiteanum, an extinct species from the late Devonian period, and the Ada specimen is reputedly the largest stump of Callixylon ever found anywhere in the world. That claim alone would have made it a prize. Its age made it something more.

## Among the First True Trees

Callixylon is the oldest petrified wood known in Oklahoma. According to the Oklahoma Geological Survey, it occurs in the Devonian-age Woodford Shale, both in the Arbuckle Mountains and near Ada, and it is about 375 million years old. The survey counts it among the most primitive true trees that ever grew on Earth. Scientists later determined that Callixylon is the wood of Archaeopteris, a leafy, spore-bearing tree that had long been classified as a separate plant. Standing beside the reconstructed stump, it takes some effort to absorb what it represents: wood that grew, died and was slowly replaced by stone in an age when trees themselves were still a new idea on the planet.

## Washington Makes Its Claim

A find like this soon drew attention from Washington. David White, principal geologist of the U.S. Geological Survey and curator of paleobotany at the Smithsonian Institution, traveled from Washington to examine the fossil himself. His verdict was clear: the stump belonged at the Smithsonian, not at East Central University. The college's supporters did not simply surrender. They raised money to erect the fragments into the shape of a tree and to move it to the campus in Ada. For years the two institutions were vying for possession, the national museum with its prestige and expertise on one side, a regional college with local determination on the other. The standoff stretched from the fossil's discovery in 1913 all the way to 1936.

## A Memorial to the Rival

The contest ended with a death. White died before enough money could be raised to move the stump to the Smithsonian, and the fossil was instead given to East Central University as a gift and a memorial. In March 1936, the university's president, Linscheid, formally accepted it. The plaque at the base of the tree is dedicated to White, the man who had wanted to take it away. It identifies the "Callixylon tree of Devonian age" from Pontotoc County and honors White as curator of paleobotany and principal geologist of the U.S. Geological Survey, with the name of John Fitts and the year 1935 also inscribed. The partially reconstructed stump still stands at the entrance to the East Central University campus, where students and visitors pass a tree that was growing some 375 million years ago.

## Flight Context

The coordinates recorded for this article, approximately 34.4646° N, 96.3992° W, fall in the rolling countryside of south-central Oklahoma, roughly 27 miles southeast of Ada. The fossil stump itself stands at the entrance to the East Central University campus in Ada, so pilots wanting to see the site should navigate there: from 2,000 to 3,000 feet above ground level, Ada is the principal town in the area, and the university campus sits within the city. Ada Regional Airport (KADH) lies just north of town and makes the natural arrival point. The Arbuckle Mountains, where the same Devonian-age Woodford Shale that yielded Callixylon also occurs, rise to the southwest. Spring thunderstorms are common in this part of Oklahoma, and summer haze can reduce long-range visibility.
