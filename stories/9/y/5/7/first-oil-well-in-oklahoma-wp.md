# First Oil Well in Oklahoma

Drilled on Choctaw land along Clear Boggy Creek between 1885 and 1888, the Faucett well struck oil and gas at 1,414 feet just as its driving force fell ill and died, and his partners capped it and walked away.

**Location:** 34.4017, -96.3736

## Introduction

After three years of delays, the drill finally reached 1,414 feet, and the hole beside Clear Boggy Creek began to show both oil and gas. It should have been a triumph. Instead, just as the work was being completed in 1888, the man behind the project, Dr. H. W. Faucett, fell ill at the job site. He hurried home to Neosho, Missouri, for treatment and died shortly afterward. Without him, his partners felt they could not go on. They capped the well and walked away. What they left behind, on Choctaw land in Indian Territory, is listed on the National Register as the First Oil Well in Oklahoma, though a brine well at Salina had struck oil by accident in 1859.

## Seeps in the Choctaw Nation

Nobody had to guess that there was oil in Indian Territory. For many years it had been observed seeping naturally to the surface. What was missing was a way to get it out in quantity. In the mid-1880s, Choctaw leaders, urged on by Faucett, took up the question and organized the Choctaw Oil and Refining Company, with former principal chief Allen Wright among those who helped lead the effort. Faucett, one of the first businessmen to recognize the potential of oil, received an exclusive right to drill for petroleum and to build pipelines to a railroad or a refinery, with 15 percent of any proceeds going to the company. The Cherokee council passed its own act in 1884 for a parallel well. For a moment, two nations of Indian Territory were betting on the same man.

## Oxcarts From Atoka

Faucett's crew raised a drilling rig on Choctaw land near Clear Boggy Creek in late 1885, and from the start the work crawled. Every piece of equipment and every supply had to travel by rail from St. Louis to Atoka, then be loaded onto oxcarts and hauled 14 miles overland to the site. Money moved just as slowly, delayed by poor communication with Faucett's backers in the East. Members of both the Choctaw and Cherokee companies began complaining openly. Faucett assured the Choctaws that everything was ready, but the Cherokee well would have to start later, and the Cherokee council responded by repealing its 1884 act. When his New York backers heard the news, they pulled out too, forcing Faucett to find new money in St. Louis. A year later Robert L. Owen tried to revive the Cherokee project, but by then neither the Cherokees nor the St. Louis investors were interested. The Cherokee well was never resumed.

## Dr. Wright Pays the Crew

The Choctaw project had a champion. Dr. Eliphalet Nott Wright, then president of the Choctaw Oil and Refining Company, backed Faucett enthusiastically, and more than once, when funds ran dangerously low, he paid the workers out of his own pocket to keep the drilling going. His commitment did more than meet a payroll. Other prominent Choctaws had begun to fear that Faucett himself was to blame for the delays, and Wright's steady support calmed those doubts. With his help, the Choctaw well kept going through the slow years after the Cherokee venture had collapsed. It is a reminder that this pioneering well was not simply an outsider's venture on tribal land. It was a Choctaw enterprise, sustained at crucial moments by a Choctaw doctor's own money.

## Capped and Remembered

The well eventually reached 1,414 feet and produced modest amounts of oil and gas, though not in quantities that could have supported a commercial operation. Faucett, who died in 1888 of typhoid fever, never saw what might have come next, and the rest of the partners felt they could not continue without him. The company capped the well, abandoned the site and went out of business. It would be nine more years before the Nellie Johnstone No. 1, near Bartlesville, came in as the future state's first commercial oil well in 1897. The Faucett well, also known as the Old Faucett Well, was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1972. Near the present-day town of Wapanucka, the capped well casing is its only remnant.

## Flight Context

The First Oil Well in Oklahoma site lies at approximately 34.4017° N, 96.3736° W, along Clear Boggy Creek near the town of Wapanucka in southeastern Oklahoma. The only surviving feature is a capped well casing, far too small to see from the air, so navigate by the terrain instead: from 2,000 to 3,000 feet above ground level, follow the wooded course of Clear Boggy Creek through the surrounding pasture and farmland. The town of Atoka, where the drilling supplies once arrived by rail, lies about 14 miles to the east along the U.S. 69/75 corridor. Durant Regional Airport (KDUA) is roughly 32 miles to the south, and Ada Regional Airport (KADH) about 32 miles to the north-northwest. Southeastern Oklahoma is humid, with frequent spring thunderstorms and summer haze that can limit visibility.
