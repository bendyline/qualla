# Boggy Depot, Oklahoma

Founded by removed Choctaw and Chickasaw people in 1837, Boggy Depot grew into a Butterfield stage stop, a Choctaw capital and a Confederate supply base before the railroad passed it by and left a ghost town.

**Location:** 34.3203, -96.3142

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Oklahoma, History of Indian Territory, Butterfield Overland Mail in Indian Territory, National Register of Historic Places in Atoka County, Oklahoma, Choctaw Nation

## Introduction

The man credited with naming Oklahoma lies in a cemetery at a town that no longer exists. Allen Wright, principal chief of the Choctaw Nation from 1866 to 1870, made his home at Boggy Depot, and in 1866 he suggested joining the Choctaw words okla, for people, and humma, for red, as a name for the territory. Today little remains at the old townsite in Atoka County beyond its cemetery. In its day, Boggy Depot was one of the most important places in Indian Territory, a crossroads of trade routes, stagecoaches and armies.

## A Town Born of Removal

Choctaw and Chickasaw people founded Boggy Depot in 1837, only a few years after the United States government forced both nations from their homelands in Mississippi and Alabama. Allen Wright himself had been a child survivor of that removal, the journey remembered as the Trail of Tears. At first the two tribes shared Choctaw land, but the Chickasaw later moved to the western part of Indian Territory and formed their own nation on land the Choctaw transferred to them. In 1834, the Dodge-Leavenworth expedition, begun under General Henry Leavenworth, had opened a military road from Camp Washita, a forerunner of Fort Washita, to Fort Gibson, and for years it served as the dividing line between Choctaw and Chickasaw lands. A later treaty fixed a formal boundary, placing Boggy Depot on the Choctaw side, within Atoka County, Choctaw Nation.

## Crossroads on the Texas Road

Boggy Depot grew into a vibrant, thriving town and a major trading center, standing on the Texas Road and on a trail that ran from Fort Smith, Arkansas, to Fort Washita. According to the Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History and Culture, it was the site of the first Chickasaw agency in the West. The Reverend Cyrus Kingsbury established its church in 1840, and in 1859 that church building served as the temporary capitol of the Choctaw Nation; between 1858 and 1860 the town sometimes served as the nation's capital. A post office opened in 1849. In 1858 the town became a stop on the Butterfield Overland Mail, the stage line that carried passengers and mail between Missouri and San Francisco. Until the railroad came, Boggy Depot was the chief town of the area.

## War and the Railroad

The Civil War brought Boggy Depot new importance and new danger. It became the major Confederate supply depot in Indian Territory, and a few miles to the northeast a Union raiding party clashed with Confederates at the Battle of Middle Boggy Depot. The war's end sealed the town's fate in a less obvious way. Under the treaties that followed, the Five Tribes had to allow a north-south railroad across their lands. When the Missouri, Kansas and Texas Railway, the Katy, arrived in 1872, it ran twelve miles east of Boggy Depot. Businesses and residents followed the tracks to Atoka, which flourished, while some people moved two miles south to found New Boggy Depot. By the early twentieth century, the original town was a ghost.

## Graves, a Park and an Album Cover

What remains is mostly the cemetery, where Allen Wright and Cyrus Kingsbury are buried, and the memory of a townsite now listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Oklahoma later made the site a state park. After the state announced it would close the park to cut costs, control passed in 2011 to the Chickasaw Nation, which now runs it as Boggy Depot Park. Boggy Depot also turned up somewhere unexpected. Jerry Cantrell, guitarist of the rock band Alice in Chains, named his first solo album Boggy Depot after the area where his father grew up. On the cover he stands waist-deep in a branch of the Boggy River, covered in mud.

## Flight Context

The Boggy Depot townsite lies at 34.3203 N, 96.3142 W in Atoka County, southeastern Oklahoma, about twelve miles west of the old Katy railroad line at Atoka. From 2,000-3,500 feet AGL, look for the creek bottoms of the Boggy River drainage; the townsite itself has little to mark it from the air. Nearest airports: Atoka Municipal Airport (KAQR), a mile north of Atoka, and Durant Regional Airport-Eaker Field (KDUA) to the south. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common.
