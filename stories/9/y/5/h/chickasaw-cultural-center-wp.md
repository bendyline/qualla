# Chickasaw Cultural Center

Built by the Chickasaw Nation over six years and opened in 2010, this 184-acre campus in Sulphur, Oklahoma, keeps Chickasaw history, language and dance alive, down to a recreated village where the stomp dance is performed on the days the center is open.

**Location:** 34.4928, -96.9958

**Categories:** Native American museums in Oklahoma, Chickasaw, Museums in Murray County, Oklahoma, Cultural centers

## Introduction

Every day the Chickasaw Cultural Center is open, the stomp dance is performed in a village built to show how the Chickasaw lived in the 1700s. There is a council house, summer and winter houses, a corn crib, a replica mound, a stickball field and a stockade fence, all recreated with historical accuracy on the grounds of a 184-acre campus in Sulphur, Oklahoma. The center sits near the Chickasaw National Recreation Area and belongs to the Chickasaw Nation, which built it to preserve and share a culture that survived the nation's forced removal from its homeland.

## A Long-Held Dream

Tribal leaders talked about a place to preserve Chickasaw culture for more than two decades before it opened, but for years the nation was not in a position to build one. In 1980, Price Waterhouse studied whether a Chickasaw and Native American theme park could succeed in the Arbuckle area and concluded that the region did not have enough population to support it. The project stayed alive anyway. In October 2000 the Chickasaw government surveyed more than 1,200 citizens of the nation for their ideas about a cultural center. U.S. Representative Tom Cole, himself a Chickasaw citizen, drafted a land exchange among the nation, the City of Sulphur and the National Park Service to secure a good site, and it was approved on September 28, 2004. Two days later, on September 30, the symbolic groundbreaking took place. Construction took six years and cost $40 million, and the center opened in 2010.

## We Are Chickasaw

The campus buildings were designed by the architectural firm Frankfurt Short Bruza. At its heart stands the Chikasha Poya Exhibit Center, whose name means "We are Chickasaw" and which houses most of the indoor exhibits, with interactive displays on tribal history, traditional dance and the Chickasaw language. The Holisso Research Center holds a large collection of tribal archives and genealogical records, a resource for Chickasaw families tracing their own histories. The Anoli' Theater seats 350 people and showcases Chickasaw films on a 2,700-square-foot screen, and it is the largest theater on the campus. Inside the exhibit center, the Spirit Forest explores the connection between Chickasaw spiritual identity and the natural world, and a welcome center and gift shop serve visitors.

## The Village and the Land

Outdoors, the recreated village, Chikasha Inchokka', is one of the center's greatest draws. A sky bridge overlooks it, and cultural re-enactments bring it to life, from stomp dance to stickball demonstrations. Hiking trails wind through the grounds past plants native to both Oklahoma and Mississippi, the Chickasaw homeland from which the United States forced the nation west in the 1830s. The campus also has an outdoor amphitheater, and a statue by sculptor and former Oklahoma state senator Enoch Kelly Haney stands on the campus, along with a garden honoring members of the Chickasaw Nation Hall of Fame.

## Culture as a Living Thing

The center treats Chickasaw culture as something present rather than past. Its seasonal exhibits include Through the Eyes of the Eagle, shown during Native American Heritage Month, which draws on children's books developed by the CDC and the Tribal Leaders Diabetes Committee to encourage Chickasaw children to eat well and exercise, in response to high rates of diabetes among Native children. During Women's History Month, the center presents the year's Dynamic Chickasaw Woman of the Year. Together with the archives, the language exhibits and the regular dances, these programs make the campus less a museum of what was lost than a home for what endures.

## Flight Context

The Chickasaw Cultural Center lies at 34.4928 N, 96.9958 W on the edge of Sulphur, Oklahoma, in Murray County, next to the Chickasaw National Recreation Area and its Lake of the Arbuckles. From 2,500-4,000 feet AGL, the lake and the Arbuckle Mountains to the southwest are the best references. Nearest airports: Sulphur Municipal Airport (FAA identifier F30, no ICAO code), about two miles northwest of town, and Ardmore Municipal Airport (KADM), with a control tower, northeast of Ardmore to the south. Summer thunderstorms and haze are common.
