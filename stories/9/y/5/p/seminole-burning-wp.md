# Seminole burning

In January 1898 a white mob near Maud, Oklahoma Territory, burned two Seminole teenagers alive for a murder they very likely did not commit - a crime that led to the first successful lynching prosecution in the Southwest.

**Location:** 35.1319, -96.7775

**Categories:** Lynching deaths in Oklahoma, Seminole Nation of Oklahoma, 1898 in Oklahoma Territory, 1898 in Indian Territory, 19th-century American trials

## Introduction

Lincoln McGeisey was eighteen. His father, Thomas, was a Seminole farmer and landowner who served as superintendent of the Seminole Nation's public schools and had been chosen for a Seminole delegation to Washington. Palmer Sampson was seventeen, the son of Sukey Natuksie and Sampson Walker, and he could neither speak nor understand English. On January 8, 1898, near the town of Maud, just inside Oklahoma Territory on its boundary with Indian Territory, a mob of more than two hundred people burned both young men alive for a murder they very likely did not commit.

## A Killing in the Night

The violence began with another death. On the evening of December 30, 1897, Mary Leard was at home with her three children while her husband was away at a friend's house. A Native American man with a distinctive scar on his cheek came to the house, left, and returned that night. He chased her outside and tried twice to shoot her, but the gun failed, and he killed her with a blow from it. He threw her infant daughter, Cora, to the floor and fled. Cora died of her injuries a few months later. The next morning the children went for help, and when their father and townspeople from Maud found Mary Leard's body, a white mob began to form. The home stood on land owned by Thomas McGeisey.

## The Hunt

Close acquaintances of the Leards organized first, but the mob soon swelled with strangers, many of whom traveled long distances for the purpose of a lynching. As it grew, it replaced the facts of the crime with inflamed and false accusations. For days, its members seized Seminole men who did not match the killer's description. They tortured a 19-year-old named Billy, whose surname was either Harjo or Thlocco, trying to force a confession. They kidnapped John Washington at gunpoint and hanged him from a tree until he lost consciousness. They arrested Lincoln McGeisey twice, even though Mary Leard's son said he was not the killer and Leard's husband thought him innocent. Lincoln said he had been with his family that night, and he kept denying the crime even with a noose around his neck. The mob released Washington in exchange for Palmer Sampson, who did not fit the description either, and chained the two teenagers together by the neck. Through a translator, Sampson allegedly confessed.

## Before Dawn at the Brush Arbor

In the dark early hours of January 8, the mob carried the two young men to a makeshift prayer site just south of Maud's post office, a brush arbor locals called the tabernacle, where Mary Leard had once confessed her sins to the community. They shackled Lincoln and Palmer by their necks to opposite sides of a tree and piled dry poles from the arbor around them. Offered a chance to pray, Lincoln prayed in his own language. Then a member of the mob lit the fire, and both were burned to death. Their remains, still chained together, were buried on Seminole land.

## A Prosecution Without Precedent

Much of the white public in Oklahoma Territory backed the lynchers, and newspapers stoked fears that the Seminole were planning revenge. Yet the killers did not escape entirely. Dozens of men were indicted, and before Judge John R. Thomas, special prosecutor Horace Speed won convictions against six, who went to prison. It was the first successful prosecution of a lynching in the Southwestern United States. On July 1, 1898, after McGeisey and Sampson had been exonerated, Congress appropriated more than $35,000 in legislation written by Senator Matthew Quay of Pennsylvania: $5,000 to each victim's family, $25,000 for the prosecutions, and smaller sums for John Washington, George Harjo, William Thlocco and other Seminole men who had been seized or tortured. The reckoning had limits. One convicted man, Mont Ballard, served seven years of a ten-year sentence and came home to a celebration by hundreds in Maud who proclaimed his innocence.

## Remembering Lincoln and Palmer

The lynching belonged to a wider pattern. Between 1885 and 1907, the territorial years, Oklahoma recorded 106 lynching victims, among them 17 Black and 14 Native American men, and the violence turned more openly racist after statehood. In 1942, one participant, James Edwards Nix, wrote a family memoir blaming alcohol for what happened. In 2016 his great-grandchild wrote that the burning was part of the ongoing genocide of Indigenous peoples in the United States. Historian Daniel F. Littlefield Jr. examined the case in his 1996 book Seminole Burning, which historian Michael J. Pfeifer praised as a "magisterial" case study while urging more research into the lynching of Native Americans, a history that remains underexamined.

## Flight Context

The site lies at about 35.1319 N, 96.7775 W near Maud, Oklahoma, a town on the Pottawatomie and Seminole county line, close to the old boundary between Oklahoma Territory and Indian Territory. This is a place for reflection rather than sightseeing. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, the small town of Maud and the surrounding farmland are the only references. Nearest airports: Seminole Municipal Airport (KSRE), three miles north of Seminole to the northeast, and Shawnee Regional Airport (KSNL), two miles northwest of Shawnee to the northwest. Spring and early summer bring strong thunderstorms to central Oklahoma.
