# Red River Bridge War

In the summer of 1931, a new free bridge over the Red River pushed Texas and Oklahoma into a standoff of barricades, Texas Rangers, National Guardsmen and a revolver-carrying governor.

**Location:** 33.8183, -96.5233

**Categories:** Political history of Oklahoma, History of Texas, Internal territorial disputes of the United States, Bridges in Oklahoma, Bridges in Texas, 1931 in Oklahoma

## Introduction

In July 1931 the governor of Oklahoma went down to the Red River carrying a revolver. William "Alfalfa Bill" Murray had declared martial law at a bridge, posted National Guardsmen on it, and come in person to face down the state of Texas. Across the river, Texas had answered with barricades and Texas Rangers. The cause of all this was not gold, oil or water, but a brand-new highway bridge that was supposed to be free. For a few tense weeks, the Red River crossing between Denison, Texas, and Colbert, Oklahoma, became the front line of the Red River Bridge War, a border conflict that made national and international headlines.

## A Toll Bridge and a Free One

Travelers on U.S. Routes 69 and 75 had been crossing the Red River here on a toll bridge run by a private firm, the Red River Bridge Company. In 1931 Texas and Oklahoma jointly built a new, free span just northwest of it. The company said the Texas Highway Commission had promised in July 1930 to buy the old toll bridge for $60,000, and according to the Handbook of Texas it also claimed an additional $10,000 for each month of a fourteen-month period in which the free bridge might be open. On July 10, 1931, the company won an injunction barring the commission from opening the new bridge. Texas Governor Ross S. Sterling responded by ordering the free bridge barricaded at the Texas end.

## Alfalfa Bill Takes the Bridge

Oklahoma's governor moved quickly. Days later he ordered the new bridge open by executive order, arguing that the land on both sides of the river belonged to Oklahoma under the Adams–Onís Treaty of 1819. Oklahoma highway crews crossed the bridge and tore down the Texas barricades. Governor Sterling answered by sending Adjutant General William Warren Sterling and three Texas Rangers to protect the Texas Highway Department workers enforcing the injunction, and the barricade went back up that night. The next day, on Murray's orders, Oklahoma crews demolished the Oklahoma approach to the old toll bridge, leaving it impassable. Now neither bridge could be used.

## Martial Law on the Red River

Pressure built on the Texas side, too. Meetings in Sherman and Denison demanded that the free bridge be opened, and on July 23 the Texas Legislature met in special session to pass a bill allowing the bridge company to sue the state. The next day Murray declared martial law at the site, enforced by the Oklahoma National Guard, and showed up in person with his revolver, just hours before a court in Muskogee enjoined him from blocking the toll bridge approach. He told the guardsmen to let anyone cross either bridge. On July 27, learning that the free bridge might be closed permanently, Murray pushed the martial-law zone across the river and stationed guardsmen at both ends of the free bridge. Then, on August 6, the injunction was dissolved and the martial-law order rescinded. According to the Handbook of Texas, the guardsmen were sent off to enforce martial law in the Oklahoma oilfields instead.

## Opening Day

The bridge at the center of the fight was formally opened on Labor Day, September 7, 1931, and no one had been killed along the way. It carried traffic for more than six decades before being replaced in 1995; the Handbook of Texas records that the old structure was demolished on December 6 of that year. Not all of it disappeared. A portion of the old free bridge was saved as a historical attraction and moved to the city park in Colbert, Oklahoma, where it stands as a reminder of the summer two states squared off over the right to cross a river for free. Author Rusty Williams later told the whole saga in a 2016 book, The Red River Bridge War: A Texas-Oklahoma Border Battle.

## Flight Context

The bridge site lies at 33.8183 N, 96.5233 W on the Red River between Denison, Texas, and Colbert, Oklahoma, where U.S. Highways 69 and 75 cross the state line. From 2,000-3,500 feet AGL, follow the highway corridor north from Denison to the river; Denison Dam and Lake Texoma lie a few miles upstream to the west and make an unmistakable reference. Nearest airports: North Texas Regional Airport/Perrin Field (KGYI) between Sherman and Denison, and Durant Regional Airport-Eaker Field (KDUA), about three miles south of Durant, Oklahoma. Spring and early summer bring strong thunderstorms to the Red River valley.
