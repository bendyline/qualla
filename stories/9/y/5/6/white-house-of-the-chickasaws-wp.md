# White House of the Chickasaws

Built in 1895 near Emet, this Queen Anne mansion was home to Chickasaw Governor Douglas H. Johnston and his family for more than seven decades, and it is now a museum owned by the Chickasaw Nation.

**Location:** 34.2025, -96.5419

## Introduction

On July 21, 1902, a baby boy was born inside a gingerbread-trimmed mansion on the north edge of Emet. His name was Johnston Murray, and both he and his father, William H. Murray, would one day govern Oklahoma, the son as the state's 14th governor. Yet the house where Johnston Murray entered the world already belonged to a governor of another nation. It was the home of Douglas Henry Johnston, Governor of the Chickasaw Nation, and it came to be known as the Chickasaw White House.

## A Queen Anne House at the Edge of Emet

The house went up in 1895, designed by Dallas architect W.A. Waltham in the Queen Anne style, dressed in the gingerbread ornament common to Victorian building in the late nineteenth century. It stood at the northern edge of Emet, a small community where Johnston ran a store. Johnston served as Governor of the Chickasaw Nation from 1898 to 1902 and again from 1904 to 1939, and he and his family made the mansion their home beginning in 1898. His descendants stayed on long after him, living in the house until 1971. Its formal address today is 6379 E. Mansion Dr., Milburn, Oklahoma, but the nickname says more about what the place was: a family home that was also the residence of a nation's elected leader.

## Cherry, Walnut, and a Wagon Load from Texas

A showpiece like this asked for more than the surrounding countryside could supply. General construction lumber was obtained locally, but most of the finishing lumber, the wood that would be seen and admired, came by wagon from Denison, Texas. The two ornate fireplaces received mantels and facings of cherry mahogany brought from Chattanooga, Tennessee. Outside, a porch originally encircled the entire house, and its ornamental woodwork was fashioned from imported walnut. Taken together, the materials trace a map of ambition, with a governor's home assembled from Texas, Tennessee, and beyond.

## From Parlor to Kitchen

Walk the center of the house from south to north and you pass through four rooms in sequence. First comes the parlor, also called the music room. Next is the sitting room, which doubled as a library, then the state dining room, and finally the kitchen. That third room's name is a reminder that this was an official residence as well as a family home. Two-room wings flanked the central section. The west wing held Governor Johnston's own bedroom and office, and an open breezeway divided it from the sitting room, so the leader of the Chickasaw Nation crossed a stretch of open air between his library and his desk.

## Small Changes, Long Memories

For a house well over a century old, remarkably little has changed. Around 1903 or 1904 the breezeway was removed and replaced with a stairway climbing to the attic, where a bedroom and bathroom had been added. Indoor bathrooms came later, and the original coal-oil lamps, some hanging and some standing, eventually gave way to newer lighting. The walls also gathered family history. Beyond Johnston Murray's birth, the mansion hosted the wedding of Julie Chisholm, a niece of the Johnstons and a granddaughter of Jesse Chisholm. Family ties ran through the house in every direction: William H. Murray had married Mary Alice Hearrell, one of Johnston's nieces, which made the mansion's most famous baby the governor's great-nephew.

## Home Again to the Chickasaw Nation

When the mansion was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on August 5, 1971, it was still owned by Governor Johnston's descendants. Today it belongs to the Chickasaw Nation, which operates it as a historic house museum restored to its appearance in 1900, early in Johnston's first term as governor. The Chickasaw Nation's website currently lists the museum as temporarily closed for renovations, but when it is open, visitors walk through rooms arranged as they were when the nation's leader was at home here, beside the same cherry mahogany mantels that once came west from Chattanooga.
