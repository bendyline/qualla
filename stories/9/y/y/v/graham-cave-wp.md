# Graham Cave

A farm family kept hogs in this broad sandstone shelter above the Loutre River until their curiosity about its artifacts opened a record of human life reaching back as far as 10,000 years.

**Location:** 38.9053, -91.5731

**Categories:** Archaic period in North America, Caves of Missouri, National Historic Landmarks in Missouri, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Protected areas of Montgomery County, Missouri

## Introduction

For a time, Graham Cave was a hog shelter. D. F. Graham kept his hogs under its wide sandstone arch in the hills above the Loutre River, and the artifacts he found there made him curious about the people who had used the shelter long before any farmer arrived. His curiosity turned into a collection, and the collection eventually led archaeologists to a historically important site. Beneath the floor lay evidence of Dalton and Archaic period peoples dating back as early as 10,000 years ago.

## A Room Carved by Frost

The cave formed where two rock layers meet, Jefferson City dolomite and St. Peter sandstone. Over time, flowing and freezing water enlarged the opening, until its entrance became a broad arch 120 feet wide and 16 feet high, reaching about 100 feet into the hillside. Debris gradually filled the lower part of the cave with some seven feet of deposits. With such a generous mouth, the cave offered good shelter to people and animals alike. The sandstone shelter sits near Mineola in Montgomery County, now protected within Graham Cave State Park.

## A Family's Accidental Archive

Robert Graham first settled in the area in 1816, buying some bottomland from a son of Daniel Boone. In 1847 he bought the property that included the cave. It was his son, D. F. Graham, who sheltered hogs there and grew interested in archaeology from the artifacts he turned up. After D. F. Graham's death, his son Benjamin offered the collection to the University of Missouri, which investigated the cave in 1930. Farm life came close to reshaping the site. In the late 1940s, Benjamin's son-in-law planned to enlarge the shelter for his livestock, and he was persuaded to wait so that proper excavations could take place first.

## Reading the Layers

Between 1949 and 1955, the University of Missouri and the Missouri Archaeological Society excavated the deposits, and the state park board dug again in 1967 and 1968. What they found linked the site to the ways of life of Dalton and Archaic peoples. Among the discoveries was a circle of stones arranged around a larger stone, at or below a layer dated to about 8,800 years ago. The Missouri Archaeological Society published W. D. Logan's first report in 1952 and Walter Klippel's second in 1971, and the site has since featured in Carl Chapman's The Archaeology of Missouri (1975) and in The Prehistory of Missouri (1998) by O'Brien and Wood. Graham Cave was designated a National Historic Landmark on January 20, 1961, and added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966. Today the cave belongs to a state park operated by the Missouri Department of Natural Resources. Visitors are allowed up to the entrance, where interpretive signs point out the most important archaeological discoveries. Standing at the arch, you look into the same shelter that people used roughly 8,000 to 10,000 years ago, and that a farmer's hogs used much later.

## Flight Context

Graham Cave lies at approximately 38.9053° N, 91.5731° W, in wooded hills above the Loutre River near Mineola in Montgomery County, close to Interstate 70. The cave itself is hidden under the tree canopy; from 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, use I-70 and the winding Loutre River valley to locate the park. Hermann Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 63M, no ICAO code) is about 23 km to the south-southeast near the Missouri River, Mexico Memorial Airport (KMYJ) about 35 km to the northwest, and Elton Hensley Memorial Airport (KFTT) at Fulton about 38 km to the west. Valley fog can linger in the river bottoms on calm mornings.
