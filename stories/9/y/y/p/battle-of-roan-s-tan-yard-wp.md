# Battle of Roan's Tan Yard

On a foggy January afternoon in 1862, Union cavalry overran a pro-Confederate recruiting camp near Silver Creek in Randolph County, Missouri, in a brief fight that helped end Missouri State Guard organizing in the center of the state.

**Location:** 39.3246, -92.5584

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Missouri, Union victories of the American Civil War, Battles of the Trans-Mississippi theater of the American Civil War, 1862 in Missouri, Randolph County, Missouri

## Introduction

Fog lay over the field near Silver Creek when the Union cavalry struck, at around 4:30 on the afternoon of January 8, 1862. Hidden somewhere in it was a camp of the pro-Confederate Missouri State Guard, raw recruits gathered in Randolph County, Missouri, to learn soldiering. For a while the fog protected them. The Guardsmen held their ground, trading volleys with the attackers through the murk. Then a mounted charge broke their line, and within thirty or forty minutes the camp was lost. The same fog that had shielded the defenders now covered their escape. History remembers the fight as the Battle of Roan's Tan Yard, also called the Battle of Silver Creek.

## A Divided State

Missouri entered the Civil War deeply divided. It was a slave state that did not secede, though its governor, Claiborne Fox Jackson, supported secession. When Jackson and his followers moved against the St. Louis Arsenal, Union Brigadier General Nathaniel Lyon thwarted them on May 10, 1861, and Jackson responded by forming the Missouri State Guard, a pro-secession militia under Sterling Price. Lyon drove them into southwestern Missouri, only to be killed and his army driven back at Wilson's Creek on August 10. Price pushed north and captured Lexington in September, then retreated when Union troops concentrated against him. In late October, at Neosho, Jackson and the pro-secession legislators, already evicted from the capital, voted to secede and join the Confederacy as a government in exile, while the anti-secession members of the legislature had voted to remain in the Union. By December, Price's Guard was confined to the southwest.

## Burning the Railroad

Price looked north for men. In December 1861 he sent recruiters into central Missouri, hoping volunteers would join his command, and planned a raid on the North Missouri Railroad for the night of December 20. Several hundred men took part, burning bridges and damaging a 100-mile stretch of the line, and in the period that followed, thousands of Missourians joined Confederate-supporting units. To gather and train the newcomers, the Missouri State Guard set up a camp near Yates in Randolph County under Colonel John A. Poindexter. An 1864 source estimated he had around 1,000 men. Union authorities had known of the camp for more than a week but could not find it. In January 1862, while scouting near Silver Creek, Major W. M. G. Torrence of the 1st Iowa Cavalry finally learned where it was.

## Forty Minutes in the Fog

Torrence gathered detachments from his own regiment and from the 1st and 2nd Missouri and 4th Ohio cavalry regiments, a force the National Park Service estimates at around 450 men; a 1908 history of the Union Army puts it at about 500. About four miles from the camp, he laid out his plan. A battalion of the 2nd Missouri Cavalry and a company of the 4th Ohio would draw Poindexter's fire. Elements of the 1st Iowa and 1st Missouri would make a mounted charge, and three more companies of the 2nd Missouri would come in from another direction. When the attack went in, the Guardsmen used the fog and the terrain as cover, and the two sides traded volleys to a stalemate. Torrence broke it by sending four companies, three of them from the 1st Iowa, straight at Poindexter's line. The defenders fell into confusion, and after thirty or forty minutes of fighting the camp was taken. Torrence had sent two companies to block the retreat, but, as he stated in his official report, fog and terrain thwarted them.

## What the Field Keeps

The Union troops seized large quantities of supplies and equipment, then destroyed the camp, and Missouri State Guard recruiting in Randolph County came to an end. Together with the Guard's defeat at Mount Zion Church the previous December, the loss led to a decrease in pro-Confederate activity across central Missouri. The human cost is uncertain. Torrence estimated that 80 to 100 of Poindexter's men were killed or wounded and reported capturing 28 more; the National Park Service and historian Frances H. Kennedy put the Guard's total loss at 80. Union casualties are generally given as four or eleven, though one source places them as high as 27. Today the battlefield is privately owned and unmarked, though a museum in nearby Huntsville interprets the fight. A 2011 National Park Service update to the Civil War Sites Advisory Commission's battlefield survey found 1,329.25 acres likely eligible for the National Register of Historic Places, and land use there relatively unchanged since 1862.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lies at approximately 39.32°N, 92.56°W in Randolph County, north-central Missouri, near Silver Creek, not far from the town of Huntsville. The site is private farmland with no on-site markers; from 2,000–3,000 feet AGL you will see the rolling, largely unchanged countryside the 1862 cavalry crossed. Omar N. Bradley Airport at Moberly (KMBY) is about 10 nm northeast; Columbia Regional (KCOU) is about 34 nm south-southeast. Fog like the one that covered the battle can form in the creek bottoms on calm winter days.
