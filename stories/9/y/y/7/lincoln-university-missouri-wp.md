# Lincoln University (Missouri)

Founded in 1866 with money raised by Black Union soldiers, Lincoln University in Jefferson City became Missouri's first Black university and the starting point of a landmark civil rights case.

**Location:** 38.5651, -92.1695

**Categories:** Historically black universities and colleges in Missouri, Land-grant universities and colleges, Buildings and structures in Jefferson City, Missouri, Universities and colleges established in 1866, Civil rights history, African-American history

## Introduction

The money came from soldiers. During the Civil War, the 62nd Colored Infantry, a Black regiment of the U.S. Army largely recruited in Missouri, ran its own educational programs for its men. When the fighting ended, the 62nd and the 65th Colored Infantry together raised more than $6,000 to start a school. It would be headed by a white abolitionist officer, Richard Foster, a former first lieutenant in the 62nd, and founded with the help of James Milton Turner, a student and protégé of John Berry Meachum. In 1866 Foster opened the Lincoln Institute in Jefferson City. It became the first Black university in Missouri, and more than 150 years later, as Lincoln University, it is still teaching in the state capital.

## Teachers for a New Era

Lincoln Institute began with a Black student body, a faculty of both Black and white teachers, and outside support from religious groups. Its early purpose was urgent and practical. Missouri was building a new public school system for Black students, and the state government paid the institute $5,000 a year to train the teachers it needed, most of them men and women who had been enslaved. The Morrill Act of 1890 brought a larger role: Missouri designated Lincoln a land-grant institution, emphasizing agriculture, mechanics and teaching, and that status helped secure funding for its operations. By 1921 it had added graduate programs, and the state officially designated it a university under the name Lincoln University of Missouri. In 1954 it opened its doors to applicants of every ethnicity. Today it is a public, historically Black, land-grant university and a member school of the Thurgood Marshall College Fund, with 1,799 students enrolled in the fall of 2023.

## The Case of Lloyd Gaines

One Lincoln graduate helped change American law. Lloyd L. Gaines earned his history degree at Lincoln in 1935 and applied to the University of Missouri School of Law, which refused him because he was Black. He declined the state's offer to pay his tuition at a law school in a neighboring state and sued instead. On December 12, 1938, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada that forcing students to leave the state for an education because of their race violated the Fourteenth Amendment. Rather than integrate, Missouri lawmakers quickly created a law school for Black students associated with Lincoln in 1939. Gaines himself vanished before the matter was settled. He disappeared in Chicago in March 1939, his fate still unknown, and without him his case was dropped. The University of Missouri awarded him an honorary law degree in 2006, and his case is often seen as an early step on the road to Brown v. Board of Education.

## Scholars and Pioneers

Lincoln's faculty has included remarkable figures. Josephine Silone Yates, who headed its natural science department, is described as the first Black woman to head a college science department, and she may have been the first to hold a full professorship at any U.S. college. The sociologist Oliver Cromwell Cox, an early world-systems theorist, taught there from 1949 to 1970, and the historian Lorenzo Greene from 1933 to 1972. The composer R. Nathaniel Dett taught there, and in the early 1950s the tennis pioneer Althea Gibson, later a Wimbledon, French and U.S. champion, worked there as an athletics instructor. The alumni list is just as wide: Dorothy Butler Gilliam, the first African American woman reporter at The Washington Post; Tuskegee Airman Wendell O. Pruitt; chemist Moddie Taylor, who worked on the Manhattan Project; jazz musicians Oliver Lake and Julius Hemphill; and Ronald Townson, an original member of the 5th Dimension.

## Blue Tigers and the Musical Storm

Campus life carries its own traditions. Founder's Day, traditionally held on the first Saturday of February, pays tribute to the university's founders, and Homecoming brings families and alumni back each October. The Marching Musical Storm, founded in 1948, is one of the largest student organizations on campus and plays every home football game. The Blue Tigers compete in NCAA Division II, in the Great Lakes Valley Conference since 2024–25, and the women's track team made Division II history by winning the outdoor track and field championship five straight times. Even the alma mater, "Lincoln, O, Lincoln," has a story: it is sung to an 1827 German folk melody that West Point and Wake Forest also use for their alma maters. On May 22, 2019, an EF3 tornado struck Jefferson City and caused significant damage to the historic President's Residence near campus.

## Flight Context

Lincoln University sits at approximately 38.57°N, 92.17°W in Jefferson City, a short distance south of the Missouri River and the domed Missouri State Capitol. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, use the Capitol and the Missouri River bridges downtown as references; the campus lies about a mile south of the Capitol. Jefferson City Memorial Airport (KJEF) sits in the river bottoms directly across the Missouri from the Capitol, about two miles northeast of the city, and Columbia Regional Airport (KCOU), the only commercial airport in mid-Missouri, lies roughly 17 miles to the north. Expect river-valley fog on calm mornings, summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
