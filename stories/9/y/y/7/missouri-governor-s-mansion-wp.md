# Missouri Governor's Mansion

On a bluff above the Missouri River in Jefferson City, an 1871 red-brick mansion with a walnut Grand Stairway and pink granite columns has housed every Missouri governor since, on ground where the state once governed from two rooms.

**Location:** 38.5778, -92.1694

**Categories:** Governors' mansions in the United States, Houses completed in 1871, Historic house museums in Missouri, Buildings and structures in Jefferson City, Missouri, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri

## Introduction

Governor Robert Marcellus Stewart once rode a horse up the steps and into the governor's residence in Jefferson City, then ordered a servant to feed it a peck of oats. That was not the house that stands today. Neither was the one before it, a building where Missouri's governor lived in two rooms while the legislature met on the floors around him, until it burned. What stands today on the bluff above the Missouri River, at 100 Madison Street, is the third answer to a question the state kept asking through the 19th century: where, exactly, should its governor live?

## Two Rooms Above the Legislature

Before the capital moved to Jefferson City, Missouri's early governors from the St. Louis and St. Charles area lived in their own homes, renting houses in St. Charles when the need arose. The move changed that. On this bluff, in the vicinity of today's mansion, the state built a combined capitol and governor's residence, completed in November 1826 at a cost of $20,000. It measured 60 by 40 feet. The Missouri House of Representatives met on the ground floor, the Senate on the second, and the governor made do with two rooms. The architect, Stephen Hills, modeled the building on the first Pennsylvania State Capitol in Harrisburg. Hills later designed Academic Hall at the University of Missouri, of which only the landmark columns on the Francis Quadrangle survive. The capitol served until 1837, when it burned, and the state rebuilt a block to the west, where the capitol stands today.

## Wet Blankets and a Pineapple Sofa

Governor Daniel Dunklin, elected in 1832, refused to move his family into the combined building. So in the autumn of 1833 construction began on a separate $5,000 mansion at the end of the same block, finished in 1834, measuring 48 by 30 feet, with a portico. Its survival was not guaranteed. When the neighboring capitol burned on November 15, 1837, the mansion was saved by wet blankets spread across its roof. It suffered a fire of its own in the 1840s, under Governor Thomas Reynolds, and in 1844 Reynolds took his own life inside the house. A sofa with pineapple-shaped legs that belonged to him is now one of the oldest pieces of furniture in the current mansion. Governor Stewart, of the horse and the oats, launched a campaign for a new residence. He also pardoned all the women in the state prison and had them work in the mansion. The General Assembly set aside $20,000 for a replacement, but the Civil War interrupted the plan.

## Barnett's House on the Bluff

After B. Gratz Brown took office in 1871, several people said they would not come to large gatherings at the old mansion because they feared disaster. In March of that year the Assembly allocated $50,000 for a new one, and the state turned to George Ingham Barnett, the architect whose work includes most of the buildings Henry Shaw commissioned for the Missouri Botanical Garden. The result, built in 1871 at a cost of $75,000, is a Second Empire house of red brick, three stories tall and 66 feet square. Its signature exterior features are a 13-foot mansard roof and four pink granite columns from Brown's quarry in Iron County, Missouri. Inside, the most striking feature is the Grand Stairway, carved of walnut. The mansion has served every Missouri governor since.

## Deep Red, White, Then Pink Again

Each era has left its mark on the house, sometimes literally in paint. In the 1890s, Governor David Rowland Francis oversaw the first major renovation, which included painting the bricks deep red to hide soot stains. Under Lloyd C. Stark, $55,000 went to replacing the columns supporting the Grand Stairway with steel brackets, the exterior was painted white, and the Starks donated 3,000 plants from their own nursery. Then, during Kit Bond's administration in the 1980s, an extensive renovation tried to return the mansion to its 1871 appearance, bringing in Renaissance Revival furniture and restoring the pink brick exterior. The house was added to the National Register of Historic Places on May 21, 1969, and lies within the Missouri State Capitol Historic District. Volunteer docents lead free guided tours on weekdays in spring and fall, with advance reservations required through Missouri State Parks.

## Flight Context

The mansion is at approximately 38.58°N, 92.17°W in downtown Jefferson City, Missouri, on a bluff overlooking the Missouri River, about a block east of the Missouri State Capitol. Use the capitol building and the river as your landmarks; the mansion is the three-story brick house with a mansard roof just east of the capitol grounds. Jefferson City Memorial Airport (KJEF) is only about 1 nm to the northeast, so you will be close to its traffic; check its airspace and contact the airport before overflying downtown.
