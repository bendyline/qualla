# 351st Missile Wing LGM-30 Minuteman Missile Launch Sites

For three decades, 150 Minuteman nuclear missiles waited in silos scattered around the small towns of west-central Missouri, controlled from 15 buried command posts and finally imploded under the START I treaty.

**Location:** 38.6125, -92.7875

**Categories:** Installations of Strategic Air Command, Cold War sites, Military history, Nuclear weapons, Whiteman Air Force Base

## Introduction

Near Blackburn and Houstonia, Pilot Grove and Syracuse, Eldorado Springs, Adrian and Odessa, the Cold War was buried in the fields. From the mid-1960s until the early 1990s, the 351st Strategic Missile Wing at Whiteman Air Force Base kept 150 Minuteman intercontinental ballistic missiles in underground silos spread across west-central Missouri, each site logged by its distance and direction from the nearest small town. Fifteen buried launch control centers, each watched over by a pair of officers, stood ready to fire them. Today almost every trace is gone, imploded under an arms treaty, with a single command post preserved to show what it was like.

## Too Rugged for the Lake

Whiteman was announced as the wing's home on June 14, 1961, and second thoughts followed. The original layout would have spread launchers into the Lake of the Ozarks region, but the rugged terrain was hard to reach and the water table was high, so those plans were scrapped. When final approval came on January 17, 1962, the launchers were instead grouped around Whiteman Air Force Base near Knob Noster. That made the 351st the most compact of all the Minuteman bases by area. The wing was activated on February 1, 1963, as the third Minuteman ICBM wing in the Air Force. Its three squadrons, the 508th, 509th and 510th, were organized over the following months, beginning that May.

## On Alert

The first Minuteman IB missile arrived from the Boeing plant at Hill Air Force Base in Utah on January 14, 1964, and silo after silo came online. By June 29, 1964, the last flight of missiles had gone on alert and the wing was fully operational. Beginning in May 1966 the Air Force swapped out the Minuteman IBs for the newer LGM-30F Minuteman II, and when the changeover finished in October 1967, the 351st became the first wing to complete the Force Modernization Program. It also carried a mission no other wing had: operating the Emergency Rocket Communications System. Several of its launch facilities held rockets designed to relay emergency orders to surviving American strategic forces in the event of a nuclear attack, and several of its control centers had consoles to run them.

## Two Officers, Ten Missiles

Each squadron had five flights, lettered A through O across the wing. A flight's 01 site was its Missile Alert Facility: an above-ground support building with a helicopter pad, a large radio tower, a big "top hat" antenna, a garage for security vehicles and recreation facilities, paired with a buried, hardened launch control center. About a dozen airmen and officers were assigned to each facility, but the underground capsule itself was run by two launch officers responsible for ten hardened launch facilities, numbered 02 through 11 and scattered across the surrounding countryside. Every control center could also command and monitor all 50 missiles in its squadron. The silos themselves were unmanned, visited only when maintenance or security crews were needed. On a map, the sites read like a gazetteer of rural Missouri, from Lowry City to Windsor to Holden.

## Standing Down

The end came quickly. In late September 1991, President George H. W. Bush ordered Minuteman II missiles off alert, ending the 351st's role as an active ICBM wing, and the Air Force removed all 150 missiles from service. Under the START I treaty, the sites were then destroyed. The first silo was imploded on December 8, 1993, and the last on December 15, 1997. The wing and its three squadrons were inactivated on July 31, 1995. The missiles' presence had not gone unnoticed in popular culture. In 1983 the ABC television film The Day After, set around Kansas City, followed an airman at a fictional Minuteman site among western Missouri's 150 silos and reported Sedalia destroyed in a nuclear exchange.

## Oscar One

One piece survives. The Oscar One Missile Alert Facility, which controlled the O flight, was the only Minuteman launch control facility built on the support base itself, and it is now preserved as a museum at Whiteman. Posts like it were called Launch Control Facilities until 1992, when the inactivation of Strategic Air Command brought the new name Missile Alert Facility. A retired Minuteman IB missile is on display at the base as well. Because it sits inside an active installation, access is restricted, with tours arranged through the 509th Bomb Wing's public affairs office. That wing now defines Whiteman: it flies the B-2 Spirit stealth bomber from the same base that once commanded 150 missiles hidden in the Missouri countryside.

## Flight Context

The listed coordinates, approximately 38.61°N, 92.79°W, fall near the former D-01 Missile Alert Facility southeast of Syracuse, one of 165 former sites spread across a wide swath of west-central Missouri around Whiteman Air Force Base. From 3,000–4,500 feet AGL the former silo sites are small and hard to spot; the region's clearest landmarks are the towns and highways between them. Whiteman AFB (KSZL), home of the B-2, lies about 42 miles to the west near Knob Noster and is a military field with its own controlled airspace. Sedalia Regional Airport (KDMO) is the most convenient public airport, roughly 22 miles to the west-northwest. Expect summer haze and thunderstorms and gusty prairie winds.
