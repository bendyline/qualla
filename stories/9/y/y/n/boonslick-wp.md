# Boonslick

Named for a salt spring worked by Daniel Boone's sons, the Boonslick country of central Missouri was the end of the road for early settlers and the start of the trail to Santa Fe, a region shaped by river commerce, by slavery and by the frontier painter George Caleb Bingham.

**Location:** 39.0830, -92.6670

**Categories:** Regions of Missouri, Pre-statehood history of Missouri, Santa Fe Trail, African-American history, Westward expansion

## Introduction

A whole region of Missouri takes its name from a salt spring. In western Howard County, water rising from the ground carried enough salt that Nathan Boone and Daniel Morgan Boone, sons of the frontiersman Daniel Boone, produced salt from it. A salt spring like this is known as a "lick," and this one became Boone's Lick. In time the name came to cover a swath of counties along the Missouri River in the middle of the state, a cultural region that played an important role in America's westward expansion and in Missouri's journey from territory to statehood. Today the spring is preserved as Boone's Lick State Historic Site.

## The Road Out of St. Louis

For settlers in the early 19th century, the way into the Boonslick was the Boone's Lick Road. It ran from St. Charles to Franklin, paralleling the north bank of the Missouri River from southeast to northwest, and it was the primary route west for people setting out from St. Louis, the Mississippi River port founded by the French in 1764 and the first capital of Missouri Territory. The migrants came largely from the Upland South: down the Ohio River from Pittsburgh and the Ohio Country, from the young states of Kentucky and Tennessee, and through the passes of the Appalachians from Virginia. They overtook the older French and Spanish colonial presence in the region. And they did not come alone. Many brought enslaved African Americans with them, people who had no say in the journey west and whose labor was not their own.

## Where One Trail Ended and Another Began

The Boone's Lick Road ended at Franklin, founded in 1816, which grew into a large port on the Missouri River and an early center of settlement and trade. From there, William Becknell blazed the Santa Fe Trail southwest across the plains toward Santa Fe, the old capital of the Spanish colonial province of New Mexico. Franklin thus stood at a hinge of the continent, the western end of one road and the eastern start of another that would later connect with the Oregon and California Trails. The river that made Franklin also unmade it. In the late 1820s, devastating floods swept away the waterfront town, and it was rebuilt on higher ground as New Franklin. Other early towns took root across the region: Arrow Rock, Boonville, Fayette and Rocheport, as well as a village called Smithton, established by the Smithton Company in 1818, whose settlers moved their cabins to the newly laid-out town of Columbia in 1821.

## The Heart of Little Dixie

The Boonslick's borders have always been a little fluid, but definitions have included the present-day counties of Boone, Callaway, Cooper, Howard and Saline. Many of Missouri's early leaders came from here. Its settlers had brought the ways of the Upland South with them, slavery included, and before and after the Civil War the region developed as the center of a larger area known as Little Dixie. The artist George Caleb Bingham, who grew up in Franklin and later worked in Columbia, illustrated the pioneer, political and river life of the Western frontier in the early and mid-19th century. He lived from 1811 to 1879, and his paintings of the region's people and rivers outlasted the frontier they recorded.

## Vineyards and a Rail Trail

Columbia grew into the largest city in the region and the seat of Boone County, home since 1839 to the flagship campus of the University of Missouri. Beyond it, the Boonslick remains predominantly rural in the 21st century. The region borders the Missouri Rhineland and has developed vineyards of its own. The Katy Trail State Park, built on former railroad lines, follows the Missouri River through the region for cyclists and hikers, giving travelers a slow way to cross the same country the Boone's Lick Road once carried settlers through. Along the bluffs and bottomlands, the salt spring, the river towns and the rebuilt Franklin still trace the outline of the frontier that gave the Boonslick its name.

## Flight Context

The reference point is approximately 39.08°N, 92.67°W in Howard County, central Missouri, near the Missouri River. From 3,000–5,000 feet AGL, follow the broad Missouri River valley; the Boonslick spreads across the rolling farmland and river bottoms on both sides, with Columbia the only large city in the region. Jesse Viertel Memorial Airport at Boonville (KVER) is about 8 nm south; Columbia Regional (KCOU) is about 26 nm southeast; Marshall Memorial Municipal (KMHL) is about 25 nm west. River valley fog is common on calm mornings; afternoon haze in summer.
