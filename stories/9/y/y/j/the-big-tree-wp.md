# The Big Tree

A bur oak that sprouted in the 1600s stands alone in a Missouri River bottom field near McBaine, having outlasted the Great Flood of 1993 and a lightning fire in its own heart.

**Location:** 38.8993, -92.4646

**Categories:** Individual oak trees, Individual trees in the United States, Flora of Missouri, Tourist attractions in Boone County, Missouri, Nature

## Introduction

At around 9:30 on the morning of October 23, 2020, the Boone County Fire Department got a call about a tree that was smoking. Lightning had struck the Big Tree directly and set its core on fire. The response showed how much this one tree matters. The press covered the fire widely, it drew much community concern, and firefighters worked hard to save a bur oak (Quercus macrocarpa) that had been growing in the Missouri River floodplain since the 1600s. Standing alone in a field near Columbia, the Big Tree is tied for National Champion as the largest tree of its species in the United States.

## Older Than the Expedition

According to the U.S. Department of the Interior, the Big Tree is somewhere between 350 and 400 years old. Its sheer size has made coring and accurate dating difficult, so no one can pin down the year it sprouted. What is clear is that it was already standing, by the National Park Service's reckoning just under 200 years old, when the Lewis and Clark Expedition passed nearby, and the tree now stands along the Lewis and Clark National Historic Trail. Measurements vary: the National Park Service puts it at about 90 feet tall, while the Missouri Department of Conservation measured 74 feet. Either way, its crown spreads about 130 feet wide and its trunk is roughly 7.5 feet in diameter. Alone in the flat bottomland, with no forest around it to hide its shape, it has long been a well-known landmark, and it has been Missouri's state champion bur oak since 1987.

## Flood, Drought and Fire

Life in a floodplain is hard on a tree. The Big Tree has survived many droughts and floods, including the Great Flood of 1993, when water stood about six feet deep around its trunk. Lightning has struck it often. In October 2020, a strike lit a fire in the core and burned out a large space inside the tree. To reach the flames, Boone County firefighters drilled holes into the tree and used biodegradable foam to put out the blaze from within. Afterward, tree experts examined the oak and concluded that it was likely to survive. The fire was not its only trouble, though. Age, drought and flooding had already put the tree into decline for several years before the lightning came.

## Neighbors and Offspring

The land around the tree is private, and the Williamson family has farmed it for six generations. Even so, the tree is publicly accessible from Bur Oak Road. The Katy Trail State Park, a popular rail trail, runs just yards away, which makes the oak a common side-trip for cyclists. The small village of McBaine is within sight, and Huntsdale lies nearby. Over the years the tree has inspired artists, folklorists, photographers and nature lovers. Its reach now extends well beyond the field. Scientists at the University of Missouri cloned the oak, and a handful of those clones now grow on farms and in yards in mid-Missouri, one of them in the owner's front yard. The original still anchors its field by the river, scarred and aging. Its descendants carry its lineage into places the old tree will never see.

## Flight Context

The Big Tree stands at approximately 38.8993° N, 92.4646° W, in the Missouri River floodplain beside the village of McBaine, southwest of Columbia in Boone County. From 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, look for a single broad-crowned oak isolated in open bottomland fields, with the Katy Trail and the Missouri River close by; the tree's 130-foot crown stands out best in low morning or evening light. Columbia Regional Airport (KCOU) is about 23 km to the east-southeast, and Jesse Viertel Memorial Airport (KVER) at Boonville about 20 km to the west. River valley fog is common on calm mornings.
