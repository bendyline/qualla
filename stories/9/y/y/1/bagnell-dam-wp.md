# Bagnell Dam

Built in under two years as the Great Depression closed in, Bagnell Dam stopped the Osage River with 148 feet of concrete and created the Lake of the Ozarks, then the largest man-made lake in the United States.

**Location:** 38.2044, -92.6231

**Categories:** Dams in Missouri, Hydroelectric power plants in Missouri, Lake of the Ozarks, Dams completed in 1931, Dams on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Buildings and structures in Miller County, Missouri

## Introduction

Bagnell Dam was designed with thirteen floodgates. Only twelve were ever installed. The thirteenth spillway opening is walled shut with concrete, because the engineers calculated that twelve gates provided a sufficient safety margin, and for nearly a century that sealed bay has sat in the face of the dam as a quiet record of their confidence. During the 2019 Midwestern floods, the working gates stood completely open. Behind them lies the Lake of the Ozarks, a reservoir that did not exist until this wall of concrete rose across the river in the shadow of the Great Depression.

## A Dream That Ran Dry

The idea of harnessing the Osage River for electricity is older than the dam. A Kansas City developer introduced the concept as early as 1912, and Ralph Street managed to put together the funding to build a dam across the river. He began laying down roads, railroads and the support structures needed for construction, planning a much smaller lake than the one that exists today. Sometime in the mid-1920s, Street's money ran out, and he had to abandon what would have been the first hydroelectric plant on the Osage. Union Electric Power and Light stepped into the gap, bringing in the Boston engineering firm Stone & Webster to design and build the dam. The site took its name from the nearby town of Bagnell, itself named for William Bagnell, who platted the town in 1883, though the structure is also informally known as the Osage Dam.

## Pouring Concrete Into the Crash

Construction began in 1929. Just months later came the stock market crash that precipitated the Great Depression, and many predicted that the $30 million project would end in disaster. It did not. The dam employed thousands of laborers, drawing hundreds of workers from other parts of the country, and for the families of the surrounding rural Ozarks, the steady wages arrived at a moment when jobs were scarce everywhere. In less than two years, the dam was finished and the Lake of the Ozarks was a full reservoir. The numbers are still startling: a concrete gravity dam 148 feet tall and 2,543 feet long, including a 520-foot spillway and a 511-foot power station. Union Electric's administration building at Lakeside went up in 1930 and is now on the National Register of Historic Places.

## A Lake Where a River Ran

When the water settled, the Lake of the Ozarks covered about 54,000 acres, stretched roughly 92 miles from end to end and traced more than 1,150 miles of shoreline. At the time it was the largest man-made lake in the United States and one of the largest in the world. The transformation cut both ways. The reservoir virtually ended commercial traffic on the upper Osage River, and the dam itself became a road: Bagnell Dam Boulevard, a narrow two-lane highway, carried U.S. 54 until 1968 and then became a U.S. 54 business route. The river's old crossing faded more slowly. The Bagnell Ferry, which had carried traffic over the Osage since 1882, kept running until 1940, nearly a decade after the dam was finished.

## Anchoring an Old Giant

Inside Ameren's Osage Powerplant, eight generators can produce up to about 235 megawatts, and the dam provides power to roughly 42,000 homes. Keeping a dam of this age sound has required repeated reinforcement. In the 1980s, crews installed 277 post-tensioned anchors to help hold the structure into the surrounding bedrock. In 2017 a larger project began, adding a new series of anchors and concrete on the downstream face. Planned to take about eighteen months and reported finishing ahead of schedule in 2018, it added 66 million pounds of concrete and cost $53 million, well beyond the entire original construction budget in nominal dollars. The dam also works beyond its own walls: from the control room at Bagnell Dam, Ameren has remotely operated its Taum Sauk pumped storage plant.

## Flight Context

Bagnell Dam is at approximately 38.20°N, 92.62°W in Miller County, Missouri. The long concrete wall across the Osage River, with the Lake of the Ozarks spreading behind it and the narrow river channel continuing downstream, is an excellent visual reference from 2,500–4,500 feet AGL. Nearby airports: Lee C. Fine Memorial (KAIZ), about 7 nm south-southeast; Grand Glaize-Osage Beach (FAA identifier K15, no ICAO code), about 6 nm south-southwest. The lake's many arms can be disorienting, so keep the dam and U.S. 54 in view. Summer haze can reduce visibility over the water.
