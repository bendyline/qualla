# Lake of the Ozarks

Built to make electricity in 1931, the dragon-shaped Lake of the Ozarks drowned old river towns, gained 1,150 miles of shoreline, and became Missouri's great summer playground.

**Location:** 38.2034, -92.6235

**Categories:** Lake of the Ozarks, Reservoirs in Missouri, Bodies of water of the Ozarks, Hydroelectric dams, Tourism

## Introduction

Somewhere under the Lake of the Ozarks lie graves that were never moved. When the Union Electric Company cleared the valley of the Osage River for its new reservoir, it had to clear the old town of Linn Creek, and some cemeteries went unfound during the work and were left beneath the rising water. Several settlements disappeared the same way. Some accounts claim more than 20 towns and villages were drowned; the real number was closer to eight, since other hamlets had already been abandoned, were moved, or sat high enough to stay dry. What replaced them is a winding reservoir so large and serpentine that it earned the nickname the Missouri Dragon.

## A Dam on the Second Try

The first attempt belonged to Ralph Street, a Kansas City developer who in 1912 set out to dam the Osage for hydroelectric power. He raised money and began building the roads, railroads and other infrastructure a dam would need, planning a much smaller lake, but his funding dried up in the mid-1920s and he gave up. Union Electric of St. Louis built the dam that followed, on a far larger scale, with Stone and Webster as its principal engineering firm. Construction of Bagnell Dam began on August 8, 1929, and finished in April 1931. The lake reached spillway elevation on May 20, 1931, and opened to boats ten days later. The dam stretches 2,543 feet and stands 148 feet tall, with 12 floodgates and a narrow two-lane road across its crest. At the time the lake was the largest man-made lake in the United States and one of the largest in the world.

## Naming the Dragon

During construction the reservoir was called Osage Reservoir or Lake Osage, and the Missouri General Assembly officially named it Lake Benton after Senator Thomas Hart Benton. None of those names stuck. People simply called it by its location at the northern edge of the Ozarks, although the utility still calls its generating station the Osage Hydroelectric Plant. The lake that resulted is enormous: 54,000 acres of water, 1,150 miles of shoreline and a main Osage Arm that runs 92 miles end to end, draining more than 14,000 square miles. Arms reach up the Niangua River, Grandglaize Creek and Gravois Creek, and countless smaller tributaries carve the shore into coves. Seen from above, the whole thing twists across four counties, Benton, Camden, Morgan and Miller, in a serpentine shape that earned its nickname and inspired local names like the Magic Dragon Street Meet.

## Houses at the Water's Edge

The lake was built to generate power for Union Electric's customers, but it quickly became a major tourist destination. Unlike the flood-control lakes the Army Corps of Engineers built across the region, most of its shoreline is privately owned, and because the surface rarely rises or falls more than five feet, homes and docks could be built within a few feet of the water. That closeness eventually caused trouble. When federal regulators renewed the power plant's lease in 2011, they found numerous homes and structures encroaching on utility land, which the Boston Globe reported had "triggered panic" in lakefront communities. In 2015 regulators allowed Ameren Missouri to pursue permits for about 215 "nonconforming" structures still left in limbo. The lake's moods have made news too: flooding in 2019 opened Bagnell Dam's floodgates, a 2021 cold wave froze the lake over for the first time in 20 years, and drought lowered it in 2022.

## Bridges Over the Coves

A lake this crooked needs bridges, and their history tracks the region's growth. The Grand Glaize Bridge at Osage Beach dates to 1930; it once carried traffic both ways on a single span, gained a second bridge in 1984 and now carries three lanes each way on U.S. Route 54. The Hurricane Deck Bridge, begun in 1934 and finished in 1936 as a truss-arch span linking Laurie with Camdenton, was replaced in 2013 by a bridge more than 2,280 feet long. The Lake of the Ozarks Community Bridge, a truss nearly 2,700 feet long built in 1998 to join the east and west sides of the lake, was the area's only toll bridge, and the only one in Missouri whose toll changed with the seasons, until its tolls ended on April 30, 2024.

## Party Cove and Polar Bears

In 1934 the federal government began assembling about 17,500 acres along the Grand Glaize Arm, much of it bought from Union Electric, as a Recreational Demonstration Area. The state took the land over in 1946 as Lake of the Ozarks State Park, now the largest state park in Missouri, and Ha Ha Tonka State Park sits on the Niangua Arm. The state park is also home to Party Cove, which a New York Times writer called the "oldest established permanent floating bacchanal in the country"; the Missouri State Water Patrol has estimated it draws up to 3,000 boats over the Fourth of July weekend. Powerboats race in the Lake of the Ozarks Shootout at summer's end, and each February, for more than thirty years, plungers have jumped into the cold water to raise money for Special Olympics. The TV series Ozark, set in Osage Beach though filmed in Georgia, raised the lake's profile further.

## Flight Context

Lake of the Ozarks centers at approximately 38.20°N, 92.62°W on the Salem Plateau of central Missouri, with Bagnell Dam at its northeastern end near Lake Ozark. From 3,000–5,000 feet AGL the lake's long, twisting arms make it one of the easiest landmarks in the state to identify, the "dragon" shape unmistakable in clear weather; the dam, the Community Bridge and the U.S. 54 bridges at Osage Beach are good visual checkpoints. Lee C. Fine Memorial Airport (KAIZ), owned by the city of Osage Beach, lies inside Lake of the Ozarks State Park near Kaiser. Expect heavy recreational boat traffic below on summer weekends, summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms, and occasional fog over the water on cool mornings.
