# Battle of Mount Zion Church

Three days after Christmas 1861, Union troops stormed a hilltop churchyard in Boone County, Missouri, routing some 900 poorly armed local volunteers of the Missouri State Guard and ending formal Confederate recruiting in the region.

**Location:** 39.0692, -92.1792

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Missouri, Union victories of the American Civil War, Battles of the Trans-Mississippi theater of the American Civil War, 1861 in Missouri, Boone County, Missouri

## Introduction

A country church stands on a high hill in northern Boone County, Missouri, with a cemetery beside it. Some of the graves hold soldiers who died in the fighting here, and a memorial marker there honors the Missouri State Guard. On December 28, 1861, three days after Christmas, this churchyard was a battlefield. Most of the men defending it appear to have been local volunteers, many of them carrying personal civilian firearms rather than military weapons. By late morning they had been swept from the hill, and a Confederate effort to organize central Missouri had been broken.

## Guarding the Railroad

The trouble began with the North Missouri Railroad. In the closing weeks of 1861, pro-Confederate forces were recruiting across central Missouri, and the line was a target. On December 24, Brigadier General Benjamin M. Prentiss set out from the Union's northern Missouri headquarters at Palmyra with five companies of the Third Missouri Cavalry and two companies of Birge's Western Sharpshooters. His mission was to protect the railroad, disrupt the organizing of the secessionist Missouri State Guard and generally overawe secessionist sentiment in the region. On December 26 his troops discovered a concentration of State Guardsmen near Hallsville. The next day, a Union company sent to Hallsville ran into a detachment under Colonel Caleb Dorsey about half a mile north of Mount Zion Church. The skirmish lasted ten minutes. The Federals took casualties, lost men as prisoners, and fell back to Sturgeon by 6 p.m.

## Three Charges Up the Hill

Prentiss answered with everything he had. At about 2 a.m. on December 28, he set out with his entire force, some 440 men, to find Dorsey. The State Guardsmen numbered around 900, but most appear to have been local volunteers, and many lacked military weapons, though many had personal civilian firearms of various kinds. On the road from Hallsville, about 100 yards east of the church, a Union detachment routed one company of Guardsmen and learned that the rest were in the churchyard on the hilltop. Their main campground lay in a thicketed hollow just east of the church. Prentiss attacked, then attacked again. At the third charge, the defenders ran out of ammunition, fell back to their wagons and were overrun. By around 11 a.m., it was over.

## Counting the Fallen

The Guardsmen paid heavily. By the commonly cited figures, Dorsey's force lost about 25 dead, 150 wounded and 60 captured, among them an officer, along with wagons, 90 horses and 105 weapons. The Union toll is usually given as 3 dead, 63 wounded and 4 captured. Behind those numbers were mostly local men, many of them fighting with their own civilian guns. The survivors were driven from northern Boone County toward Columbia. Dorsey led what remained westward into Perche Township, in the county's northwest corner toward the Missouri River, and after negotiations and an exchange of prisoners he made camp near Everett. Within days, orders came from the Guard's commander, Major General Sterling Price: scatter the force.

## The End of Open Recruiting

Mount Zion, together with the Battle of Roan's Tan Yard in Randolph County the following month, effectively ended attempts at formal Missouri State Guard organization in central Missouri. Remaining elements of Dorsey's command crossed the Missouri River in February 1862. The war in the region did not end; guerrilla fighting reappeared to the south and west of Columbia. The next sizable battle nearby came in July 1862 at Moore's Mill in neighboring Callaway County, and a Confederate army did not return to the region in force until Price's Raid in the autumn of 1864. Today Mount Zion Church and its cemetery are listed on the National Register of Historic Places. In the quiet churchyard, the graves of soldiers who perished that December keep the memory of the fight close to the hill where it happened.

## Flight Context

Mount Zion Church stands at approximately 39.07°N, 92.18°W near Hallsville in northern Boone County, central Missouri. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, look for a small church and cemetery on a rise amid farmland; the site is small and best found by cross-referencing the Hallsville area. Columbia Regional (KCOU) is about 15 nm south; Mexico Memorial (KMYJ) is about 18 nm east-northeast; Omar N. Bradley Airport at Moberly (KMBY) is about 26 nm north-northwest. Morning fog can linger in the hollows in cool seasons.
