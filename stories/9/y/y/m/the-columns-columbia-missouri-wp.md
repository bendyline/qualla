# The Columns (Columbia, Missouri)

Six limestone columns that survived the 1892 fire at the University of Missouri's first building, then survived the university's own plan to tear them down, now stand at the heart of Mizzou's Francis Quadrangle.

**Location:** 38.9463, -92.3288

**Categories:** University of Missouri buildings and structures, Greek Revival architecture in Missouri, Burned buildings and structures in the United States, University and college buildings completed in 1843, National Register of Historic Places in Boone County, Missouri

## Introduction

"The Columns could not be pulled down by a herd of elephants." So declared Jerry S. Dorsey, a leading citizen of Columbia, Missouri, in 1893, when the University of Missouri's leadership decided that the six stone columns standing in the ruins of its first building had to go. The president of the university's Board of Curators, Gideon F. Rothwell, answered that the columns were coming down "even if he had to dynamite them." Neither elephants nor dynamite were ever tested. The Columns still stand, 43 feet tall at the center of Francis Quadrangle, and they have become the most recognizable landmark of the university that once wanted them gone.

## The First Building West of the River

Academic Hall rose between 1840 and 1843 as the first building of the University of Missouri, the first state university in the Louisiana Purchase Territory. Its designer was Stephen Hills, an accountant as well as an architect, who had also designed the first Missouri State Capitol building in Jefferson City. The portico's columns were assembled from drums of limestone quarried in the Hinkson Creek Valley, just south of the campus. Academic Hall served the young university for nearly half a century. Then, in 1892, it burned to the ground, and the painter George Caleb Bingham's full-length portrait of James S. Rollins was lost in the blaze. When the smoke cleared, only the columns of the portico were left standing in the rubble.

## Mule Teams and a Writ

Not everyone saw the survivors as worth saving. In the months after the fire, many people considered the Columns an eyesore that blocked the view of the new buildings, later known as Red Campus, going up on the Quadrangle, and some worried that the columns were structurally unsound and a safety hazard. In August 1893 the board of curators voted to remove the Columns as unsafe and ugly, and a local newspaper reported that Rothwell had ordered two mule teams to pull them down and haul them away. Dorsey led the protest. He obtained a judicial writ that halted the immediate destruction, and an architect reported that the Columns were structurally sound. Faced with public opposition and assurance that the stone posed no hazard, Rothwell and the other curators had a change of heart in December 1893. The Columns stayed.

## Walking Through

More than a century later, the university has built its rituals around the very columns it once tried to topple. At the start of each academic year, new freshmen take part in Tiger Walk, passing through the Columns to symbolize their move from the wider community into the university. On Tap Day, the school's secret societies gather beneath them to announce their new members. The Columns have also framed graduations, concerts, weddings, pranks and protests, and Mizzou's school song mentions them. Standing at the south end of the Avenue of the Columns, with Jesse Hall rising beyond, they form one of the most photographed sites in Missouri. They are a contributing structure to the Francis Quadrangle National Historic District, and in 2017 they underwent a major preservation effort. That fall the university even offered a course called "The Geology of the Columns."

## Columns in Paint, Glass and Print

Their image has traveled far beyond the Quadrangle. A mural of James S. Rollins with the Columns hangs in the office of the Missouri Governor in the State Capitol, and they appear in a monumental stained-glass window, Missouri at Peace, in the Missouri House Chamber. Academic Hall and the Columns also feature in murals in the Boone County Courthouse and the Columbia Municipal Court. Even literature has noticed them: John Williams describes the Columns briefly in his 1965 novel Stoner. For columns built of limestone drums from the Hinkson Creek Valley, it is quite a career, and one that very nearly ended behind a pair of mule teams in 1893.

## Flight Context

The Columns stand at approximately 38.95°N, 92.33°W on Francis Quadrangle at the University of Missouri, in the center of Columbia. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, look for the long green rectangle of the quadrangle with Jesse Hall at one end; the Columns stand in the middle of the lawn, though they are small from the air. Columbia Regional Airport (KCOU) is about 9 nm southeast, so expect to be near its airspace; Jefferson City Memorial (KJEF) is about 23 nm south-southeast. Best seen in clear conditions with low sun, which throws long shadows across the quad.
