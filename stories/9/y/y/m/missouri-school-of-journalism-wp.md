# Missouri School of Journalism

The world's first school of journalism opened in Columbia, Missouri, in 1908, published a newspaper on its first day, and built a century of firsts on the idea that journalism is learned by doing it.

**Location:** 38.9481, -92.3282

**Categories:** University of Missouri, Journalism schools in the United States, Universities and colleges established in 1908, Media history, Civil rights history

## Introduction

On September 14, 1908, a new school opened at the University of Missouri in Columbia, and by the end of that first day its students had already published a newspaper. That first issue of the University Missourian, today the Columbia Missourian, set the pattern for everything that followed. The Missouri School of Journalism, widely recognized as the world's first school of journalism, was built on a simple conviction: reporters learn their craft by practicing it, on real deadlines, for real readers. More than a century later, its students still staff a daily newspaper, a commercial television station and a public radio station.

## Pulitzer's Nudge

Before 1908, newspapers usually trained their people through apprenticeships, and the idea of teaching journalism at a university was a hard sell. A bill to create a chair of journalism at the University of Missouri had been defeated in the state Senate 13 years earlier. The Missouri Press Association began backing the idea in 1896, and Walter Williams, editor of the Columbia Herald and a university curator, lobbied for it. Joseph Pulitzer urged the school's founding too, and Williams became its official founder and first dean. He also wrote the Journalist's Creed, a statement of the profession's principles that hangs on a bronze plaque at the National Press Club in Washington. Among the first faculty was Charles Griffith Ross, who later became press secretary to President Harry S. Truman. Classes started in Switzler Hall, and in 1910 the school launched its annual Journalism Week.

## A Century of Firsts

The school kept inventing its own field. Jay H. Neff Hall, its first dedicated building, was funded by 1913 graduate Ward A. Neff in honor of his father, a former Kansas City mayor and publisher, and his gift was then the largest in university history. In 1921 Missouri offered the world's first master's degree in journalism, and in 1934 the world's first Ph.D. in the subject. Broadcast courses began in 1936. In 1944 professor Clifton C. Edom and his wife, Vi, created the News Pictures of the Year competition, known today as Pictures of the Year International, and a year later they added a college photography contest. In 1958 the school opened the Freedom of Information Center, the world's first academic center devoted to that subject.

## The Missouri Method

Hands-on work is the heart of what the school calls the Missouri Method. In 1953 the university launched KOMU-TV, now mid-Missouri's NBC affiliate and one of only two commercial television stations in the United States run by a public university, where students produce the news. In 1971 radio news moved to KBIA, an NPR member station. Students also report for the Columbia Missourian, a daily general-circulation paper, as well as the entertainment magazine Vox, the digital newsroom Missouri Business Alert, a state government bureau in Jefferson City and a student-run advertising agency. The campus itself tells the story, from Walter Williams Hall and the stone-lion arch between it and Neff Hall to Lee Hills Hall and the Reynolds Journalism Institute, which opened in 2008 after the Donald W. Reynolds Foundation gave $31 million in 2004, the largest private donation the university had ever received.

## A Door Held Shut

The school's history includes a painful chapter. In 1939 Lucile Bluford, a reporter for the Kansas City Call, was accepted into its graduate program, but when she arrived in Columbia to enroll, officials who had not known she was Black turned her away. She sued, and in 1941 the Missouri Supreme Court upheld the judgment barring her admission, reasoning that Lincoln University, the state's university for Black students, had not refused to set up a journalism program. The School of Journalism later closed its graduate program, saying it could not operate properly while most of its professors and students were serving in World War II. Bluford never attended. She went on to spend nearly seventy years at the Call, rising to editor and publisher. In 1957 George McElroy, a pioneering Black journalist from Texas, became the first African American to earn a master's degree in journalism at the university. In 1989 the university gave Bluford an honorary doctorate, and it has since named a residence hall for her.

## Graduates in Every Newsroom

The school has been ranked the best journalism school in the country more than once in the NewsPro-RTDNA survey, and its alumni have reached nearly every corner of media. Jim Lehrer anchored the PBS NewsHour, Elizabeth Vargas co-anchored ABC's 20/20, and Major Garrett became CBS News' chief White House correspondent in 2012 before becoming chief Washington correspondent in 2018. Gerald M. Boyd rose to managing editor of The New York Times, and several graduates have won Pulitzer Prizes. Some famous names took other paths: horror writer Richard Matheson, author of I Am Legend, studied here, Adrian Holovaty went on to co-create the Django web framework, and actors George C. Scott and Brad Pitt both attended without finishing journalism degrees.

## Flight Context

The school sits on the University of Missouri campus in Columbia at approximately 38.95°N, 92.33°W. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, look for the large university campus near downtown Columbia, with the columns of the Francis Quadrangle and the stadium as reference points, and Interstate 70 crossing the north side of the city. Columbia Regional Airport (KCOU), the only commercial airport in mid-Missouri, lies about 11 miles (9 nm) to the southeast. Expect summer haze and thunderstorms, gusty spring winds, and occasional winter icing.
