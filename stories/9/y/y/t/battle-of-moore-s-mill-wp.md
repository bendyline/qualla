# Battle of Moore's Mill

On July 28, 1862, Confederate recruiter Joseph C. Porter chose to ambush rather than run, and the hours-long fight that followed along Auxvasse Creek helped end Southern recruiting in northern Missouri.

**Location:** 38.9098, -91.8468

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Missouri, Union victories of the American Civil War, Battles of the Trans-Mississippi theater of the American Civil War, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Callaway County, Missouri

## Introduction

Colonel Odon Guitar's Union troopers had no warning. On July 28, 1862, they were advancing along Auxvasse Creek in central Missouri when rebels concealed in the trees and brush beside the path unleashed two devastating volleys into them before they could react. Colonel Joseph C. Porter, the Confederate recruiter whom Union forces had been pursuing for more than a week, had decided not to run. The fight that followed near modern-day Calwood, remembered as the Battle of Moore's Mill, would last for hours. Together with the battles at Kirksville and Compton's Ferry, it would drive the main Southern recruiters out of northern Missouri.

## Decrees of No Quarter

Missouri had been plagued by guerrilla warfare from the start of the conflict, and in early 1862 it intensified as the weather warmed. On March 13, General Henry W. Halleck issued General Order Number 2, warning Missourians that if they joined any guerrilla band they would not be treated as ordinary prisoners of war if captured, "but will be hung as robbers and murderers." Confederate President Jefferson Davis answered on April 21, attempting to legitimize guerrilla warfare by authorizing commissions for men who formed bands of "partisan rangers." By summer the state had been stripped of nearly all its troops except the new volunteer regiments of the Missouri State Militia Cavalry, and guerrilla raids and Confederate recruiting pressed harder with every week. Union commanders began to close in on Porter's recruiters and the guerrillas riding with them, fighting small actions at Vassar Hill on July 19, at Florida on July 22, and at Santa Fe on July 24.

## An Order That Forced a Choice

On July 22, General Schofield, with the aid of Missouri's provisional governor Hamilton Rowan Gamble, ordered a compulsory militia enrollment. His General Orders No. 19 required every able-bodied loyal man to join what became the Enrolled Missouri Militia, and required the disloyal to register and state their sympathies. The measure did raise a force for garrison duty and local policing, freeing the mounted militia to hunt guerrillas and recruiters. It also left men of Southern loyalties with nowhere to stand. Thousands chose the brush, joined guerrilla bands, or sought out recruiters for the Southern army, and the commands of Joseph C. Porter and John A. Poindexter benefited at once as sympathizers flocked to their banners. The new militia would need several weeks before it was ready to take the field.

## The Empty Camp at Brown's Spring

In Jefferson City on July 27, Guitar, colonel of the 9th Missouri State Militia Cavalry, received word that Porter and the guerrilla leader Alvin Cobb were camped together at Brown's Spring, only 11 miles north of Fulton. He took 100 men of his regiment and a section of artillery from the 3rd Indiana Battery, crossed the Missouri River, and rode to Fulton, where 54 men of Captain Duffield's 3rd Iowa Cavalry joined him. At Brown's Spring a small skirmish broke out nearby, but the camp had recently been abandoned. Learning that Lieutenant Colonel W.F. Shaffer had arrived in the area with 500 men, Guitar camped for the night. Porter, meanwhile, had at least 260 men under his overall command, among them 65 Boone County men of the Blackfoot Rangers under Lieutenant John Bowles and 75 guerrillas under Captain Cobb. Rather than slip away, he laid an ambush along the banks of Auxvasse Creek.

## Two Guns in the Brush

The next morning Shaffer's column joined Guitar's, giving the Federals 733 men. Guitar tried to trap Porter by sending Shaffer east across the creek with 464 men in a flanking movement while he advanced west of the creek with 269. He moved too fast. Shaffer's wing had not finished its maneuver when the volleys came out of the trees. Guitar ordered his men to dismount, take cover in the woods and bring the cannon forward: two 6-pounder guns, served by Lieutenant Adolphus G. Armington's section. Porter had no artillery, and the Federals hoped the guns would unnerve his men. For an hour they held. Then Porter called out, "Boys, we can't stand this, we shall have to charge them—Forward! Charge!" His men shot down the gunners, overran at least one of the two pieces and drove the Federals back in disorder. Shaffer's troops, who had countermarched at the sound of volley fire and artillery, now reached the field, and one company surged forward, retook the guns and sent the rebels reeling back to their original positions.

## Names in the Fulton Telegraph

The firing went on for several more hours until Porter's men ran short of ammunition and the order came to withdraw. Many Confederates later reported that this was when many of their casualties fell, as men stood or left cover and exposed themselves. The Union troops were too exhausted to pursue. How many died can only be estimated. Guitar's report claimed 52 killed and 125 to 150 wounded, figures other accounts do not support. The Fulton Telegraph of August 1 printed the names of the Confederates known to be dead, captured and wounded, and noted several wounded men who refused to give their names. Joseph A. Mudd, who fought there and later wrote about it, quoted a Shelby County history reporting eleven killed and twenty-one severely wounded. Porter's campaign ended in defeat at Kirksville on August 6, and he ordered his men to scatter south. Guitar fell ill and missed that victory. Southern recruiting in northern Missouri never recovered, and in 2015 the Moore's Mill Battlefield was added to the National Register of Historic Places.

## Flight Context

The listed coordinates (38.91 N, 91.85 W) are the historical marker on Route JJ south of Calwood in Callaway County, Missouri, about seven miles northeast of Fulton; the battle was fought along Auxvasse Creek, in rolling farm and woodland country. Terrain here lies roughly 800-900 feet MSL, so 2,500-3,500 feet MSL gives a good view of the creek's wooded bottomland winding between fields. The Missouri River and Jefferson City lie to the southwest. Nearby airports: Elton Hensley Memorial Airport in Fulton (KFTT) about 8.5 nm west-southwest of the site, Columbia Regional Airport (KCOU) about 18 nm west-southwest, and Jefferson City Memorial Airport (KJEF) about 24 nm southwest. Expect summer haze and afternoon convection over central Missouri.
