# Cheyenne Bottoms

A 41,000-acre sink in the middle of Kansas, once slated for draining, now feeds hundreds of thousands of migrating shorebirds as the largest wetland in the interior United States.

**Location:** 38.4628, -98.6528

**Categories:** Wetlands of Kansas, Ramsar sites in the United States, Nature reserves in Kansas, Protected areas of Barton County, Kansas, Great Plains

## Introduction

About 45 percent of all the shorebirds in North America use one shallow basin in the middle of Kansas. Cheyenne Bottoms is a natural sink of roughly 41,000 acres, some 64 square miles, pressed into the plains a few miles northeast of Great Bend, and it is the largest wetland in the interior United States. In spring, as many as 600,000 shorebirds from 39 species drop onto its mudflats to feed; in fall, up to 200,000 more come through. For a place so vital, it has spent much of its recorded history being schemed over, nearly drained, flooded by surprise and patched back together.

## A Sink in the Plains

Nobody is entirely sure how the basin formed. One of the most popular theories holds that it began as a sinkhole, created when incoming fresh water dissolved underground salt, though critics counter that there is not enough salt in the base to explain it. Others point to structural movement in the base, to an old Pleistocene drainage system involving the Smoky Hill River, or to sand dunes that scoured the flatlands and blocked the drainage. Whatever shaped it, the sink now gathers water from modest sources. Two small local streams, Blood Creek and Deception Creek, flow in from the northwest, and an inlet canal brings water from Walnut Creek to the southwest. On the far side, an outlet canal drains southeast to Little Cheyenne Creek, which empties into Cow Creek, a tributary of the Arkansas River. The whole shallow bowl lies at an elevation of about 1,795 feet.

## Blood Creek and the Coursing Hounds

Even the names carry stories. According to legend, an 1825 battle between the Cheyenne and the Kiowa, or perhaps the Pawnee, turned one of the streams blood red, and Blood Creek still flows into the lowlands. Six decades later the bottoms hosted a very different contest: greyhound racing in the United States traces its start to a coursing event held here in 1886. Early schemes treated the basin as a problem to be engineered. In 1900 a project called the Koen Ditch tried to divert Arkansas River water 12 miles to the Bottoms for irrigation, until a flood washed the ditch out. During the 1920s, various plans proposed draining the wetland and converting it to farmland. Residents downstream in Hutchinson objected, protesting that draining the Bottoms would create flooding problems for them.

## The Night It Became a Lake

In 1925 Kansas created the Forestry, Fish and Game Commission to develop and care for the Bottoms. Two years later the wetland showed what it could do on its own. In August 1927, fourteen inches of rain fell upstream, and overnight the basin became "Lake Cheyenne," with flooding downstream along Little Cheyenne Creek. Kansas politicians including Clifford Hope, Charles Curtis, Henry Allen and Arthur Capper pleaded for federal money to turn it into a National Wildlife Refuge, without success. The state carried on. Once dikes, roads and hunting blinds were built, part of the area opened to public hunting in 1952. A new canal from the Arkansas River followed in 1957, but drought and other claims on the water meant relatively little was ever pumped in. In the 1990s an extensive renovation subdivided the marshes and made them more self-sustaining, although an adequate water supply and the management of water levels remain critical problems.

## Mudflats on the Flyway

The payoff is written in wings. Cheyenne Bottoms is especially noted for its concentrations of migratory shorebirds, which come to feed on the mudflats, and at least 340 bird species have been observed here. It is critical habitat for many endangered species, including the whooping crane, and on October 19, 1988, it was designated a Ramsar Wetland of International Importance. Management today is a partnership. The Kansas Department of Wildlife and Parks owns the core 19,857-acre Cheyenne Bottoms Wildlife Area, while The Nature Conservancy, which began acquiring adjacent land in the 1990s, owns roughly another 7,000 to 8,000 acres. Both are managed toward the same goal: a diverse marsh habitat for migrating and breeding waterfowl and shorebirds.

## A Wonder Beside the Highway

For all its scale, the Bottoms is easy to reach. U.S. Route 281 runs north and south two miles to the west, Kansas Highway 4 passes to the north, and Kansas Highway 156 cuts through the wetland's southeast portion. Inside the wildlife area, the Kansas Wetlands Education Center interprets the marshes and their wildlife for visitors. On January 29, 2008, Cheyenne Bottoms and Quivira National Wildlife Refuge were jointly named one of the 8 Wonders of Kansas. Stand on a dike in spring, with water and sky on every side and flocks lifting off the flats, and it is hard to believe anyone once wanted to plow it under.

## Flight Context

Cheyenne Bottoms is centered near 38.463°N, 98.653°W at about 1,795 feet elevation, roughly six miles northeast of Great Bend and four miles southeast of Hoisington, Kansas. The 64-square-mile basin of pools, dikes and marsh stands out clearly against surrounding cropland from 3,000-6,000 feet; U.S. Route 281 runs two miles to the west and Kansas Highway 156 crosses the southeast corner. Great Bend Municipal Airport (KGBD) lies about five miles west of Great Bend, and Salina Regional Airport (KSLN) is farther northeast. Expect very large concentrations of birds during spring and fall migration; keep a generous altitude over the wildlife area. Visibility is usually good, but spring and summer thunderstorms build quickly over central Kansas.
