# Victoria, Kansas

Founded by a Scottish dreamer as a colony for British gentry and named for Queen Victoria, this Kansas town was made to last by Volga German farmers from Russia.

**Location:** 38.8536, -99.1475

**Categories:** Cities in Kansas, Cities in Ellis County, Kansas, Volga German settlements, Populated places established in 1873, Railroad towns

## Introduction

The town was named for a queen and settled first by her subjects, yet within a few years Kansans were calling the surrounding country "Little Russia." Victoria, Kansas, holds both stories at once. In one cemetery lies George Grant, the Scottish entrepreneur who arrived in 1873 intending to build a ranching colony for young British gentlemen. In another stand more than a hundred iron crosses left by the German settlers from Russia who took over his half-empty townsite and made it a lasting home.

## Rails Across Cheyenne Country

Victoria's story begins with the railroad. In 1867 the Kansas Pacific Railway pushed into western Kansas, aiming to link St. Louis with Denver and open the land to settlement. Its sudden rerouting west of Junction City was opposed by a Cheyenne military society, the Dog Soldiers, who rejected the treaty that permitted the construction. They attacked the Campbell camp, a crew building a bridge grade across the North Fork of Big Creek at this spot, and killed a party of workers excavating the bridge approaches. The immediate U.S. military response, commanded by Captain George Armes, led to the Battle of the Saline River and two years of open conflict. The Railroad Workers Cemetery still preserves the graves of the men who died here, a quiet reminder that the line was contested from its first days.

## A Colony Fit for Gentry

George Grant had built a fortune and a clientele that counted British nobility among it. In 1873 he led a party of 30 young people of Scottish and English noble families to Kansas, among them remittance men, sons sent away by their families to live on stipends. It was the first of several organized transatlantic settlements in Ellis County, and some of the lesser nobles hoped to carve out large frontier estates. They brought a herd of Aberdeen Angus cattle, some reportedly from Queen Victoria's own stock. Including four bulls, that herd is credited with establishing the American Angus breed. The settlement took the Queen's name, and the railroad quickly built an elaborate stone station-hotel, Victoria Manor, with lodgings suited to gentry awaiting their new homes and a ballroom on the ground floor. More than 200 Britons followed.

## The Grid That Broke the Dream

Grant brought the British architect Robert William Edis to design his manor house and lay out his dream town. Grant's Villa became a historic landmark, but the town plan that emerged was an unprepossessing grid of three blocks by three. When the remittance men's families learned of it, they cut the stipends, and the colonists began to leave. Grant lost his fortune and died suddenly in 1878. He was buried before the steps of St. George Church, a church that was never consecrated. His niece, who had been his caretaker and became executor of his estate, was among the remaining colonists who platted the Victoria townsite in 1880. Every 50 years the American Aberdeen-Angus Breeders' Association returns to commemorate the 1873 herd, and for the centennial in 1973 the Hays artist Pete Felten Jr. installed a limestone sculpture of a Black Angus.

## Little Russia on the Prairie

When the Volga German settlers first arrived at the station, according to an account by the Rev. Laing, Victoria consisted of the station-hotel and one other building. Their own village, Herzog, grew rapidly. The German-Russian settlers of Ellis and Rush counties brought western Russian farming practice and community structure, along with Russian dress and elements of the Russian language, which is why Kansans called their settlements Little Russia, and Herzog came to be known as "Russia Minor." Herzog eventually adopted the station's name, and in 1913 the two towns merged as Victoria. Their Catholic faith produced a series of ever-larger churches, culminating in 1911 with the twin-towered St. Fidelis, the "Cathedral of the Plains." Its cemetery holds more than 100 traditional German iron cross grave markers, the most of any Volga German settlement cemetery, along with gravestones John Linenberger carved from the region's unusual Fencepost limestone.

## Bombers, Then the Bypass

The 20th century brought new traffic. In 1942 the U.S. Army built Walker Army Airfield three miles northeast of town, where thousands were stationed during World War II, most of them training to operate the Boeing B-29 Superfortress. The base closed in 1946; the new Air Force designated it Victoria Auxiliary Field in 1949 but never operated it. The Golden Belt Road, followed by U.S. 40, once ran straight through Victoria, until Interstate 70 was completed through Ellis County in 1966 and carried the traffic north of town. The city had 1,129 residents at the 2020 census. Each August they hold Herzogfest, celebrating their German heritage with concerts, a tractor pull and games for the children, a festival named for the village that outlasted the queen's colony.

## Flight Context

Victoria sits at 38.854°N, 99.148°W on the east side of the North Fork of Big Creek, in the Smoky Hills about 9 miles north of the Smoky Hill River. From 2,000-4,000 feet, the twin 141-foot towers of the Basilica of St. Fidelis mark the town clearly; Interstate 70 and U.S. 40 run east-west about a mile to the north. The site of the abandoned Walker Army Airfield lies roughly three miles northeast. Hays Regional Airport (KHYS) is the nearest airport with services, to the west. Expect long visibility on most days, strong prairie winds, and summer thunderstorms.
