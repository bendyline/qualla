# Rohwer, Arkansas

A quiet Desha County community in the Arkansas Delta where, from 1942 to 1945, the federal government held thousands of Japanese Americans, and where a cemetery built by the incarcerated still stands.

**Location:** 33.7608, -91.2756

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in Desha County, Arkansas, Unincorporated communities in Arkansas

## Introduction

Long before George Takei became famous as Sulu on Star Trek, he was a small boy living in a government camp in the flat farm country of southeast Arkansas. The future sculptor Ruth Asawa lived there too. Their families were among thousands of Japanese Americans uprooted from the West Coast during World War II and sent to Rohwer, an unincorporated community in Desha County. The barracks are long gone. What remains is a cemetery, a set of monuments made by the people who were confined here, and a community that still carries the name.

## A Camp in the Delta

The camp was designed by Edward F. Neild, an architect from Shreveport, Louisiana, and it opened on September 18, 1942. The government called it a relocation center, a gentle name for a place where families were held because of their ancestry. They came from the West Coast, and at its peak the camp held 8,475 people, living in tar-papered buildings spread across some 500 acres. It was a large population for a rural Delta community, and not one of those residents had chosen to come. Summers here are hot and humid, and winters run from mild to cool, a climate very different from the coastal places many of them had called home. The camp operated for a little more than three years, closing on November 30, 1945.

## Monuments Built by Their Own Hands

Little of the camp survives above ground except its cemetery and the smokestack of the camp hospital. The cemetery is the heart of what remains, and it is now a National Historic Landmark; the site was added to the National Register of Historic Places on July 30, 1974. The monuments there were made by the incarcerated people themselves during their confinement. One honors Japanese American young men who died fighting for America in World War II. It is hard to stand before that monument without feeling its weight. These soldiers went to war for a nation that was, at the same time, confining Japanese American families in camps like this one. The people who made the tribute knew both halves of that story intimately.

## Children of Rohwer

Among the young people who lived at Rohwer were two who would later become widely known. Ruth Asawa grew up to be a celebrated sculptor. George Takei became an actor, and he has returned to the story of the camp in a way that lets new visitors hear it in a familiar voice. In 2012 a walking tour of the site was developed, with interpretive panels, kiosks and an audio presentation narrated by Takei. The panels and recordings help visitors picture what they cannot see: the barracks and daily routines of a camp that has otherwise vanished. Rohwer was never just a place on a map for the children who lived here. It was where part of their childhood happened, under circumstances they did nothing to deserve.

## The Community That Remains

Today Rohwer is a small unincorporated community. It was once served by the Delta Special School District, which ran Delta Elementary School and Delta High School. In 2004 the Arkansas Legislature passed a law forcing districts with fewer than 350 students to consolidate, and on July 1, 2004, the Delta Special district merged into the McGehee School District. McGehee kept both Delta schools open at first, but by October 2005 the campus served elementary students only, and by October 2006 it had closed. Rohwer has produced notable people of its own, too. Sheilla Lampkin, who went on to serve as an Arkansas state legislator, was born here.

## Flight Context

Rohwer lies at 33.7608°N, 91.2756°W in Desha County, in the flat, intensively farmed Arkansas Delta a few miles west of the Mississippi River. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL, the landscape is a patchwork of large fields, drainage ditches and scattered tree lines; little of the camp survives, and the small cemetery and the hospital smokestack are its most visible remnants. The town of McGehee lies to the southwest. The airport at Greenville, Mississippi (KGLH), is about 22 nm to the southeast across the river. Summer brings heat, humidity and afternoon thunderstorms; winter mornings can bring low fog over the fields.
