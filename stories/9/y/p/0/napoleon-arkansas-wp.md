# Napoleon, Arkansas

Napoleon was a bustling, ill-starred river port at the mouth of the Arkansas River until the Civil War emptied it and a Union shortcut through the Mississippi helped the river swallow it whole.

**Location:** 33.7956, -91.1071

**Categories:** Destroyed populated places, Former populated places in Arkansas, American Civil War sites in Arkansas, Arkansas populated places on the Mississippi River, History of Desha County, Arkansas

## Introduction

In Life on the Mississippi, Mark Twain tells a tale he made up: a deathbed confession, $10,000 hidden behind a brick in a building in Napoleon, Arkansas, and a trip to collect it. The punchline is that there was nothing to collect. When he went looking, the entire town had been washed away. Twain invented the treasure, but not the vanishing. From the 1830s to 1874 Napoleon was a river port, and from 1843 until the early 1870s the seat of Desha County, perched where the Arkansas River meets the Mississippi. Then the rivers that made it took it back, and today only a few ruins remain, visible when the water runs low.

## Grand Plans on a Muddy Bank

Frederick Notrebe founded the town on the west bank at the mouth of the Arkansas and named it in honor of "his old commander." Exactly when is uncertain. A post office opened in 1832 under the name Mouth of Arkansas, and a primary school on December 10, 1838, and in May 1839 Father Peter Donnelly, sent by Bishop Joseph Rosati of St. Louis to establish a Catholic church in Arkansas, bought land and held the first Mass there.

Napoleon sat on one of the most common thoroughfares along the Mississippi, and its leaders dreamed accordingly. They built a large wharf, lobbied for a federal Marine Hospital, and aspired to a railroad linking Napoleon to the Pacific Ocean. Reality was humbler. The town quickly earned a sour reputation for filth, bugs and crime, and Supreme Court Justice Peter Vivian Daniel, visiting in 1851, wrote his daughter of a "miserable place" whose wooden houses were "tumbling down without ever having been finished." Still, between 1850 and 1860 the population nearly doubled.

## The Hospital That Knew Better

Congress authorized seven Marine Hospitals in 1837 and, on August 29, 1842, chose Napoleon as one location. Major Stephen Long of the U.S. Topographical Engineers, commissioned in 1849 to build it, saw the danger at once. "I cannot but regard the site selected for this hospital objectionable," he wrote, urging the high bluffs at Helena instead. He was ignored.

Flooding hampered work on the foundation and cellar, and construction did not truly start until August 1850. By spring 1851, with sickness rampant and contracts expiring, the supervisor suggested suspending the work. It resumed that October and crawled on for three more years; the hospital was finished in August 1854 and took its first patients in 1855.

Long had been right. By March 11, 1868, the river had eroded the land to within 52 feet of the hospital's doors. Less than a month later, nearly four years after Long's death, a corner fell in. On May 19 the Weekly Arkansas Gazette reported that about half the building had "broken off and caved into the river."

## Cannons and Firewood

On February 12, 1861, months before Arkansas seceded that May, Governor Henry Rector ordered the seizure of the Marine Hospital and of ammunition shipments. That April, secessionists set up two cannons at Napoleon to force riverboats to stop for inspection.

By September 1862 the Union army occupied the town and the Union Navy controlled the rivers. The once bustling port was all but deserted. Its economy lay in ruins, slavery was coming to a sudden end, and river traffic moved entirely under Union control. Over the winter of 1863 the town was largely pillaged for firewood, and the Catholic church's pews were ripped apart. The Marine Hospital was the only structure left standing, and Federal forces did not even use it as a hospital, sending patients elsewhere.

## Cutting the Beulah Bend

East of town, the Mississippi swung through the Beulah Bend, a 10-mile arc that began at the mouth of the Arkansas and curved back to within a mile of it. Confederate soldiers learned to walk east from Napoleon, hide in the woods near the bend, and fire on passing Union ships. The loop was so tight that they could shoot at a vessel heading downstream, move their cannons a few hundred yards, and wait for the same ship to come around.

Lieutenant Commander Thomas O. Selfridge, patrolling in the USS Conestoga, grew furious. Noticing that floodwaters had made a shortcut feasible, he set his men to digging a canal only a few hundred yards long, even though a similar scheme, Grant's Canal, had failed miserably at Vicksburg. This time the river did the work. Within a single day the water was a raging torrent, carrying off even large trees. The next morning Selfridge ran the new channel at full speed, estimating the current at 12 knots. Admiral David Porter reported on April 16, 1863, that the cut saved a distance of over 10 miles.

## What the River Kept

The cut helped Union shipping and hastened Napoleon's end. The Mississippi was soon eroding the town's banks. After the war Napoleon was practically a ghost town; a few families returned, but most did not. The Napoleon News came back in 1870 claiming silver had been discovered nearby, but no boom followed. The floods continued, and a flood in 1874 completely submerged what was left of the town.

The whole town was swallowed just twenty-five years after Stephen Long first objected to building there. The Catholic church's bell is now in McGehee. Twain, for his part, wrote that De Soto, Marquette and Joliet, and La Salle had all passed "the site of the future town of Napoleon, Arkansas." The explorers came and went. So, in the end, did the town.

## Flight Context

The site lies at 33.796°N, 91.107°W, at the confluence of the Arkansas and Mississippi rivers on the Arkansas side, opposite Bolivar County, Mississippi. From 3,000-5,000 ft AGL, follow the Arkansas River's final miles to its junction with the much broader Mississippi; the looping oxbow of Lake Beulah, the old Beulah Bend, lies just to the east. There is no town to see, and the few remaining ruins appear only at low water. Nearby fields: McGehee Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 7M1; no ICAO code), about 17 nm southwest; Cleveland Municipal Airport (KRNV), about 17.5 nm east in Mississippi; and Mid Delta Regional Airport (KGLH) at Greenville, about 20 nm south. The river bottomlands are low and featureless, so the two rivers are the best navigation references; expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
