# Louisiana Purchase State Park

In a swamp in eastern Arkansas, a stone standing in a foot of water marks the 1815 initial point from which the lands of the Louisiana Purchase were surveyed.

**Location:** 34.6457, -91.0540

**Categories:** Louisiana Purchase, Historic surveying landmarks in the United States, National Historic Landmarks in Arkansas, Daughters of the American Revolution monuments and memorials, State parks of Arkansas, Wetlands

## Introduction

At the end of a boardwalk in an eastern Arkansas swamp, a stone marker about six feet tall stands in roughly a foot of swamp water. It does not look like the center of anything. Yet surveys measured from this spot eventually reached across most of Arkansas, Missouri, Iowa, Minnesota and the Dakotas, laying out the public lands of the Louisiana Purchase. Somewhere along the way, the place where it all began was simply forgotten, until surveyors found two old blazed trees. Louisiana Purchase State Park now protects the initial point, and a patch of wetland that looks much as it did when the first survey crews arrived in 1815.

## Land for the Soldiers

On April 30, 1803, negotiators for the United States and France signed the Louisiana Purchase agreement, and the young country acquired some 830,000 square miles west of the Mississippi River, doubling its size. Lewis and Clark spent 1804 to 1806 traveling west along the Missouri and associated rivers to the Pacific Coast. Mapping a continent, though, is different from dividing it into parcels someone can own.

That practical work began in 1815, when the federal government ordered a survey of the territory so that land could be awarded in an orderly way to veterans of the War of 1812. Two men, Prospect K. Robbins and Joseph C. Brown, were commissioned to identify a starting point for the survey in what is now eastern Arkansas.

## Where Two Lines Crossed

Robbins led his team north from the mouth of the Arkansas River. Brown's party worked west from the mouth of the St. Francis River. On November 10, 1815, Robbins' crew crossed the east-west line Brown had laid down, and at that intersection the Fifth Principal Meridian was formally established.

Neither route was easy. The 55 miles Robbins covered remain some of the most difficult terrain in the state to cross even today. Brown's 26 miles were described by turns as "good for farming" and as holding "briers and thickets in abundance." From the meeting point, Brown's party eventually surveyed as far west as present-day Little Rock, while Robbins pushed on north to the Missouri River. Before moving on, the surveyors blazed two trees near the site to mark where their lines met.

## The Point Nobody Remembered

Survey work based on this point continued for years, eventually reaching into six states, but the initial point itself slipped out of memory. It was rediscovered in 1921 by surveyors running the line between Phillips and Lee counties, who came upon the blazed trees.

The L'Anguille Chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution, from nearby Marianna, took up the cause of marking the spot. On October 27, 1926, they dedicated a stone about 4.5 feet wide at its base. Its inscription reads: "This stone marks the base established November 10, 1815, from which the lands of the Louisiana Purchase were surveyed by United States engineers. The first survey from this point was made to satisfy the claims of the soldiers of the War of 1812 with land bounties." At the ceremony, four local landowners each gave deeds for 2 acres around the point, enough for an eight-acre park, a detail of the day that was itself later forgotten.

## A Swamp Kept Whole

Arkansas designated the site a state park in 1961 but appropriated no money to buy or develop land. It was the Arkansas Natural Heritage Commission, recognizing the swamp's natural significance, that funded the purchase of the 37.5 acres that make up the park today, and it holds a conservation easement to limit development. The marker was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1972 and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1993.

The park's forested wetland is remarkably unchanged since 1815, and that is part of its value. Wetlands like this one are becoming increasingly rare in the region as land is drained and cleared for farming. A 950-foot boardwalk leads out to the marker, with interpretive signs along the way explaining the swamp's ecology and the landmark's meaning. The park sits at the eastern end of Highway 362, southeast of Blackton, where Lee, Monroe and Phillips counties meet.

## Flight Context

The initial point lies at 34.646°N, 91.054°W, at the junction of Lee, Monroe and Phillips counties southeast of Blackton, Arkansas. From 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, look for a small, dark block of forested swamp standing out amid flat Delta farmland at the eastern end of Highway 362; the 37.5-acre park is tiny, so low altitude and slow speed help. Nearby fields: Holly Grove Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 2A6; no ICAO code), about 7 nm southwest; Marianna/Lee County Airport - Steve Edwards Field (FAA identifier 6M7; no ICAO code), about 14.5 nm northeast; and Thompson-Robbins Airport at West Helena (KHEE), about 19 nm east. Humid summer haze can blur the flat terrain; cooler months usually bring clearer air.
