# Battle of Cotton Plant

On July 7, 1862, a hungry Union army living off the Arkansas countryside fought off Texas cavalry at a plantation crossroads near the Cache River, then marched on to seize Helena.

**Location:** 35.0365, -91.2930

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Arkansas, Battles of the Trans-Mississippi theater of the American Civil War, Union victories of the American Civil War, History of Woodruff County, Arkansas, 1862 in Arkansas

## Introduction

Colonel James R. Taylor's Texas horsemen had swung wide through the little town of Cotton Plant, hoping to cut off a Union advance guard from its army. As they neared the road junction at Parley Hill's plantation, they heard the tap of a drum, and a large body of Union soldiers rose to their feet in front of them. The Texans turned back. That moment, on July 7, 1862, near the Cache River in what is now Woodruff County, Arkansas, summed up a day in which Confederate cavalry repeatedly tried, and failed, to crack a Union advance guard.

## An Army Living off the Land

Four months earlier, General Samuel Ryan Curtis's Army of the Southwest had won at Pea Ridge. Now it was stranded. Ordered to take Little Rock, Curtis learned in May that his troops could no longer be supplied from the railhead at Rolla, Missouri, and abandoned the drive. A relief flotilla steaming up the White River suffered a costly setback at Saint Charles on June 17, when a Confederate shot pierced the steam drum of the USS Mound City, killing most of its crew, and it later stalled at Clarendon in low water. Curtis decided to march to meet it. Cutting his supply line, he left Batesville at the end of June with his men living off the countryside, and they foraged, pillaged and, by one estimate, destroyed some $1.5 million in property along the way. For thousands of enslaved people, the passing army was a chance at freedom. With the soldiers' help, many fled their plantations; by one count more than 3,000 joined the march, while others set out for Missouri.

## Ambush at Hill's Plantation

On the morning of July 7, the army found the Cache River low enough to ford at James Ferry. Colonel Charles E. Hovey, whose brigade included the 33rd Illinois, a regiment that drew students from the Illinois teachers' college he had led, sent Colonel Charles Harris of the 11th Wisconsin ahead with 400 men. Confederate General Albert Rust had gathered 5,000 troops at Cotton Plant, but he was able to bring only two Texas cavalry regiments into action, led by Colonel William H. Parsons. Around 9 a.m. Parsons learned the Federals were crossing and sent 20 skirmishers across a cypress swamp. Harris's Wisconsin companies pushed past the Hill house and a cornfield into the woods, then ran into the main body of the 12th Texas. Harris was wounded but kept directing his men as the Texans charged on horseback, driving them back. Edward M. Pike earned the Medal of Honor for saving the Union cannon from capture.

## A Volley from the Corn

Hovey pulled the retreating troops back toward the crossroads and hid three Illinois companies in the cornfield. When Parsons's horsemen burst from the trees in pursuit, the hidden infantry met them with a shattering volley and heavy fire. Many saddles were emptied, and the Texans fell back behind the swamp. Rust's attempt to flank the Federals with the 17th Texas ended at the sound of that drum. Soon 200 troopers of the 1st Indiana Cavalry arrived with two more cannon, and after an artillery barrage Hovey attacked again. After about twenty minutes of fighting, the Confederates gave way, and when General William P. Benton's brigade joined the pursuit, the retreat toward Des Arc became a rout. That evening the Confederates crossed the lower Cache and destroyed their boats to prevent pursuit. The Federals reported 6 killed and 57 wounded. Confederate losses are uncertain; estimates range from 30 killed and 50 to 60 wounded to as many as 250 casualties.

## The Road to Helena

Curtis reached Clarendon on July 9 to find that the supply flotilla had left the day before. He ordered cannons fired and sent couriers racing downstream, but the boats disappeared downriver. With resupply now his highest priority, Curtis turned his columns east toward the Mississippi. After a punishing march through intense heat and clouds of dust, the Army of the Southwest occupied Helena without opposition on July 12. Soldiers hailed passing transports on the river, and the army was soon fed and supplied again. Curtis set up headquarters in the mansion of Confederate General Thomas Hindman. Union forces would hold Helena for the rest of the war, and on July 4, 1863, its garrison repulsed a Confederate assault on the town.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lies at about 35.04°N, 91.29°W near the town of Cotton Plant in Woodruff County, Arkansas, just east of the Cache River. The Cache's meandering, heavily wooded bottomland, now largely protected in wildlife refuge lands, is the dominant landmark, with the larger White River to the south and west. Today the area is a mix of flat farmland and wooded river bottoms. A pass at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL shows how the river bottoms and swamps channeled the armies onto a few roads. Stuttgart Municipal Airport (KSGT) lies to the south-southwest, and Helena's Thompson-Robbins Airport (KHEE) lies to the southeast near the Mississippi River, at the end of Curtis's march. Summer brings heat, haze and thunderstorms, much as the soldiers endured in 1862.
