# Arkansas Post

Founded in 1686 as a French trading post among the Quapaw, Arkansas Post outlived French, Spanish, territorial and Confederate chapters, though the river swallowed nearly every fort it ever had.

**Location:** 34.0167, -91.3453

**Categories:** Arkansas Post, 1686 establishments in New France, American Revolutionary War sites, American Civil War sites in Arkansas, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Arkansas

## Introduction

On April 17, 1783, Captain James Colbert led British irregulars and Chickasaw warriors against a Spanish fort on the Arkansas River. The defenders held, and a surprise sally by Spanish and Quapaw forces sent the raiders retreating in disarray. It was one of the last battles of the American Revolutionary War and the only one fought in present-day Arkansas. By then the place under attack had already been a trading post for nearly a century. Arkansas Post, now Arkansas Post National Memorial, was the first European settlement in what is now Arkansas, and across nearly two centuries it served French traders, Spanish commandants, an American territorial government and a Confederate garrison, while the river quietly erased their forts one by one.

## A Post Among the Quapaw

In 1686, Henri de Tonti established the post on behalf of Louis XIV of France, near a Quapaw village called Osotouy. Its purpose was trade with the Quapaw Nation, and that relationship shaped everything that followed. The post was the first European settlement in the Mississippi Alluvial Plain, and here the French conducted the first documented Christian services in Arkansas.

France, Spain and later the United States all considered the site strategically valuable. During the fur trade years a series of fortifications protected it, spread across three known sites and possibly a fourth. Erosion and flooding along the waterfront have since claimed some of the historic structures.

## Gifts, Feasts and the Red Bluff

After a Chickasaw raid and continued threats of attack, commander Ensign Louis Xavier Martin de Lino moved the post upriver, farther from Chickasaw territory east of the Mississippi and closer to the Quapaw villages that were its main trading partners and potential allies. The new site, about 45 miles from the mouth of the Arkansas, was called Écores Rouges, or Red Bluff, at "the heights of the Grand Prairie," on a bend in the river and higher ground.

When Spain took over, most of the post's people remained French, which complicated Spanish diplomacy. In 1772, Commander Fernando de Leyba was ordered to assert dominance over the local French and to cut back the feasts and gifts they provided to the Quapaw, which were costing the colonial government too much. The Quapaw nearly came to blows with the Spanish. In the end Leyba conceded to the old practice and restored the goods, and the conflict was avoided.

## Forts the River Kept Taking

The Spanish built Fort Carlos III in July 1781, a cluster of small buildings inside a stockade, and it was this fort that Colbert's raiders struck in 1783. The river proved a more patient enemy. Fort Carlos III suffered constant erosion, so the Spanish moved the garrison about half a mile back from the waterfront and in March 1791 built Fort San Estevan, with a commandant's house, large barracks, a storehouse and a kitchen, all surrounded by a stockade.

When the United States acquired the territory with the Louisiana Purchase in 1803, San Estevan became Fort Madison. In 1805 the government opened a federal trading house at the north end of the post, run by Jacob Bright, and the post became a major stop for travelers heading west. Explorers including Stephen Harriman Long and Thomas Nuttall passed through before the trading house closed in 1810.

## Capital, Then Battlefield

Arkansas Post served as the capital of Arkansas from 1819 until 1821, when the territorial government moved to Little Rock. The territory's first newspaper, the Arkansas Gazette, was founded at the post in 1819 by William E. Woodruff.

During the Civil War, Confederates held the post with a fortification known as Fort Hindman. On January 9 through 11, 1863, Union forces under John A. McClernand and David D. Porter attacked it, and the garrison under Thomas J. Churchill surrendered; 4,791 Confederates were captured. The Union victory relieved much of the Confederate harassment on the Mississippi, though its effect on the Vicksburg campaign itself was limited.

## Reading What Remains

The former site became a state park in 1929, beginning with 20 acres donated by Fred Quandt, a descendant of German immigrants, and grew with Works Progress Administration labor. It was designated a National Memorial on July 6, 1960, and a National Historic Landmark that October.

When Preston Holder led the first excavations in 1956 and 1957, his team found the eighteenth-century French colonial village, with trenches later identified as the traditional French colonial building pattern known as poteaux-en-terre. By then the remains of the 1752 La Houssaye fort, Fort Carlos III, Fort San Estevan and Fort Hindman all lay underwater in the old river channel, and erosion has left no archaeological evidence of them. Later digs turned up the state bank, colonial-style residences and thousands of ceramic shards. In the 1990s, archeologist John Walthall called the archaeological resources the most valuable at the memorial, including nearly unexplored Quapaw settlements.

## Flight Context

Arkansas Post National Memorial lies at 34.017°N, 91.345°W on the lower Arkansas River in Arkansas County. From 2,500-4,000 ft AGL, look for the memorial grounds beside the water-filled former channel of the Arkansas River, surrounded by flat Delta farmland. Nearby fields: Billy Free Municipal Airport at Dumas (FAA identifier 0M0; no ICAO code), about 12 nm southwest; De Witt Municipal Airport / Whitcomb Field (FAA identifier 5M1; no ICAO code), about 15 nm north; and Pine Bluff Regional Airport, Grider Field (KPBF), about 31 nm west-northwest. The river bends are the best visual reference in this flat terrain. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms; fall and winter usually bring clearer air.
