# Mound Bayou, Mississippi

Founded in 1887 by formerly enslaved people from the plantation of Jefferson Davis's brother, Mound Bayou became a self-governing Black town that presidents praised and the civil rights movement relied on.

**Location:** 33.8806, -90.7281

**Categories:** Cities in Bolivar County, Mississippi, Populated places in Mississippi established by African Americans, 1887 establishments in Mississippi, African-American history, Civil rights movement

## Introduction

Mound Bayou's story begins, improbably, on a plantation owned by the older brother of the Confederacy's president. Joseph E. Davis, elder brother of Jefferson Davis, ran Davis Bend in Mississippi, and it was people freed from Davis Bend who would build something new after the Civil War. In 1887, led by Isaiah T. Montgomery, they founded Mound Bayou on the Delta frontier as an independent Black community, governing itself in the heart of Mississippi's cotton country. More than a century later it remains one of the most predominantly African American communities in the United States, about 97 percent Black in 2020.

## Out of Davis Bend

Joseph Davis, influenced by the utopian thinker Robert Owen, set out in the 1820s to build what he imagined as a model slave community. He encouraged self-leadership among the people he held in bondage, provided better nutrition and medical and dental care, and allowed some to become merchants. However benevolent its owner considered it, Davis Bend was still a place where human beings were owned. After the war Davis sold the property to Benjamin Montgomery, a formerly enslaved man who had run a store there and been a leader of the community, and Davis Bend became an autonomous free settlement. It could not last. An agricultural depression, falling cotton prices, Mississippi River floods and white hostility ground it down. In 1887 Benjamin's son Isaiah and Isaiah's cousin, Benjamin Titus Green, started again in the undeveloped Delta bottomlands, where freedmen could clear land and use the profits to buy more. Montgomery became the new town's first mayor.

## An Object Lesson Full of Hope

Mound Bayou quickly became a symbol celebrated by African Americans across the country. The American Missionary Association founded a school there in 1892, and a Baptist industrial college opened in 1900. Early in the twentieth century President Theodore Roosevelt ordered his train to make a special stop, and from the platform he called the town "an object lesson full of hope for the colored people and therefore full of hope for the white people, too." In 1912 Booker T. Washington told a crowd of thousands that it was a place where a Black man could find inspiration in what his people had accomplished. Hard times followed. Burdened by debt and farming troubles, most of the Black farmers who in 1900 made up two-thirds of the bottomland landowners had lost their land and become sharecroppers by 1920, and falling cotton prices brought severe decline through the 1920s and 1930s. Mayor Benjamin A. Green, a Harvard Law School graduate and the first person born in the town, guided it from 1919 until his death in 1960.

## A Hospital and a Movement

Revival came in 1942, shortly after a fire destroyed much of the business district, when a Black fraternal order, the International Order of Twelve Knights and Daughters of Tabor, opened the Taborian Hospital. For more than two decades it gave low-cost care to thousands of Black patients across the Delta. Its chief surgeon, Dr. T. R. M. Howard, became one of the wealthiest Black men in Mississippi and built the state's first swimming pool for Black residents. In 1952 a young Medgar Evers moved to Mound Bayou to sell insurance for Howard's company, and Howard drew him into activism through the Regional Council of Negro Leadership, which boycotted service stations that refused Black customers the use of their restrooms. The council's annual rallies here drew ten thousand people or more. During the 1955 trial of Emmett Till's killers, Black reporters and witnesses stayed in Howard's home, and he sent them to the courthouse in Sumner under armed escort.

## An Oasis That Endures

One writer, Michael Premo, called Mound Bayou "an oasis in turbulent times." While segregation ruled the rest of Mississippi and Black citizens elsewhere risked death to register, residents here voted in every election and supported their own credit unions, insurance companies, hospital and five newspapers. The town's pioneering spirit carried into the 1960s: Delta Health Center, founded in Mound Bayou in 1967, was the first rural community health center in the United States. Ed Townsend, co-writer of the Marvin Gaye hit "Let's Get It On," later mentored young musicians here, and the town is featured in the 2022 film Till and mentioned in the 2025 film Sinners. Today about 1,500 people live in Mound Bayou, and its historic district is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Flight Context

Mound Bayou lies at about 33.88°N, 90.73°W in Bolivar County, in the flat cotton country of the Mississippi Delta. U.S. Routes 61 and 278 bypass the small town on its west side, running 9 miles south to Cleveland and 27 miles north to Clarksdale, and make an easy line to follow. The town covers less than a square mile, so look for its compact grid set among wide, geometric fields. Flying at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL over this featureless terrain, the highway is your best reference. Cleveland Municipal Airport (KRNV) is just to the south, and Fletcher Field (KCKM) at Clarksdale lies to the north. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common; fall and winter usually bring the clearest views.
