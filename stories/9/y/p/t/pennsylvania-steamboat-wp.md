# Pennsylvania (steamboat)

In June 1858 the sidewheeler Pennsylvania blew up on the Mississippi below Memphis, killing by most modern counts between 60 and 160 people, among them Henry Clemens, the younger brother Samuel Clemens had helped find a place aboard.

**Location:** 34.6594, -90.4807

**Categories:** Steamboats of the Mississippi River, Paddle steamers, Maritime boiler explosions, Shipwrecks of the Mississippi River, Mark Twain, 1854 ships, Maritime incidents in June 1858, Disasters on the Mississippi River

## Introduction

Samuel Clemens did his younger brother a favor. Just before he left the steamboat Pennsylvania in June 1858, the young cub pilot arranged a job aboard for Henry Clemens as a "mud clerk." Days later, on June 13, the Pennsylvania's boiler exploded on the Mississippi River near Ship Island, below Memphis, Tennessee. Henry was aboard. Sam, who would one day write as Mark Twain, was not, and he could only race upriver to find out whether his brother had survived.

## A Working Sidewheeler

The Pennsylvania was a side-wheel steamboat built in 1854, big enough to carry a full 486 tons of cargo. She had already survived one accident before the one that destroyed her. On November 26, 1857, while 28 miles north of New Orleans, Louisiana, she was struck by the steamer Vicksburg. The collision put her out of service until February 17, 1858. Once repaired, she went back to work on the river, carrying passengers and freight between the cities strung along the Mississippi. For a few months more she was just one boat among many on the river, until June 13, 1858.

## The Cub Pilot and the Pilot

The Pennsylvania's most famous crew member was a young man learning the river. Samuel L. Clemens served aboard as a cub pilot from September 27, 1857, until June 5, 1858, with a two-month break while the boat was repaired after the Vicksburg collision. His time aboard ended badly. Clemens had a stormy relationship with the boat's pilot, William Brown, and he later described the clash in his 1883 book Life on the Mississippi. Before he departed, Clemens arranged a post for Henry as the boat's "mud clerk," a junior clerk's position. It was a generous gesture from an older brother, and it put Henry aboard the Pennsylvania when Sam himself was no longer there.

## Ship Island

On June 13, 1858, the Pennsylvania was steaming near Ship Island, about sixty miles below Memphis, when her boiler exploded. Estimates at the time put the number of people aboard at 450, and the initial loss of life at 250; later counts were lower, ranging from about 60 to 160. Behind those numbers were passengers and crew whose journey ended in scalding steam and a sinking hull. The Imperial, one of the steamers that came to the rescue, picked up several survivors. The Diana took many others to Memphis, several of them seriously injured, and the death toll kept climbing. Among the dead was Tennessee Supreme Court justice William R. Harris. Eyewitnesses testified that just before the explosion the engineer was not at his post in the engine room but in the company of some women. Jeremiah A. Brown, who would later serve in the Ohio legislature, is said to have been aboard as a crew member and to have survived.

## At the Bedside in Memphis

Sam Clemens was already heading upriver aboard the steamer A. T. Lacey, which had left New Orleans on June 11, when news of the explosion reached him. He reached Memphis on June 15, in time to find his brother alive. Henry lay among the wounded, his skin and lungs badly scalded by the steam. A Memphis newspaper, the Eagle and Enquirer, reported that when Sam approached his brother's bedside, he was so overcome by the sight of him that he sank to the floor. Henry held on for more than a week after the explosion, then succumbed to his injuries on June 21. He was one of the victims whose deaths came slowly, in the days after the wreck, and his story survives in large part through his brother's pages in Life on the Mississippi.

## Flight Context

The coordinates given for the wreck, 34.6594°N, 90.4807°W, fall on the Mississippi River along the Arkansas–Mississippi border, northeast of Helena–West Helena, Arkansas. Nothing of the boat is visible; the Mississippi has changed course in many places since 1858, so treat the position as approximate. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL, follow the broad river's bends and sandbars with the Arkansas Delta to the west and the Mississippi Delta to the east; the Helena Bridge crosses the river to the southwest. Thompson–Robbins Airport (KHEE), northwest of Helena–West Helena, is about 11 nm to the west-southwest. Expect river haze and summer thunderstorms.
