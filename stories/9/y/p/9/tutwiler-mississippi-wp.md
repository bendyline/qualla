# Tutwiler, Mississippi

On a Tutwiler train platform around 1903, W. C. Handy heard a guitarist play slide with a knife and sing about the Yellow Dog, giving this small Delta railroad town its claim on the birth of the blues.

**Location:** 34.0147, -90.4317

**Categories:** Towns in Mississippi, Towns in Tallahatchie County, Mississippi, Blues, Mississippi Blues Trail, Mississippi Delta

## Introduction

W. C. Handy had fallen asleep on a train platform in Tutwiler, Mississippi, waiting for an overdue train to Memphis, when a man beside him began to play guitar, pressing a knife against the strings "in a manner popularized by Hawaiian guitarists who used steel bars," and singing the same line three times over: "goin' where the Southern cross the Dog." Handy called it "the weirdest music I had ever heard." The year was around 1903. That moment on the platform gives this small Delta town its claim, one shared by many of its neighbors, to being the birthplace of the blues.

## A Schoolroom Above the Depot

Tutwiler began with the railroad. In 1899 Tom Tutwiler, a civil engineer for a local line, set up his headquarters seven miles northwest of Sumner, and the new town took his name. The first depot was a two-story building, and the railroad let the town use its top floor as a public school. Captain H. B. Fitch built and ran the first store, and his wife took charge of the school, which opened with five pupils.

The Illinois Central, running from Yazoo City to Lambert, crossed at Tutwiler and built a railroad yard there. The town was incorporated in 1905 with W. E. Fite as mayor, and in 1928 it built a high school for $40,000. Tutwiler grew rapidly until 1929, when the railroad yard moved to Clarksdale. The population then stood at 1,010. After the yard left, businesses and finally residents began to leave too.

## Where the Southern Cross the Dog

Handy is often said to have "discovered" the blues that day, and the quotation marks matter. He had heard something akin to the blues as early as 1892, and the music on the platform belonged to the itinerant bluesman playing it. Legend says he was a local field hand named Henry Sloan.

The lyric was a piece of Delta geography. "Where the Southern cross the Dog" meant the junction, farther south, of the Southern Railway and the Yazoo and Mississippi Valley Railroad, which locals called the "Yellow Dog." On November 25, 2009, a Mississippi Blues Trail marker honoring Handy was placed at the site where he heard it.

## A Rock Singer Pays His Respects

The marker was paid for by Robert Plant, the English singer of Led Zeppelin, who came to the dedication and spoke about the bluesman who had influenced him: Sonny Boy Williamson II. "The first record my mom bought me was by Sonny Boy," Plant said. "I played it until there were no more grooves on the record."

Tutwiler has its own claim on that influence. Sonny Boy Williamson II, the blues singer and harmonica player, lies buried just outside town, though where he was born is disputed. Some sources place John Lee Hooker's birth in or near Tutwiler, while others say near Clarksdale, and Frank Stokes is also counted among the town's bluesmen. For a community that has never been large, it has sent an outsized share of sound into the world.

## Cotton, Then a Prison

"Except for cotton, there has never been much to Tutwiler's economy," wrote Peter T. Kilborn of The New York Times in 2001. Because the economy was so poor, around 1998 the town's leaders agreed to a prison nearby that promised hundreds of jobs. Tutwiler built a sewage lagoon and a water tower to serve it, and the state and county paid half the cost of training its correctional officers. When the $35 million facility opened in 2000 with 351 prisoners, 322 of them from Wisconsin and far from home, Kilborn wrote that it "seemed the salvation of" the town. Its workforce was later cut to 40. By 2010 it was also holding people sentenced in California.

Other changes came from within. In 1993 Robert Grayson, a former corrections officer at the Mississippi State Penitentiary at Parchman, was elected Tutwiler's first African-American mayor. Catholic sisters started community services in town, mainly at the Tutwiler Community Education Center, whose current building was completed in 1992, and in November 2010 the sisters opened a gymnasium, funded by donors from outside the area, that Kilborn called "worthy of a university."

## Flight Context

Tutwiler lies at 34.015°N, 90.432°W in the flat Mississippi Delta of Tallahatchie County, about seven miles northwest of Sumner and roughly 70 miles south of Memphis. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL look for the small town grid along the rail line, with the Tallahatchie River winding to the east and open farmland on all sides. Nearby fields: Selfs Airport at Marks (KMMS), about 15 nm northeast; Ruleville-Drew Airport (FAA identifier M37; no ICAO code), about 15 nm south; and Fletcher Field at Clarksdale (KCKM), about 18 nm north. The Delta is nearly featureless, so highways and rail lines make the best references. Expect summer haze and pop-up thunderstorms.
