# Mississippi State Penitentiary

Parchman Farm, a state-run prison plantation in the Mississippi Delta, became a byword for brutality, a crucible of the blues, a jail for Freedom Riders and the target of a landmark federal ruling.

**Location:** 33.9317, -90.5508

**Categories:** Prisons in Mississippi, Mississippi Blues Trail, Agricultural labor in the United States, Buildings and structures in Sunflower County, Mississippi, Capital punishment in Mississippi

## Introduction

When folk song collectors John and Alan Lomax stopped at Parchman Farm in 1933, on a recording trip across the South, they set up their equipment and recorded the prisoners' songs. "Here were the people that everyone else regarded as the dregs of society, dangerous human beings, brutalized," Alan Lomax later wrote, "and from them came the music which I thought was the finest thing I'd ever hear coming out of my country." The singers were prisoners of the Mississippi State Penitentiary, a vast cotton plantation in the Delta worked by convicts, most of them Black. Their songs became one of Parchman's most enduring legacies.

## A Plantation by Another Name

Mississippi established Parchman in 1901 on thousands of acres of fertile Delta soil, a state-run plantation meant as an alternative to the disorganized system of leasing out convicts. Inmates raised cotton, soybeans, livestock, poultry and milk, worked in the fields from mid-February to mid-November, and also built railroads and tapped pine trees for turpentine gum. In the 1930s the prison's brickyard, factories, gin and machine shop turned a profit for the state, while women prisoners sewed bedding and uniforms or chopped cotton. Most men wore "ring arounds," horizontally striped shirts and trousers. Order was kept largely by other prisoners. Armed trusties, known as trusty shooters and marked by vertical stripes, guarded the fields and could earn a pardon for shooting an escaping inmate. As late as 1971 the prison employed fewer than 75 free staff. Historian David Oshinsky called it "the closest thing to slavery that survived the Civil War," and a former superintendent put it more simply: "A life sentence in Parchman is an eternity."

## Songs Against the Hoe

Many African American prisoners sang work chants in a tradition traced back to West Africa, a leader calling out lines while the others answered as they worked. The verses carried stories: a man swimming the Sunflower River to throw off the bloodhounds, prisoners handing their hoes back to the captain and refusing to work on, a beautiful woman named Rosie waiting just beyond the prison boundary. The Lomaxes returned again and again, recording work songs, field hollers and blues for the Library of Congress, and a song Alan Lomax recorded here in 1959 later appeared on the soundtrack of the film O Brother, Where Art Thou? The blues musician Bukka White was among Parchman's inmates. Survival demanded wit as much as strength. In folklorist William Ferris's account of prisoner Johnny Lee Thomas, nicknamed "Have Mercy" after he protested the beating of a fellow inmate, Parchman was "a world of fear in which only the strong and intelligent survive." In 2010 the Mississippi Blues Trail honored Parchman with its 113th marker, and in 2023 and 2024 producer Ian Brennan recorded prisoners singing in the prison chapel, releasing the albums under the name Parchman Prison Prayer.

## Freedom Riders Behind the Wire

In the summer of 1961, the state sent Freedom Riders arrested in Jackson for challenging segregated bus travel to Parchman's new Maximum Security Unit, where officials aimed to make them as uncomfortable as possible. Among the Freedom Riders held there were John Lewis, Stokely Carmichael and James Farmer. They kept singing freedom songs to their jailers. A decade later, the prison itself was put on trial. In a class action brought by prisoner Nazareth Gates and others, federal judge William Keady ruled in 1972 that conditions at Parchman violated the Constitution. The decision in Gates v. Collier ended the trusty-shooter system, racially segregated housing and work assignments, and abusive corporal punishment, and placed the prison under federal supervision.

## Parchman in the American Imagination

Some of Parchman's practices surprised outsiders. Mississippi was the first state to allow conjugal visits, definitely by 1918 and probably from the prison's earliest days, and at first only Black men were permitted them, a policy rooted in racist assumptions and in officials' hope for more productive field labor. White prisoners were allowed visits by the 1930s, and by 1962 most camps had a "red house" near the main gate where men could spend time with their wives. Writers have returned to the prison for generations. William Faulkner sent his convicts from Parchman into the 1927 flood and had a character call it "destination doom." August Wilson's characters in The Piano Lesson served time there, John Grisham set The Chamber on its death row, and the film version was shot on site. In Jesmyn Ward's Sing, Unburied, Sing, a boy who died at Parchman returns as a ghost. In 2008 the prison held 4,181 people, and more than 3,000 of them were Black men.

## Flight Context

Mississippi State Penitentiary lies at about 33.93°N, 90.55°W in Sunflower County, in the flat heart of the Mississippi Delta. From altitude, look for an enormous expanse of cultivated fields broken by scattered clusters of low institutional buildings, the separate camps and units spread across roughly 18,000 acres. This is an active prison, so keep a respectful altitude of at least 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL and avoid circling. Fletcher Field (KCKM) at Clarksdale lies to the north, and Cleveland Municipal Airport (KRNV) is to the southwest. The Delta's flat terrain offers long visibility, though summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
