# Sumner, Mississippi

The small Delta county seat of Sumner held the 1955 trial of Emmett Till's killers in its courthouse, and decades later turned that same building into a place of apology and remembrance.

**Location:** 33.9700, -90.3697

**Categories:** Towns in Mississippi, County seats in Mississippi, Civil rights history, Emmett Till, Mississippi Delta

## Introduction

In September 1955, inside the Tallahatchie County Courthouse in Sumner, Mississippi, Mose Wright pointed at J. W. Milam and said, "There he is." Wright was the great-uncle of Emmett Till, a 14-year-old boy from Chicago who had been staying at his home that summer, and Milam was one of two white men on trial for the boy's murder. It may have been the first time in the South that a Black man testified in court to the guilt of a white man and lived. It did not change the verdict. The courthouse still stands, and today it tells that story on purpose.

## Cleared From the Bottomland

The heavily wooded swamp along the Tallahatchie River was once part of the Choctaw Nation, one of the nations historically labeled the "Five Civilized Tribes" of the Southeast. After ceding large amounts of territory, the Choctaw were forced to remove to Indian Territory in the 1830s under the Indian Removal Act, and the U.S. government sold the land to European-American families. The Sumner family was among the early settlers. Pioneers cleared bottomland thick with trees, vines and underbrush, and the wealthiest men built their holdings on the labor of enslaved African Americans, whose work on cotton plantations produced the crop that anchored the economy for decades.

## A Courthouse on a Donated Lot

Sumner itself was not incorporated until 1900, named for its founder and first mayor, Joseph Burton Sumner. Two years later the county was split into two districts on either side of the Tallahatchie River, which was a barrier to travel across the county. Charleston, east of the river where settlement began, remained a seat, and Sumner became the seat of the western district.

The Tallahatchie County Courthouse went up in 1902 on a lot donated by Joseph B. Sumner, who also gave the lot for the jail. It burned in 1908 and was rebuilt in 1909. Around it grew a market town for the western district, with cotton remaining the area's major commodity crop well into the twentieth century. In 1955 the courthouse became the setting for a trial that would stir outrage across the nation.

## Sixty-Seven Minutes

Emmett Till was lynched and murdered that year in neighboring Leflore County. Because his beaten and mutilated body was found on the bank of the river in Tallahatchie County, the trial of J. W. Milam and Roy Bryant was held in Sumner. It lasted five days. The jury was all white and all male; both women and Black citizens had been barred from serving. After deliberating for 67 minutes, it acquitted both men. One juror later remarked, "We wouldn't have taken so long if we hadn't stopped to drink pop."

In 1956, Milam and Bryant admitted in an interview with Look magazine that they had tortured and murdered Till, taking payment for their story. The brutality of the killing and the acquittal generated outrage and activism across the nation. In Chicago, Till's mother, Mamie Till, insisted on an open-casket funeral, and tens of thousands of people lined the street outside the mortuary to view his body. He became an icon of the Civil Rights Movement and a symbol of the violence Black Southerners endured.

## An Apology, Decades Late

In 1990 the Mississippi Department of Archives and History designated the courthouse a state landmark. In 2007 the Tallahatchie County Board of Supervisors appointed the Emmett Till Memorial Commission to explore commemoration and reconciliation, and the commission made a formal apology to the Till family for their son's lynching. It also set out to restore the courthouse and adapt it as a place to remember him.

The Emmett Till Interpretive Center opened in Sumner in 2012 and has sponsored art and history workshops and events. In 2023 the courthouse was designated part of the Emmett Till and Mamie Till-Mobley National Monument, carrying the mother's name alongside her son's in the same building where his killers walked free.

## Leaving the Delta

Sumner's population peaked in 1940. After that, many African-American residents left the town and county, some for California and the West Coast during and after World War II, drawn by defense-industry jobs and seeking to escape southern oppression and violence. Mechanized farming had also made much farm labor unneeded. They were part of the Great Migration, in which some 5 million Black Southerners left the Deep South between 1940 and 1970. By 2000, Sumner counted 407 residents.

Two very different artists also came from here. The blues singer Red Nelson was born here in 1907, and the photographer William Eggleston, renowned worldwide for his color work, grew up on a plantation in Sumner.

## Flight Context

Sumner sits at 33.97°N, 90.37°W on the flat Mississippi Delta, on the west side of the Tallahatchie River in Tallahatchie County; the other county seat, Charleston, lies east of the river. From 2,500-3,500 ft AGL the town is a small grid of streets amid broad Delta farmland; the winding, tree-lined Tallahatchie River to the east is the key landmark. Nearby fields: Ruleville-Drew Airport (FAA identifier M37; no ICAO code), about 14 nm southwest; Selfs Airport at Marks (KMMS), about 16 nm north; Fletcher Field at Clarksdale (KCKM), about 21 nm north-northwest; and Greenwood-Leflore Airport (KGWO), about 32 nm south-southeast.
