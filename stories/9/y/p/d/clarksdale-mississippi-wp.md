# Clarksdale, Mississippi

A Delta cotton town where the blues took shape, sharecroppers boarded trains for Chicago, civil rights organizers stood their ground, and a crossroads legend still draws pilgrims.

**Location:** 34.2006, -90.5702

**Categories:** Cities in Coahoma County, Mississippi, County seats in Mississippi, Mississippi Blues Trail, Blues, Civil rights movement

## Introduction

When the 2025 film Sinners opened, a story set in and around Clarksdale in 1932, the people of Clarksdale had nowhere to watch it. Every cinema in town had closed. Residents launched a petition, and Warner Bros. answered by turning the Clarksdale Civic Auditorium into a temporary cinema for a series of screenings in late May, with director Ryan Coogler in attendance on May 29. It was a fitting gesture for a Mississippi Delta town whose stories the rest of the world has long enjoyed. Many African American musicians developed the blues here and carried it north, and Clarksdale has spent decades learning to hold on to that inheritance.

## The Golden Buckle on the Cotton Belt

Clarksdale grew where two old Native American trade routes crossed, the Lower Creek Trade Path and the Chakchiuma Trade Trail. John Clark bought land here in 1848 and started a timber business, and the settlement became a trading center. Clark married the sister of James Lusk Alcorn, a planter who held 77 people in slavery in 1860 and who went on to serve as governor of Mississippi and as a U.S. senator. Cotton made the town rich enough to earn a nickname, "The Golden Buckle on the Cotton Belt." Emancipation brought Black families some independence as sharecroppers and tenant farmers, but white planters controlled the credit, the supplies and the final settlement of each harvest. White paramilitary groups, including the Red Shirts of the 1870s, worked to suppress Black voting, and by 1875 conservative white Democrats had retaken the legislature. Bill Peace, a freedman who had served in the Union Army, had persuaded his former owner to let him form a security force to guard the plantation against theft. In October 1875 rumors spread among whites that "General Peace" was preparing his men to plunder the town, and a white militia formed to suppress his so-called revolt.

## The Train to Chicago

Before 1920, Delta plantations constantly needed hands, and Black families poured in to sharecrop. Lebanese, Italian, Chinese and Jewish merchants opened shops, and the Jewish community founded Beth Israel Synagogue in the 1930s. Then cotton prices fell, and people began to leave. The Illinois Central depot offered a direct route to Chicago and became the primary departure point for many. Musicians went too, carrying the blues north with them. McKinley Morganfield, known as Muddy Waters, is believed to have lived in a cabin on the Stovall plantation from 1915 until 1943, when he left for Chicago after mistreatment by an overseer. The roll of musicians born in or near Clarksdale reads like a blues and soul hall of fame: Sam Cooke, Son House, Ike Turner, Junior Parker and, by some accounts, John Lee Hooker among them. W. C. Handy lived here for six years, Robert Johnson in the 1930s. At the old intersection of Highways 61 and 49, three giant guitars now mark the spot that legend names as the crossroads where Johnson sold his soul to the devil.

## Standing Up in the Delta

Clarksdale's freedom struggle was hard-won. On September 10, 1919, during the Red Summer of racial violence, Black veteran L. B. Reed was lynched here. In August 1951 two Black women, Leola Tates and Erline Mills, were raped at gunpoint. The two white teenagers they identified admitted the encounter but claimed it was consensual, and despite overwhelming evidence, a justice of the peace court judge set them free. That injustice marked the starting point of the local civil rights movement. Martin Luther King Jr. came to Clarksdale on May 29, 1958, for a meeting of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference. In 1960 local pharmacist Aaron Henry became state president of the NAACP and led a two-year boycott of Clarksdale businesses, and in 1962 King returned to urge a crowd of 1,000 to "stand in, sit in, and walk by the thousands." In the 1960s Clarksdale became the first district in Mississippi to win accreditation for both its Black and white schools, beginning desegregation.

## Keeping the Blues Alive

Preserving the music took stubbornness. In late 1979 Sid Graves, director of the Carnegie Public Library, began a small blues display, and when the library board denied him space he stored exhibits in the trunk of his car. National attention arrived after Billy Gibbons of ZZ Top discovered the fledgling museum. Today the Delta Blues Museum occupies the restored Illinois Central freight depot, and in 1995 a guitar dealer named Skip Henderson bought the Illinois Central passenger depot to save it from demolition. Blues Trail markers now stand at the Riverside Hotel, which lodged traveling musicians and is where Bessie Smith died after a car crash on Highway 61, and in front of the New Roxy Theater in honor of Sam Cooke. The Sunflower River Blues & Gospel Festival and the Juke Joint Festival draw visitors to town, Jimmy Page and Robert Plant titled a 1998 album Walking Into Clarksdale, and in 2025 CNN named it one of America's best towns to visit.

## Flight Context

Clarksdale sits at about 34.20°N, 90.57°W on the banks of the Sunflower River in Coahoma County, in the flat, intensively farmed Mississippi Delta. U.S. Routes 49, 61 and 278 converge on the city, making it an easy target to identify from the air, and the Mississippi River lies to the west, forming the county's western boundary. A pass at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL shows the town's grid, the rail corridor of the old Illinois Central and the patchwork of cotton and soybean fields beyond. Fletcher Field (KCKM) lies about 7 nautical miles north-northeast of downtown, and Cleveland Municipal Airport (KRNV) is to the south. Expect long visibility over flat terrain, with summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms in the warm months.
