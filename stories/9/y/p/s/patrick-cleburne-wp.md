# Patrick Cleburne

An Irish immigrant who became a Helena pharmacist and lawyer, Patrick Cleburne rose to Confederate major general, proposed arming enslaved men in exchange for freedom, and came home to Helena in a coffin in 1870.

**Location:** 34.5417, -90.5928

**Categories:** Confederate States Army major generals, Irish emigrants to the United States, Military personnel from County Cork, People from Helena, Arkansas, American Civil War

## Introduction

Before he ever saw Arkansas, Patrick Ronayne Cleburne was a British soldier at Fort Westmorland, on Spike Island in Cork Harbour, a great fortress then serving as a convict depot. The Great Irish Famine was filling its cells, and the wretched state of the prisoners he saw there strengthened his resolve to leave Ireland. He left, and he ended as a Confederate major general, buried on ground overlooking the Mississippi River at Helena, Arkansas. The author Shelby Foote called him "probably the best division commander on either side." He is also remembered for a proposal that his own army could not bring itself to discuss.

## A Druggist's Counter in Helena

Cleburne was born on March 16, 1828, in Ovens, County Cork. Three years after joining the British Army's 41st Regiment of Foot, he bought his discharge and emigrated to the United States with two brothers and a sister. After a short stay in Ohio he settled in Helena, a Mississippi River town on the Arkansas side, where he worked as a pharmacist and was readily accepted into local society. By 1860 he was a naturalized citizen, a practicing lawyer and very popular among his neighbors.

When the secession crisis came, Cleburne sided with the Southern states. He claimed not to care about slavery and said he chose out of affection for the Southern people who had adopted him as one of their own. He joined Helena's militia company, the Yell Rifles, as a private and was soon elected captain.

## Stonewall of the West

He rose quickly. After the Army of Tennessee fell back to its namesake state in late 1862, Cleburne took command of a division and led it at Stones River, where it advanced three miles, routing the Union right wing and driving it back to the Nashville Pike. He was promoted to major general that December.

In 1863 his men fought at Chickamauga. At Missionary Ridge they held the northern end against a much larger Union force under William T. Sherman, and at Ringgold Gap in northern Georgia they stopped Joseph Hooker, protecting the army's retreat to Tunnel Hill. The Confederate Congress formally thanked Cleburne and his troops. Robert E. Lee is quoted as calling him "a meteor shining from a clouded sky," and in his day he was hailed, as Foote put it, as "the Stonewall Jackson of the West," well known and adored by his men.

## The Proposal No One Would Discuss

By late 1863 Cleburne had concluded that the Confederacy was losing because its manpower and resources were running out. In 1864 he gathered the leadership of the Army of Tennessee and proposed "emancipating the whole race upon reasonable terms," in order to "enlist their sympathies" and then enlist Black men in the Confederate Army to win Southern independence.

It was freedom offered as a war measure, and a limited one. Cleburne argued that emancipation need not bring Black equality, predicting that "necessity and wise legislation" would keep relations between Black and white Southerners from materially changing. Even so, the proposal met polite silence. General William H. T. Walker, who strongly supported slavery and saw Cleburne as a rival for promotion, attacked it as an "abolitionist conspiracy" and worked to convince army commander Braxton Bragg that Cleburne was politically unreliable. Cleburne remained a division commander until his death.

## Sword Raised at Franklin

Before the 1864 campaigns began, Cleburne became engaged to Susan Tarleton of Mobile, Alabama. They never married. On November 30, 1864, at Franklin, just south of Nashville, he was killed in an ill-conceived assault on Union fortifications, an attack he had opposed. After his horse was shot from under him, he was last seen advancing on foot toward the Union line with his sword raised. Accounts say he was found just inside that line, and his body was carried back to a field hospital along the Columbia Turnpike.

His former corps commander, William J. Hardee, offered an epitaph: "Where this division defended, no odds broke its line; where it attacked, no numbers resisted its onslaught, save only once; and there is the grave of Cleburne."

## The Long Way Back to Helena

Cleburne was first buried at Rose Hill Cemetery in Columbia, Tennessee. At the urging of the army chaplain Bishop Quintard and of Judge Mangum, his staff officer and his law partner back in Helena, his remains were moved to St. John's Episcopal Church near Mount Pleasant. Cleburne had seen the church on the army's march into Tennessee and said he would like to be buried there, for its beauty and its resemblance to his Irish homeland. He rested there for six years.

In 1870 he was brought home to Helena with much fanfare and buried in the Confederate section of Maple Hill Cemetery, overlooking the Mississippi. Foote, who admired him, said he had been "largely forgotten." In Franklin the ground where he fell is now Cleburne Park, though its small monument actually marks where the Carter family's cotton gin once stood.

## Flight Context

Cleburne's grave in Helena lies near 34.542°N, 90.593°W, where Crowley's Ridge meets the Mississippi River on the Arkansas side. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL, look for the wooded rise of the ridge ending at the river's edge, with Helena's downtown and riverfront below it. Thompson-Robbins Airport at West Helena (KHEE) is about 4.5 nm west-northwest. Fletcher Field at Clarksdale (KCKM) is about 15 nm south-southeast and Tunica Municipal Airport (KUTA) about 15 nm northeast, both in Mississippi. Crowley's Ridge stands out clearly from the flat Delta around it, making it an easy reference; watch for summer haze and river fog on cool mornings.
