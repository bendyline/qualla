# Helena, Arkansas

A Mississippi River town that survived earthquakes, cave-ins, floods and a Civil War battle, and gave the blues a radio home with King Biscuit Time.

**Location:** 34.5292, -90.5861

**Categories:** Arkansas populated places on the Mississippi River, Populated places in Phillips County, Arkansas, American Civil War sites in Arkansas, Blues, 1833 establishments in Arkansas Territory

## Introduction

In November 1941 Helena's brand-new radio station, KFFA, offered a group of blues musicians airtime on one condition: they had to find a sponsor. A flour brand agreed, and on November 21, 1941, harmonica player Sonny Boy Williamson II and guitarist Robert Lockwood Jr. went on the air as the King Biscuit Entertainers. King Biscuit Time is still broadcasting, the longest-running daily radio program in the United States. It began in a river town on the west bank of the Mississippi that had already weathered earthquakes, collapsing streets, floods and a Civil War battle, and that blues musicians had been visiting for years.

## A Sink of Crime and Infamy

Europeans came to this stretch of the river long before the town existed. In the late 1730s Jean-Baptiste Le Moyne de Bienville, governor of French Louisiana, built Fort St. Francis here as a supply depot for a campaign against the Chickasaws, though it soon proved vulnerable and was abandoned. A Spanish-era settlement called Big Prairie was devastated by the New Madrid earthquakes of 1811 and 1812. Sylvanus Phillips, whose name the county carries, was in the area as early as 1797, and the town founded here in 1833 took the name Helena; local legend says it honors his daughter, who had died. It did not start respectably. In 1834 the British geologist George William Featherstonhaugh was warned by people in Tennessee to avoid Helena, which they called "a sink of crime and infamy." Four years later the steamboat General Brown exploded at the Helena wharf when all three of its boilers burst, tearing much of its upper structure to splinters and killing or injuring many aboard.

## At the River's Mercy

The Mississippi that built Helena also tried to take it back. On September 26, 1866, erosion caused part of the town's eastern end to collapse into the river, carrying away several buildings at the corner of Main Street, now Cherry Street, and Elm Street, an event remembered as the Cave-in of '66. In the spring of 1897 a major flood pushed city leaders to sacrifice the north end of town, cutting the levee there to relieve pressure on the lower levees protecting the center. Even so, local papers reported at least three feet of water on Cherry Street. Helena's dreams of a rail link west proved nearly as frustrating. Citizens incorporated the Arkansas Midland Railroad in 1853 to run about 115 miles to Little Rock, but when track was finally completed in 1872 it reached only Clarendon, about 48 miles away.

## Fort Curtis and the Fourth of July

Union troops held Helena for most of the Civil War, and in August 1862 soldiers and freed people built Fort Curtis, which served as a Union command post on the river. On July 4, 1863, Confederate General Theophilus Holmes attacked the town, hoping to relieve pressure on besieged Vicksburg. In fact, Vicksburg's defenders had already arranged to surrender to Ulysses S. Grant that very morning. His main assault broke through Battery C, but Union troops sealed the breach, and a final charge toward Fort Curtis was stopped by heavy artillery. By day's end the Confederates had withdrawn, with 1,636 casualties to the Union's 206. Many of the battle sites survive, and a reproduction of Fort Curtis, built in 2012, is free to visit. The town's name even traveled west. When a group of men met on October 30, 1864, to name the town now called Helena, Montana, former Confederates in the room raised an uproar, insisting it be pronounced the way it is in Arkansas.

## Blues Capital of the Delta

Music writer Robert Palmer called mid-1930s Helena "the blues capital of the Delta." Robert Johnson, Howlin' Wolf, Elmore James, Robert Nighthawk, Johnny Shines, Honeyboy Edwards and Sonny Boy Williamson II all played here regularly. In the 1940s and 1950s, Black workers from rural Arkansas and the Delta came for jobs, and the town's juke joints kept pianists such as Sunnyland Slim, Memphis Slim and Roosevelt Sykes busy. Levon Helm of The Band and country star Conway Twitty also have ties to Helena. Every October the King Biscuit Blues Festival fills Cherry Street downtown, one of the largest blues festivals in the world; it briefly carried another name before B.B. King's performance at its 25th anniversary in 2010 restored the original. Since January 1, 2006, Helena has been joined with its neighbor as the single city of Helena-West Helena, still built on cotton and river barges, and still broadcasting the blues.

## Flight Context

Helena lies at about 34.53°N, 90.59°W on the west bank of the Mississippi River in Phillips County, Arkansas, where Crowley's Ridge meets the river. The broad Mississippi, with its barge traffic and the highway bridge to Mississippi, makes an unmistakable reference, and the old downtown along Cherry Street sits just behind the levee. A pass at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, following the river, shows the town, the levees and the flat Delta fields spreading west. Thompson-Robbins Airport (KHEE) is about 6 miles northwest of town, and Fletcher Field (KCKM) at Clarksdale, Mississippi, lies across the river to the south-southeast. River fog can linger on cool mornings, and summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
