# Tom Lee Park

Memphis's riverfront park honors Tom Lee, a riverworker who could not swim but pulled 32 people from the Mississippi in 1925, and now hosts a spring music festival and a sweeping 2023 redesign.

**Location:** 35.1365, -90.0627

**Categories:** Parks in Memphis, Tennessee, Mississippi River, African-American history, Memphis in May, Urban parks

## Introduction

Tom Lee could not swim. Late in the afternoon of May 8, 1925, the 39-year-old African-American riverworker was steering his 28-foot skiff, the Zev, back upriver after delivering an official to Helena, Arkansas. Also on the Mississippi that day was the steamboat M.E. Norman, carrying members of the Engineers Club of Memphis, the American Society of Civil Engineers and their families. In the swift current at Cow Island Bend, about 15 miles downriver from Memphis, the steamboat capsized. Lee went to the people in the water anyway, and by the end 32 of them were alive because of him. The long green park on the Memphis riverfront carries his name.

## Into the Swift Water

Lee made several trips with his small boat, hauling survivors from the river and ferrying them to shore, and he kept searching after night fell. He acted with little regard for his own safety. Twenty-three passengers and crew still drowned, and investigators never settled on a clear cause for the sinking.

The Engineers Club of Memphis raised enough money to buy a house for Lee and his wife in Klondike, a neighborhood in North Memphis. Lee died of cancer on April 1, 1952, at John Gaston Hospital. Two years later, the park along the riverfront was named in his honor, and a granite obelisk went up in his memory.

## A Mile Along the Bluff

Tom Lee Park runs south from Beale Street for about a mile, a ribbon of roughly 30 acres never more than 400 feet wide, with the Mississippi on its west side and Riverside Boulevard on its east. From its lawns the view opens across the river to the shores of Arkansas, while luxury homes and condominiums line the bluff above. It is a popular spot for walkers, joggers, skaters and cyclists.

The park's memorials have had to survive the weather. The obelisk was destroyed in 2003 in the aftermath of the storm known as Hurricane Elvis, and again by strong storms in May 2017. In October 2006 a bronze sculpture by the artist David Alan Clark was added to commemorate the rescue, showing a survivor being saved from drowning in the Mississippi.

## Festivals in May

For decades each May turned the park into one of the city's great stages. The Beale Street Music Festival kicked off Memphis in May with three days of music, more than 60 acts on four stages ranging across blues, hip-hop and metal. On the third weekend, the World Championship Barbecue Cooking Contest drew hundreds of competitors from around the world to what is described as the world's largest pork barbecue cooking competition. That arrangement has since broken up. After a dispute with the park's operator over damage to the grounds, the barbecue contest moved to Liberty Park, and the Beale Street Music Festival was suspended in 2024 and 2025 after it was removed from Tom Lee Park. In 2026 the park's first weekend in May, May 1 to 3, belonged to a different festival, RiverBeat.

For years the Memphis Symphony Orchestra closed out the month here with the Sunset Symphony, its largest annual performance, held over Memorial Day weekend. That concert has since been discontinued and replaced by a "Celebrate Memphis" event marking the end of the monthlong festival.

## Remade for the River

In 2017 the architecture and urban design firm Studio Gang proposed remaking the park as part of a master plan, developed with the Mayor's Riverfront Task Force and the Memphis River Parks Partnership, that reimagined six miles of the city's riverfront as a connected network. The redesigned park, by Studio Gang and the landscape architects SCAPE, opened in September 2023 on land that had been sparse lawn and heavily compacted soil. Native Tennessee plants returned, along with more than 1,000 new trees.

Five new entrances extend from major streets into winding paths that link lively recreation areas with quieter corners for contemplation. Playgrounds designed by Monstrum take the form of giant creatures: a sturgeon, a caterpillar, a salamander and a family of river otters. A canopy of glulam and steel shades multi-use courts brightened by a mural from Memphis-born artist James Little. At the northern entrance, a switchback path descends the bluffs to the water's edge, the first fully accessible connection between Memphis and its river, a fitting way to reach the water in a park named for a man who could not swim yet went out on that river to save others.

## Flight Context

Tom Lee Park lies at 35.137°N, 90.063°W along the east bank of the Mississippi River in downtown Memphis, running about a mile south from Beale Street. From 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, look for the long, narrow strip of green between the river and Riverside Boulevard, just below the downtown towers, with the Mississippi River bridges to the north and south and the Arkansas shore opposite. Memphis sits under Class B airspace, so coordinate with approach control. General DeWitt Spain Airport (FAA identifier M01; no ICAO code) is about 4 nm north. The main Memphis airport, Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM), is about 7 nm southeast, and West Memphis Municipal Airport (KAWM) is about 8.5 nm west across the river. The river valley can hold haze and morning fog.
