# Peabody Hotel

Memphis's grand Peabody Hotel has hosted presidents, big bands and Louis Armstrong, nearly died with downtown in the 1970s, and still sends five mallards marching across its lobby every day.

**Location:** 35.1425, -90.0518

**Categories:** Hotels in Memphis, Tennessee, Historic Hotels of America, Hotel buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Hotel buildings completed in 1925, Animal traditions

## Introduction

Every day at 11 a.m., an elevator carries five mallards down from the roof of the Peabody Hotel in downtown Memphis. To the strains of John Philip Sousa's King Cotton march, the ducks, one drake and four hens, cross a red carpet in the lobby and settle into the fountain, where they spend the day before marching back out at 5 p.m. It is an absurd ritual performed with complete seriousness, and it has made the ducks celebrities. The building around them, though, has a longer and stranger history than any duck parade, stretching from a hotel given away as a wedding present to a near-death in the 1970s.

## A Hotel Given Away

The first Peabody rose in 1869 at the corner of Main and Monroe streets. Its builder, Robert Campbell Brinkley, named it for his friend George Peabody, who had recently died, to honor his contributions to the South. The hotel had 75 rooms with private bathrooms and numerous elegant public rooms, and it was a huge success. Not long after it opened, Brinkley gave it away as a wedding gift to his daughter, Anna Overton Brinkley, and her husband, Robert B. Snowden.

The guest list read like a roll call of the era: Presidents Andrew Johnson and William McKinley, and the Confederate generals Robert E. Lee and Nathan Bedford Forrest. Jefferson Davis, the former president of the Confederacy, lived there in 1870 while working as president of an insurance company. The original hotel closed in 1923.

## Big Bands on the Skyway

Its successor opened on Union Avenue on September 1, 1925, an Italian Renaissance building designed by the noted Chicago architect Walter W. Ahlschlager. It rises 13 floors, though you will not find a 13 on the elevator panel. Wary of the superstition, management named the top floor the Skyway and gave it an "S" button. Red neon "The Peabody" signs crown the Skyway Ballroom and the central elevator shaft.

During the Big Band era, the Skyway was a popular night spot, and its ballroom was one of only a handful of places in America from which the CBS radio network broadcast live weekly programs. Tommy Dorsey and the Andrews Sisters were regular headliners. Radio station WREC, and later WREC-TV, now WREG, worked out of the basement for years.

Not every performance was a simple celebration. When Louis Armstrong and his Orchestra played the Peabody in October 1931, Armstrong had been arrested the night before over his contact with a white woman who turned out to be his manager's wife. He dedicated "I'll Be Glad When You're Dead You Rascal You" to the Memphis Police Department.

## Five Mallards and a Red Carpet

The duck custom dates to the 1930s; the hotel itself counts from 1933. General manager Frank Schutt had just returned from a weekend hunting trip in Arkansas, and he and his friends thought it would be amusing to leave three of their live English call ducks in the lobby fountain. The guests loved it. In 1940 a bellman named Edward Pembroke, a former circus animal trainer, volunteered to care for the ducks, taught them to march, and served as Duckmaster until 1991.

The ducks have since appeared, with their Duckmaster, on The Tonight Show Starring Johnny Carson, Sesame Street, the sitcom Coach and The Oprah Winfrey Show, and in People magazine. Guests from Oprah Winfrey and Kevin Bacon to Patrick Swayze and Queen Noor of Jordan have served as Honorary Duckmaster. On December 3, 2008, for the tradition's 75th anniversary, the hotel unveiled a roughly $200,000 rooftop "Duck Palace," a 24-by-12-foot enclosure with granite floors, ceiling fans, a fountain decorated with bronze ducks, a scale replica of the hotel and a large window for admirers.

## Dark Years, Bright Lobby

Grandeur did not guarantee survival. As downtown Memphis decayed in the early 1970s, the hotel struggled, and the Sheraton-Peabody closed in December 1973. An Alabama investment group bought it in 1974 and briefly reopened it under its original name, only to declare bankruptcy on April 1, 1975. The doors closed again.

Jack A. Belz bought the hotel and spent several years and $25 million restoring it, and the Peabody celebrated its grand reopening in 1981. It had been added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1977, and today it belongs to Historic Hotels of America, a program of the National Trust for Historic Preservation. In summer, the rooftop hosts bands for Thursday-night parties, with views across the Memphis skyline. The Peabody has turned up on screen too, in The Firm (1993), starring Tom Cruise, which was partly filmed at the hotel. Down in the lobby, the ducks keep their schedule.

## Flight Context

The Peabody stands at 35.143°N, 90.052°W on Union Avenue in downtown Memphis, a few blocks east of the Mississippi River. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL, look for the downtown cluster of towers near the riverfront; the Peabody's red neon rooftop signs can help identify it after dark. Memphis sits under Class B airspace, so coordinate with approach control. General DeWitt Spain Airport (FAA identifier M01; no ICAO code) is about 3.5 nm north. The main Memphis airport, Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM), is about 7 nm southeast, and West Memphis Municipal Airport (KAWM) is about 9 nm west across the river in Arkansas. Summer brings haze and thunderstorms; river fog can linger on cool mornings.
