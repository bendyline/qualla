# Memphis tanker truck disaster

Two days before Christmas 1988, a propane tanker crashed on a notoriously awkward Midtown Memphis interchange, and the explosion that followed killed eight people on the highway and in the homes beside it. A ninth died in the traffic jam it caused.

**Location:** 35.1516, -90.0215

**Categories:** 1988 disasters in the United States, Transportation disasters in Tennessee, Gas explosions in the United States, Interstate 40, Transportation in Memphis, Tennessee

## Introduction

At about 10:15 on the morning of December 23, 1988, two days before Christmas, a tractor-trailer carrying 9,511 gallons of pressurized liquefied propane skidded off a flyover ramp at the junction of Interstate 40 and Interstate 240 in Midtown Memphis. Within seconds the highway was inside a fireball estimated at about 700 feet wide and 700 feet tall. Nine people died: five motorists on the interstate, three people in the homes beside it, and the driver of a second truck who ran into the stopped traffic about an hour later. The interchange where it began had long been considered unsafe and poorly designed, and its story starts with a highway that was never finished.

## A Ramp for a Road Never Built

The interchange was built in the late 1960s and opened to traffic on July 14, 1971. It was planned as a four-way junction, with I-40 continuing straight east. That route would have cut through Overton Park, and many residents opposed it, filing a series of lawsuits that culminated in the 1971 U.S. Supreme Court case Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe. In 1981 the Tennessee Department of Transportation abandoned the plan to run I-40 east of the interchange and instead redesignated the northern loop of I-240 as part of I-40.

At the junction, I-40 now shifted from an east-west to a north-south alignment, and eastbound traffic used a flyover ramp with an advisory speed limit of 25 miles per hour. It was considered poorly designed and had already been the site of several accidents. In early 1988 the state began preliminary work on a reconstruction then estimated at $100 million. The work had not yet been done.

## Seconds on the Flyover

The rig was a 1986 Mack tractor pulling a 10,450-gallon steel tank built in 1964. Carrying eastbound I-40 traffic, it skidded off the ramp, rolled about one and a quarter times, and slammed into a retaining wall at the base of an overpass. The impact punctured a small hole in the front of the tank.

Propane poured out and spread into a vapor cloud across both sides of the interstate. Seconds later it ignited in a boiling liquid expanding vapor explosion, or BLEVE. Vehicles and buildings caught fire, and five motorists, including the truck's driver, were killed instantly. Seven cars besides the truck were completely destroyed.

## The Tank That Flew

Even then the tank was not finished. Driven by the gas still rushing and burning from inside it, it launched off the highway, struck a nearby overpass bridge, left the freeway right-of-way, bounced off the ground, clipped the top of a tree, and crashed into a duplex about 125 yards from the crash site. One person inside was killed instantly, and the fire it started spread to other buildings and cars. Most of the tractor, meanwhile, was flung into the southbound lanes of I-40.

About ten people were injured, several of them treated for severe burns. Some of those who died were burned beyond recognition.

## Nine Names

The dead were ordinary travelers and neighbors. Randall Benson, 29, of West Memphis, Arkansas, was driving the tanker. Harrison G. Lee, 34, of Brighton, Tennessee, and Robert Wardlow, 40, of Memphis were on the road that morning, as were Tina Wiles, 30, and Warner Wiles, 70, both of Vicksburg, Mississippi. David Bailey, 29, of West Helena, Arkansas, was also killed, in a separate crash about an hour later, when his truck struck a car in the traffic jam that followed. In the homes struck by the blast, Shelanda Towles, 10, Iva J. Rubesheim, 87, and James M. Malone, 57, lost their lives; two of them died the following day from their injuries.

National Transportation Safety Board investigators arrived the next day. They found that the truck had passed an inspection in Arkansas the day before, and that Benson, an employee of Wooten Transports, knew the route. He had picked up the load in West Memphis and was bound for Ellendale, about 20 miles northeast of Memphis.

## Rebuilding the Junction

The fix came slowly. Before work could begin, the state had to acquire more right-of-way, and opposition from some residents delayed the project by several years. Between June 2003 and December 2006, the Tennessee Department of Transportation spent a reported $53 million to completely rebuild the interchange. The unused ramps and bridges were torn down, and the junction became a directional T interchange. The neighboring interchange at Jackson Avenue was modified, and new auxiliary lanes and slip ramps were added to reduce crashes caused by sudden lane changes.

The flyover of 1988 is gone. The names of the nine people who died beside it are worth keeping.

## Flight Context

The interchange lies at 35.152°N, 90.021°W in Midtown Memphis, where I-40 bends from its east-west alignment onto the north-south path of the old I-240 loop. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL (observe Memphis Class B airspace), look for the large directional T interchange with the green expanse of Overton Park to the east and the downtown skyline and Mississippi River bridges to the west. General DeWitt Spain Airport (FAA identifier M01; no ICAO code) is about 3.3 nm north-northwest. The main Memphis airport (KMEM) is about 7 nm south-southeast; West Memphis Municipal Airport (KAWM) is about 10.5 nm west in Arkansas. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
