# Memphis: Where the Blues Were Born and Elvis Came Home

Tennessee's second-largest city where Beale Street created the blues, where Sun Studio launched Elvis and rock and roll, where Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated, where FedEx invented overnight shipping, and where the barbecue is dry-rubbed or wet-sauced but always serious.

**Location:** 35.1494, -90.0489

**Categories:** tennessee, memphis, city, music, blues, elvis, civil-rights

## Introduction

Memphis sits on the bluffs above the Mississippi River where the Delta blues came north and transformed into something the whole world would hear. W.C. Handy wrote 'Memphis Blues' on Beale Street in 1912; B.B. King and Howlin' Wolf and Muddy Waters played the clubs; Sam Phillips opened Sun Studio and recorded Elvis Presley, Johnny Cash, Jerry Lee Lewis, and Carl Perkins - rock and roll born in a small building on Union Avenue. Elvis bought Graceland and lived there until he died in 1977; hundreds of thousands still visit annually to see the jungle room and the grave. Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated at the Lorraine Motel in 1968, now the National Civil Rights Museum. Stax Records created Southern soul with Otis Redding and Isaac Hayes. FedEx, founded here in 1971, invented overnight shipping. The barbecue is legendary - dry-rubbed ribs or wet with tangy sauce, rib tips and pulled pork, a cuisine as serious as the music. Memphis made American culture; American culture keeps coming back.

## Beale Street and the Blues

W.C. Handy, the 'Father of the Blues,' came to Memphis in 1909 and heard the music that Delta sharecroppers brought north - the wailing melodies, the bent notes, the stories of hardship and love. He wrote it down, published 'Memphis Blues' in 1912, and gave the music a name. Beale Street became the center of African American musical culture in the South - clubs, theaters, restaurants serving a community that had money to spend. B.B. King named his guitar 'Lucille' after a woman who caused a fire in a Beale Street club. Today the street is a tourist district, live music pumping from a dozen clubs, neon signs glowing, crowds spilling between venues. The music is real even if the context has changed - this is where the blues went electric, where the sounds that would become rock and roll were invented, where American music found its voice.

## Sun Studio and Rock and Roll

Sam Phillips opened Memphis Recording Service at 706 Union Avenue in 1950, recording blues artists for labels that would release 'race records.' He launched Sun Records in 1952, looking for something new - 'a white man with the Negro sound and the Negro feel.' Elvis Presley walked in on July 18, 1953, paid $3.98 to record two songs as a gift for his mother, and Phillips heard what he was looking for. 'That's All Right' launched Elvis and launched rock and roll in 1954. Sun Studio recorded Johnny Cash's early hits, Jerry Lee Lewis's piano-pounding anthems, Carl Perkins's 'Blue Suede Shoes.' The Million Dollar Quartet session in December 1956 captured Elvis, Cash, Lewis, and Perkins jamming together. The studio still operates - tours during the day, recording sessions at night - in the same small room where American music changed forever.

## Graceland and Elvis

Elvis Presley bought Graceland in 1957 for $100,000, a colonial revival mansion on a thirteen-acre estate south of downtown. He lived there until his death on August 16, 1977, at age 42. The house is preserved as he left it - the shag carpet and mirrored ceilings of the 1970s, the jungle room with its waterfall, the racquetball court, the meditation garden where Elvis, his parents, and his grandmother are buried. Graceland is America's second-most-visited house after the White House, drawing 600,000 visitors annually to walk through rooms where the King of Rock and Roll watched television and ate peanut butter and banana sandwiches. The complex has expanded to include museums of cars, costumes, and awards. Lisa Marie's private jet is parked on the grounds. Elvis Presley Boulevard runs past the entrance, tacky and devoted and somehow perfect.

## Civil Rights and Soul

Martin Luther King Jr. came to Memphis in April 1968 to support striking sanitation workers - the 'I Am a Man' strike that demanded dignity and fair wages. On April 4, standing on the balcony of the Lorraine Motel, he was assassinated by James Earl Ray. The motel is now the National Civil Rights Museum, the balcony and room preserved, the story of the movement told through exhibits that trace from slavery to the present. Stax Records, on McLemore Avenue in South Memphis, created Southern soul in the 1960s - Otis Redding, Isaac Hayes, the Staple Singers, Sam & Dave recording hits in a converted movie theater. The Stax Museum occupies the site now, the theater's marquee recreated, the music preserved. Memphis's civil rights and musical histories intertwine - the same community that created the blues and soul also demanded equality and mourned King's death.

## Bluff City Position

Frederick W. Smith International Airport (MEM, formerly Memphis International) is FedEx's global hub - the world's busiest cargo airport, planes arriving all night to sort packages for morning delivery. I-40 runs east to Nashville; I-55 runs north to St. Louis and south through Mississippi; I-240 loops the city. The Mississippi River forms the western border, Arkansas visible across the water, the Hernando de Soto Bridge carrying I-40 into West Memphis. From altitude, Memphis appears on the river bluffs - downtown's modest skyline, the airport's cargo operations visible at night as a hive of activity, the Mississippi wide and brown below. What appears from the air as Tennessee's largest western city is where the blues were born, where Elvis came home, where Martin Luther King was killed, and where barbecue is religion. Memphis made American music; the music made Memphis worth visiting forever.
