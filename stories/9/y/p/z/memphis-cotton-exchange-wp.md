# Memphis Cotton Exchange

Founded in 1874 on Memphis's Cotton Row, the Memphis Cotton Exchange set the rules for buying and pricing the Mid-South's cotton, and its old trading floor is now the Cotton Museum.

**Location:** 35.1433, -90.0546

**Categories:** Financial services companies established in 1874, 1874 establishments in Tennessee, Commodity exchanges in the United States, Cotton industry in the United States

## Introduction

Above the old trading floor, a ceiling-high chalkboard runs the length of an upper gallery some ten feet up. Workers called chalkboard markers once stood before it, writing out ever-changing cotton prices as quotes came in from around the world, while members of the Memphis Cotton Exchange traded on the floor below. The board still hangs in the Cotton Exchange Building at the corner of Front Street and Union Avenue, in the downtown district known as historic Cotton Row. The trading stopped long ago. The story of the crop that built Memphis did not.

## Rules for a Booming Market

The exchange was founded in 1874, when the cotton trade in Memphis was strong in the years after the Civil War. Merchants needed a trade organization to regulate the city's cotton marketing, and they had seen how much the New York and New Orleans cotton exchanges did for their members. Once established, the Memphis exchange wrote rules and regulations for cotton trading and set standards for buying and pricing cotton in Memphis and across the Mid-South. Its members agreed on a common method for grading cotton. It operated as a spot market, where cotton was bought and sold for delivery rather than as futures contracts, and apart from two short-lived experiments it never developed futures trading. It also became a source of information about world markets, and cotton merchants found they had to join as members in order to compete. And it promoted "Memphis cotton" in major markets such as New York and London.

## Three Buildings, One Exchange

The exchange's first building went up in 1885. In 1910 it moved into what is now called the Exchange Building at 9 North Second Street, a tall, 19-story tower credited to N. M. Woods that for a time housed both the Cotton and Merchant exchanges. When the two organizations decided they wanted separate locations, the Memphis Cotton Exchange built a multi-story building of its own on Union Avenue, completed in the 1920s. That building became the exchange's lasting home. The earlier Exchange Building on Second Street has had a different fate. After the exchanges declined in the late twentieth century, as the city's economy diversified, it was renovated into apartments.

## Members Only

Cotton trading in the Union Avenue building took place on the first floor, and only members of the exchange were allowed to trade there. The trading floor was where the rules the exchange had written became prices, and the great chalkboard made those prices visible to everyone in the room at once. For decades, members traded here in person. Then technology overtook the floor. In 1978 the trading floor closed in favor of computer trading, and the room where merchants had watched the chalkboard went quiet.

## The Cotton Museum

In March 2006 the historic floor reopened as the Cotton Museum. It teaches visitors about the industry and agriculture of cotton, the commodity crop that built the wealth of Memphis for decades. That wealth began in the fields, where the hard labor of planting and picking cotton fell for generations on enslaved people and, after the Civil War, on sharecroppers and tenant farmers, many of them Black. The merchants on Front Street traded what those workers produced. A visit to the old exchange is a chance to hold both parts of the story together: the trading floor and its chalkboard, and the fields across the Delta that filled it with something to trade.

## Flight Context

The Memphis Cotton Exchange stands at 35.1433°N, 90.0546°W at Front Street and Union Avenue in downtown Memphis, a short walk from the Mississippi riverfront. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL, look for the downtown skyline along the bluff, Mud Island across the narrow harbor channel to the northwest, the Hernando de Soto Bridge farther northwest, and the Memphis Pyramid to the north. Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM) is about 7 nm to the south-southeast, and downtown lies under its Class B airspace, so coordinate with Memphis Approach and expect heavy cargo traffic. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common.
