# Clayborn Temple

The Memphis church that served as headquarters and sanctuary for the 1968 sanitation workers' strike was in the midst of a long restoration when an intentionally set fire gutted it in April 2025.

**Location:** 35.1353, -90.0511

**Categories:** Memphis sanitation strike, Civil rights movement, Churches in Memphis, Tennessee, National Register of Historic Places in Memphis, Tennessee, Church fires in the United States

## Introduction

Every day of the 1968 Memphis sanitation strike, the marchers set out from the same place. They gathered at Clayborn Temple, a Romanesque Revival church of rough limestone at the corner of Pontotoc and Hernando, and walked 1.3 miles to City Hall. The church became the strike's headquarters, its meeting hall and, on one violent March day, its refuge. More than half a century later, with a multimillion-dollar restoration underway, fire gutted it on the morning of April 28, 2025. Investigators later confirmed the fire had been set on purpose.

## A Church for Second Presbyterian

The building began as the new home of Second Presbyterian Church. In 1888 the congregation decided to buy the corner lot at Pontotoc and Hernando, and ground was broken on February 2, 1891, with the cornerstone laid that May 14. At the dedication service on Sunday, January 1, 1893, every Presbyterian pastor in the city joined the congregation. The architects, Kees & Long and E.C. Jones, worked in the Romanesque Revival style, with cross-gabled roofs, walls of rusticated limestone blocks and heavy timber roof trusses, and they set the chancel in a corner of the sanctuary rather than at its center. In 1949 the church was sold to the African Methodist Episcopal Church, which renamed it Clayborn Temple after one of its bishops.

## Two Deaths and a Strike

For years, Memphis labor unions had tried to change a city Public Works system marked by discrimination, unfair working conditions and drastically insufficient wages. Then, on February 1, 1968, two city sanitation workers, Echol Cole and Robert Walker, died. Their deaths united the sanitation workers, labor unions, religious communities and the Black middle class into a grassroots movement. On February 12, 1,300 sanitation workers walked off the job, led by T.O. Jones, an organizer for AFSCME Local 1733, and Jerry Wurf, the union's national president. Reverend Malcolm Blackburn opened Clayborn Temple's offices, classrooms and sanctuary to strategy meetings and community gatherings, and from then on the strikers marched from its doors to City Hall every day.

## Tear Gas in the Sanctuary

On Thursday, March 28, 1968, marchers began gathering at Clayborn Temple in anticipation of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., and nearly 15,000 people took part. Only half a mile into the march, a rumor that police had killed a Hamilton High School student that morning set off anger among young people in the crowd, and it erupted into vandalism, looting and rioting. The police response was brutal and fell on nonviolent protesters and youths alike. Reverend Lawson and Reverend Jackson urged the marchers to return to Clayborn Temple, and they retreated there as police surrounded the building. Anyone who tried to leave was attacked with mace, tear gas and clubs, and at one point officers entered the church itself, swinging clubs and firing tear gas canisters. The building filled with injured and terrified people. "The interior of Clayborn looked like the aftermath of a war," Kathy Pittman Black reported.

## Restoration Interrupted

The AME church later left the building vacant for more than a decade. In October 2015 a group of Memphians began rehabilitating it, with plans that included a museum and courtyard. The building, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979 for its local architectural significance, was upgraded to national significance in 2017 because of its role in the strike. The restoration, reported at the time of the fire as a roughly $25 million project and funded in part by the National Trust for Historic Preservation's African American Cultural Heritage Fund, was slated for completion in 2026. On the morning of April 28, 2025, with work still underway, fire swept through the landmark, and the interior was described as "a total loss." After the ruin was stabilized on May 14, the Memphis Fire Department investigated, and on May 21 it confirmed that the fire had been set intentionally, asking the public for help identifying a person caught on camera near the building. Losses were estimated at $3 million to the structure and $2.5 million to property inside. A year after the fire, only a few walls still stood. The nonprofit that owns the temple, The Big We, has said it plans to rebuild it as a replica and hopes to open part of it by the end of 2028, and no arrest in the arson had been reported.

## Flight Context

The remains of Clayborn Temple stand at 35.1353°N, 90.0511°W at Pontotoc Avenue and Hernando Street on the south side of downtown Memphis, a short distance east of the Mississippi River bluffs. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL, use the downtown towers and the Mississippi River bridges to the west as references. Memphis's main airport, Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM), is about 6.7 nm to the southeast inside busy Class B airspace, so obtain clearance before overflying the city. General DeWitt Spain Airport (FAA identifier M01; no ICAO code listed) is about 4 nm to the north, and West Memphis Municipal Airport (KAWM) is about 9 nm to the west across the river in Arkansas. Summer brings haze and thunderstorms.
