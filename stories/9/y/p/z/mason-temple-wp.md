# Mason Temple

Headquarters of the Church of God in Christ and resting place of its founder, Memphis's Mason Temple is where Martin Luther King Jr. gave his final speech, the night before he was killed.

**Location:** 35.1215, -90.0493

**Categories:** Church of God in Christ, Churches in Memphis, Tennessee, Pentecostal churches in Tennessee, Churches completed in 1941, 20th-century Pentecostal church buildings, Evangelical megachurches in the United States, Megachurches in Tennessee

## Introduction

Few churches come with their own post office. Mason Temple in Memphis, Tennessee, was built with one, along with a nursery, a salon, a photographic booth, a first aid and emergency ward, and a room for checking baggage and registering guests. It was designed to receive the faithful by the thousands, as the international sanctuary and central headquarters of the Church of God in Christ, the largest African American Pentecostal group in the world. It is also where, on a spring night in 1968, Martin Luther King Jr. stood before a crowd and gave the last speech of his life.

## The Bishop's Church

The temple carries the name of Bishop Charles Harrison Mason, founder of the Church of God in Christ. Mason was born in 1864 to formerly enslaved parents, and the denomination he built, known as COGIC, grew quickly in Memphis before spreading to other parts of the world, including Latin America and Asia. Estimates of its membership today run from about 6.5 million to more than 7.5 million. The church's original "Tabernacle" in Memphis burned down in 1936. Four years later, in 1940, Mason began building its replacement, and the man who had founded the denomination set out to give it a true headquarters.

## Built on Many Small Gifts

Mason assembled a team for the job. Riley F. Williams chaired the building commission, Ulysses Ellis Miller supervised construction, and Henry Taylor served as architect. Money was the obstacle. When construction began, the church had little in its building-fund treasury, so church members and local citizens stepped in and raised enough to see the project through. What they paid for was substantial: a three-story building of brick, stone, steel and concrete. When it opened, Mason Temple was the largest church building owned by a predominantly Black Christian denomination in the United States. And it had been paid for, in large part, by the people who would fill it.

## Room for Thousands

The main auditorium seated five thousand people, and the balcony and the assembly room could each hold up to two thousand more. The extra facilities, from the baggage check to the emergency ward to the post office, reveal how the building was meant to be used. They suggest a building planned for big gatherings of visitors from out of town, people who would need somewhere to leave their bags, a way to send mail and help if they fell ill. The building was made for crowds, and in 1968 it would hold one for a famous speech.

## The Mountaintop

In 1968 Memphis's Black sanitation workers were earning low wages and were treated differently from their white co-workers, and their struggle for better conditions drew Martin Luther King Jr. to the city. On April 3, 1968, he spoke at Mason Temple. The speech became known as "I've Been to the Mountaintop," and its closing lines have echoed ever since: "I've seen the Promised Land. I may not get there with you. But I want you to know tonight, that we, as a people, will get to the Promised Land." The next day King was assassinated outside his room at the Lorraine Motel. A plaque on an exterior wall near the church entrance now tells the story of that night.

## Where the Founder Rests

Bishop Mason died in 1961, and he was laid to rest inside the temple he built, in a marble crypt, so the building serves as both headquarters and memorial. Visitors come for different reasons. Members of the denomination come to the heart of their church and to the resting place of its founder. Others come to stand where King spoke his final public words to a crowd. Mason Temple holds both histories under one roof, and it would be hard to tell the story of Memphis, or of the civil rights movement, without it.

## Flight Context

Mason Temple stands at 35.1215°N, 90.0493°W, just south of downtown Memphis. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL, use the downtown skyline, the Mississippi riverfront and the Hernando de Soto Bridge to the north as references; the Lorraine Motel, now the National Civil Rights Museum, lies roughly a mile to the north-northwest, and Beale Street is a short distance beyond it. Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM) is about 6 nm to the southeast, and this area sits under its busy Class B airspace, so coordinate with Memphis Approach and expect heavy cargo traffic. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common.
