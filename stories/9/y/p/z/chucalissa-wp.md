# Chucalissa

On the southwestern edge of Memphis, a Mississippian mound village uncovered by Civilian Conservation Corps workers is now the C. H. Nash Museum at Chucalissa, a National Historic Landmark run by the University of Memphis.

**Location:** 35.0625, -90.1289

**Categories:** Archaeological museums in Tennessee, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Civilian Conservation Corps in Tennessee, Middle Mississippian culture, Museums in Memphis, Tennessee, National Historic Landmarks in Tennessee, Native American museums in Tennessee, University museums in Tennessee, University of Memphis, National Register of Historic Places in Memphis, Tennessee

## Introduction

Chucalissa, the name archaeologists gave the site, means "abandoned house" in the Choctaw language. It is a fitting name for a town above the Mississippi River whose builders left long ago. The village belonged to people of the Mississippian culture, and it was occupied roughly between AD 1000 and 1500. Today it lies within the city limits of Memphis, Tennessee, where the C. H. Nash Museum at Chucalissa exhibits what was found in its soil and invites visitors to walk among its mounds.

## Found While Digging a Swimming Area

The rediscovery happened almost by accident. In the late 1930s, an African American company of the Civilian Conservation Corps was developing T. O. Fuller State Park, the neighboring park created in the 1930s for the use of African Americans in the segregated South. The Tennessee Encyclopedia describes it as the nation's second oldest state park created for African Americans. CCC Company 1464 arrived to begin park development in April 1939. The company had been reduced to just 44 men, but it rapidly grew to as many as 175, and those men did the physical work of turning the land into a park. While conducting routine excavation for a swimming area, the CCC workers uncovered evidence of a Native American village and burial mound from the Mississippian period. What had been planned as a place for recreation turned into the start of decades of archaeology, as excavations of the mound complex began.

## Forty Years of Careful Digging

Since 1962, the University of Memphis has operated the museum and the surrounding village grounds. Staff and students manage the archaeological site together, which makes Chucalissa a working classroom as well as a museum. Its collection is extensive, built from artifacts recovered over a 40-year period of systematic excavations. The museum is named after its founding director, C. H. Nash. In archaeologists' records the site is known as 40 SY 1, a string of letters and numbers that marks it as the first site on the list for Shelby County. The formal recognition followed the fieldwork. Chucalissa Indian Village was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1973.

## Among the Best Preserved

In 1994 Chucalissa was declared a National Historic Landmark, recognized as one of the region's best-preserved major prehistoric settlement sites. The Mississippian mound complex is the heart of the site, rising from grounds where a community lived and built centuries before Memphis existed. Visitors today can walk those grounds, follow a nature trail through an arboretum, and get hands-on experience in an archaeology lab. The museum's exhibits explore the history and lifeways of Native Americans of the southeastern United States, both before and after European contact. Chucalissa belongs to a wider landscape of Mississippian sites in the Mid-South, including Parkin in Arkansas and the Nodena site.

## Flight Context

Chucalissa lies at 35.0625°N, 90.1289°W in southwestern Memphis, beside T. O. Fuller State Park on the wooded bluffs near the Mississippi River. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL, look for the green, tree-covered expanse of the state park on the bluffs above the Mississippi floodplain; downtown Memphis and the Hernando de Soto Bridge lie roughly 6 nm to the northeast. Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM) is about 7.5 nm to the east, and the area sits beneath its Class B airspace, so coordinate with Memphis Approach and expect heavy cargo traffic. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common.
