# Kaw Mission

A two-story stone school in Council Grove housed thirty Kaw boys for three years in the 1850s, a small chapter in the long story of the people Kansas is named for and the land they were made to leave.

**Location:** 38.6658, -96.4939

**Categories:** Kaw tribe, Kansas state historic sites, Native American museums in Kansas, Santa Fe Trail, National Register of Historic Places in Morris County, Kansas, Methodist churches in Kansas

## Introduction

Kansas carries their name, but the Kaw were removed from it in the 1870s. The state was named for the Kaw, or Kansa, who lived in the country that became Kansas for many generations. In Council Grove, a two-story building of native stone preserves one short chapter of their story: a mission school where thirty Kaw boys lived, studied and worshipped from 1851 to 1854, during the years the United States was steadily shrinking the land the Kaw could call their own.

## Water, Grass and Timber

Council Grove sits on the Neosho River, and in the days of the Santa Fe Trail that made it a natural stopping place. The trail was a commerce route between Missouri and Mexico, and what is now Kansas lay at the western edge of American settlement. Traffic bound for the Spanish town of Santa Fe passed through here, where travelers found water, grass and timber. In 1825 the U.S. government negotiated with the Osage for passage across their lands. That treaty secured the Santa Fe Trail's right-of-way through Osage country, and the council itself inspired the town's name. Then, in 1846, a treaty with the Kaw reduced their lands to a tract 20 miles square that included present-day Council Grove. Traders and government agents moved quickly to the new location, and in 1847 Seth M. Hays, the first white settler at Council Grove, set up a home and trading post along the trail.

## A School Paid for by Treaty

The 1846 treaty set aside an annual payment of $1,000 to advance the education of the Kaw in their own country. In 1850 the Methodist Episcopal Church South, which had ministered to the tribe since 1830, received government approval to build a mission and school, and the building was finished by February 1851. It was substantial for the frontier: two stories and eight rooms of native stone, with space for 50 boarding students along with teachers, missionaries and farmers. School opened in May 1851 under Thomas Sears Huffaker, a 24-year-old teacher who had served at the Shawnee Manual Labor School near present-day Kansas City. The boys studied spelling, reading, writing and arithmetic. They also learned the principles of agriculture, though they received no instruction in the trades.

## Orphans at the Desks

The Kaw did not embrace the school. They considered the mission's purpose degrading, and they sent only boys who were orphans or dependents of the tribe; girls were not allowed to attend at all. The thirty boys who lived, studied and worshipped in the mission over those years were drawn from those children alone, boys whose schooling was decided for them by circumstance. Money ended the experiment. The school's costs were excessive, about $50 a year for each student, and in 1854 classes for the Kaw stopped. After just three years the mission ended its school for the Kaw, leaving behind a building that outlasted the arrangement it was built to serve.

## A Shrinking Homeland

The mission's brief life unfolded against a steady loss of land. The Kaw had been moved to the Neosho Valley, and they would live there for less than 30 years. In 1859 another change cut their holdings to a tract just 9 by 14 miles. In the 1870s the Kaw lands were relinquished, and despite an impassioned plea by Chief Allegawaho, the U.S. government removed the Kaw to Indian Territory, now Oklahoma. The tribe moved to a reservation there, and the Kaw still hold tribal lands in Oklahoma today. Meanwhile the mission land passed to the teacher who had once run the school. Thomas Huffaker bought it in 1865 and owned it for 14 years, and in 1926 his son, Carl I. Huffaker, bought the part on which the mission building stands.

## Kaw Mission State Historic Site

In 1951 the Kansas legislature authorized the purchase of the mission property, and twenty years later, in 1971, it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Today the Kansas Historical Society administers it as Kaw Mission State Historic Site. Its displays include Kaw items alongside mid-19th-century frontier objects and furnishings. The building is the two-story stone mission finished in 1851, the one where Huffaker began teaching that May. Inside it, the whole arc of Council Grove comes into focus: an Osage council that named a town, wagons rolling west toward Santa Fe, and a people whose name became a state's even as they were moved away from it.

## Flight Context

Kaw Mission stands in Council Grove, Kansas, at approximately 38.666°N, 96.494°W, in Morris County on the Neosho River, where the Santa Fe Trail once crossed. From about 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, follow the Neosho River into town and look for the historic district; the mission itself is a modest two-story stone building. Council Grove Municipal Airport is about 4 nm west (FAA identifier K63; no ICAO code). Emporia Municipal Airport (KEMP) lies about 24 nm southeast, and Manhattan Regional Airport (KMHK) about 30 nm north. Expect open, often windy conditions over the Flint Hills.
