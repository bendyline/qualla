# Hermit's Cave on Belfry Hill

A small park in Council Grove, Kansas, was built on two good stories, a hermit priest in a cave and a bell that warned of raids, and historians now doubt one and have disproven the other.

**Location:** 38.6622, -96.4942

**Categories:** Santa Fe Trail, Parks on the National Register of Historic Places in Kansas, National Register of Historic Places in Morris County, Kansas, Folklore, Council Grove, Kansas

## Introduction

The bell never warned anyone of a raid, and the hermit may never have lived in the cave. Neither fact has stopped Belfry Hill from becoming one of Council Grove's landmarks. This small public park in the Kansas town that was once the Santa Fe Trail's last major supply stop was built on two appealing stories, and on the determination of one woman who saw in a neglected hillside a way to put her town's history on display.

## A Priest Who Might Not Have Been

The hermit's story comes from a newspaperman named John Maloy. According to him, in 1863 an Italian Roman Catholic priest lived as a hermit for five months in a cave west of Council Grove. It is a vivid image, but it rests on thin ground. Maloy's account was not published until 1890, more than a quarter century after the events it describes, and only one contemporary reference to the hermit has been found in local publications. That one notice, in the Council Grove Press of April 27, 1863, does place an Italian hermit in a cave just west of town, but nothing has corroborated which cave, and the gap casts real doubt on the story's accuracy. Another local writer, Henry Inman, added to the tale, and it grew popular enough that residents came to recognize the cave on Belfry Hill as the place where the hermit had lived.

## The Bell That Held Flowers

The bell has a sturdier history, though not the one it was later given. It came to Council Grove in 1863, when the city reportedly bought it from a church in Lawrence to serve as a church and school bell. Later accounts claimed it had been rung to warn residents of Native American raids on white settlers, a detail that has since been disproven. In 1884 a wind storm destroyed the tower that held it. The bell did not stay idle: a local resident put it to work as a flower pot until Kate Aplington led the town's schoolchildren in raising money in 1901 for a stone monument that held the old bell and honored the assassinated President William McKinley.

## Kate Aplington Cleans Up the Hill

Aplington was a local booster, and Belfry Hill was her project. The land belonged to the city but had been poorly maintained, and she led private development and cleanup efforts to turn it into something worth visiting. Council Grove established the park in 1901 largely because of her work. She understood the power of a good story, and she promoted the new park by spreading its alleged connections to the Santa Fe Trail through its two landmarks, the hermit's cave and the bell tower that gave the hill its name. Whatever the historical record said, the stories drew people up the slope.

## Tourists on the Old Trail

Timing helped. In the early twentieth century, auto tourism along historic routes like the Santa Fe Trail became popular, and Council Grove began attracting visitors curious about its past as a major stop on the trail. Belfry Hill became part of the town's pitch. When the local women's club hosted a regional meeting in 1907, the program included a carriage tour of the park and other nearby trail sites. The trail's centennial in 1921 brought fresh investment, including a stone stairway that made the cave easier to reach, and the city placed historical markers at eleven of its Santa Fe Trail landmarks, Hermit's Cave among them. Local tourist material and the Works Progress Administration's 1939 guide to Kansas kept the legend going. On April 14, 2015, the park was added to the National Register of Historic Places. Hermit or no hermit, the hill stands as a record of how a small town chose to tell its own story.

## Flight Context

Belfry Hill rises in Council Grove, Kansas, at approximately 38.662°N, 96.494°W, in Morris County on the Neosho River. The park itself is small, so fly over the town at about 1,500-2,500 feet AGL and look for the hill and its stone bell monument. Council Grove Municipal Airport is about 4 nm west (FAA identifier K63; no ICAO code). Emporia Municipal Airport (KEMP) lies about 24 nm southeast, and Manhattan Regional Airport (KMHK) about 30 nm north. The Flint Hills around Council Grove are open and often windy.
