# Tallgrass Prairie National Preserve

In the Flint Hills of Kansas, an 1880s cattle ranch has become a national preserve for one of the rarest landscapes in North America, kept alive by fire, grazing and an unusual public-private bargain.

**Location:** 38.4328, -96.5589

**Categories:** National preserves of the United States, Grasslands of the North American Great Plains, Prairies, Protected areas of Chase County, Kansas, Nature Conservancy preserves, Bison

## Introduction

In many years, large stretches of this prairie are set on fire, on purpose. It sounds like destruction, but in the Flint Hills of Kansas it is the opposite. Fire and grazing animals are what keep trees from taking over the grassland, and grassland like this has become precious. Of the more than 170 million acres of tallgrass prairie that once covered the North American continent, less than 4 percent remains intact, most of it here in the Flint Hills. Tallgrass Prairie National Preserve, just north of Strong City, protects a nationally significant piece of it.

## Thirteen Bison from Wind Cave

The preserve's most famous residents arrived in 2009. That year the National Park Service and The Nature Conservancy established a bison herd with 13 animals brought from Wind Cave National Park. The Tallgrass Prairie bison herd has since grown to roughly 85 animals, and they do more than decorate the hills. The bison are a vital part of the tallgrass ecosystem, and they also help preserve the genetic integrity of American bison. They share the landscape with cattle, which dot much of its hills and valleys; the Conservancy manages the grazing leases, and a 35-year lease on the ranch was prepaid in the 1990s by Texas businessman Edward Bass. Together, the burns and the grazers maintain the open prairie, while the Park Service and the Conservancy share the story of the land's ranching legacy, its American Indian history and its diverse grassland ecosystem.

## Stone Walls on the Open Range

The preserve grew out of a ranch. Spring Hill Ranch began in 1878, when Stephen F. Jones, a cattle rancher originally from Tennessee, started buying land in the Flint Hills. He completed the ranch headquarters in 1881 and became one of the region's early adopters of enclosed ranching, in which stone walls, fences or barbed wire limited where cattle could roam. That shift swept across the Plains in the 1880s, driven in large part by overgrazing on the open range. Jones eventually expanded the ranch to 7,000 acres. His Second Empire style ranch house was built in 1881 at an estimated cost of $25,000, and he donated land for the Lower Fox Creek School. On the ranch he raised purebred cattle of several breeds, along with purebred hogs and sheep. In 1888 he sold it all to Barney Lantry.

## Broken Up and Pieced Back Together

The Lantry family kept the ranch until 1907, raising mostly thoroughbred cattle. Afterward Spring Hill, the core of their holdings, was divided into smaller parcels. In 1935, though, George Davis, a grain dealer from Kansas City, reassembled it through a series of purchases. After Davis died in 1955, the property eventually went into a trust and operated as the Z-Bar Ranch. Its history mattered enough that in 1997 the entire ranch property was designated a National Historic Landmark District, primarily for its association with Jones and the end of the open-range ranching era. Visitors today can still see the 1881 ranch house, a limestone barn and the one-room schoolhouse, the physical remains of a Flint Hills cattle operation from the decade when fences replaced open country.

## A Park on Private Land

Turning the ranch into a park took an unusual compromise. Legislation introduced in 1991 called for a preserve, but local interests objected to the National Park Service owning all of it. U.S. Senator Nancy Kassebaum brought stakeholders together, and beginning in January 1992 the group worked out a different model: a public-private partnership, managed by the Park Service, on land that stayed privately owned. In 1994 Kassebaum and Senator Bob Dole introduced Senate bill S. 2412, which limited Park Service ownership to no more than 180 acres, with the rest held by the National Park Trust. The bill became law on November 12, 1996. On September 20, 2002, the Trust donated about 32 acres, including the historic buildings, to the Park Service. In 2005, as the law allowed, the Trust transferred its roughly 10,800 acres to The Nature Conservancy, which now manages the prairie cooperatively with the Park Service.

## Flight Context

Tallgrass Prairie National Preserve lies at approximately 38.433°N, 96.559°W in Chase County, Kansas, just north of Strong City in the heart of the Flint Hills. From about 2,000-3,000 feet AGL the rolling, treeless grassland stands out, and in burn season you may see blackened patches beside green or tawny prairie; look for the ranch headquarters with its limestone barn and Second Empire ranch house. Nearby airports: Cottonwood Falls Airport, about 4.5 nm south (FAA identifier 9K0; no ICAO code); Council Grove Municipal Airport, about 15 nm north (FAA identifier K63); and Emporia Municipal Airport (KEMP), about 18 nm east. Prescribed burns can produce smoke that reduces visibility, and the open hills are often windy.
