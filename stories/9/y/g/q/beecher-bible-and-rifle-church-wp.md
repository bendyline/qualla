# Beecher Bible and Rifle Church

Free-state settlers in Kansas received Sharps rifles that legend says were smuggled in crates marked as Bibles, and the limestone church they finished in 1862 still carries the name of that pairing.

**Location:** 39.1444, -96.3456

**Categories:** Churches in Kansas, Churches on the National Register of Historic Places in Kansas, Churches completed in 1862, Buildings and structures in Wabaunsee County, Kansas, Historic American Buildings Survey in Kansas, 1862 establishments in Kansas, National Register of Historic Places in Wabaunsee County, Kansas, Bleeding Kansas

## Introduction

According to local legend, the shipping crates were marked as Bibles, and anyone who pried one open on the road into Kansas would have found no scripture inside. The cargo was rifles, paid for in part by the abolitionist preacher Henry Ward Beecher and his Brooklyn congregation and sent to a colony of New Englanders on the prairie. The disguise cannot be substantiated, but the nickname stuck to the guns anyway, and in time it stuck to a church. On the southeastern corner of Chapel and Elm Streets in the small community of Wabaunsee, Kansas, the Beecher Bible and Rifle Church still stands in local limestone, named for both halves of that famous pairing.

## A Sermon Sends Them West

About seventy emigrants from New Haven, Connecticut, reached the Wabaunsee townsite in April 1856. They called their venture the Connecticut Kansas Colony, though it also went by the New Haven Colony and, more pointedly, the Beecher Rifle Colony. Before they left, they heard an address by Henry Ward Beecher, a well-known abolitionist, and Beecher and his Brooklyn congregation backed his words with money to help arm the men so they could defend themselves. Kansas in those years was the ground fought over in the struggle remembered as Bleeding Kansas, and a free-state settlement needed more than conviction to survive. The weapons were Sharps rifles. Local legend says they traveled through pro-slavery Missouri in crates marked as Bibles, though that tale cannot be substantiated. What is documented is the pairing: Beecher's congregation sent a check for the rifles along with twenty-five Bibles, and newspapers across the country ran the headline "Bibles and Rifles for Kansas." Before long, the Sharps rifle itself was known as a Beecher's Bible.

## A Station on the Road to Freedom

Wabaunsee did not keep its convictions to itself. The town was staunchly anti-slavery, and in late 1856 it became part of the Underground Railroad, the secret network that helped people escaping slavery make their way toward freedom. Every person who came through was staking a life on the courage of strangers, and the settlers of Wabaunsee chose to be those strangers. The town's loyalty to the free-state cause reached beyond its own streets. When Quantrill's Raid brought massacre to Lawrence, Wabaunsee sent help. None of this was abstract politics for the colonists. They had come to Kansas precisely because they believed its future was worth contesting, and they had arrived carrying the means to hold their ground.

## Four Years of Stone

The church rose slowly. Construction took four years, and the building was finally completed in 1862, while the Civil War was in its second year. Robert Banks, a member of the congregation, built it from local limestone, setting accents of contrasting stone into the walls. The style is described as Plains Vernacular, which is another way of saying that the settlers built with what the land gave them. A gable roof crowns the structure, and above it rises a wooden bell tower. There is nothing grand about it. It looks like what it was, a meeting house raised by settlers who expected it to last. It has lasted. The building was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1971, and it is also listed on the Register of Historic Kansas Places.

## Men on One Side, Women on the Other

Step inside and the arrangement of the room tells you how the founders lived. Visitors enter through a vestibule, where stairs climb to the choir loft. The main space was divided down the center into a men's side and a women's side. Heat came from wood stoves placed around the perimeter, each connected by metal stovepipes to a central brick chimney. On a January Sunday on the prairie, those stoves mattered. The church is still in use. An independent, non-denominational congregation holds Sunday services in the original stone building, more than a century and a half after Banks finished it. The rifles are long gone, but the name keeps their story in plain sight, a reminder that this quiet stone church was born in an armed and dangerous time.
