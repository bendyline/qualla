# Johnny Kaw

A Kansas State horticulture professor invented a giant who dug the Kansas River Valley and tamed tornadoes, and Manhattan built him a 24-foot concrete statue that shrugged off a real tornado within a month of its dedication.

**Location:** 39.1802, -96.5736

**Categories:** Folklore of the United States, Tall tales, Roadside attractions in Kansas, Outdoor sculptures in Kansas, Culture of Kansas, Fictional giants

## Introduction

Kansas, as George Filinger told it, did not simply happen. A giant settler named Johnny Kaw made it. He dug the Kansas River Valley, planted the wheat, invented the sunflower and grew potatoes of enormous size. When tornadoes came, he lopped the funnels off; when drought set in, he wrung out the clouds. Filinger made him up in 1955, and today a 24-foot Johnny Kaw stands in Manhattan City Park, scythe in hand, as if still waiting for the next chore.

## A Professor's Centennial Giant

Filinger was a professor of horticulture at Kansas State University, and he created Johnny Kaw to celebrate the centennial of Manhattan, Kansas. He meant the character to be Kansas's answer to heroes like Paul Bunyan and Pecos Bill. The stories first ran in the city newspaper, The Manhattan Mercury, during the centennial, and Filinger later collected them in a self-published book. Elmer Tomasch of the Kansas State University Art Department illustrated the tales with ink drawings. In Filinger's telling, Kaw shaped the landscape, the geography and even the pioneer trails of the territory. His pets were a wildcat and a jayhawk, the mascots of the state's two universities, and the pair enjoyed a good fight. Their brawling, the stories claimed, was what caused the Dust Bowl.

## The Legend in Print

Filinger's book was not republished after 1969, but the giant found a second storyteller. Kansas author Jerri Garretson gave him new life with a children's picture book in 1997, illustrated by another Kansas State art instructor, Diane A. Dollar. A color edition followed in September 2011, and the original version was included in the 2008 anthology Kansas Tall Tales. His statue even turned up in the funny pages, appearing in a Zippy the Pinhead comic strip on September 17, 2005.

## Beheaded, Run Over, Rebuilt

Before the big statue, there were small ones, three of them, and the first had a hard life. Mrs. Walter O'Neill of Manhattan sculpted it for the centennial, and it stood in City Park during the 1955 celebration until vandals beheaded it. It was then moved to a farm, where someone backed a wagon over it. The statue that stands today went up in 1966, eleven years after the centennial that inspired the legend. Filinger worked hard to promote it and donated a large share of the money. The project cost about $7,000, far more than the estimates of $3,000 to $3,500, and local citizens donated the money it required. They hoped the statue would establish Johnny Kaw as a local legend and draw travelers as a roadside attraction.

## Built to Last

The builders took the giant's durability seriously. Johnny Kaw is made of concrete over a steel beam framework, a design intended to stand up to wind and weather and be easy to maintain. It was tested almost immediately. The statue was dedicated on May 15, 1966, and less than a month later a tornado swept through Manhattan. Johnny Kaw came through unscathed, proof, locals say, that he really was built to last. It is hard to imagine a better ending for a folk hero whose specialty was cutting tornadoes down to size. Sixty years on, he still stands in City Park, the tall-tale Kansan with the scythe, keeping an eye on the town that invented him.
