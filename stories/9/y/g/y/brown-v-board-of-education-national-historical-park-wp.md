# Brown v. Board of Education National Historical Park

Topeka's Monroe Elementary, a handsome 1926 school built for Black children under segregation, now tells the story of the 1954 Supreme Court ruling that separate schools are inherently unequal.

**Location:** 39.0381, -95.6764

**Categories:** National Historic Sites in Kansas, Civil rights movement, Historically segregated African-American schools in the United States, African-American museums in Kansas, Museums in Topeka, Kansas

## Introduction

Linda Carol Brown was a third grader when her walk to school became a national question. Each day she had to walk six blocks to a bus stop to ride to Monroe Elementary, the segregated Black school across town, while Sumner Elementary, a white school, stood just a few blocks from her house. Her father, Oliver Brown, a welder in the shops of the Santa Fe Railroad and an assistant pastor at his church, became the named plaintiff in a case that would reach the U.S. Supreme Court. Monroe Elementary still stands in Topeka, Kansas, and it is now the heart of Brown v. Board of Education National Historical Park.

## A Good Building, and Still Unequal

Monroe was no shack. The current building is the third Monroe school on the corner of Fifteenth and Monroe streets. The first met in a small rented building from 1868 until a permanent structure went up in 1874, and the present school was built in 1926, just south of the old one. It was designed by Thomas W. Williamson, a prominent Topeka architect whose firm, Williamson and Co., was hired by the Topeka Board of Education to design a series of progressive schools between 1920 and 1935. The result was a two-story brick and limestone building in the Italian Renaissance Revival style, made with some of the best materials and most modern technology of its day. Monroe was the newest of the four segregated elementary schools serving Topeka's African American community; the others were Buchanan, McKinley and Washington.

## Separate, by Itself

That quality is exactly what makes Monroe such a telling place. Brown v. Board of Education was brought by thirteen Topeka parents on behalf of their 20 children, and the Court's opinion did not rest on a finding that Black schools in Kansas were worse built or poorly supplied. Its point was deeper: segregation by itself was an unfair detriment to the education of Black children. On May 17, 1954, the Supreme Court agreed unanimously, in an opinion by Chief Justice Earl Warren. The Court asked whether segregating children in public schools solely on the basis of race, even when the physical facilities and other "tangible" factors may be equal, deprives minority children of equal educational opportunities. "We believe that it does," it answered. It declared that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal" and, as such, violated the Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee of "equal protection of the laws." That holding, that separateness alone was unconstitutional, is what made Brown the landmark case in school desegregation.

## Warehouse, Church, Auction Block

History did not protect the building right away. Monroe closed in 1975 because of declining enrollment, and the school district used it as a warehouse, parking buses and servicing vehicles on the grounds. In 1980 the district sold it to private owners, and it stayed in private hands for about eleven years. One set of owners planned to turn it into offices or a private school but sold it in 1982 to the Church of the Nazarene, which used it as a community outreach center and meeting place. In 1988 a local builder bought it for use as a warehouse, and in 1990 he announced plans to auction it off. The Brown Foundation began a crusade to save the school. After letter-writing campaigns and meetings with local congressional leaders and the Trust for Public Land, the Trust purchased the property in 1991.

## From Topeka to Summerton

On October 26, 1992, President George H. W. Bush signed legislation establishing Brown v. Board of Education National Historic Site, and the title passed to the National Park Service in December 1993. The site consists of Monroe Elementary and its adjacent grounds. On May 13, 2022, it was redesignated a National Historical Park and expanded beyond Topeka. The park is set to include Summerton High School and Scott's Branch High School in Summerton, South Carolina, once the property is acquired. Five more schools were authorized as affiliated areas, which the Park Service will not own but will support with financial and technical help to exhibit their history: Robert Russa Moton School in Farmville, Virginia; Howard High School in Wilmington, Delaware; Claymont High School in Claymont, Delaware; Hockessin Colored School #107 in Hockessin, Delaware; and John Philip Sousa Junior High School in Washington, D.C.

## Flight Context

Monroe Elementary, the core of Brown v. Board of Education National Historical Park, stands at Fifteenth and Monroe streets in Topeka, Kansas, at approximately 39.038°N, 95.676°W. The Kansas State Capitol dome lies less than a mile to the north and makes a useful visual reference from about 2,000-2,500 feet AGL. Philip Billard Municipal Airport (KTOP) is about 3 nm northeast, and Topeka Regional Airport (KFOE) about 5 nm south; watch for traffic at both.
