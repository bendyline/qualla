# Topeka State Hospital

For more than a century Topeka State Hospital housed Kansans with mental illness, and its history, from Kirkbride-plan ambitions to forced sterilizations and a cemetery of 1,157 graves with fewer than twenty headstones, is one the state began to acknowledge with a memorial at the turn of the millennium.

**Location:** 39.0601, -95.7128

**Categories:** psychiatric-hospitals, kirkbride-plan, kansas, eugenics, cemeteries, demolished-buildings

## Introduction

On the northeast corner of the old grounds lies a cemetery holding 1,157 graves. Fewer than twenty of them have headstones. The people buried there were patients of Topeka State Hospital, laid to rest over a span of 75 years, most of them without a marker to say who they were. The institution that cared for them, and sometimes failed them, operated from 1872 to 1997. Most of its buildings are gone now. The cemetery remains.

## An Asylum by Design

It began as the Topeka Insane Asylum, a publicly funded institution for the care and treatment of the mentally ill. Kansas had believed the Osawatomie State Hospital would be enough, but Osawatomie became overcrowded, and in 1879 a new hospital opened at 2700 West 6th Street in Topeka. Its first buildings, like Osawatomie's, were designed by John G. Haskell, one of the architects of the Kansas State Capitol. The hospital was laid out according to the Kirkbride Plan, a formal design for institutions of its kind. Among its patients was one famous name: Boston Corbett, the man who shot John Wilkes Booth after Abraham Lincoln's assassination, was committed here in 1887 after a judge ruled him insane. He escaped the following year.

## Chains and Rumors

Care did not always match the plan. By the early 1900s there were rumors of patients being abused, neglected or raped, and patients were often left confined or chained for long periods. Worse was written into law. In 1913 the Kansas legislature passed the state's first sterilization law, aimed at people it labeled "habitual criminals, idiots, epileptics, imbeciles, and insane," the clinical and legal vocabulary of the era. A 1917 law targeted the same groups and removed the requirement for court approval. Fifty-four sterilizations followed in the first seven years, a slow pace that reflected lingering doubt about the laws. After 1921, with new laws and wider acceptance, the numbers rose rapidly until 1950, then declined steadily until sterilizations ceased altogether in 1961. At the peak, in the mid-1930s, the rate was about 10 per 100,000 residents each year. Early on, most of Kansas's forced sterilizations were performed here, on patients who had been placed in the state's care.

## Danger on the Wards

Stephanie Uhlrig worked at the hospital as a music and activity therapist. One of the hospital's patients had been found not guilty by reason of insanity on an aggravated battery charge. Transferred to Topeka in 1987, he was placed in the Adult Forensic Ward, a unit secluded from the others because it held higher-risk patients. When that unit closed because of budget constraints, he was eventually moved into the general population. On February 23, 1992, Uhlrig and another therapist took him and other patients off the grounds to see a movie. After they returned and the other patients had been dropped off, he attacked and killed her. In 1995 a federal appeals court called her murder "undeniably tragic" but ruled it was not the result of "conscience shocking" conduct by state administrators. Years later, in 2001, a psychologist at the hospital who had been sexually assaulted by a patient sued the hospital and the state for sexual harassment. A jury found that a sexually hostile work environment existed but split over whether the hospital was legally responsible, and an appeals court later sent the case back for further proceedings.

## Closing the Doors

The decline showed first on paper. In 1988 the hospital lost its accreditation to receive federal Medicare and Medicaid payments after the federal Health Care Financing Administration determined that the state had omitted two patients from an inspection-of-care review; the hospital appealed and lost. By the 1990s, mental health care was moving away from large hospitals toward community-based programs, and the Kansas Legislature decided to close one of its three mental hospitals, partly because the community model appeared effective, but mostly because it was cheaper. Topeka State Hospital was chosen. It closed on May 17, 1997. In June 2010, the historic center building and several others were torn down.

## Names Without Stones

After the hospital closed, the cemetery was assigned to the Kansas Department of Administration. For most of those buried there, no stone ever carried a name. That began to change during the Legislature's 1999-2000 session, when lawmakers authorized a memorial for the people in the cemetery: a plaque identifying the site, fencing around the grounds, and the inscription of the names of the dead. After decades in anonymous ground, the people buried there were to have their names inscribed where visitors could read them, each one remembered as a person.

## Flight Context

The former hospital grounds lie at approximately 39.06°N, 95.71°W on the west side of Topeka, along West 6th Street. Few original buildings remain, so the site is best identified relative to the city: the Kansas State Capitol dome stands about 2 nm to the east-southeast, and the Kansas River curves along the north side of the city. A viewing altitude of 2,500-3,500 feet AGL works well, but stay clear of airport traffic patterns. Philip Billard Municipal Airport (KTOP) lies about 4 nm to the east, just north of the river, and Topeka Regional Airport at Forbes Field (KFOE) is about 7 nm to the south-southeast. Kansas summers bring haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
