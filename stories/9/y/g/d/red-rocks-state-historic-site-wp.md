# Red Rocks State Historic Site

Red Rocks, the Emporia home built of Colorado sandstone where Pulitzer Prize-winning editor William Allen White lived from 1899 until 1944 and hosted five presidents, is now a National Historic Landmark and state historic site.

## Introduction

For a small-town newspaper editor's house, the guest list was extraordinary. William Allen White and his wife, Sallie, hosted five presidents at Red Rocks, their home on Exchange Street in Emporia, Kansas, Theodore Roosevelt among them. White owned and edited the Emporia Gazette, and from this modest prairie city he became an influential voice of small-town America, a Pulitzer Prize winner and adviser to presidents who earned the nickname "The Sage of Emporia." The house where he lived from 1899 until his death in 1944 is now the Red Rocks State Historic Site, a National Historic Landmark preserved by the Kansas Historical Society.

## Stone From the Garden of the Gods

The house began as someone else's unfinished dream. Judge Almerin Gillette started construction in 1887, sourcing the red stone for its exterior from the Garden of the Gods area in Colorado. Financial difficulties stopped him before he could complete it. In 1899, William Allen White and Sallie bought the unfinished shell and completed the interior, and the house came to be known as Red Rocks. The result is a distinctive blend. The first floor is finished in red sandstone, while the upper levels are red brick and stucco crossed with wood strips to resemble half-timbering, a Tudor Revival look that came with the 1920s renovation. It stands in a residential neighborhood northeast of downtown Emporia, at the southwest corner of Exchange Street and East 10th Avenue, looking every bit the comfortable home of a prosperous Midwestern family.

## The Editor Who Spoke for Small Towns

White and Sallie had moved to Emporia in 1895, when he bought the Gazette. A year later he made his name nationally with a fiery editorial, "What's the Matter with Kansas?," which attacked the Populist movement for its influence on the state and drew attention far beyond Kansas. Over the first three decades of the twentieth century, White played a central role in organizing the progressive elements of the Republican Party. In 1912 he helped organize Theodore Roosevelt's Bull Moose Party candidacy. His pen eventually earned him a Pulitzer Prize: his editorial "To an Anxious Friend," a statement in defense of free speech, won the prize in 1923. Through it all he kept his paper, and his home, in Emporia, speaking to the nation from a town of ordinary size.

## Fire, Grief and a House Next Door

The 1920s tested the family. In 1920 a fire gutted the upper floors of Red Rocks, and the Whites had them rebuilt to a plan by the Kansas City architects Wight & Wight. The following year brought a far deeper loss. The Whites' teenage daughter, Mary, died from injuries in a horseback riding accident, and White wrote a moving eulogy for her that ran in newspapers across the country. Family was always close at hand here. Beside the main house stands the Mother's House, built in 1903 or 1904 of brick and limestone, with Classical Revival styling on what is essentially an American Foursquare plan. White's mother, also named Mary, lived there until her death in 1924, a few steps from her son's front door.

## A Home That Stayed in the Family

The Whites' circle reached well beyond Kansas. Their connections extended to figures such as H. G. Wells and Albert Einstein, and their son, William Lindsay White, became a well-known radio broadcaster and later an editor of the Gazette himself. William Allen White died in Emporia on January 29, 1944, after completing a chapter of his autobiography about his daughter Mary's death. The house remained in the White family, and in its last years was held by the White Corporation, until 2001, when it was donated to the state. It had already been declared a National Historic Landmark in 1976, and in 2001 it became a state historic site. Today the grounds include the main house, the Mother's House and a small visitors center, preserving the place where a Kansas editor spent nearly half a century talking to America.
