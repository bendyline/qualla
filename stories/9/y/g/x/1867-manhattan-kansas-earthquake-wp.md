# 1867 Manhattan, Kansas earthquake

The strongest earthquake known to originate in Kansas shook the prairie near Manhattan on an April afternoon in 1867, stopping clocks, toppling chimneys and knocking horses off their feet, and the fault system behind it still shapes how engineers protect the Tuttle Creek Dam.

**Location:** 39.2000, -96.3000

**Categories:** earthquakes, natural-disasters, kansas, geology, 19th-century-history

## Introduction

On an April afternoon in 1867, horses in Louisville, Kansas, were knocked off their feet. In Leavenworth a man was thrown from a load of hay, and near Manhattan clocks stopped while frightened residents reported feeling electrical shocks. The earthquake struck on April 24, around 2:30 in the afternoon, with its epicenter near Manhattan and Wamego in the Kansas River valley, not far from where the Big Blue River joins the Kansas. Measured at 5.1 on a scale based on how widely it was felt, with other estimates running as high as 5.5, it remains the strongest earthquake known to originate in Kansas, a state few people associate with shaking ground.

## A Roar Like Thunder

The quake reached a maximum intensity of VII, "very strong," on the Mercalli scale. Minor damage spread across 22 Kansas counties: walls fractured, chimneys fell, and stones worked loose. Three miles south of Wamego the ground on a farm liquefied, and waves roughly two feet high rolled along the Kansas River. In Paola, the quake destroyed one wall of a large Republican newspaper office, and people who were standing were nearly knocked over. At Solomon, a Pacific Railroad train shook so violently that it was stopped and evacuated for fear its boiler would explode. The Chicago Tribune's report from Kansas City described homes jolted by a sudden burst and a resonating roar like thunder. An aftershock followed between 3 and 4 a.m. the next day.

## Town by Town

The reports read like a map of frontier Kansas. In Lawrence, three shocks came within 30 seconds, rattling doors, cracking plaster, knocking stones off a church and overturning a stove. Leavenworth also counted three shocks; saws leaning against walls slid six inches, and one woman felt an electrical shock from spring water. Buildings in Junction City moved several inches. In Topeka, the capital, the ceiling of a Methodist church was bent and nearly all the windows in one schoolhouse were destroyed. In Emporia, windows rattled, small boxes flew off shelves and people fled buildings, while brick and stone houses fared worse than frame ones. In Atchison, lamps and bottles fell at a drug store, and even though no buildings were damaged, people ran into the streets. Yet in Montgomery County, people riding in moving vehicles never felt a thing.

## Far Beyond Kansas

Earthquakes in the middle of the continent travel far. Kansas quakes tend to be shallow, and the region's stiff soil carries their energy across wide areas. The 1867 quake followed that pattern: estimates of its felt area range from about 95,000 to 300,000 square miles. In St. Joseph, Missouri, windows broke, women fainted and the brick walls of a new school cracked several feet above the ground. Dubuque, Iowa, felt three shocks that set gas burners vibrating and punched holes in brick walls, while in Des Moines buildings shook and people were rocked in their rocking chairs. The shaking was reported in Indiana and Illinois too. One dramatic report from Carthage, Ohio, claimed an acre of ground had sunk ten feet, but that account has been questioned.

## The Ridge Beneath the Prairie

Manhattan sits near the Nemaha Ridge, a long anticline bounded by several faults. Along its eastern edge runs the Humboldt Fault Zone, which has produced a large portion of the state's earthquakes. Later quakes have included another powerful one ten miles north of Manhattan in Pottawatomie County. A report in the Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America put the frequency of moderately strong Kansas earthquakes at one every 40 to 45 years, and more than 500 have been measured in the state since 2013. A 2016 U.S. Geological Survey one-year forecast put the chance of severe shaking at about 1% or lower anywhere in Kansas, though it gave a 5 to 12 percent chance of damaging shaking in southern Kansas and north-central Oklahoma, where wastewater injection has induced quakes.

## Guarding Tuttle Creek

Much of the modern concern centers on Tuttle Creek Dam, just north of Manhattan. The Humboldt Fault Zone lies about 12 miles east of Tuttle Creek Reservoir, and an earthquake there could cripple the dam, flooding the area below and threatening roughly 13,000 people and 5,900 homes. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers concluded that a quake between magnitude 5.7 and 6.6 "would cause sand underneath the dam to liquefy into quicksand, causing the dam to spread out and the top to drop up to three feet." The Kansas Geological Survey puts a magnitude 5.7 quake on that fault at about once in 1,800 years and a 6.6 at about once in 10,000. Rather than wait, the Corps strengthened the dam, building more than 350 underground cement walls through the sandy foundation, work completed around 2010, and equipping it with warning sensors.

## Flight Context

The listed epicentral coordinates are approximately 39.20°N, 96.30°W, in the Kansas River valley near Wamego, east of Manhattan. From 4,000-6,000 feet AGL the broad river valley is easy to follow east-west, and Tuttle Creek Lake stretches north from Manhattan along the Big Blue River, with Tuttle Creek Dam at its southern end. Manhattan Regional Airport (KMHK) lies about 18 nm to the west-southwest. Topeka's Philip Billard Municipal Airport (KTOP) is roughly 33 nm to the east-southeast. Marshall Army Airfield (KFRI) at Fort Riley lies farther west beyond Manhattan; check NOTAMs for military activity. The surrounding Flint Hills and river bottoms offer good visibility, though spring thunderstorms can build quickly.
