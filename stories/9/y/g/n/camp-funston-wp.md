# Camp Funston

Built almost overnight on Fort Riley in 1917 to train World War I infantry, Camp Funston recorded some of the first American cases of the 1918 influenza pandemic and later trained soldiers for Iraq and Afghanistan.

**Location:** 39.0962, -96.7265

**Categories:** Military facilities in Kansas, Spanish flu pandemic, World War I, Fort Riley, Conscientious objection

## Introduction

On March 4, 1918, Albert Gitchell, an army cook at Camp Funston, Kansas, was recorded as a case of influenza. He was not truly the first to fall ill, since there had been cases before him, but his is the name attached to that first case. Within days of that first case, 522 men at the camp had reported sick. Camp Funston had been built to prepare soldiers for one war in Europe. It became one of the places where some of the first recorded American cases of a worldwide influenza pandemic, the one known as the "Spanish flu", appeared.

## A City of Barracks

The camp rose fast. Construction began in the summer of 1917 on the grounds of Fort Riley, southwest of Manhattan, Kansas, and eventually spread across about 2,000 acres, with estimates of the number of buildings ranging from about 1,400 to as many as 4,000. It was one of sixteen camps established at the outbreak of World War I to train infantry divisions, and it was named for Brigadier General Frederick Funston, who had died in 1917. The 164th Depot Brigade administered the garrison, receiving, housing, equipping and training the enlistees and draftees who arrived; its commanders included George King Hunter. Two divisions under Major General Leonard Wood, nearly 50,000 recruits in all, trained here. Among the units were the 89th Division, which deployed to France in the spring of 1918, the 10th Division, and Black soldiers assigned to the 92nd Division.

## Men Who Would Not Wear the Uniform

Not everyone at Camp Funston had come to fight. The camp also served as a detention camp for conscientious objectors, many of them Mennonites. Hutterite communities faced a particular bind. Because military service was compulsory, they sent their young men to the camps, but their faith did not allow those men to obey military commands or put on a uniform. In a place designed to turn civilians into soldiers as quickly as possible, these young men held to their convictions, living inside the war machine while refusing to become part of it. Their presence is a reminder that the camp's story was not only one of drill and deployment, but also of the pressure the wartime draft placed on communities whose beliefs set them apart.

## The Flu and the Long Goodbye

The influenza cases that surfaced in March 1918 at Camp Funston are among the earliest recorded in the United States in what became a worldwide pandemic. The illness moved fast, with 522 men reporting sick within days of the first case on March 4. The war went on. After the Armistice of November 11, 1918, the 164th Depot Brigade, now commanded by William J. Glasgow, turned to the work of mustering soldiers out and processing their discharges. Then the camp itself wound down. In July 1921 the Army abandoned Camp Funston, salvaging most of its frame buildings, except those intended for the cavalry, while keeping the heating plant and waterworks in case of a future emergency. In December 1922, the Army sold off the remaining barracks.

## Discipline Without Walls

The name lived on. Until October 1992, Camp Funston was home to the United States Army Correctional Activity, formerly the U.S. Army Retraining Brigade. Its official mission was to prepare military prisoners for life as useful citizens with general discharges or, in a few select cases, for a return to duty. What set it apart was method. For most of a typical prisoner's stay, control came from military discipline rather than walls and bars. The brigade's doctrine held that a minimum-custody setting, combined with correctional treatment, education, and military and vocational training, best prepared first-time offenders for a crime-free life, and the system was asserted to be cheaper to build and run than a traditional prison. The camp even had a cinema open to Fort Riley residents beyond Camp Funston.

## Advisers for Distant Wars

In the twenty-first century, Camp Funston trained soldiers again, this time for Iraq and Afghanistan. Military transition teams had been prepared at several installations, most notably Fort Carson in Colorado, Camp Atterbury in Indiana, Fort Hood in Texas and Camp Shelby in Mississippi. In early 2006 the Army decided to consolidate all of that training at Fort Riley to standardize and improve it, and Camp Funston became the place where every team was trained. The first teams began on June 1, 2006, and that October the 1st Brigade, 1st Infantry Division, took over command of the mission, responsible for forming and training the teams. Later in 2009 the mission moved to Fort Polk, Louisiana, closing another chapter on ground that had first sent soldiers to war in 1917.

## Flight Context

Camp Funston lies on the Fort Riley military reservation at approximately 39.096°N, 96.727°W, southwest of Manhattan, Kansas. Fort Riley is an active Army post, so check charts and NOTAMs for military airspace and stay clear of active areas; from about 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, the post's layout spreads out between Manhattan and Junction City. Manhattan Regional Airport (KMHK) is about 4 nm northeast, and Marshall Army Air Field (KFRI), the Fort Riley airfield, is about 3 nm southwest; it is a military field, so civilian pilots should not plan to land there. Freeman Field at Junction City (FAA identifier 3JC; no ICAO code) is about 6 nm southwest.
