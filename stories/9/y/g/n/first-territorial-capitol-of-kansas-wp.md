# First Territorial Capitol of Kansas

The unfinished limestone building where Kansas Territory's proslavery 'bogus legislature' met for just five days in July 1855 is the last remnant of the vanished town of Pawnee, now a museum on Fort Riley.

**Location:** 39.0853, -96.7620

**Categories:** History museums in Kansas, Museums in Geary County, Kansas, Former state capitols in the United States, Kansas state historic sites, Government buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Kansas, National Register of Historic Places in Geary County, Kansas, Capitols of Kansas

## Introduction

When the first Kansas territorial legislature arrived in the town of Pawnee on July 2, 1855, its capitol was not ready. The new limestone building had neither floor nor roof. "The floors were not completed while we stayed," one participant recalled, "so we had to legislate with open doors." The lawmakers did not stay long. Two days after arriving, they voted to leave, and after only five days Pawnee's moment as the seat of government was over. The stone building they abandoned survived anyway. It is now the only remaining structure from the ghost town of Pawnee, standing on the grounds of Fort Riley.

## A Legislature Few Kansans Chose

Territorial Governor Andrew Reeder called the legislature to Pawnee, a town beside the Fort Riley military reservation, far from Missouri's proslavery influence. The stakes were enormous. This body was expected to help choose a permanent seat of government, shape a constitution and settle whether Kansas would be a free or slave state. But the election that produced it had been compromised. Its members were chosen on March 30, 1855, with the aid of Missourians who crossed the border to vote. Free-state partisans contested the results, and Reeder ordered new elections in some disputed districts, but proslavery partisans boycotted them, and most of the legislators who reached Pawnee were sympathetic to the proslavery cause. Antislavery Kansans soon had a name for the body, the "bogus legislature," and for the measures it would pass, the "bogus laws."

## Five Days at Pawnee

The building they met in was roughly 40 by 80 feet, built of native limestone and rising two stories. The thirteen-member council met upstairs and the twenty-six-member house met on the first floor, working amid unfinished floors and open doorways. The majority wasted little time leaving. On July 4, 1855, the legislature passed a bill establishing a temporary capital at the Shawnee Methodist Mission, much closer to the Missouri border. Governor Reeder vetoed it, but both houses promptly overrode his veto, and the legislature reconvened at Shawnee Mission on July 16. The capital later moved again, to Lecompton. Pawnee had served as the territory's capital for just five days, yet according to the Kansas Historical Society, the actions of the legislature that met here helped lead to the Civil War.

## Stone Walls That Outlived a Town

Pawnee itself did not last. The town was dismantled and absorbed into Fort Riley, and the former capitol was put to practical use. Its first floor housed the fort's commissary department, and over the years the building served as an Army warehouse among other roles. Its solid limestone walls outlasted every other structure in town, and interest in saving it grew in the early twentieth century. The Union Pacific Railroad ultimately contributed more than $20,000 toward a full restoration, which included acquiring historic furnishings and landscaping the grounds with flagstone walkways. By 1928 the building that had been abandoned in such haste was ready for a second debut.

## A Second Opening Day

That debut came on August 1, 1928, when the capitol became a history museum. The celebration drew about ten thousand people. Union Pacific Solicitor General Nelson H. Loomis dedicated the restored building as a memorial to those "who toiled and suffered" to build Kansas. Governor Ben Paulen reviewed National Guard troops, and barbecue was served to the crowd. Lawmakers in 1850s attire held a mock session, with Paulen reading Governor Reeder's message. A costumed man claiming to be from Missouri demanded a seat, and the performance then turned to a staged "Indian attack" outside, a piece of 1920s pageantry that cast Native people as frontier villains. In 1961, legislators met here for a real one-day session to mark the state's centennial. Today the museum's exhibits cover Kansas Territory, rail and river travel and the history of Pawnee. Because it sits on an active Army base, visitors need photo identification, and the museum is open seasonally, from April to early October.

## Flight Context

The First Territorial Capitol sits at 39.0853°N, 96.762°W on the Fort Riley military reservation in Geary County, Kansas, in the Kansas River valley between Junction City and Manhattan. Marshall Army Airfield (KFRI) lies only about 2 miles to the south, and Manhattan Regional Airport (KMHK) about 6 miles to the northeast. Fort Riley is an active Army installation with restricted airspace over its training ranges, so check charts and NOTAMs carefully and stay clear of military operations; a pass along the valley at 2,500–3,000 feet AGL, with Milford Lake visible to the west, is the best way to take in the setting. Expect gusty winds and strong spring thunderstorms typical of the central Plains.
