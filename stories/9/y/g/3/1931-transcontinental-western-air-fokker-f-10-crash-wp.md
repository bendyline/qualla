# 1931 Transcontinental & Western Air Fokker F-10 crash

When a wooden wing failed over the Kansas Flint Hills in 1931, it killed Knute Rockne and seven others, and the public grief that followed forced the U.S. government to open its crash investigations to the public.

**Location:** 38.2358, -96.5867

**Categories:** aviation-history, disasters, kansas, flint-hills, sports-history

## Introduction

Knute Rockne's stop in Kansas City was a father's visit. His two eldest sons, Bill and Knute Jr., were at boarding school there at Pembroke Hill, and the famed Notre Dame football coach had paused to see them before heading west. On March 31, 1931, he boarded a Transcontinental and Western Air Fokker F-10, a three-engine airliner scheduled from Kansas City Municipal Airport to Los Angeles with a stop in Wichita. It never finished that first leg. A few miles southwest of Bazaar, Kansas, in the Flint Hills of Chase County, the wooden structure of one wing failed. The airliner went down, and all eight people on board were killed.

## Moisture in the Wood

The Fokker's wing was built of wood, and wood and glue do not forgive water. The investigation found that the wing had grown moist over time, and that the moisture weakened the glue connecting the wing to the fuselage until the wing separated. However the final moments unfolded, the structural condition of that wooden wing is widely agreed to have been at least a significant contributory factor. The finding pointed at something unsettling for anyone who had ever bought an airline ticket: a weakness that developed slowly, inside the aircraft's own structure, where passengers could never see it.

## A National Loss

Rockne's sudden death startled the country. The outpouring of grief was compared to the mourning that follows the death of a president, and President Herbert Hoover himself called it "a national loss." Thousands of people from around the world gathered for the funeral. Seven others died on that airplane too, and the memorial that stands at the crash site honors every one of them. Newspapers across the nation told the story at length, and it refused to fade. Driven by public feeling for Rockne, the coverage gradually turned into a national demand for a public inquiry into the causes and circumstances of the crash.

## Opening the Files

The first consequences were practical. All Fokker Trimotors in U.S. airline service were temporarily grounded, and from then on they had to undergo more frequent and rigorous inspections and maintenance. That expense, compounded by the bad publicity of Rockne's death, almost sank TWA, while Fokker suffered a serious blow to its reputation and sales. The more lasting change came inside the Aeronautics Branch of the U.S. Department of Commerce, forerunner of the Federal Aviation Administration. Its policy had been to keep the results of crash investigations secret. Intense public interest in the Rockne crash forced it to abandon that policy, and a new expectation took hold: the government would review crashes objectively and release its findings. Those public reports began to pinpoint and publicize blame, and in doing so they pushed both government and industry toward safer flying.

## Metal Takes the Sky

The crash's influence reached the drawing boards as well. Boeing developed the 247, often called the first truly modern airliner, with features that later became standard: a hollow-shell, all-aluminum monocoque structure, retractable landing gear, streamlining NACA engine cowlings, supercharged engines, de-icing equipment, and enough reserve power to take off fully loaded on a single engine. Douglas then produced the DC-2, developed after the Rockne crash to give TWA an all-metal replacement for its wooden Fokkers, which carried the 247's advances further with greater speed, range and payload. Its fuselage was rounded out into the wider, 21-seat DC-3, widely described as the first airliner to truly make airlines profitable. Within a few years, the question raised by a moisture-weakened wooden wing in Kansas had been answered in metal.

## The Boy Who Kept the Stone

James Easter Heathman was 13 years old in 1931, and he was one of the first people to reach the wreckage. For many years afterward he maintained the memorial at the crash site, a tall, engraved stone marker topped with the name "Rockne" and enclosed by a wire fence on wooden posts. Heathman died in 2008. The site is now part of the Heathman family estate, on private land off the road, open only by arrangement with the landowners or during commemorations. Every five years since the crash, a ceremony has been held at the memorial and a nearby schoolhouse, drawing victims' relatives and Rockne and Notre Dame fans from around the world. On the 80th anniversary in 2011, more than 150 people came, including former Football Hall of Fame director Bernie Kish. There were speeches and a bagpipe, and at the exact minute of the crash a small plane passed overhead. Travelers who cannot reach the site can stop at the Kansas Turnpike's Matfield Green service area, about eight miles away, where a third Rockne memorial was dedicated in 2019.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies at approximately 38.24°N, 96.59°W, a few miles southwest of the small community of Bazaar in Chase County, Kansas, amid the open grassland of the Flint Hills. The memorial itself is on private land and is too small to pick out from the air; an altitude of 3,000-4,500 feet AGL gives a good view of the rolling, largely treeless hills. The Kansas Turnpike runs nearby, with its Matfield Green service area about eight miles from the site. Emporia Municipal Airport (KEMP) is roughly 20 nm to the northeast. The doomed flight departed from what is now Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) in Kansas City and was bound for Wichita, whose Wichita Dwight D. Eisenhower National Airport (KICT) lies about 50 nm to the southwest. Spring and summer thunderstorms build quickly over the Flint Hills; check convective forecasts.
