# Battle of Turkey Springs

At a waterhole near the Kansas line in September 1878, Northern Cheyenne families fleeing hunger and disease on an Indian Territory reservation fought off a U.S. cavalry force in what is often called the last battle between Native Americans and the Army in Oklahoma.

**Location:** 36.9000, -99.0000

**Categories:** Cheyenne, Battles involving the Cheyenne, Forced migrations of Native Americans in the United States, Native American history of Oklahoma, Woods County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

On September 13, 1878, an Arapaho scout named Chalk, also called Ghost Man, rode toward a hilltop in northwestern Indian Territory with a message for two Cheyenne chiefs, Dull Knife and Little Wolf: return to the reservation. The chiefs declined. Behind them were some 350 Northern Cheyenne men, women and children trying to return home to the northern Great Plains, and in front of them was a unit of the U.S. Army. What followed at the waterhole called Turkey Springs is often described as the last battle between Native Americans and the United States Army in what is now Oklahoma.

## A Year in the South

The road to Turkey Springs began in defeat. In November 1876 the Dull Knife and Little Wolf bands of the Northern Cheyenne lost the Dull Knife Fight to the U.S. Cavalry, and the chiefs, Dull Knife also known as Morning Star, surrendered with their followers at Camp Robinson, Nebraska. From May to August 1877, soldiers escorted them south to the Southern Cheyenne reservation near Fort Reno, about 50 kilometers west of present-day Oklahoma City. Nine hundred and thirty-seven Cheyenne arrived. Life there was hard, with shortages of food and outbreaks of measles and malaria. Dull Knife and Little Wolf pleaded to be allowed to return north and were turned down. In September 1878 the two leaders and about 350 followers left the reservation anyway, hoping to rejoin Northern Cheyenne relatives in Montana. Ninety-two of them were warriors; the rest were women, children and elderly people.

## Two Days at the Waterhole

On September 10, Captain Joseph Rendlebrock, a veteran soldier, left Fort Reno with 85 officers and men and two Arapaho scouts. The Cheyenne were well mounted, and he reported that he hoped to catch them near the Arkansas River by Dodge City, Kansas. Instead he found them waiting at Turkey Springs, in hilly country near the Kansas border, where they had killed two cowboys nearby. After Chalk's message was refused on the 13th, Cheyenne warriors began moving around the soldiers' flanks and the shooting started. Firing stayed sparse because the Cheyenne were saving their limited ammunition, but it went on all day; two soldiers were killed and three wounded. After dark, seven desperately thirsty soldiers tried and failed to reach the springs. By morning the Cheyenne had surrounded the troops, and Rendlebrock ordered a retreat through ravines under fire from above. Another soldier died before the column reached the Cimarron River. Five Cheyenne were wounded. Rendlebrock was later court-martialed for the disorderly retreat.

## The Long Road North

The day after the battle, the Cheyenne divided into several groups to confuse anyone tracking them and pressed north. Their route crossed Kansas, where they camped and rested at the Big Basin in Clark County, won several more fights with the Army, including the Battle of Punished Woman's Fork, and killed seven cowboys in southern Kansas. In all, accounts differ widely on how many soldiers and civilians took the field to capture them, from roughly 1,300 to as many as 13,000. In Nebraska the people split in two. Little Wolf led one half to join the Northern Cheyenne in Montana. Dull Knife's group tried to reach the Sioux in South Dakota but was captured and imprisoned at Camp Robinson, the same post where the chiefs had surrendered after the Dull Knife Fight. In bitterly cold January 1879, 149 Cheyenne broke out and fled west; nearly all were killed or recaptured. In the end, the survivors of the Northern Cheyenne Exodus were allowed to remain in their homeland on the northern plains.

## Flight Context

The battle area lies at approximately 36.90°N, 99.00°W in Woods County, Oklahoma, in hilly country roughly 7 miles south of the Kansas state line, with the Cimarron River not far to the south. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, low morning or evening light brings out the relief of the hills and ravines. Alva Regional Airport (KAVK) is about 20 miles to the east-southeast. Expect gusty winds and spring and summer thunderstorms typical of northwestern Oklahoma.
