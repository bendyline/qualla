# Greensburg tornado

On May 4, 2007, the first tornado ever rated EF5 on the Enhanced Fujita scale destroyed most of Greensburg, Kansas, and killed 11 people, and the town chose to rebuild itself as a showcase of green design.

**Location:** 37.6042, -99.2931

**Categories:** Tornadoes in Kansas, Tornadoes of 2007, F5, EF5 and IF5 tornadoes, Kiowa County, Kansas, Green building

## Introduction

Of everything in Greensburg, Kansas, on the evening of May 4, 2007, the landmark best built to survive was a hole in the ground. The hand-dug Big Well, 109 feet deep, came through the night intact. The water tower and gift shop above it did not, and neither did most of the town around it. When a tornado 1.7 miles wide had finished with Greensburg, 95 percent of the city was damaged or destroyed, and 11 people would die. It was the first tornado ever rated EF5 on the Enhanced Fujita scale, and the town's response would draw eco-tourists from around the world.

## A Storm That Kept Growing

The most intense supercells of a central U.S. severe weather outbreak formed that evening across northwestern Oklahoma and southwestern Kansas. Near the state line, south and southeast of Dodge City, storms began developing around 7:30 p.m. and soon merged into one large supercell that would produce 22 tornadoes over about eight hours. The Greensburg tornado touched down south of town at about 9:03 p.m. and strengthened rapidly as it moved north, widening as it went. In the countryside it destroyed oil tanks and strewed oil across pastures and a road. A University of Massachusetts team tracking the storm with mobile radar detected at least five satellite tornadoes around the main funnel, the largest about 1.85 miles to its southeast. South of Greensburg the tornado reached its full width of 1.7 miles. At 9:41 p.m., the National Weather Service office in Dodge City issued a tornado emergency: "a violent tornado was on a direct path for portions of Greensburg this is an emergency situation for Greensburg."

## Minutes That Mattered

Warnings reached the public up to 39 minutes before the tornado struck, and the upgrade to a rare tornado emergency came 10 to 12 minutes ahead, after storm chaser Lance Ferguson told the Weather Service that a large tornado was on the ground near Greensburg. Some residents later told NBC News that the warnings saved their lives by giving them time to reach shelter. A later federal damage survey found that the town had no public tornado shelters; people rode out the storm in other shelters and cellars. By the most widely cited count, 11 people died in Greensburg, though published tallies range from 10 to 13. One of the victims, a police officer, died later in a hospital. With Kiowa County Memorial Hospital destroyed, about 90 people were taken to hospitals in Dodge City, Pratt, Kinsley and Wichita within the first day. Visiting afterward, President George W. Bush put it simply: "Fortunately, a lot of folks had basements here in this part of the world and lived to see another day. Unfortunately, too many died."

## A Town Undone

The tornado entered Greensburg from the south and, still at EF4 strength, leveled a section of Delmer Day Elementary School. Downtown was devastated: City Hall, Fleener's Furniture, Dillon's grocery, Sutton's True Value hardware, a Daylight Donuts and the local bank were all heavily damaged or destroyed. Three schools were lost, and the power went out. Trees were stripped of their bark, 53 houses slid off their foundations, and seven homes received EF5 damage. A large grain elevator on the north side was one of the few structures left standing. Beyond the city limits, the tornado curled northwest in a counterclockwise arc and died in a farm field at 10:05 p.m., after one hour and two minutes on the ground and a track of 28.8 miles. Meteorologist Howard Bluestein described it as "likely the most intense since the tornado that struck Oklahoma City and Moore, Oklahoma, on 3 May 1999."

## Life in Femaville

Recovery began among the ruins. FEMA set up 200 temporary housing units in rows, and 500 of the 750 residents who stayed in town lived there for up to eighteen months, in a camp some called "Femaville." About 500 National Guard soldiers arrived, and 7,604 volunteers registered through AmeriCorps logged 57,786 hours. By April 2008 around 800,000 cubic yards of debris had been hauled away. Classes moved into FEMA trailers, because, as superintendent Darin Headrick explained, "if we didn't have a school in the city as quickly as possible, people would not have a reason to move back." Hospital workers decided to rebuild within 24 hours of losing their building.

## Rebuilding Green

Governor Kathleen Sebelius said she wanted Greensburg to become "the greenest city in the state," and a federal long-term recovery plan set out to make it so, requiring buildings in the city limits to meet LEED Platinum standards and backing a wind farm of ten 1.25-megawatt turbines. The 5.4.7 Arts Center, named for the date of the storm, opened in 2008 as the first LEED Platinum building in Kansas, built by students with wood siding salvaged from a World War II ammunition plant. The rebuilt Kiowa County Memorial Hospital became the first hospital in the United States to achieve carbon neutrality, and a new countywide school opened in 2010, partly built with salvaged bricks. The Big Well reopened in 2012 inside a circular museum telling the town's story before and after the storm. Recovery has not been simple, and higher home prices have made new residents hard to attract, but visitors still come to see the town that rebuilt itself green.

## Flight Context

Greensburg lies at approximately 37.60°N, 99.29°W in Kiowa County, Kansas, on the open High Plains. From 2,000-3,500 feet AGL, the rebuilt town's grid stands out against surrounding farmland, and the damage track once ran north through the city from open country to the south. Pratt Regional Airport (KPTT) is about 30 miles to the east-northeast, and Dodge City Regional Airport (KDDC) about 39 miles west-northwest. Spring is peak severe-weather season here; stay well clear of supercell thunderstorms.
