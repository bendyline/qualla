# Big Well (Kansas)

Dug by hand in the 1880s to water the railroads, Greensburg's Big Well survived the 2007 tornado that flattened most of the town and now anchors a museum that tells both stories.

**Location:** 37.6056, -99.2936

**Categories:** Water wells in the United States, Museums in Kiowa County, Kansas, National Register of Historic Places in Kiowa County, Kansas, History of Kansas, Tornadoes

## Introduction

Most wells are something you peer into. In Greensburg, Kansas, you pay a fee and walk down into one, following an illuminated stairway to the bottom of a shaft that crews dug by hand in the 1880s. The Big Well has collected a shelf of honors. It joined the National Register of Historic Places as the Greensburg Well in 1972, the year it was also reportedly designated a National Museum; the American Water Works Association named it an American Water Landmark in 1973; and in 2008 it was chosen as one of the 8 Wonders of Kansas. Yet the chapter that reshaped it came not from underground but from the sky.

## Picks, Ropes and Barrels

Construction began in 1887, at a cost of $45,000, to supply water for the Santa Fe and Rock Island railroads, and the work was finished in 1888. The tools were the tools of the age. According to the Kansas Sampler Foundation, crews of twelve to fifteen men worked with pickaxes, shovels, ropes, pulleys and barrels. The stones that line the shaft came from the Medicine River, roughly twelve miles south of town, hauled in by wagon. Even the spoil had a purpose. The dirt coming out of the hole was carted off in slatted wagons, and whenever a driver reached a low spot, he opened the slats and let the load fall through, leveling the ground around the site as the well went deeper.

## Braced Every Twelve Feet

Digging a wide shaft into Kansas soil was dangerous work, and the crews protected themselves as they went. Every twelve feet, the shaft was cribbed and braced with two-by-twelve planks to keep the walls from closing in on the men below. Using those braces, they hoisted soil to the surface in barrels and kept digging. Once the stone lining was fitted around the bracing, the planks were sawed away. At roughly 109 feet down, the diggers drove perforated pipe horizontally into a layer of water-bearing gravel, drawing water into the basin. The Big Well served as Greensburg's municipal water supply until 1932.

## The Night of May 4

Around 9:03 on the evening of May 4, 2007, a tornado touched down in open country south of Greensburg and reached the town roughly 45 to 50 minutes later. It was the first rated EF5 on the Enhanced Fujita scale, which the United States had adopted that February, and at its widest it measured 3,000 yards, about 1.7 miles across. Approximately 95 percent of the city was damaged or destroyed, and sources count between ten and thirteen people killed. The Big Well's visitor center was among the buildings lost. Three days later came word that its prize exhibit was missing: the Brenham pallasite, a half-ton meteorite recovered from the area and insured for $1 million. It turned up beneath a collapsed wall. While Greensburg rebuilt, the meteorite went on temporary display at the Sternberg Museum of Natural History in Hays.

## A Museum of the Storm

The well reopened on May 26, 2012, inside a new visitor center known as the Big Well Museum, and the building now tells the town's whole arc. A circular timeline runs through three stages: the beginnings of Greensburg, the tornado, and the eco-friendly rebuilding that followed, after Kansas governor Kathleen Sebelius said she wanted Greensburg to be "the greenest city in the state." Exhibits explain how tornadoes form, with interactive pull-outs, screens, cards and infographics covering interviews, survival kits and loss. Storm debris is part of the display: stop signs, street signs, clocks and warning sirens, including the remains of the Federal Signal Thunderbolt siren the tornado destroyed. A Sentry 10v took its place during the rebuilding. The meteorite came home, too. It was once billed as the world's largest single-piece pallasite, a title other specimens actually hold, but in Greensburg it carries a different distinction: it survived.
