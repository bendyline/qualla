# Choctaw Capitol Building

Built in 1884 at Tushka Homma, the "home of the red warrior," the Choctaw Nation's red-brick capitol fell silent as federal law dissolved tribal government before Oklahoma statehood and has returned to life as the national museum of a reconstituted nation.

**Location:** 34.6417, -95.2786

**Categories:** Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma, Native American museums in Oklahoma, Government buildings completed in 1884, Buildings and structures in Pushmataha County, Oklahoma, National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma

## Introduction

In the fall of 1884, a newspaper in Muskogee made a bold claim. "The capitol building is the finest structure in the Territory," the Indian Journal declared. It was describing the new seat of government of the Choctaw Nation, a two-story building of red native brick and sandstone standing two miles north of present-day Tuskahoma, in what is now Pushmataha County, Oklahoma. For the next two decades, the Choctaw Senate, House, Principal Chief and Supreme Court would all do their work under its mansard roof.

## Home of the Red Warrior

The Choctaw came to this building after decades of constitutional experimentation, during which they moved their national capital among several locations. In 1883 the National Council authorized construction of a permanent seat of government at a place called Tushka Homma. The name means "home of the red warrior" in the Choctaw language, and its spelling has since been standardized as Tuskahoma. An older name survives in postal records: from February 6, 1872, to June 30, 1880, a U.S. post office operated here under the name Council House, Indian Territory. Postal operations later moved to nearby Lyceum, site of the Choctaw girls' academy, and to Tuskahoma. In the days of Indian Territory, the Council House stood in Wade County, Choctaw Nation.

## Brick, Sandstone and Local Timber

The capitol was completed in September 1884, built of red native brick, sandstone and timber from nearby, at a cost of about $25,000. It measures 72 feet 11 inches long and 62 feet 4 inches wide, and it rises 54 feet 2 inches to the tops of its chimneys, with two full stories and an attic under a mansard roof. Inside, the building held the full machinery of a nation. There were chambers for the Senate and the House of Representatives, rooms for the Principal Chief and the Supreme Court, and offices for constitutional officers, including the National Attorney and the National Auditor. For a people who had rebuilt their government in Indian Territory, it was a statement in brick: permanent, formal and their own.

## The Custodian

The capitol served as the seat of Choctaw government until the last council convened there in 1905, shortly before Oklahoma became a state in 1907. Congress had already acted: the Curtis Act of 1898 required the dissolution of tribal governments before statehood would be granted. With annexation, the chambers emptied. The building fell into disuse and disrepair, a government house without a government. One person stayed with it. Jane Austin McCurtain was appointed custodian of the Council House, and she kept that role until her death in 1924, watching over the building through the years when the nation it was built for no longer officially existed.

## A Nation's Museum

The story did not end there. The Choctaw Nation was successfully reconstituted, and while its executive offices are now in Durant, Oklahoma, the old capitol has found new life as the nation's national museum. Every year the Choctaw Nation holds its Labor Day festival on the grounds, drawing nationally known country-western singers and bands and tens of thousands of attendees. A Choctaw war veterans' memorial stands on the capitol grounds, with a special section honoring the Choctaw Code Talkers, who pioneered the use of Native American languages as military code. Their first exploits came in World War I, and Choctaws and other tribes repeated them in World War II. In May 2017 the tribe added a Ten Commandments monument inscribed in both Choctaw and English. The capitol is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.
