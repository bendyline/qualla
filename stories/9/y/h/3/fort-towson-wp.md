# Fort Towson

Founded in 1824 on the Red River frontier, Fort Towson watched over the Choctaw Nation's arrival after removal, served as a Confederate headquarters, and saw Stand Watie, the last Confederate general to lay down arms, surrender nearby in 1865.

**Location:** 34.0278, -95.2564

**Categories:** forts, oklahoma, choctaw-nation, indian-territory, civil-war, historic-sites

## Introduction

Every building at the second Fort Towson was painted white. Three log officers' quarters, a story and a half tall on three-foot limestone foundations, stood along the bluffs of Gates Creek with their covered porches facing south over the parade ground. Barracks and offices lined the sides of a half-acre rectangle. It was a tidy, deliberate place, built near the meeting of the Kiamichi and Red rivers in what is now Choctaw County, Oklahoma. It was also a place bound up with some of the hardest chapters in the history of Indian Territory.

## Tents on the Border

The first post hardly deserved the name fort. Colonel Matthew Arbuckle established it in May 1824 on the southern edge of Indian Territory, and it was called Cantonment Towson after Nathan Towson, Paymaster General of the Army. It was a border outpost in the years when Texas, across the Red River, was Mexican territory. Meant only as a temporary station, it had nothing but tents and a few wooden shacks. In the 1820s a military trace, a wagon road, was built to connect it to forts in Arkansas. The Army abandoned the cantonment in April 1829 and moved the garrison to Fort Jesup.

## The Choctaw Arrive

The Army came back because of removal. In November 1830 it ordered a permanent fort built, since the region had been assigned to the Choctaw, whom the U.S. government was forcing from their homeland in what became Mississippi under the Indian Removal Act. A new site was chosen about six miles from the old one. The post was first called Camp Phoenix, established to protect the Choctaw Nation, and it was renamed Fort Towson in 1831. As displaced families arrived in the West, the fort served as a point of dispersal. A settlement grew up around it, and nearby Doaksville, which began as a trading post, became a thriving commercial center and, at times, the seat of Choctaw government.

## Life Inside the Rectangle

The new fort was far more substantial than the old camp. Four one-story buildings faced each other along its sides, set on high foundations that effectively created basements, where the kitchens and dining halls were. The two closest to the officers' quarters held sub-officers' rooms, the quartermaster's office, a school room and an amusement parlor; the other two were barracks for common soldiers. A hospital stood about 250 feet to the east. Stables, shops and gardens lay outside the rectangle on that side, while the sutler's store, the dairy and the poultry yards stood to the west. About 300 yards farther west was the cemetery. After Fort Washita was built 70 miles to the west in 1842, Fort Towson lost importance. It was garrisoned until June 1854, when it was turned over to the Choctaw Indian Agency.

## Storm, Fire and War

Misfortune followed the soldiers' departure. A storm that same year blew the roofs off several buildings, and a few years later a fire destroyed everything except one barracks and the hospital. When the Civil War began, the Confederacy took over what remained. It had allied with the Choctaw, and its 1861 treaty with the Choctaw and Chickasaw offered them a seat in the Confederate House and the option of statehood within the Confederacy. The fort became the headquarters of Confederate General Samuel B. Maxey. Among the last Confederate forces still in the field were those commanded by General Stand Watie, a Cherokee and a principal chief of his nation, and on June 23, 1865, Watie surrendered his command near Fort Towson, the last Confederate general to lay down his arms.

## Ruins and Return

The post was abandoned at the close of the war. The soldiers buried in its cemetery were moved to Fort Gibson, the buildings fell into ruin, and local residents hauled away foundation stones for use in other buildings. In the 1960s the Oklahoma Historical Society acquired the property and began restoring it. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1970 and is now an Oklahoma Historic Site. Visitors today find a visitor center, a reconstructed sutler's store, a teamster's cabin and a walking trail through the ruins of the fort on the bluff.

## Flight Context

Fort Towson Historic Site lies at approximately 34.03°N, 95.26°W, a few miles east of the town of Fort Towson in Choctaw County, Oklahoma, near where the Kiamichi River meets the Red River, the Texas-Oklahoma border. The ruins are hard to pick out from the air, so use the Red River and Kiamichi valleys as references; 2,500-3,500 feet AGL gives a good view of the river bottoms. Stan Stamper Municipal Airport (KHHW) at Hugo is about 14 nm to the west. Cox Field (KPRX) near Paris, Texas, lies about 25 nm to the south-southwest across the Red River. Southeastern Oklahoma is humid, with haze common in summer and thunderstorms in spring.
