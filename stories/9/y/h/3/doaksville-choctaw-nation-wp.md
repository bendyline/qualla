# Doaksville, Choctaw Nation

Founded by Choctaw families forced west from their homelands, Doaksville became a Red River trading town and at times the seat of Choctaw government, witnessed the last surrender of a Confederate general in the field, and then vanished when the railroad passed it by.

**Location:** 34.0336, -95.2728

**Categories:** Geography of Choctaw County, Oklahoma, Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma, Historic districts in Oklahoma, Ghost towns in Oklahoma, History of Indian Territory, Populated places established in 1824, Populated places disestablished in 1903, Archaeological sites in Oklahoma

## Introduction

The last Confederate general in the field did not lay down his arms in Virginia or Georgia. Stand Watie, a Cherokee officer, surrendered on June 23, 1865, in Doaksville, a Choctaw town near the Red River in what is now southeastern Oklahoma. Today there is almost nothing there to see. Doaksville is a ghost town, its streets reduced to an archaeological preservation site in Choctaw County, and the story it holds begins not with a war but with an exile.

## A Town Built by Exiles

Doaksville was founded sometime between 1824 and 1831 by Choctaw people who had been forced from their homes in the Southeastern United States by the federal government and made to resettle in the region it set aside for them in Indian Territory. They arrived having lost their homelands, and they built again anyway. The settlement took its name from Josiah Stuart Doak, co-owner of the local trading post. Josiah and his brother first set up their post at the mouth of the Kiamichi River. After the U.S. Army established Fort Towson in 1824, they moved it to a site one mile west of what is now the Fort Towson-Doaksville Cemetery. The real growth began in 1831, when the Army reactivated Fort Towson just across the creek to the east, and the trading post began to grow into a town.

## River Town and Capital

Water made Doaksville matter. Steamboats could reach it on the Red River, and that access made it one of the principal towns of the Choctaw Nation. By 1840 it had several stores, a gristmill, a blacksmith and a hotel, the everyday machinery of a frontier market town. It also became a place where nations did business with one another. In 1837, the Chickasaws and the Choctaws signed the Treaty of Doaksville here, an agreement that allowed the Chickasaw Nation to lease the western part of the Choctaw Nation for settlement. From 1850 Doaksville intermittently served as the seat of Choctaw government, and from 1860 to 1863, during the Civil War, it was the regular meeting place of Choctaw leaders. Its fortunes, though, were tied closely to the fort next door. The Oklahoma Historical Society dates the start of the town's decline to 1854, the year the Army abandoned Fort Towson.

## The Last Surrender

By the summer of 1865, the Confederacy had collapsed. Robert E. Lee had surrendered at Appomattox that April, and other commands had followed, yet in Indian Territory one Confederate brigadier general was still in the field. Stand Watie, a Cherokee, commanded the First Indian Brigade of the Army of the Trans-Mississippi. Even after General Edmund Kirby Smith surrendered the Trans-Mississippi Department's troops at Galveston on June 2, word of which took weeks to reach Indian Territory, Watie held out. On June 23, 1865, he finally accepted that the war was over and surrendered his command to Union Lieutenant Colonel Asa C. Matthews at Doaksville. With that signature, the last Confederate general still in the field gave up the fight, more than two months after Appomattox, in a Choctaw town far from the battlefields most Americans remember.

## The Train That Didn't Stop

Peace brought little recovery. The war had destroyed the plantation economy of the surrounding country, an economy that here, as across the South, had depended on the labor of enslaved people. Doaksville declined, and the final blow arrived in the form of a railroad line that bypassed the town entirely. The Oklahoma Historical Society dates that bypass to 1870, though the town of Fort Towson, where the businesses then moved, was not established until 1902. Most businesses packed up and moved to Fort Towson, which sat on the rail line. The shift was nearly total. Doaksville, which had once welcomed steamboats and hosted treaty makers, emptied out, and by 1903 it had ceased to exist as a community. What remains now lies underground and in the records, preserved as an archaeological site, along with the cemetery that still bears the names of both the fort and the town.

## Flight Context

The Doaksville site lies at approximately 34.04°N, 95.27°W in Choctaw County, Oklahoma, a mile or two west of the Fort Towson Historic Site, just outside the present-day town of Fort Towson and several miles north of the Red River, which forms the Texas border. Little of the old town is visible from the air, so use the Red River and the town of Fort Towson as references from about 2,000-3,000 feet AGL. Stan Stamper Municipal Airport (KHHW) in Hugo is about 13 nm west, and Cox Field (KPRX) in Paris, Texas, is about 25 nm south-southwest. Summer thunderstorms build quickly over the Red River valley.
