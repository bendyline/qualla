# Wheelock Academy

Founded in 1832 by a missionary couple who had traveled west with the Choctaw on their removal, Wheelock Academy became the first Choctaw national academy and outlasted war, fire and the federal push to close tribal schools until 1955.

**Location:** 33.9939, -94.9883

**Categories:** Choctaw, Native American schools in Oklahoma, National Historic Landmarks in Oklahoma, Defunct schools in Oklahoma, Buildings and structures in McCurtain County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

Alfred and Harriet Wright did not come to Indian Territory as strangers. They traveled with the Choctaw when the U.S. government expelled the tribe from its homeland in the southeastern United States and forced it to emigrate west. In 1832, near what is now Millerton in McCurtain County, Oklahoma, Alfred Wright, a physician and Presbyterian missionary, started a school for Choctaw girls. Wheelock Academy would go on to serve Choctaw students for more than 120 years, and the Choctaw Nation still owns it today.

## A Borrowed Name

Wright named the school after Eleazar Wheelock, founder of Moor's Indian School, the institution later known as Dartmouth College. He also co-founded the nearby Wheelock Church. The school gained a reputation quickly. Within a year, the Superintendent of the Choctaw Agency reported that Wheelock Academy had become a model for Indian education, and it would serve as the model for the academies of the tribes then known as the Five Civilized Tribes. In 1839 Wright expanded it with a large dormitory for boarding students, and in 1842 the school he founded became the first Choctaw national academy. For a nation rebuilding its life after removal, Wheelock was a place where Choctaw girls could be educated in their new country.

## War, Fire and a Second Beginning

The Civil War shut Wheelock down. The American Board of Missions ordered Rev. Edwards to close the school and the church and return to the North. Yet the school did not simply disappear. A woman who had been a Wheelock student before the war and her husband maintained the buildings and kept the facility running as a day school, until a fire in 1869 destroyed many of the buildings. Even then, classes resumed in the less damaged ones. Between 1880 and 1884, the Choctaw Nation rebuilt the academy with help from the Southern Presbyterian Church. The Choctaw Council created a National School Board in 1882, and beginning in 1890 the school operated as a contract school, run on tribal funds with staff and superintendents provided by the American Board of Missionaries. Presbyterian mission boards and the federal government grew involved, but Wheelock remained owned and financially supported by the Choctaw Nation.

## The Long Closing

The end came from Washington. The Curtis Act of 1898 required the gradual closure of all tribal schools, along with the dissolution of tribal governments, before statehood would be granted. One by one, the Choctaw schools disappeared, and by 1930 only Wheelock and Jones Academy in Hartshorne, Oklahoma, remained. In 1932 Wheelock became a United States Indian School. In 1955 its functions were merged into Jones Academy, which became coeducational, and the Wheelock site closed for good after more than 120 years. Recognition followed the closure. Wheelock was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1965 and added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1966. Today the site, a few miles east of Millerton, belongs to the Choctaw Nation, which keeps a museum there and has begun environmental assessments and cleanup planning for the campus.

## Paint, Roofs and a Hope for a College

Preserving the old academy has been a long effort. In 1999 a news program reported that Delton Cox, treasurer of the Choctaw Nation, was leading a project to restore it. By then, all of the remaining buildings had been painted and reroofed at a cost of $70,000, and complete restoration was estimated at $3 million. Cox said the Choctaw Nation would like to turn the restored campus into a college, which would be the first tribal-owned college in Oklahoma. The need was real. A 2001 report to Congress, National Historic Landmarks at the Millennium, listed Wheelock Academy among the "Threatened Landmarks in America," citing deterioration, looting and vandalism. The school that began in the aftermath of one uprooting now depends on the Choctaw Nation to keep its story standing.

## Flight Context

Wheelock Academy lies a few miles east of Millerton in McCurtain County, southeastern Oklahoma, at approximately 33.994°N, 94.988°W. The historic campus is small and set in rural country, so descend to about 1,500-2,000 feet AGL to look for its cluster of old buildings. McCurtain County Regional Airport at Idabel is about 8 nm southeast (FAA identifier 4O4; no ICAO code), and Stan Stamper Municipal Airport at Hugo (KHHW) is about 28 nm west. The region is humid, and summer afternoon thunderstorms are common.
