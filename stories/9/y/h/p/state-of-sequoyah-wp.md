# State of Sequoyah

In 1905 the Five Tribes of Indian Territory wrote a constitution for a Native-governed state named for Sequoyah, won an overwhelming vote, and were refused by Congress, yet much of their work lives on in Oklahoma's own constitution.

**Location:** 35.0000, -95.5000

**Categories:** native-american-history, indian-territory, oklahoma, proposed-states, political-history

## Introduction

In the fall of 1905, a state that would never exist had a name, a constitution of some thirty-five thousand words, and a vote of 56,279 to 9,073 in its favor. It was to be called Sequoyah, after Sequoyah, the Cherokee polymath who created the writing system for his people's language, and it was to be carved from Indian Territory in what is now eastern Oklahoma. Its backers came from the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek and Seminole nations, and their purpose was simple and urgent: to keep control of their own lands under a constitution and government of their own.

## A Deadline for Nations

Since the Oklahoma Organic Act of 1890, the land that became Oklahoma had been divided in two, with Oklahoma Territory to the west and Indian Territory to the east. Indian Territory had already been reduced by land cessions forced after the Civil War, by land runs and by other treaties with the United States. Then came the Curtis Act of 1898. Although the territory had been exempt from the 1887 General Allotment Act, the new law let Congress set a date, March 4, 1906, for breaking up tribal governments and communal lands. Until 1903 the tribes had generally opposed every proposal for statehood. As the deadline approached, that changed, and tribal leaders turned to statehood as a way to retain their historic authority.

## The Convention at Muskogee

In July 1905, Cherokee Chief W.C. Rogers and Choctaw Chief Green McCurtain called for a constitutional convention, and on August 21 delegates gathered at the Hinton Theater in Muskogee, in the Creek Nation. Creek Principal Chief Pleasant Porter was chosen president, and the Creek journalist Alexander Posey served as secretary. The executive officers of the Five Tribes became vice-presidents, including Rogers, McCurtain and Seminole Principal Chief John Brown. Two non-Native men filled the remaining seats: Charles N. Haskell represented the Creeks, since Porter was presiding, and William H. Murray, a white man married into the Chickasaw Nation, was appointed by Chickasaw Governor Douglas H. Johnston, who himself opposed the convention. On November 7, 1905, voters in the territory approved the constitution and a petition for statehood by an overwhelming margin.

## Congress Says No

The vote did not matter in Washington. Representative Arthur Phillips Murphy and Senator Porter James McCumber filed bills to admit Sequoyah, but Congress refused to consider them. Instead, on November 16, 1907, Indian Territory and Oklahoma Territory were joined and admitted together as the single state of Oklahoma. Sequoyah's legacy survived in an unexpected form. Its constitution served in large part as the basis for the later Oklahoma Constitution, carrying over the delegates' shared populist distrust of elected officials. The convention also launched Haskell and Murray into public life, and Haskell became Oklahoma's first governor while Murray became the first Speaker of its House. Once in power, the two men had racial segregation written into Oklahoma law.

## The Long Road Back

For the Five Tribes, statehood brought diminished power, and through the first half of the twentieth century they faced further marginalization and discrimination, both racial and political. Since allotment, their chiefs had generally been appointed by the President rather than chosen by their own people. That changed in 1970, when Congress passed an act allowing each of the Five Tribes to elect its principal officer. The nations rebuilt governments of their own and negotiated separate agreements with the state to protect their autonomy. Then, on July 9, 2020, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in McGirt v. Oklahoma that Congress had never disestablished the Muscogee (Creek) reservation, which remained Indian Country for purposes of criminal law, and that reasoning has since been extended to the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw and Seminole reservations.

## An Idea That Endures

The name Sequoyah has never quite left the conversation. In late 2024, historian Donald Fixico, who is Muscogee, Seminole, Shawnee and Sac and Fox, suggested that the Five Tribes could revive the movement for separate statehood in the wake of McGirt. The idea has also found a home in fiction: in Harry Turtledove's alternate-history Southern Victory series, Indian Territory becomes a State of Sequoyah in which the Five Tribes are granted autonomy and special protections. On a real map, the proposed state is easy enough to picture. It is eastern Oklahoma, the country where the 1905 delegates hoped their nations could go on governing themselves.

## Flight Context

The State of Sequoyah was never created, so there is no single site to see; the coordinates of approximately 35.00°N, 95.50°W mark a point near McAlester in the heart of former Indian Territory, which covered eastern present-day Oklahoma. From 6,000-8,000 feet AGL the region's wooded hills, river valleys and large reservoirs, including Eufaula Lake to the north, spread out below. McAlester Regional Airport (KMLC) lies about 15 nm to the southwest. The Sequoyah convention met in Muskogee, served by Muskogee-Davis Regional Airport (KMKO) about 40 nm to the north. Expect haze in humid summer months and strong spring thunderstorms.
