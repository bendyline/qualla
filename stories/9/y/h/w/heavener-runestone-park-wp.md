# Heavener Runestone Park

On a wooded slope of Poteau Mountain in eastern Oklahoma, a 55-acre park surrounds a sandstone slab carved with eight runic symbols, a puzzle that has drawn theories, festivals and a town's determination to keep the gates open.

**Location:** 34.9000, -94.5781

**Categories:** Parks in Oklahoma, Protected areas of Le Flore County, Oklahoma, Protected areas established in 1970, 1970 establishments in Oklahoma

## Introduction

Eight symbols run across the face of a sandstone slab on Poteau Mountain, deep in the scrub-forest foothills of the Ouachita Mountains. They are runes, the letters of old Scandinavia, carved into rock in eastern Oklahoma, a long way from any fjord. Nobody has proved who put them there. Around that riddle grew Heavener Runestone Park, 55 acres of woods and trails just outside the town of Heavener in Le Flore County, and the question still draws people up the hill to stand in front of the stone and wonder.

## A Wall of Letters

The stone is no pebble. The Oklahoma Historical Society describes it as a large, vertical sandstone slab about ten feet wide and twelve feet high, and its eight carved characters run across that broad face. According to the society, a Choctaw hunting party came across the slab in the 1830s, and white trappers noted it in 1874. In the society's account, its modern story begins in 1923, when the Smithsonian Institution identified the markings as Scandinavian runes. Five years later, in 1928, a Heavener resident named Gloria Farley saw the stone and recognized that its carvings resembled characters of the runic alphabet. She became the stone's leading champion, and for the rest of the century the question of who carved it never really went away.

## Glome's Valley

What does it say? The eight characters were first read as GNOMEDAL, later reinterpreted as GLOMEDAL, and the inscription is usually understood as a claim marker meaning "Glome's Valley" or "valley belonging to Glome." Who Glome might have been is another matter. Theories have ranged widely, from Vikings traveling inland along the rivers to members of La Salle's French expedition around 1687 or Swedish colonists in the early 1700s. One reading even treats the symbols as a date, November 11, 1012. The evidence is less romantic. No trace of any Norse presence has been found in the area, and runologist Henrik Williams has put the stone at probably nineteenth century, with about a 20 percent chance of dating to the tenth or eleventh. Two other sandstone slabs, each bearing short runic markings, turned up about a mile to the north and to the south. Scandinavians settled early in the nearby town of Heavener, and researcher Lyle Tompsen, who judges the stone best considered a modern creation, regards them as the likeliest source of the carving. The Oklahoma Historical Society itself says only that scholars have given various opinions and that the question of who carved the characters, and when, remains open.

## Saved by the Town

The park opened in 1970, dedicated on October 25 of that year and built around the stone itself. For four decades it was an Oklahoma state park. Then, in March 2011, the Oklahoma Tourism and Recreation Department announced that budget cuts would force it to close Heavener Runestone State Park on August 15. The town did not wait to see what would happen to its landmark. The City of Heavener agreed to take over ownership and operation of the 55-acre park starting July 1, 2011, a month and a half before the state's closing date. Day-to-day care now falls to the Friends of Heavener Runestone, a volunteer-run nonprofit organization that keeps the park going through its own fundraising and hard work.

## Swords, Songs and a Hike to the Stone

The same year the city took charge, the park became home to the annual Heavener Runestone Festival, a fundraiser that doubles as a celebration. Celtic and folk music fills the woods. There is swordplay, a spread of merchant booths, games and overnight camping, and a hike leads visitors up to see the runestone itself. The cast and volunteers all dress in costume, and many attendees join them, so the crowd itself becomes part of the show. Beyond festival weekend, the park offers picnic tables and outdoor grills, primitive campsites with showers, an amphitheater, a playground, hiking trails and educational programs about the stone. The upper portion of the grounds can be rented for weddings, reunions and birthdays, which means a good many family celebrations in Le Flore County have unfolded within a short walk of a question that may reach back a thousand years, or only as far as the town's own early settlers.

## Flight Context

Heavener Runestone Park sits at 34.9000°N, 94.5781°W on the slopes of Poteau Mountain, just outside Heavener in Le Flore County, Oklahoma, where the forested ridges of the Ouachita Mountains begin. The runestone itself is far too small to spot from altitude, so use the town of Heavener and the ridgeline of Poteau Mountain as references from about 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, keeping well clear of the surrounding high terrain.
