# Hugo, Oklahoma

A railroad junction in the old Choctaw Nation, named for Victor Hugo, became a winter home for traveling circuses, and its cemetery keeps a special place for showmen and rodeo riders.

**Location:** 34.0094, -95.5147

**Categories:** Cities in Oklahoma, County seats in Oklahoma, Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma, Circus, Railroad towns

## Introduction

In one corner of Mount Olivet Cemetery in Hugo, Oklahoma, the graves belong to people who spent their lives on the road. The section is called Showmen's Rest, where circus owner D. R. Miller is buried, and the rodeo cowboys Freckles Brown, Lane Frost and Todd Whatley are laid to rest in the same cemetery. It is a fitting resting place in a town that circus people have called home since 1941, a small county seat in the far southeast of the state, about 9 miles north of the Texas border.

## A Surveyor's Wife and a French Novelist

Hugo owes its name to a reader. The wife of local surveyor W. H. Darrough was a fan of the French writer Victor Hugo, and she recommended his name for the new town. The town itself grew up where railroads crossed. In 1902 the St. Louis, San Francisco and New Orleans Railroad, later part of the St. Louis-San Francisco Railway known as the Frisco, built a line from Hope, Arkansas, to Ardmore. It crossed the north-south line the Frisco had built in 1887 between Monett, Missouri, and Paris, Texas, and at that junction Hugo took shape. At its founding the town lay in Kiamitia County, part of the Pushmataha District of the Choctaw Nation. When Oklahoma became a state, Hugo was already a population center, and it was designated the seat of the redefined Choctaw County. The 2020 census counted 5,166 residents.

## Swing Low

This was Indian Territory, where the U.S. government forcibly relocated Native American tribes in the 1830s. The Choctaw Nation was removed here from Mississippi, and some Choctaw slaveholders brought enslaved African Americans with them. One of them was a man named Wallace, enslaved in Mississippi and brought to Indian Territory during Indian Removal by his enslaver, a wealthy Choctaw farmer named Britt Willis. Living near present-day Hugo, Wallace Willis is traditionally credited with writing "Swing Low, Sweet Chariot" around 1840, a date that rests on a family recollection. By that account he wrote it while enslaved, in a land to which removal had brought both him and the people who held him. After the Civil War, the federal government required the Choctaw to emancipate the people they had enslaved and to grant the freedmen full citizenship rights.

## Where the Circus Spends the Winter

The circus arrived in 1941, when the Al G. Kelly and Miller Brothers Circus relocated to Hugo from Kansas. Others followed. Hugo's mild climate made it a popular wintering place for circus and rodeo companies, and in the decades after World War II the city served as winter quarters for circus companies and performers. An estimated twenty circuses have been based in Hugo at one time or another. In 2018 three still operated here, Carson and Barnes, Kelly Miller, and Culpepper & Merriweather, all tent circuses that travel the region by road. The rodeo has its own place in the town's memory. Lane Frost, born on October 12, 1963, became a world champion bull rider and died on July 30, 1989, at just 25; he is buried in Mount Olivet Cemetery, where circus people and fellow rodeo cowboys also rest.

## The Depot and the Harvey Girls

The railroad that built Hugo also left behind its depot. The Frisco depot, built in 1914 and in service into the 1960s, is now the Frisco Depot Museum, holding an eclectic mix that includes a model railway, a miniature circus and an old Southwestern Bell telephone switching center. A former Harvey House lunchroom has been restored, and the quarters of the Harvey Girls who worked there are preserved on the second floor. Next door, the Frisco Depot RV Park welcomes travelers. Beyond town, water frames the landscape. Hugo Lake, on the Kiamichi River, lies to the northeast, and Roebuck Lake, an oxbow that was once a channel of the Red River, lies to the south.

## Hometown Voices

For a small town, Hugo claims a remarkable list of notable people. Singer B. J. Thomas, known for the number-one hits "Raindrops Keep Fallin' on My Head," featured in the 1969 film Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid, and "(Hey Won't You Play) Another Somebody Done Somebody Wrong Song," is among them. So is journalist Bill Moyers, White House press secretary from 1965 to 1967 under Lyndon B. Johnson. Buckskin Bill Black, who became a long-time children's television host in Baton Rouge, Louisiana, grew up here, and the list also includes entrepreneur James Ling, founder of Ling-Temco-Vought. Clancy Smith, born and raised in Hugo, went on to serve on the Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals.

## Flight Context

Hugo lies in Choctaw County, southeastern Oklahoma, at approximately 34.009°N, 95.515°W, about 9 miles north of the Texas border. From about 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, look for the rail junction in town, Hugo Lake to the northeast and the oxbow of Roebuck Lake to the south. Stan Stamper Municipal Airport (KHHW) is about 2 nm northwest of town. Cox Field at Paris, Texas (KPRX), is about 23 nm south, and Durant Regional Airport-Eaker Field (KDUA) about 44 nm west. Expect summer heat and afternoon thunderstorms in this corner of Oklahoma.
