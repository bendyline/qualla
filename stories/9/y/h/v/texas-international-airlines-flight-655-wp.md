# Texas International Airlines Flight 655

On a stormy night in 1973, a Texas International turboprop flying without an instrument flight plan strayed far north of its route and struck Black Fork Mountain in western Arkansas, a crash that showed the cost of leaving the airline's stored instrument flight plan unused.

**Location:** 34.7014, -94.3333

**Categories:** aviation-accidents, disasters, arkansas, ouachita-mountains, aviation-safety

## Introduction

"Minimum en route altitude here is forty-four hun..." The first officer never finished the sentence. He had been studying an en-route chart, worried that he did not know where the airplane was or how much clearance it had above the ground, and he was trying to warn his captain that they were far too low. The cockpit voice recorder stopped as Texas International Airlines Flight 655 struck Black Fork Mountain in the Ouachita Mountains of western Arkansas on the night of September 27, 1973. All eleven people aboard, eight passengers and three crew members, were killed.

## A Milk Run Through Arkansas

Flight 655 was a Convair 600 turboprop, registered N94230, a 25-year-old airplane with 51,208 hours in its logbooks and two Rolls-Royce Dart engines. Its route was a string of short hops: Memphis, then Pine Bluff, El Dorado and Texarkana in Arkansas, and finally Dallas. On the ground at El Dorado, the crew talked with Flight Service Station staff and with another set of pilots about a line of thunderstorms to the west. After spotting what looked like a break in the line, about 15 miles wide and some 35 miles west-northwest of the airport, they took off into the night under visual flight rules, bound for Texarkana.

## Around the Storm

No controller heard from the flight after it left El Dorado. Inside the cockpit, the first officer was at the controls while Captain Ralph Crosman, 41, called out headings and altitudes to take them around the weather. The detour carried them far off course, roughly 100 nautical miles to the north. First Officer William Tumlinson, 37, said he did not know their position or what the terrain clearance was in that area. When the captain ordered a descent to 2,000 feet, Tumlinson consulted the chart and warned that they were too low. The figure he was reading aloud, forty-four hundred feet, was more than twice the altitude he had just been told to fly. Black Fork Mountain, a rugged ridge some thirteen miles long, rises past 2,600 feet near the crash site, and the airplane hit it at about 2,025 feet.

## Three Days of Searching

When the flight was overdue at Texarkana, search and rescue teams were called out. They combed the planned route and found nothing, because the airplane was nowhere near it. The break came from a controller at the Fort Worth air traffic control center, who remembered watching an unidentified target on his radar leave El Dorado heading northwest before the flight went missing. With that clue, searchers shifted their focus, and after three days they found the wreckage. There were no survivors. The search carried its own loss: an Arkansas Army National Guard helicopter heading for the search area crashed, killing its three crew members.

## Outside the System

The National Transportation Safety Board found that the crew had not discussed their intended route with Flight Service and had not activated the instrument flight plan stored for the flight with air traffic control. On an instrument flight plan they would have been followed by radar and offered assistance. Flying visually, they were not, and they never asked for the radar service that Fort Worth's air traffic control center could have provided on request. Regulations also required that the dispatcher be told the flight was proceeding visually. The board concluded the airplane was probably in bad weather when it crashed. It named as the probable cause the captain's attempt to fly visually in night instrument conditions without using all the navigation aids available to him, and his deviation from the planned route without adequate position information. It also found that he had directed a descent to an altitude below the terrain, and it faulted the airline for not adequately monitoring the flight.

## After the Report

The NTSB adopted its report in April 1974 without formal safety recommendations, saying it was considering recommendations on airline pilots' professional standards committees and on the relationship between pilots and dispatchers. The lesson in the findings is plain: on the instrument flight plan already stored for the flight, routes and altitudes are set to keep an airplane clear of terrain, and radar follows its progress, the very safeguards Flight 655 lacked. Black Fork Mountain itself became part of a federally designated wilderness in 1984, a ridge of shortleaf pine, oak and sandstone rock flows in the Ouachita National Forest.

## Flight Context

The crash site is recorded at approximately 34.70°N, 94.33°W on Black Fork Mountain in the Ouachita National Forest of western Arkansas, near the Oklahoma line. The mountain is a rugged ridge about thirteen miles long that rises past 2,600 feet near the crash site; keep generous terrain clearance, which is the whole lesson of this accident. Much of the ridge lies within the Black Fork Mountain Wilderness, where pilots are requested to stay at least 2,000 feet above the surface, so 4,500-5,500 feet MSL is a sensible viewing altitude. Mena Intermountain Municipal Airport (KMEZ) lies about 11 nm to the south-southeast. Low cloud and fog can settle in the valleys, and summer thunderstorms are common.
