# Hoo-Hoo Monument

In the small Arkansas town where a delayed train gave birth to the Concatenated Order of Hoo-Hoo, a granite block crowned with two snarling bronze black cats honors the lumbermen's fraternity and its Egyptian-inspired sense of mystery.

**Location:** 33.9203, -93.1539

**Categories:** Monuments and memorials on the National Register of Historic Places in Arkansas, Buildings and structures completed in 1909, National Register of Historic Places in Clark County, Arkansas, 1909 establishments in Arkansas, Relocated buildings and structures in Arkansas, Egyptian Revival architecture in Arkansas

## Introduction

Two black cats crouch on top of a granite block on North First Street in Gurdon, Arkansas, snarling at passersby, their tails curled into the shape of the number nine. They are bronze, and they belong to a fraternity with a gloriously odd name: the Concatenated Order of Hoo-Hoo. The men from the lumber trade who founded it in 1892 were, by most accounts, simply stuck in town waiting for a train. The monument that commemorates them has outlasted that wait by well over a century.

## Stranded in Gurdon

The order was established on January 21, 1892, at the Hotel Hall in Gurdon, by six men from the lumber trade, five of them stranded by a delayed train with hours to kill. Out of that idle wait came an international fraternal society of the lumber trade. Its name was part joke and part badge. "Concatenated" means linked together, and "Hoo-Hoo" traced back to one of the founders, Bolling Arthur Johnson, who about a month earlier in Kansas City had used the term to describe an unusual tuft of hair on the head of a man named Charles McCarer. McCarer went on to become the first Snark of the Universe, as the order's president is titled, and received membership number one. The group adopted a black cat as its mascot, its tail curved into a nine, and built its rituals around the number, holding gatherings on the ninth day of the ninth month at nine minutes past the ninth hour.

## An Egyptian Temple in Bronze

In 1909, the order dedicated a bronze plaque by the sculptor George Julian Zolnay commemorating its founding, cast from pennies donated by members. Zolnay reached for ancient Egypt. His plaque is in the Egyptian Revival style, topped by a prominent cavetto cornice with a winged sun-disc at its center. A two-headed bird fills the pediment, and pairs of Egyptian figures flank the inscription like temple guardians. The effect is grand and a little theatrical, which suits an organization with a black cat for a mascot and a Snark for a president. The plaque lists the names of the order's founders, shows an image of the Hotel Hall where it all began, and states the organization's purpose: "the promotion of the health, happiness, and long life of its members." On the opposite face of the granite, engraved names record the Snarks of the Universe through 1988.

## Granite, Cats and a Move

The plaque is set into one side of an ashlar-faced block of Barre granite that stands 116 inches high, 107 inches wide and 44 inches deep. On top sit the two snarling bronze cats, replicas of the Hoo-Hoo emblem. Nobody knows who made the stone or the cats. The plaque did not start out here. Zolnay's bronze was first affixed to a building, and when that building was demolished in 1927, the work was moved to its present site, mounted on the granite base and rededicated. The list of leaders kept growing until 1988, when the granite ran out of room. Two smaller granite stones were then added on either side of the monument to carry the names of the order's presidents since.

## Still Home to the Hoo-Hoo

Gurdon never let go of its peculiar claim to fame. The monument stands near the Hoo-Hoo international headquarters and the Hoo-Hoo International and Forestry Museum on Main Street, so the fraternity's history and its present-day home share the same few blocks of a small town. The monument joined the National Register of Historic Places on September 2, 1999, recognition that a lumbermen's inside joke had become a piece of Arkansas heritage. There is something fitting about it. The Hoo-Hoo were born because people who worked in timber found themselves with time on their hands, and the memorial to them asks the same of anyone who passes: stop for a moment, look up at the cats and read the names of the leaders who have carried the title of Snark.
