# 2010 Arkansas floods

Before dawn on June 11, 2010, the Little Missouri River rose from about three feet to more than twenty-three in the Ouachita Mountains, sweeping through the campgrounds at Albert Pike and killing 20 people, at least six of them young children.

**Location:** 34.3667, -93.8833

**Categories:** floods, natural-disasters, arkansas, ouachita-mountains, national-forests

## Introduction

On an ordinary day, the Little Missouri River near Langley, Arkansas, runs at a level of about three feet. Before dawn on June 11, 2010, it rose past twenty-three. Six to eight inches of heavy, localized rain had fallen over the Ouachita National Forest through the evening of June 10 and the early morning hours, and the Little Missouri and Caddo rivers climbed as fast as eight feet an hour. As many as 200 to 300 people were camping along the rivers that night. Many of them woke to rapidly rising water.

## Albert Pike, Before Dawn

The worst of it came at the Albert Pike Recreation Area, a popular campground along the Little Missouri about six miles north of Langley. In the dark hours before dawn, with the river's peak at the campground estimated by the U.S. Geological Survey between 4:00 and 4:30 a.m., the flood swept through a heavily occupied campsite and destroyed a number of cabins. Warnings had been issued in advance, but in such a remote place they may never have reached the people who needed them. When the water fell, at least 20 people were dead. Among them were at least six children age seven or younger. Most of the victims had traveled from Louisiana and Texas to camp in these mountains; others were from Arkansas.

## Searching Without a List

The American Red Cross estimated that more than 200 people had been in the flooded areas when the water hit, and at one point about 24 were missing. National Guard helicopters joined the search, but rescuers faced a basic problem: nobody knew how many people to look for. A logbook at Albert Pike that would have helped track the whereabouts of hikers had been swept away, and the missing were not necessarily confined to campsites. Floodwater had made roads impassable, in places stripping the pavement away entirely, so some searchers went out in canoes and kayaks. A call center set up by the Arkansas Department of Emergency Management fielded inquiries about 73 people who might have been missing.

## Not Since 1968

In the records kept since 1988, there was nothing like it: a flood of this size had not occurred in the area in all that time. One local resident recalled nothing comparable since May 1968, when, in the resident's words, the site "was not the popular camping spot it is today." President Barack Obama offered condolences, saying he and Michelle extended "heartfelt condolences to the families and friends of those who lost their lives during this horrible flash flood," along with prayers for those still waiting for news of the missing.

## Above the Floodplain

After the flood, the U.S. Forest Service shut down overnight camping at Albert Pike, though its trails stayed open to daytime hikers. For more than a decade the campground sat empty at night. In 2022, Arkansas Congressman Bruce Westerman introduced legislation to bring camping back by moving sites above the 100-year floodplain, and the EXPLORE Act, enacted on January 4, 2025, included measures directing the Forest Service to establish campsites outside that floodplain. The idea behind it is simple, and it was learned at terrible cost: in these steep, narrow valleys, a river can rise twenty feet in the dark.

## Flight Context

The flood area centers on approximately 34.37°N, 93.88°W near Langley, in the Ouachita Mountains of west-central Arkansas; the Albert Pike Recreation Area lies about six miles north along the Little Missouri River in Montgomery County. The terrain is steep, forested ridge-and-valley country, so keep 2,000 feet or more above the ridgetops, roughly 4,500-5,000 feet MSL. Mena Intermountain Municipal Airport (KMEZ) is about 19 nm to the northwest, and Hot Springs Memorial Field (KHOT) lies about 40 nm to the east-northeast. Summer thunderstorms can drop heavy rain quickly over these mountains, and valley fog is common on cool mornings.
