# Washington, Arkansas

A tiny Hempstead County town that once stood on the Southwest Trail to Texas, Washington, Arkansas, saw Sam Houston and Davy Crockett pass through, is where the first bowie knife was reportedly forged, and served as Arkansas's Confederate capital before the railroad left it behind.

**Location:** 33.7740, -93.6827

**Categories:** arkansas, small-towns, civil-war, former-state-capitals, southwest-trail, historic-sites

## Introduction

The 2020 census counted 94 people in Washington, Arkansas. For two years in the 1860s, this was a state capital. Long before that, it was a crossroads on the Southwest Trail to Texas, and its visitors included Stephen Austin, Sam Houston and Davy Crockett. Today much of the old town survives inside Historic Washington State Park, where the courthouse, the houses and a blacksmith shop make the town's outsized past easy to picture.

## A Tavern on the Trail

Washington began on a sandy hill where a Methodist preacher, William Stevenson, set up the Ebenezer Campground. Around 1818 Elijah Stuart built a log house there that served as an inn and tavern, and in 1824 it was designated Hempstead County's first permanent seat of government. The following year the county's Court of Common Pleas opened in a building next door to Stuart's tavern. The town grew up along the Southwest Trail, the territorial-era road linking St. Louis to Texas, and for the pioneers who traveled that rugged route, Washington was an important stop.

## Knives and Revolutions

Some of the travelers became legends. Jim Bowie, Sam Houston and Davy Crockett all passed through on their way to Texas, and Houston is believed to have planned parts of the strategy for the Texas revolt in a Washington tavern in 1834. In his shop in town, blacksmith James Black reportedly fashioned the first bowie knife for James Bowie in 1831, working from a design by Bowie, though historians dispute which smith made Bowie's first knife; the blade became an American icon. When war with Mexico began in 1846, Washington became a rally point for volunteers on their way to join the U.S. Army. Later the town settled into the role of a service center for the area's planters, merchants and professionals.

## Journeys Not Chosen

Not everyone who came down the road was traveling by choice. Between 1832 and 1839, thousands of Choctaw people, forced from their homelands by the United States government's removal policy, passed through Washington on their way to Indian Territory, and Chickasaw people traveled through as well. The prosperity of the planters Washington served rested on another kind of forced labor. By 1860, the federal census counted 5,398 enslaved people in Hempstead County, compared with a white population of 8,589. Behind that figure were thousands of men, women and children whose unpaid labor sustained the plantation agriculture that dominated the county.

## Capital in Exile

The Civil War brought Washington its most unexpected role. Twenty companies of Confederate soldiers were raised locally. After Union forces captured Little Rock in 1863, Arkansas's Confederate state government moved its offices to Hot Springs for a short time, then settled in Washington. The 1836 Hempstead County Courthouse served as the state capitol until 1865. The town's newspaper, the Washington Telegraph, won the printing contract for the Confederate state government and kept going through the war, pausing only briefly during the 1864 Camden Expedition; when supplies were cut, it was even printed for a time on wallpaper. The courthouse still stands in the park today, a modest building that once held the government of a state at war.

## Bypassed, Then Saved

The railroad decided Washington's future. The Cairo and Fulton line was built eight miles to the south, connecting much of the state to Little Rock, and Hope, sitting on the new route, took over Washington's role. Fires in 1875 and 1883 hastened the decline, and in 1939 the county seat moved to Hope. Preservation began early, though. In 1929 the state created a commission to restore the old courthouse, and in 1958 local citizens formed the Pioneer Washington Restoration Foundation. A state park followed in 1973, today called Historic Washington State Park. Its 101 acres preserve 54 buildings, including houses from the 1830s to the 1850s, a re-creation of Black's blacksmith shop, a print museum and a weapons museum.

## Flight Context

Washington lies at approximately 33.77°N, 93.68°W in north-central Hempstead County, about 10 miles northwest of Hope, with U.S. Route 278 running through town as Columbus Street. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for the small grid of the historic town northwest of Hope. Hope Municipal Airport (FAA identifier M18), a World War II-era airfield with long runways, lies only about 3.5 nm to the south-southeast. Texarkana Regional Airport (KTXK) is about 25 nm to the southwest. The Red River flows about 14 miles to the southwest at Fulton. Expect humid haze in summer and occasional low clouds and fog in cooler months.
