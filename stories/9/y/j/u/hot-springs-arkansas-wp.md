# Hot Springs, Arkansas

Built around 47 thermal springs whose water mostly fell as rain some 4,400 years ago, Hot Springs, Arkansas, grew from the nation's oldest federal reserve into a spa town that hosted spring training, gangsters, casinos and wounded soldiers.

## Introduction

The water steaming out of the ground in Hot Springs mostly fell as rain about 4,400 years ago. U.S. Geological Survey scientists, working with the National Park Service and using radiocarbon dating, have traced its slow journey: down through the rock until it reaches superheated depths, then a rapid rise back to the surface through 47 springs on the western slope of Hot Springs Mountain. Roughly 750,000 to 950,000 gallons of 143°F water emerge every day, a flow that peaks in winter and spring. A whole city, with its bathhouses, grand hotels, ballparks and back-room casinos, grew up around that steady, ancient heat.

## A Reserve Before the Parks

Members of many Native American tribes gathered in this valley for untold years to bathe in the springs, and the waters figured in the legends of several nations. In December 1804, the explorers George Hunter and William Dunbar found only a lone log cabin and a few rough shelters used by visitors seeking a cure. The Quapaw ceded the land around the springs to the United States in 1818, and in 1832 Congress created the Hot Springs Reservation to protect the thermal waters. It is the oldest federal reserve in the United States, and in 1921 it was renamed Hot Springs National Park. The town around it was incorporated on January 10, 1851, and settled into life as a spa.

## Ashes and Arlingtons

The Civil War nearly erased it. Many residents fled to Texas or Louisiana, and guerrilla bands loosely tied to either side pillaged and burned the near-deserted town, leaving only a few buildings standing. The rebuilding was extensive. By 1873 six bathhouses and 24 hotels and boardinghouses stood near the springs, and that year Hot Springs became the seat of the new Garland County. A narrow-gauge railroad from Malvern arrived in 1875, the same year the first Arlington Hotel, the area's first luxury hotel, opened its doors. Disaster struck again on September 5, 1913, when a fire on Church Street swept across 60 blocks, destroying the county courthouse, the high school, four hotels and at least a hundred businesses before desperate dynamiting helped save Central Avenue.

## The First Boys of Spring

Hot Springs calls itself the birthplace of spring training. Chicago White Stockings president Albert Spalding and player-manager Cap Anson brought their team here to get fit before the season, and the Pittsburgh Pirates, Brooklyn Dodgers, New York Yankees, Boston Red Sox and others followed. Some 134 members of the Baseball Hall of Fame are documented to have trained or played in town. On St. Patrick's Day, 1918, Babe Ruth, playing first base in an exhibition at Whittington Park, hit two home runs; the second, estimated at 573 feet, landed in the Arkansas Alligator Farm. Honus Wagner donated black-and-gold basketball uniforms to Hot Springs High School, which adopted the Pirates' colors for good. The Homestead Grays trained here in 1930 and 1931 with Josh Gibson, the Pittsburgh Crawfords followed from 1932 to 1935 with Gibson and Cool Papa Bell, an 18-year-old Hank Aaron played in the 1952 Negro American League championship at Majestic Park, and Jackie Robinson played an exhibition there in 1953.

## Wide Open Town

Illegal gambling took hold after the Civil War, and rival factions, the Flynns and the Dorans, fought for control through the 1880s. The feud helped lead to a March 1899 gunfight in which county sheriff's deputies killed three Hot Springs police officers, and a part-time deputy also died. From 1927 to 1947, the town hit its wagering peak, with at least ten major casinos running wide open under Owney Madden and others, while Al Capone favored the Arlington Hotel. Mayor Leo McLaughlin's political machine controlled local law enforcement and bought hundreds of poll tax receipts, many in the names of dead or fictitious people. A former sheriff who tried to enforce the gambling laws was murdered in 1937; no one was ever charged. Returning World War II veterans led by Sid McMath broke the machine in 1946, but the casinos reopened under Governor Orval Faubus. As Variety described it in 1959, each week management simply paid its fine in local court "and goes back to open up." In 1967 Governor Winthrop Rockefeller sent state troopers to close the casinos and burn their gaming equipment.

## Healing and Bathing

The waters drew the military too. The Army and Navy Hospital, funded by Congress in 1882 at the urging of Senator John A. Logan, a former Union general, opened to patients in 1887 and later treated, among others, Joe DiMaggio. During World War II the Army took over most of the town's hotels as a redistribution center, processing more than 32,000 service members before it closed in December 1945. Today Bathhouse Row's eight historic buildings line Central Avenue inside the national park. The Fordyce became the visitor center in 1989, the Buckstaff has operated continuously since 1912, and the Superior now brews beer with spring water. Above it all, the 216-foot Hot Springs Mountain Tower looks out over the city that grew up around the springs, the boyhood home of Bill Clinton.

## Flight Context

Hot Springs lies at approximately 34.50°N, 93.06°W at the southeastern edge of the Ouachita Mountains, about 55 miles southwest of Little Rock. From 3,000-4,500 feet AGL, look for the compact downtown tucked into a narrow valley along Central Avenue, with Bathhouse Row at the foot of Hot Springs Mountain and the 216-foot observation tower on the ridge above it; Lake Hamilton, a reservoir on the Ouachita River, spreads south of the city. Hot Springs Memorial Field (KHOT) is about 2.5 nm to the southwest. Bill and Hillary Clinton National Airport (KLIT) in Little Rock lies about 43 nm to the east-northeast. The surrounding forested ridges can hold morning fog, and spring is the wettest season.
