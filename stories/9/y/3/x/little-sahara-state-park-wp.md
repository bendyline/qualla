# Little Sahara State Park

Once a nuisance that forced Highway 281 off its route, the dunes south of Waynoka were rebranded as Oklahoma's Little Sahara by a chamber of commerce leader with hired outlaws and a pair of camels, and became a playground for riders on balloon tires.

**Location:** 36.5331, -98.8819

**Categories:** State parks of Oklahoma, Dunes of the United States, Protected areas of Woods County, Oklahoma, Landforms of Oklahoma, Landforms of Woods County, Oklahoma, 1960 establishments in Oklahoma

## Introduction

Before anyone thought to sell tickets, the sand was simply in the way. The dunes south of Waynoka, in northwest Oklahoma's Woods County, crept onto Highway 281 so persistently that the road had to be rerouted around them. To most people they were a nuisance. Grace Ward Smith, head of the Chamber of Commerce in nearby Alva, saw something else in those restless hills: a desert, close to home, that people might pay to visit. She gave it a name borrowed from North Africa and set about luring them in. The name stuck, and so did the visitors.

## Sand From a Vanished River

The dunes are older than any highway. They formed over time from terrace deposits, remnants of prehistoric times when the Cimarron River flowed across this entire area. The sand it left behind has been heaped into hills that today cover more than 1,600 acres. They range from 25 to 75 feet high, rolling and pale against the surrounding plains, and the resemblance to the Sahara Desert gave the park its name. The park is also called the Waynoka Dunes. The highest dunes, known as Competition Hill and Buttercup, rise in the western and northwestern parts of the park. The first of those names hints at how people here have come to use them. These are not dunes for quiet contemplation. They are dunes for climbing, and mostly for climbing on wheels.

## Hired Outlaws and a Pair of Camels

Smith founded Little Sahara in the early 1950s as a city park, and she understood that a desert needed a little theater. She hired locals to guide visitors across the sand and to play outlaws, adding a dash of Wild West drama to the dunes. In 1958 she went further and helped buy a pair of camels. The investment in showmanship paid off. In September 1960, the state of Oklahoma, using a $12,500 legislative appropriation, bought the 339-acre parcel, turning the city attraction into a state park. Later that same year, some 4,000 visitors turned out for a Christmas pageant, and the stars of the show were the camels. It is hard to imagine a more fitting cast for a Christmas pageant staged on a stretch of Oklahoma sand that, not long before, had been known mainly for blocking the highway.

## Balloon Tires and Hay-Rake Roll Bars

By 1963 the park had grown to 1,600 acres, and that spring it found the identity that would define it. A local auto dealer took to the dunes in a homemade machine later described as a "makeshift steampunk vehicle with wide rims and balloon tires, topped by hay-rake wheels serving as roll bars." Riding the sand caught on quickly, and local businesses still rent out vehicles for the purpose. The contraptions have changed considerably since then. Motorcycles, dirt bikes, ATVs, UTVs, side-by-sides, SUVs and Jeeps all share the hills now, each required to fly a solid orange whip flag. Dune buggies and four-wheel-drive vehicles must carry seat belts and a roll bar, a rule the auto dealer's hay-rake wheels anticipated by decades, and helmets are required for anyone under 18 who is not in a roll-caged vehicle and strongly suggested for everyone else.

## Camping at the Edge of the Sand

Little Sahara is open year-round. The park offers showers and picnic areas, along with RV sites with water, electric and sewer hookups and well over a hundred tent sites, and seasonal concessions sell refreshments, with fuel and groceries available nearby. What began as a promotional scheme to lure visitors has become a fixture of this corner of Woods County. The sand that once had to be engineered around, pushing a highway off its line, now draws riders here on purpose, bound for the crests of Competition Hill and Buttercup. Grace Ward Smith's wager was that a nuisance could become a destination. More than seventy years after she opened her city park, the dunes are still proving her right.

## Flight Context

Little Sahara State Park is centered near 36.5331° N, 98.8819° W, just south of the town of Waynoka in Woods County, northwest Oklahoma, along U.S. Highway 281. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet above ground level, the dunes are unmistakable: a broad patch of bare, pale sand, with vehicle tracks scrawled across it, standing out sharply against the surrounding farmland and grassland. The broad sandy valley of the Cimarron River lies nearby and makes a useful reference. Alva Regional Airport (KAVK), just south of Alva, is roughly 20 miles to the northeast, and West Woodward Airport (KWWR) lies about 36 miles to the west. Northwest Oklahoma is often windy, and blowing sand and dust can reduce visibility over the dunes; spring and summer also bring afternoon thunderstorms, so calm morning conditions offer the best views.
