# Gloss Mountain State Park

Mesas capped with glittering selenite rise above the plains of Major County, Oklahoma, where a state park that sat leased for grazing for twenty years was finally opened and is still kept running by its neighbors in Fairview.

**Location:** 36.3667, -98.5789

**Categories:** State parks of Oklahoma, Protected areas of Major County, Oklahoma, Protected areas established in 1997

## Introduction

Even the name comes in two versions. The flat-topped hills rising from the plains of Major County, Oklahoma, are the Glass Mountains on some maps and the Gloss Mountains on others, and the state park at their feet answers to both. Either word fits. Selenite in the gypsum that caps these hills is glossy, and when sunlight strikes it the slopes throw back a glass-like shine. Long before anyone argued over the vowel, Americans had a grander name for them. In 1821, they called these the Shining Mountains.

## Mountains That Aren't

Strictly speaking, they are not mountains at all. The Glass Mountains are mesas and buttes that rise 150 to 200 feet above the flat surface of Major County, part of the Blaine Escarpment that runs across northwestern Oklahoma. Their highest point approaches 1,600 feet above sea level. Their story begins underwater. An epeiric sea, a shallow inland sea that once covered the region, left behind layers of shale and siltstone, and those layers were capped with a layer of gypsum. Flooding and the downcutting of river channels have since worn the land into the mesa-strewn landscape visible today, a country of flat tops and steep, abrupt sides. The park lists its attractions plainly: the lay of the land, the geological formations, the selenite gypsum, the scenery and the wildlife.

## A Park on Hold

The park was bought long before it was finished. In 1977, the state of Oklahoma appropriated $125,000, matched by another $125,000 from the federal Land and Water Conservation Fund, to purchase 640 acres along U.S. Highway 412 for a public park. Then the project stalled. The state could not fund the completion of the park it had planned, so instead of welcoming hikers, the land was leased out for grazing. It stayed that way for twenty years. The mesas were public property, bought with public money for public use, yet for two decades the plan to open them sat on hold.

## Kept by Its Neighbors

The breakthrough came in 1997, when representatives of the state's Tourism and Recreation Department met with residents of nearby Fairview. The locals formed a conservancy to manage the park, and the arrangement they worked out is a genuine partnership. The state provided the land, while the citizens of Fairview maintain the facilities. The conservancy even pays a gatekeeper who opens the park each day and locks it again at night. The result is a modest place with a clear purpose. Gloss Mountain State Park is a day-use park, open 365 days a year from sunrise to sunset for hiking and picnicking. There are no campsites and no overnight accommodations. Visitors find a restroom, pavilions, picnic areas, grills and a public water supply, along with an accessible trail leading to a historical marker.

## Up Cathedral Mountain

The park's main event is the climb. A hiking trail leads from the parking lot at the base to the top of Cathedral Mountain, then continues across the mesa, where the view opens out over the valley floor and across to Lone Peak Mountain. Up top, the geology stops being abstract. Underfoot is the gypsum cap itself, and its selenite catches the light just as it did for the Americans who called this range the Shining Mountains in 1821. Below, the plains of Major County stretch away, flat enough to make 200 feet of height feel like a summit. For a park that spent its first twenty years leased for grazing, the view from Cathedral Mountain is a fair reward for the neighbors who finally opened the gate.

## Flight Context

Gloss Mountain State Park lies at approximately 36.3667° N, 98.5789° W in Major County, northwestern Oklahoma, along U.S. Highway 412 about six miles west of Orienta and near the town of Fairview. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet above ground level, look for a cluster of flat-topped mesas and buttes rising abruptly 150 to 200 feet above otherwise level plains; U.S. 412 runs east-west past their base and makes an easy line to follow. The highest ground in the range approaches 1,600 feet above sea level. Alva Regional Airport (KAVK) is roughly 28 miles to the north, and Enid Woodring Regional Airport (KWDG) is about 44 miles to the east. Vance Air Force Base (KEND), a military pilot-training base near Enid, generates heavy training traffic, so monitor local frequencies when approaching from the east. Low-angle morning or evening sun gives the best chance of seeing the selenite glint on the mesa slopes.
