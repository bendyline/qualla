# Dwight D. Eisenhower Presidential Library, Museum and Boyhood Home

In Abilene, Kansas, the house where Dwight Eisenhower grew up became the seed of a presidential library, museum and chapel, much of it built while he was still alive to watch, and now his final resting place.

**Location:** 38.9122, -97.2108

**Categories:** Presidential libraries, Presidential museums in the United States, Dwight D. Eisenhower, Tombs of presidents of the United States, Museums in Dickinson County, Kansas, Landmarks in Kansas

## Introduction

The first time admirers tried to buy the house in Abilene, Ida Eisenhower said no. It was 1945, the war in Europe was ending, and her son Dwight was the Supreme Commander of Allied forces there. Local supporters wanted to honor him with a museum built on the property where he had grown up. His mother, still alive and still the owner, refused to sell. What eventually rose on that ground became a presidential library, museum and boyhood home, and the place where Eisenhower himself was laid to rest.

## A Mother Who Would Not Sell

The effort began with local admirers such as Charles L. Brainard, who in 1945 created a non-profit foundation in the general's name. Its plan was to purchase the boyhood home and build a museum beside it, housing artifacts from veterans and the honors Eisenhower himself had received. Ida's refusal paused everything. After she died in 1946, the foundation tried again, but its fundraising tactics offended Eisenhower so much that the project nearly collapsed. Rescue came through family. After discussions with Ike's youngest brother, Milton, the foundation adopted new fundraising rules, and all of the Eisenhower brothers agreed to them. On June 22, 1947, the house opened to the public as a museum.

## The House on the Property

Eisenhower was born on October 14, 1890, in Denison, Texas, the third of seven sons of Ida Stover and David Jacob Eisenhower. In 1892 the family moved to Abilene, the town he considered his hometown. He lived in this house from 1898 until 1911, when he was appointed to West Point, and it still stands on the grounds. The museum rose on the same property while its subject was climbing toward the White House. Its construction coincided with the general's announcement of his presidential candidacy in June 1952, and it was completed in 1954. By then Eisenhower was the 34th president of the United States, and he attended its formal opening on November 11 of that year. The museum was later restored and rededicated in 1971.

## A Library Built in His Lifetime

Much of the Eisenhower complex rose during his own lifetime and even his presidency: the museum was finished in 1954, and ground was broken for the library before he left office. Because fundraising limits still restrained the foundation in 1954, the Kansas Legislature stepped in and authorized a separate Eisenhower Presidential Library Commission. Over the next five years it raised enough money to build. Once again the president was present, this time when ground was broken on October 13, 1959. Construction took three years. On May 1, 1962, Vice President Lyndon B. Johnson joined the now-retired Eisenhower for the dedication. Today the library is one of the presidential libraries operated by the National Archives and Records Administration, and a visitors center with a gift shop and theater shows a short film about the president several times a day.

## The Place of Meditation

Across the grounds stands a chapel known as the Place of Meditation. Eisenhower died in Washington, D.C., on March 28, 1969, and he came home to Abilene to be buried there. Mamie Eisenhower, the First Lady, rests beside him, along with their first-born son, Doud, who died of scarlet fever at the age of three. Outside, a statue of General Eisenhower by Robert L. Dean, Jr., presented by the Harry and Edith Darby Foundation, looks out over the campus. Nearby, five pylons carry inscribed phrases that trace his life from birth through the presidency. Walking between them, a visitor moves through the whole arc: a modest Kansas house, a war won in Europe, eight years in the White House, and a quiet chapel only steps from where it all began.
