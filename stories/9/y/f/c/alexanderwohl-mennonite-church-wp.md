# Alexanderwohl Mennonite Church

A Mennonite congregation that moved from the Netherlands to Prussia to Russia crossed the Atlantic whole in 1874 and rebuilt itself on the Kansas prairie near Goessel, where its church still stands.

**Location:** 38.2619, -97.3344

**Categories:** Mennonite church buildings in Kansas, Russian Mennonite diaspora in the United States, German-Russian culture in Kansas, Buildings and structures in Marion County, Kansas, Immigration history

## Introduction

On July 18, 1874, passports were handed out inside the Alexanderwohl church, in the Molotschna Mennonite colony of South Russia (now Ukraine). Within weeks the entire congregation, about 800 people counting the other families who joined them, would board two ships for America. No other large Mennonite group made the move from Russia to North America as a complete congregation. Today the Alexanderwohl Mennonite Church stands in the farm country near Goessel, Kansas, carrying the name a Russian czar inspired and a continuous history that reaches back to 16th-century Europe.

## A People Always Moving

The families of Alexanderwohl trace their roots to the Dutch Anabaptists of the 16th century. Between 1600 and 1650 these families, now called Mennonites, migrated to West Prussia and settled in the Danzig (now Gdańsk, Poland) area between the Vistula and Nogat rivers. Their mother church, at Przechówko, gathered Mennonites who had settled near Schwetz and Culm on the Vistula. Growing Prussian militarism and continuing discrimination pushed them on again. In 1820 and 1821, much of the Przechówko congregation followed Peter Wedel to the Molotschna Mennonite colony in South Russia, where they were promised exemption from military service, their own schools and self-government in their villages. On the way, they met Czar Alexander I, who wished them well, wohl in German, and the new village took the name Alexanderwohl.

## Leaving as One

Fifty years later the promises began to unravel. In 1870 the Russian government announced that the special privileges of its German colonists would end by 1880, and Mennonites feared losing control of their schools and their exemption from military service. In 1873 Jacob Buller and Dietrich Gaeddert, ministers of the Alexanderwohl congregation, joined a delegation to North America and traveled to Kansas. By the time they returned that September, their people had already decided to go. When Eduard Totleben came on the czar's behalf in May 1874 with verbal promises, they were unconvinced. They sold grain and animals at full price, but land and other possessions went for half their value or less. Buller and Gaeddert arranged the finances so that even the poorest families could make the trip. Buller's group sailed from Hamburg on the Cimbria on August 12, 1874, and Gaeddert led 130 families aboard the Teutonia four days later, a voyage of about 18 days to New York.

## An Ocean of Grass

The Gaeddert group reached Topeka on September 10, 1874, with the Buller group close behind, and the Santa Fe Railroad housed them for a month while they bought horses, cattle, tools, building materials and food. The Topeka Commonwealth called the wider Russian Mennonite community's purchase of about 100,000 acres of railroad land "one of the largest bona fide land sales ever made in Kansas, perhaps in America." It also quoted one of their elders: "in three years ... that ocean of grass will be transformed into an ocean of waving fields of grain, just as we left our Molotschna colony." By then the two groups had parted. Buller's people settled near present-day Goessel, while Gaeddert's settled east of Buhler and founded the Hoffnungsau congregation, whose name came from J.C. Dick, who looked over the open prairie and called it "a true meadow of hope."

## Worship in Immigrant Houses

The railroad built immigrant houses in Harvey, Marion, McPherson and Reno counties where families could live while they built homes on their own land. Two of those structures stood near the middle of the section where the Alexanderwohl church stands today, and once the families had moved out, the buildings were moved together to form a place of worship. The congregation grew quickly, from 265 members in 1874 to 467 by 1880. Schools came quickly. By the winter of 1874 the congregation had opened three schools, and on September 13, 1882, the Emmental Central School for training Mennonite teachers opened in one of them. It moved to Halstead the following year as Halstead Seminary and eventually became Bethel College. The congregation joined the General Conference Mennonite Church in 1878 to work with other Kansas Mennonites on teacher training and mission work.

## A Church That Stayed

After three great migrations across nearly three centuries, the congregation finally put down lasting roots. In 1886 it completed a new church on the present site, built in the Dutch Mennonite style. The building was remodeled extensively in 1928, gained an education wing in 1961 and a north addition in 1983. Alexanderwohl reports about 500 members and is affiliated with Mennonite Church USA and the Western District Conference, with active programs in youth work, music, education, community outreach and missions. Southwest of the church stand a Kansas historical marker, The Mennonites in Kansas, and a threshing stone, reminders of the immigrant farm families who built a new life here.

## Flight Context

The Alexanderwohl Mennonite Church stands at 38.262°N, 97.334°W in rural Marion County, Kansas, a short distance from the small town of Goessel. From 1,500-3,000 feet, look for the church amid the square-mile grid of farmland typical of the Kansas plains, near the small grid of Goessel. Newton City-County Airport (KEWK) lies to the south, and Wichita Dwight D. Eisenhower National Airport (KICT) is farther south. Expect good visibility most days, with strong winds and spring and summer thunderstorms.
