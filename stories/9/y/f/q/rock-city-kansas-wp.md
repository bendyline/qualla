# Rock City, Kansas

Two hundred giant stone spheres sit on a strip of prairie above the Solomon River, concretions that formed deep underground and were exposed when the softer sandstone around them wore away.

**Location:** 39.0909, -97.7355

**Categories:** Geology, National Natural Landmarks in Kansas, Protected areas of Ottawa County, Kansas, Geography of Kansas, Rock formations

## Introduction

Glacial boulders. Corals. Lumps of limestone. Ordinary chunks of sandstone left behind by erosion. Over the years, the giant stone spheres on a hillside above the Solomon River in Ottawa County, Kansas, have been explained as all of these, and every explanation was wrong. The real story of Rock City is stranger. These 200 boulders took shape underground, inside sand laid down by an ancient river, and only came into the light when the softer rock around them wore away.

## A Field of Stone Cannonballs

Rock City occupies a patch of prairie about 500 meters long and 40 meters wide, 3.6 miles south of Minneapolis, Kansas, and just over half a mile west of highway K-106 on Ivy Road. Within that narrow strip sit three clusters of huge, round boulders, 200 in all. Geologists call them cannonball concretions, a name that fits their shape if not their scale. They range from 3 to 6 meters across, roughly 10 to 20 feet, with an average of 3.6 meters, and they lie 2 to 8 meters apart. One is shaped like a turtle. In May the view toward Minneapolis runs across rippling wheat fields, and Hesperis matronalis grows beside the rocks. A local non-profit corporation operates the park, which has a small gift shop and picnic tables, and a small admission fee pays for its upkeep. The site has been designated a National Natural Landmark.

## Sand from an Ancient River

The boulders began as sand. It accumulated in a river channel that is part of the Dakota Sandstone, laid down on a low-lying coastal plain. That sand became the host rock around the concretions: a well-sorted, medium-grained sandstone, highly porous and crumbly, held together only weakly by small amounts of iron oxide. At Rock City that iron sometimes shows as Liesegang rings, bands of color in the stone. The boulders are made of the same kind of sand, more than 95 percent quartz, but tightly cemented by calcite. The cement did more than bind grains together. About 20 percent of the original sandstone, mostly feldspar grains, has been replaced by calcite. Scattered through that cement are microscopic crystals of pyrite, now oxidized to goethite, along with small, knobby pyrite concretions typically about a foot across. Some boulders even hold smaller calcite concretions, engulfed as the larger ones grew around them.

## A Parade of Wrong Answers

The remarkable size and roundness of the Rock City boulders was first noted by Bell, and later they were noted or described by Gould, Landes, Shaffer, Ward and Swineford. Interpretations wandered. The boulders were taken for glacial boulders, for corals, for concretionary masses of limestone, and for ordinary erosional remnants of sandstone. Shaffer was the first to recognize them for what they are, calcite-cemented concretions. Later, McBride and others examined the boulders' mineralogy and the carbon and oxygen isotopes of their calcite cement. They concluded that the concretions formed after the sandstone had been deeply buried, as calcium diffused through the rock and calcite precipitated within it. The carbon and calcium came from marine limestone, shells, anhydrite or some combination of them, together with bicarbonate from oxidized methane in strata outside the Dakota Sandstone but hydrologically connected to it.

## What Erosion Left Behind

Once formed, the concretions were far tougher than the rock that held them, and that difference is what made Rock City. The weakly cemented host sandstone erodes much more easily, and over time differential erosion stripped it away, leaving the calcite spheres standing free on the hillside. The same process gave them their character. Cross-bedding inherited from the original river sand, combined with uneven cementation and later erosion, created the ornamentation that marks the surfaces of these boulders. Kansas has other striking rock formations, among them Castle Rock, Monument Rocks and Mushroom Rock State Park. Here, the story is written in spheres: a buried river channel, turned to stone and then uncovered, now sits in three clusters on a hillside overlooking the Solomon River.

## Flight Context

Rock City lies at approximately 39.091°N, 97.736°W in Ottawa County, Kansas, about 3.6 miles south of the town of Minneapolis and just west of highway K-106. The boulder field is compact, only about 500 meters long, so descend to around 1,000-1,500 feet AGL where permitted and look for a line of rounded shapes on the hillside above the Solomon River valley. The closest field is Minneapolis City County Airport, less than 1 nm to the east (FAA identifier 45K; no ICAO code). Salina Municipal Airport (KSLN) is about 18 nm to the south and makes a good larger base. Kansas afternoons can be gusty, so plan low passes for calmer morning air.
