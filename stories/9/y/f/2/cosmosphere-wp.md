# Cosmosphere

In Hutchinson, Kansas, a museum that began as a fairground planetarium now bills itself as holding the largest combined collection of American and Russian spaceflight artifacts in the world, including a Mercury capsule raised from the bottom of the Atlantic.

**Location:** 38.0653, -97.9213

**Categories:** Aerospace museums in Kansas, Smithsonian Institution affiliates, Planetaria in the United States, Hutchinson, Kansas, Space exploration

## Introduction

What is billed as the largest collection of Russian space artifacts outside Moscow is not in Europe or Washington. It is in Hutchinson, Kansas, beside the community college, at the corner of Plum Street and 11th Avenue. The Cosmosphere holds more than 10,000 spaceflight artifacts, billed as the largest combined collection of U.S. and Russian spaceflight artifacts in the world, and its American holdings are described as second only to those of the National Air and Space Museum. Outside, authentic Redstone and Titan II launch vehicles used in the Mercury and Gemini programs stand upright flanking the building like sentries on the prairie.

## From Fairground Stars to Spaceflight

It began modestly, as a planetarium set up on the Kansas State Fairgrounds in 1962. Over the decades the old Kansas Cosmosphere grew into a 105,000-square-foot international science education center, and it is now the only Smithsonian affiliate museum in Kansas. The exhibits tell the story of spaceflight in order, starting with the earliest experiments in rocketry around World War II, moving through the Space Race and the Cold War, and continuing to the Space Shuttle, the International Space Station, SpaceShipOne and commercial spaceflight. Everything on display falls into one of four categories: an actual flown object, a flight-ready backup identical to one that flew, an engineering model, or a historically accurate replica.

## Rockets of War, Rivals in Orbit

The early galleries are sobering. A restored V-1 flying bomb and a restored V-2 rocket, both authentic, show where the technology of spaceflight began, alongside the rocket engine from a Messerschmitt Me 163 Komet. A section of the Berlin Wall stands for the Cold War divide behind the race to come. Then the Soviet side of the story unfolds in a depth rare outside Russia: a flight-ready backup of Sputnik 1, an engineering model of Sputnik 2, a Vostok capsule from the program that competed with Mercury, and a Voskhod 2 capsule from the rival to Gemini. The collection also includes prototype and flown Russian spacesuits and an RD-107 engine of the kind used on the Soyuz rocket, while a full-scale replica of the Apollo-Soyuz craft recalls the moment the two rivals finally met in orbit.

## The Capsule That Sank

One American spacecraft here has a story all its own. On July 21, 1961, Gus Grissom flew Liberty Bell 7 on a 15-minute suborbital flight, becoming the second American in space. At splashdown the hatch blew prematurely, the capsule flooded, and Grissom nearly drowned before being rescued. Too heavy with seawater for the recovery helicopter, Liberty Bell 7 sank nearly three miles to the bottom of the Atlantic. There it stayed for 38 years, until a 1999 expedition led by Curt Newport and assembled by the Discovery Channel raised it on July 20 of that year. The Cosmosphere's SpaceWorks team then took it apart and restored it. It remains the only spacecraft flown by NASA that is owned by someone other than NASA or the Smithsonian.

## Hollywood's Hardware Shop

Restoration is a specialty. The Cosmosphere's SpaceWorks division has restored flown U.S. spacecraft for museums and exhibits around the globe, including pieces in the Smithsonian's own collection. Its most famous patient is also on display in Hutchinson: Odyssey, the Apollo 13 command module that brought its crew home. SpaceWorks also turned its skills to storytelling, building roughly 80 percent of the artifacts and props for the movie Apollo 13 and replica spacecraft hardware for Magnificent Desolation: Walking on the Moon 3D and the television miniseries From the Earth to the Moon. The collection even pairs a replica of the Bell X-1 Glamorous Glennis, used in filming The Right Stuff, with the flown engine from Chuck Yeager's real X-1.

## Blackbirds and Moon Rock

The flown treasures keep coming. A Lockheed SR-71 Blackbird reconnaissance plane, the Gemini 10 capsule and a docking collar from an Agena target vehicle share space with engines from the X-15 program, an authentic Apollo White Room and F-1 engine components recovered from the ocean. A full-scale replica of the left side of Space Shuttle Endeavour stands near a piece of tile from the Columbia disaster, and a Moon rock from Apollo 11, the first crewed landing, is one of the prized items on display. Visitors can also sit under the Justice Planetarium, converted to digital projection in 2015, catch a film in the Carey Digital Dome Theater, or watch the explosive live rocketry show in Dr. Goddard's Lab. The museum has weathered scandal as well: in 2005 a federal jury convicted Max Ary, its former president and chief executive, on 12 counts including theft and fraud over artifacts sold from the collection, and he was sentenced to three years in prison. Ary has said the items were accidentally mixed with his own private collection.

## Flight Context

The Cosmosphere is at 38.065°N, 97.921°W on the northeast corner of Plum Street and 11th Avenue in Hutchinson, Kansas, next to Hutchinson Community College. From 1,500-3,000 feet above ground, look for the tall Titan II and Redstone rockets standing beside the building, with the Kansas State Fairgrounds, where the museum began, elsewhere in town. Hutchinson Regional Airport (KHUT) serves the city, and Wichita Dwight D. Eisenhower National Airport (KICT) lies to the southeast. Central Kansas usually offers good visibility, with strong winds and spring and summer thunderstorms.
