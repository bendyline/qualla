# Strataca

Ninety seconds by elevator beneath Hutchinson, Kansas, Strataca lets visitors walk a working salt mine carved from a 275-million-year-old deposit, where Hollywood's film negatives are stored in the dark.

**Location:** 38.0437, -97.8676

**Categories:** Mining museums in Kansas, Salt mines in the United States, Salt museums, Hutchinson, Kansas, Museums in Reno County, Kansas

## Introduction

The ride down takes ninety seconds. Visitors crowd into a double-deck elevator, up to 28 at a time, and emerge 650 feet below the Kansas prairie in a world that never changes: 68 degrees, humidity around 45 percent, with walls and ceilings of solid salt. This is Strataca, a museum set inside the working Hutchinson Salt Company mine and carved from one of the world's largest deposits of rock salt. There are roughly 15 salt mines in the United States, but this is billed as the only one with an underground museum open to tourists.

## Salt from the Permian

The salt bed beneath Hutchinson formed about 275 million years ago, during the Permian Period, the sixth and last period of the Paleozoic Era. The museum's Geology Gallery explains the animals of that distant time and why no fossil record survives in the salt itself. The rock does preserve something small and strange, though. Throughout the mine, tiny bubbles of water are trapped inside the salt, fluid inclusions believed to date from the Permian. Because the salt contains impurities, mostly shale and anhydrite, the rock salt mined here is used mainly on roads and for feeding livestock. Elsewhere in Reno County, Cargill and Morton produce salt by a different method, solution mining and evaporation.

## Rooms and Pillars

Mining began here in 1923, when the operation was the Carey Salt Company. Miners use the room-and-pillar method, sinking a shaft to the salt and then removing it in a checkerboard: large square rooms alternate with square pillars of solid salt, each 40 feet on a side, left standing to hold up the rock above. The pattern also helps keep fresh air flowing to the working areas. The mine now spreads across about 980 acres, and if every excavated room were lined up end to end, the chamber would stretch 150 miles. Blasting breaks the salt loose, crushers reduce it, and the skip, as miners call the mine elevator, hauls four tons at a time to the surface, making a round trip every three minutes.

## Freezing an Aquifer

For decades, visitors had to squeeze into the same elevator the miners used, ten at a time, often getting sprinkled with salt, and production stopped whenever people rode. A museum needed its own shaft. The idea grew out of a long-range planning process the Reno County Historical Society began in 1998, and the society shared the shaft's cost with Underground Vaults & Storage, a company that operates in the mine. There was a formidable obstacle: an aquifer. The Equus Beds, an extension of the vast Ogallala Aquifer, lie about 130 feet thick at the site. To dig through without flooding, contractors sank 24 pipes to a depth of 150 feet and pumped in liquid nitrogen and salt brine until the ground froze. Crews then drilled, blasted and lined the shaft with concrete, 15 feet at a time, from March 2004 until the sinking was finished that December.

## Saltcrete, Mantrips and Total Darkness

Down below, the chambers range from 2,500 to 15,000 square feet with ceilings 11 to 17 feet high. Much of the flooring is Saltcrete, a concrete made with salt instead of sand that sheds a fine salt dust while it cures and blisters if it gets wet. Since 1954 everyone entering the mine has been required to wear a hard hat and rescue breather, though by the museum's account no visitor or employee has ever needed the breather, and federal mine-safety inspectors consider this one of the safest mines in the world. Galleries display mantrips, the train-like cars that once carried miners, and a Joy Loader used from the 1940s until 1983, lowered in pieces and welded back together below, since equipment that comes down usually stays down. On the optional Dark Ride tram tour, visitors pause in complete darkness, then stop at a salt pile to fill a souvenir bag. The museum took its current name, Strataca, in 2013.

## Hollywood in the Salt

The mine keeps another kind of treasure. In a secured 26-acre area closed to the public, Underground Vaults & Storage keeps valuables from all 50 states and many foreign countries, including medical records, oil and gas charts, television show masters and the original camera negatives of films such as Gone with the Wind and Ben Hur. A gallery re-creates the look of the storage operation, and a temporary exhibit of loaned movie memorabilia has included Batman and Mr. Freeze costumes from Batman & Robin, James Dean's shirt from Giant, the Snowman from the 1998 film Jack Frost and Agent Smith's costume from The Matrix.

## Flight Context

Strataca is at 38.044°N, 97.868°W at the northwest corner of Yoder Road and Avenue G on the southeast side of Hutchinson, Kansas. The museum itself is underground, so from 1,500-3,000 feet look for the mine's surface buildings at the corner of Yoder Road and Avenue G, with the rest of Hutchinson spreading to the northwest. Hutchinson Regional Airport (KHUT) serves the city, and Wichita Dwight D. Eisenhower National Airport (KICT) lies to the southeast. Central Kansas usually offers good visibility, with strong winds and spring and summer thunderstorms.
