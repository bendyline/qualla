# Warkentin House

A Mennonite miller who helped bring Turkey Red wheat to Kansas built this Victorian home in Newton in 1886–1887, and about 80 percent of its original furnishings are still inside.

**Location:** 38.0433, -97.3397

**Categories:** Historic house museums in Kansas, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Kansas, German-Russian culture in Kansas, Russian Mennonite diaspora in the United States, Newton, Kansas

## Introduction

The rugs are the reddest thing in the house. Bernhard Warkentin bought them in Russia, brought them to the Kansas plains, and laid them in the music room of the Victorian home he and his wife, Wilhelmina, built in Newton between 1886 and 1887. They have since moved to the master suite for safekeeping, but they still say something about the man who chose them: a Mennonite miller who crossed an ocean, helped put a hardy wheat into Kansas soil, and left behind a house that keeps about 80 percent of its original furnishings.

## Seed Wheat and a Miller's Daughter

Warkentin was born on June 18, 1847, in the village of Altonau in the Molotschna Mennonite settlement, in what is now Ukraine. In 1872 he came to the United States to study the country's agricultural, economic and political climate, and in 1873 he settled in Halstead, Kansas, where he built Harvey County's first grist mill and a farmstead on the Little Arkansas River. Planning that mill took him to milling operations across the Midwest. At Conrad Eisenmayer's mill in Summerfield, Illinois, he met the owner's daughter, Wilhelmina, and they married on August 12, 1875. Railroad companies, meanwhile, saw what Mennonite farmers could do for the Great Plains and urged them to settle there. Between 1874 and 1884, thousands of Mennonites emigrated from Russia to North America, many of them to Kansas, and Warkentin encouraged them to bring Turkey Red hard winter wheat. Tradition holds that families packed the seed before leaving, and historians still debate how much they carried and who first introduced the variety. What is documented is that hard winter wheat was planted around Goessel after the 1874 arrivals, and that Warkentin himself later arranged large seed shipments from Russia, in 1885 and again in 1900. That hardy, high-yield wheat went on to give Kansas its enormous productivity.

## Mills, Banks and a Newton Address

Warkentin's business grew with the wheat. He owned mills and elevators in Newton, in Halstead and in Blackwell, Oklahoma, and he helped organize the Kansas State Bank, Bethel Deaconess Hospital and Bethel College, and he served as a director of the Halstead State Bank. He and Wilhelmina raised two children, Edna and Carl, and their Newton house reflected the family's standing. His life ended far from Kansas: in 1908 he died of an accidental gunshot during a trip to the Holy Land. Wilhelmina stayed in the Newton home until her death in 1932 and left it to the Bethel Deaconesses, who used it until 1970. Ownership then passed to a group dedicated to restoring and maintaining the house, which was added to the National Register of Historic Places. The city took ownership in 1973, and the house now operates as a museum, listed on both the Kansas and National registers as a splendid example of Victorian architecture and furnishings.

## Etched Glass and Leatherette

The welcome starts at the main entrance, where a cut and etched glass panel imported from France is set into the door. Inside, English carpenters crafted the main staircase and the ball-and-spindle fretwork of the foyer, and leatherette wainscoting lines the foyer, the stairwell and a small lavatory near the front door. That lavatory let dusty travelers freshen up at an English cabinet with teardrop pulls and an Italian marble washbasin, beside an English lion-head towel holder. Each room carries its own wood. The parlor's woodwork is cherry-finished walnut, framing a fireplace with a cherry mantelpiece and Italian tiles, the music room returns to oak, and the dining room is walnut. The pocket doors between the music and dining rooms were built of two woods, each side matching its room. Crystal chandeliers from Czechoslovakia hang in the parlor and music room, where daughter Edna's Steinway upright piano stands on one of the house's many Anglo-Persian carpets.

## Ceres Above the Table

In the dining room, Italian tile on the fireplace portrays the goddess of agriculture, Demeter to the Greeks and Ceres to the Romans, a fitting presence in a home built on grain. Jeweled and stained glass windows on either side of the fireplace predate the house and are believed to be of Russian origin. The Warkentins entertained elegantly, and the china closet still holds some of their original serving pieces, the most striking of them a chocolate pot. Luxury stopped at the kitchen door. Electricity replaced gas for lighting soon after the house was finished, yet the old wood and coal cookstove stayed in use for many years. Indoor plumbing arrived two years after completion, and it ran on human effort: at the start of each day, a servant pumped water into a large copper-lined reservoir in the third-floor attic. The three kitchen sinks are slate.

## Thistles Underfoot

Upstairs, parquet floors laid in several different woods run throughout. The two-room master suite has a sitting room to the southwest, and this is where the bright red Russian rugs, patterned with thistles, now rest after being moved from the music room to preserve them. The brass double bed in the northwest bedroom belonged to the Warkentins, and the trunk at its foot went with them on their travels, though much of the bedroom furniture did not start out in this house. The library holds the most unusual fireplace of all, Italian tile again, with a strange diagonal band across the hearth. Bernhard's roll-top desk no longer stands on the east wall; Wilhelmina's oak writing desk does. His favorite chair has lion's heads on its arms and claw feet, and the coal scuttle's design echoes the etched glass of the front door. One walnut bookcase, built in Leavenworth and still holding family books, is among the few furnishings native to Kansas.

## Flight Context

The Warkentin House stands in Newton, Kansas, at approximately 38.043°N, 97.340°W, in Harvey County north of Wichita. The house itself is a residential-scale building, so descend to about 1,500-2,500 feet AGL over Newton to pick out the town's older neighborhoods; the town of Halstead, where Warkentin built his first mill on the Little Arkansas River, lies a short distance to the west. Nearby airports: Newton City-County Airport (KEWK), about 3 nm east; Wichita Dwight D. Eisenhower National Airport (KICT), about 24 nm south; and Hutchinson Municipal Airport (KHUT), about 25 nm west. Expect Wichita-area traffic, including McConnell Air Force Base (KIAB) to the south, and check winds, which can be gusty on the open plains.
