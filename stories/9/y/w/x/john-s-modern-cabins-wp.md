# John's Modern Cabins

A Depression-era Route 66 tourist court near Newburg, Missouri, bypassed by the interstate and left to the woods, whose hopeful name has become a gentle irony.

**Location:** 37.9375, -91.9444

**Categories:** Buildings and structures on U.S. Route 66, U.S. Route 66 in Missouri, Buildings and structures in Phelps County, Missouri, Unused buildings in Missouri, Roadside architecture

## Introduction

"Photograph these / while you're here / the wrecking ball / is looming near." The rhyme once ran across mock Burma-Shave signs beside old Route 66 near Newburg, Missouri, and it said what every visitor could see. John's Modern Cabins were falling apart, structurally unsound and bypassed for decades by the traffic that had once kept them in business. The name on the sign had been a promise to motorists. After half a century of abandonment it has become an unwitting irony, and the story of how that happened is the story of the highway itself.

## Six Log Cabins and a Dance Hall

It began in 1931, when Beatrice and Bill Bayless opened Bill and Bess's Place on Route 66, then the main road across this part of Missouri. They offered six log cabins and a dance hall. During the Depression, cabins and campgrounds like theirs were a popular, low-cost alternative to expensive hotels. The Bayless family sold the property in the 1940s, as the region filled with "all kinds of cabins" to house personnel around Fort Leonard Wood during World War II. The court changed hands several times before John and Lillian Dausch bought it in 1951 for $5,000. John soon earned the nickname "Sunday John" for selling beer seven days a week, in open defiance of the local laws of the day.

## When the Highway Moved On

Progress came for the cabins in stages. In 1957 the widening of Route 66 forced the property to be moved back from the original road, and the dance hall was removed; nearby Vernelle's Motel lost its restaurant and filling station to the same project. By 1965 the state was building Interstate 44 through the area. It took some of the Dausches' land, and worse, it gave travelers no easy way to reach the little court. Not long after the new highway bypassed the site, Lillian died, and John closed the business. When he died in 1971, the property was left empty. Loretta Ross of St. Charles, Missouri, bought it in 1976, but her family's plans for the site were soon abandoned, and for the next 25 years the cabins sat untouched, slowly falling apart.

## Photograph These

In 2002 the Ross family proposed demolishing the unstable buildings. Route 66 preservationists fought back after the issue was publicized in Route 66 Magazine and online, and the demolition was stopped, though nothing was done to halt the decay. By 2011 the roof of John's larger living cabin had collapsed, along with two of the newer cabins he had built. The road itself has faded too. This stretch of old 66 is usable but no longer maintained, and the two-lane pavement dead-ends at a demolished bridge near the ghost town of Arlington. Around 2005 Interstate 44 was realigned away from its original 1960s path, which now survives as an abandoned pair of gravel roads, so the cabins can no longer be seen from the freeway at all. For years, photographers came anyway, expecting each visit might be the last.

## One Cabin Left

By early 2024, according to Route 66 News, only one cabin still stood, shored up in recent years by the president of the Route 66 Association of Missouri. That February the site changed hands again, sold by the family of longtime owner Loretta Ross to a couple who also own the nearby Vernelle's Motel. The new owners announced plans to restore the John's neon sign, put a carport over the surviving cabin to shield it from the weather, and add two or three cabins for overnight stays. Whether the plans come to pass or not, it is no longer safe to enter what remains, and the clearing beside the old road still carries a name full of hope.

## Flight Context

John's Modern Cabins sit at approximately 37.94°N, 91.94°W beside an old alignment of Route 66 near Newburg in Phelps County, in the wooded Ozark hills west of Rolla. The ruins themselves are hidden under trees and too small to pick out from the air; from 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, use Interstate 44 and the Gasconade River valley near the Arlington ghost-town site as references. Rolla Downtown Airport (FAA identifier K07) lies about 7 miles to the east, and Rolla National Airport (KVIH) at Vichy is farther to the northeast. Fort Leonard Wood's restricted areas lie to the southwest, so check airspace before flying west along the interstate. Expect valley fog on calm mornings.
