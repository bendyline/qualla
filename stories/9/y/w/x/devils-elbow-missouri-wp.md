# Devils Elbow, Missouri

A hamlet tucked inside a hairpin bend of the Big Piney River, named by timber rafters who lost their loads there, and home to a rare curved Route 66 bridge that its county fought for years to save.

**Location:** 37.8458, -92.0639

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in Pulaski County, Missouri, U.S. Route 66 in Missouri, Bridges, Ozarks, Rivers

## Introduction

The railroad ties came down the Big Piney River lashed into rafts, floated from the forests upstream toward the railroad yard at Arlington on the Gasconade. Before they got there, the rafters had to get through one tight, looping bend where the river doubled back on itself. Rafts sometimes broke apart there, and ties were lost to the current. The men who worked the river called it a "devil of an elbow," and the name stuck to the bend and to the hamlet that later grew up on its inside curve. Devils Elbow, Missouri, about five miles east of St. Robert, has been a lumber route, a sportsman's retreat and a Route 66 stop, and it still has the bridge to prove it.

## Timber on the Big Piney

Through the 19th and early 20th centuries, lumbermen worked the country upstream, cutting shortleaf pine and hacking railroad ties out of the hardwoods. The river was their highway. Rafters floated the planks and ties down the Big Piney toward the Gasconade, and the bend at Devils Elbow was the obstacle that tested them, a tight meander incised into the Ozark bedrock. The same scenery that made the bend dangerous made it beautiful. Beginning in the late 19th century, St. Louis sportsmen discovered the Big Piney as a fine fishing stream and the surrounding forest as good hunting ground. Before improved highway access, they rode the train to Dixon, 12 miles north, and finished the trip by wagon. Early motorists who followed took State Highway 14, part gravel and part dirt, which ran between St. Louis and Springfield.

## Steel Across the Bend

In 1923, as part of improving Highway 14, a steel truss bridge went up across the lower portion of the elbow. Three years later, in 1926, Highway 14 was renumbered U.S. Highway 66, and the quiet river bend suddenly sat on one of the country's great roads. In 1927 Walter E. Graham opened a post office named Devil's Elbow inside the office and store of his Graham's Resort and Camp, a quarter mile south of the bridge. A few years later the Munger-Moss Sandwich Shop, named for Nelle Munger and her husband, went up on the north side of the bridge, and Dwight Rench built the native-rock Devil's Elbow Cafe on the south side. Graham and Rench rented cabins to the growing stream of tourists. By the 1930s the village was a regular stop on Route 66, and the State Planning Commission listed its bluffs among the seven beauty spots of Missouri.

## Boom, Then Bypass

World War II brought a rush of people. Fort Leonard Wood was built five miles to the west, and Devils Elbow filled with new rental cabins, small houses and a two-story market and apartment building. The boom did not last on the old road. In late 1945 a divided four-lane section of Route 66 opened, bypassing the community to the north, and traffic fell away. The Munger-Moss business, for one, moved west to Lebanon that year and grew into the Munger Moss Motel. Devils Elbow stayed where it was, and so did its piece of the original 1926 alignment, now called Teardrop Road. Today three bridges cross the Big Piney here: the modern Interstate 44 span, the later Route 66 alignment on Highway Z made possible by the Hooker Cut through a steep hillside, and the original bridge on Teardrop Road.

## Saving the Dogleg Bridge

That original bridge is the heart of the place. At 588 feet of steel truss, it is one of only two surviving Route 66 bridges with a dogleg curve, and the only one still open to traffic; the other, the Chain of Rocks Bridge in St. Louis, is closed to vehicles. Its last major maintenance came in the 1970s, and rust kept eating at the steel. When Route 66 was decommissioned in 1985, the bridge became Pulaski County's responsibility. In 2002 county commissioners committed to saving it, but an early estimate of $2.5 million far exceeded local resources. The answer took years and six funding partners, from the Federal Highway Administration to the county itself. Work began with a groundbreaking on October 24, 2013, and partly by sealing the lead-based paint in place instead of stripping it, the project came in at $1.3 million. The bridge reopened on May 22, 2014, and in April 2017 it became part of the Devil's Elbow Historic District on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Flight Context

Devils Elbow lies at approximately 37.85°N, 92.06°W, on the inside of a sharp meander of the Big Piney River in Pulaski County, a few miles above the river's junction with the Gasconade. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL the hairpin loop of the Big Piney between wooded bluffs is the unmistakable landmark, with the Interstate 44 bridge crossing the river nearby. Fort Leonard Wood lies about five miles to the west; Waynesville-St. Robert Regional Airport at Forney Field (KTBN), a joint public and military-use field on the post, is roughly 8 miles to the southwest. Check for active restricted airspace over the fort's ranges. River valleys here often hold fog on cool, calm mornings.
