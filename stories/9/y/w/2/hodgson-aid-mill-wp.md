# Hodgson-Aid Mill

A red timber gristmill on Bryant Creek, powered by a spring pouring from the base of an Ozark cliff, that gave its name to a flour brand sold far beyond Ozark County.

**Location:** 36.7078, -92.2664

**Categories:** Grinding mills on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Buildings and structures in Ozark County, Missouri, Ozarks, Industrial history, Springs

## Introduction

In 1969 Ken and Teena Harrington, a couple who loved hunting for antiques, were visiting Ozark County when they stumbled on an old red mill beside Bryant Creek. At the foot of a nearby cliff, a spring poured out to fill its mill pond. The mill was barely working, grinding only a couple of hundred pounds of flour a day. The Harringtons took over the lease anyway. Within a few years the name on that building, Hodgson, was on flour sold to grocers and wholesalers, and the old mill near the hamlet of Sycamore was straining to keep up.

## Where the Spring Leaves the Rock

Everything here begins with Hodgson Spring. It discharges at the base of a cliff, where a limestone barrel vault was built over the outflow, and the water feeds a man-made mill pond beside the mill. Estimates of the flow vary by source, but all put it above 20 million gallons a day. The first mill on the site, according to regional histories, was built by William Holeman in 1861. In 1884 Alva Hodgson and his mother, Mary Elizabeth Hodgson, bought the mill for $500 from Manuel and Elizabeth Smith, whose daughter, also named Mary Elizabeth, would later become Alva's wife. Alva kept Holeman's old mill running, but he was already planning something better.

## A Wager on White Flour

Around 1870 the invention of the "middlings purifier" had made it possible to produce white, or "patent," flour, a higher-quality flour than had been produced before. Hodgson wanted a state-of-the-art mill that could make it. He fitted his new building with roller mills and two vertical steel water turbines, known as Leffel turbines, a type now called Francis turbines. On November 25, 1897, the local paper reported that "Mr. Hodgson is making a good grade of flour on his new roller mill," and around that time his brother George bought a one-third interest. The new building did far more than grind grain. It also housed a general store, the Sycamore post office, a sawmill and a cotton gin, and like other rural gristmills it was likely the economic heart of the community.

## From Lease to Label

In 1934 businessman Charles Theodore Aid bought the mill, and it stayed in the Aid family until 1998, which is how it came to carry both names. It was on Aid property that the Harringtons began, selling antiques, renting canoes for the creek and offering flour and other products in their store. Demand grew so quickly that they incorporated as Hodgson Mill Inc. By 1973 sales had risen 500 percent, and the old building was straining at its maximum of a million pounds of wheat and corn a year. The couple added two more buhrstones and bought a site in the industrial park at nearby Gainesville for a modern plant. In 1976 production moved there, and large-scale milling at the old mill ended.

## Patching the Old Frame

Since then the mill has operated only now and then, mostly as an attraction for visitors, and sometime in the 1970s a concrete-block building went up beside it. The mill itself has kept its old character: a timber frame sheathed in red-painted weatherboards, the pond at its side and the stone vault at the cliff. It goes by several names, Hodgson Water Mill and Aid-Hodgson Mill among them. Later owners Hank and Jean Macler raised money in the community to restore it. They stabilized the foundation, brought in Amish craftspeople to repair damage left by floods and installed equipment to bottle the spring water. They also registered the building, and in 2002 the Hodgson-Aid Mill was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. It remains privately owned.

## Flight Context

The mill sits at approximately 36.71°N, 92.27°W on Bryant Creek near Sycamore in Ozark County, in the forested hills of the southern Missouri Ozarks. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, follow the winding, tree-lined course of Bryant Creek and look for the small mill pond and the red mill building tucked against a bluff; it is a small target in heavily wooded terrain, so a GPS fix helps. Gainesville, where Hodgson Mill production moved in 1976, lies to the southwest. West Plains Regional Airport (KUNO) is the nearest public airport with services, roughly 20–25 miles to the northeast. Ridges and valleys here can hold fog on cool mornings, and summer brings haze and pop-up thunderstorms.
