# Alley Spring, Missouri

In the Shannon County hills, a spring pouring out more than 80 million gallons a day once turned the turbine of the famous red Alley Mill, the heart of a small Ozark hamlet now preserved within the Ozark National Scenic Riverways.

**Location:** 37.1442, -91.4439

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in Shannon County, Missouri, Unincorporated communities in Missouri, Bodies of water of Shannon County, Missouri, Springs of Missouri

## Introduction

The Old Red Mill was not always red. When it went up in the 1890s beside a spring in the Missouri Ozarks, the building was left unpainted. Later it wore white with green trim, and only after that did it take on the deep red of Alley Mill, a building said to be one of the most photographed mills in the world. Today the mill stands at the heart of Alley Spring, a tiny unincorporated community in Shannon County, six miles west of Eminence on Route 106, inside the Ozark National Scenic Riverways.

## A Name Too Long for the Mail

The place went through several names before it found one short enough to keep. It was first called Mammoth Spring, and later Barksdale Spring. Both names were deemed too long by the Post Office Department of the time, so the village was renamed for a prominent local citizen, John Alley, a miller and the original owner of the town site. A post office called Alley opened in 1884. In 1950 its name was changed to Alley Spring, and in 1974 the post office closed for good. Mail for the community now comes from Eminence. The name, though, has outlasted the post office that gave it official standing, and it lives on in the spring, the mill and the hamlet around them.

## Power From the Spring

Everything here begins with the water. Alley Spring discharges more than 80 million gallons a day, a flow strong enough to run machinery, and people put it to work early. A mill stood at the site as early as 1868. The building that stands today was constructed in 1893 and 1894 by George Washington McCaskill as a merchant mill. Its power came from a 35-inch Leffel turbine set in a pit beneath the back porch, driven by water from the spring. The mill ground both wheat and corn, turning the spring's steady force into flour and meal for the surrounding hills.

## Wheat Mill in Corn Country

For all its beauty, the mill was never a great business. It was designed to produce wheat flour in an area where corn was the main crop, a mismatch that hindered its profits. Recurring floods added to the trouble, and the operation was only marginally successful. In 1902 John Knotts bought the 80-acre property and expanded it with a store and a blacksmith shop. Alley became more than a mill. It was a home, a farm and a school, and it was a gathering place, hosting dances, baseball games and even roller skating. For the families scattered through these hills, the little hamlet at the spring was where community happened.

## The Hamlet Preserved

Today the mill is part of the Ozark National Scenic Riverways and operates as a museum of Ozarks history. Nearby, a one-room schoolhouse and a general store round out the feeling of a restored historic hamlet. The National Park Service runs a large campground at Alley, and a small convenience store and canoe rental serve visitors. Canoeing is very popular on the Jacks Fork River, which runs through Alley. On the second weekend of October in recent years, the community has hosted the Haunting in the Hills, a free event of traditional Ozark demonstrations capped by a storytelling program called Tales from Ozark's Dark Side, when the old mill's red walls glow against the autumn woods.

## Flight Context

Alley Spring lies at 37.1442°N, 91.4439°W in Shannon County, Missouri, at about 1,330 feet elevation along the Jacks Fork River, six miles west of Eminence on Route 106. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, follow the winding Jacks Fork valley west from Eminence; the red mill beside its spring pool and the National Park Service campground mark the spot among heavily forested Ozark hills. The area lies within the Ozark National Scenic Riverways, where pilots are asked to stay at least 2,000 feet above the surface to limit noise. West Plains Regional Airport (KUNO) is roughly 27 nm to the southwest. Morning valley fog is common, especially in spring and fall.
