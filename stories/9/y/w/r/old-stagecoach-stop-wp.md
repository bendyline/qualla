# Old Stagecoach Stop

A log stagecoach stop on Waynesville's town square that became a Civil War hospital, a century-old hotel for soldiers and Route 66 travelers, and finally a museum saved by bake sales.

**Location:** 37.8294, -92.2000

**Categories:** Hotel buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Buildings and structures in Pulaski County, Missouri, U.S. Route 66, American Civil War sites, Museums

## Introduction

In December 1982 the City of Waynesville sent the owner of a vacant old hotel on the town square a condemnation notice: the building violated an ordinance against "hazardous buildings." Only two years earlier it had been listed on the National Register of Historic Places. It was reputed to be the oldest original building in Pulaski County, it had outlived a war, a stage line and more than a century of owners, and now it was in danger of being torn down. Over its life it had been called the Waynesville House, the Johnson House, the Black Hotel and the Tourist Hotel. Today it goes by the name that tells where it all began: the Old Stagecoach Stop.

## Two Log Pens and a Breezeway

William Walton McDonald built it in the early 1850s on the east side of the square as a one-story, double-pen log building, two rooms joined by an open passage known as a dogtrot. The dogtrot was soon enclosed, making three rooms. Before the Civil War the building served as a stagecoach stop and post office, and the South-Western Stage Company served Waynesville until the railroad crossed Pulaski County in 1869. The ground here already held a painful past. A short walk from the square, along spring-fed Roubidoux Creek, groups of Cherokee people camped in 1837 and again in 1839 during their forced removal from their homelands to what is now Oklahoma; Laughlin Park there is a certified site of the Trail of Tears.

## A Hospital Below the Fort

War reached the square in June 1862, when the 13th Missouri State Militia occupied Waynesville under Colonel Albert Sigel. The regiment was reorganized and renumbered the 5th Missouri State Militia in March 1863, and Sigel built an earthen and wooden palisade fort on the hill overlooking the county seat. He also commandeered McDonald's building for the post hospital, so the log rooms that had served stage passengers and the local post office now served the garrison's sick and wounded. Union soldiers occupied Waynesville for the rest of the conflict, holding the town until the war ended in 1865, and the building came out of the war still standing.

## Ten Rooms for Soldiers and Travelers

In 1870 Alexander Bryan bought the building, added a second story and a rear lean-to, and turned the little stage stop and tavern into the ten-room Waynesville House. Over the next 110 years it passed through a long line of owners and sheltered soldiers during both world wars, the Korean War and the early years of the Vietnam War, as well as tourists driving Route 66. Then the world outgrew it. In the 1950s and 1960s, as nearby Fort Leonard Wood reopened and expanded, new motels went up with amenities the old hotel could not match, and its owner made no substantial improvements. By the late 1960s it stood empty. Through the 1970s it kept decaying, its history unknown to the transient military population but valued by longtime residents. In 1980 it was rechristened the Old Stagecoach Stop and placed on the National Register.

## Saved One Bake Sale at a Time

National Register status did not rescue the building; its neighbors did. In June 1983 local citizens formed the Old Stagecoach Stop Foundation to buy it and preserve it so that "its educational value" could be "appreciated by future generations." For nine years they held fundraisers ranging from bake sales to concerts, until the original $50,000 note was paid off in the spring of 1992. They kept going. Restoring the interior and buying the rest of the historic property, out to Highway 17, took more than twice the original purchase price. Today the old hotel is a house museum, open on Saturdays from April through October, with each of its ten rooms restored to reflect a period or use from its history. The foundation also publishes an annual heritage newspaper, the Old Settlers Gazette.

## Flight Context

The Old Stagecoach Stop stands on the east side of the Waynesville town square at approximately 37.83°N, 92.20°W, in the Ozark hills of Pulaski County. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, look for the small courthouse square in the valley of Roubidoux Creek, with historic Route 66 running through town and Interstate 44 passing nearby; the large cantonment of Fort Leonard Wood spreads out to the south. Waynesville-St. Robert Regional Airport at Forney Field (KTBN), a joint public and military-use field on Fort Leonard Wood, lies about 7 miles to the south-southeast. Check for active restricted airspace over the Fort Leonard Wood ranges before flying the area. Valleys can hold morning fog, and summer brings haze and thunderstorms.
