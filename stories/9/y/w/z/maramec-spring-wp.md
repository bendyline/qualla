# Maramec Spring

Nearly 100 million gallons a day rise at Maramec Spring near St. James, Missouri, water that once powered the first commercially viable ironworks west of the Mississippi and now feeds a trout park kept open to the public by a family foundation.

**Location:** 37.9556, -91.5325

## Introduction

The water rising at Maramec Spring once had a job to do. For fifty years, from 1826 to 1876, its flow helped drive the machinery of the Maramec Iron Works, turning high-grade ore from a nearby pit into iron, including iron for cannonballs and gunboats during the Civil War. The furnaces are ruins now, and the spring's work is gentler. Nearly 100 million gallons a day still well up here in the east-central Ozarks near St. James, Missouri, feeding a trout stream, a hatchery and a park that a descendant of the ironworks' founders left open to the public.

## A River From Underground

Maramec Spring sits beside the Meramec River in karst country, a landscape of dissolving rock riddled with springs and caves. Water moves through the stone underground and emerges here in volume. The spring's average discharge is 153 cubic feet per second, nearly 100 million gallons every day, which places it among the largest springs in Missouri. That steady flood of spring water gives the place its character. In October 1971 the spring was declared a National Natural Landmark. The branch that runs from the spring down to the river is popular with trout anglers, and the Missouri Department of Conservation operates a trout hatchery and fishery right at the spring.

## Iron West of the Mississippi

Thomas James and Samuel Massey founded the Maramec Iron Works here in 1826. It became the first commercially viable iron facility in the United States west of the Mississippi River. The operation brought together everything iron-making needed. High-grade hematite came from a pit nearby, and the spring's flow was harnessed to help power the machinery that processed it. For half a century the works produced iron in these hills, and the people who ran its furnaces and dug its ore left their mark on the land. Some of them never left. A cemetery in the park is the final resting place of workers from the iron works, a quiet reminder of the labor behind the ruins.

## Iron for a War

When the Civil War came, Maramec's iron went to the fight. The works produced iron for cannonballs and for the gunships of James B. Eads, which were built in St. Louis. Ore dug from a pit in the Ozarks, worked with the power of a spring, ended up in warships built for the fight on the rivers. Production continued until 1876. Today the ruins of the Maramec Iron Works still stand beside the spring, and the Maramec Iron Works District was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1969. A drive through the park passes a scenic overlook, the workers' cemetery, the old iron pit mine and the sites of a few homesteads, offering a glimpse of life here while the furnaces were running.

## A Family Gift

The spring's modern life began with a descendant of its first industrialists. Lucy Wortham James, whose ancestors had launched the ironworks, acquired the property, and at her death in 1938 she provided for it to become a public park, which opened in 1947. She reportedly considered it the most beautiful spot in Missouri. The James Foundation now owns the spring and more than 1,500 acres around it and maintains the land as a public park. A museum at the site tells the story of the spring and the iron works, and a second museum displays agricultural tools used in the area over the years. The park has 58 campsites, 30 of them with electric hookups, along with picnic areas, six reservable shelters and several playgrounds. In 2026, the ironworks marks two hundred years since its founding.

## Flight Context

Maramec Spring lies at 37.9556°N, 91.5325°W in Phelps County, Missouri, beside the Meramec River about five miles southeast of St. James and Interstate 44. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, follow the winding Meramec River valley through wooded Ozark hills; the park's cleared grounds, spring branch and trout-rearing pools stand out near the river. Rolla National Airport (KVIH) at Vichy lies about 15 nm to the northwest. The terrain is rolling and forested, and fog often fills the river valleys on cool mornings, especially in spring and fall.
