# Munger-Moss Motel

Born as a Route 66 sandwich shop at Devil's Elbow and rebuilt as a cabin court in Lebanon, Missouri, the Munger Moss has outlasted wartime bypasses, chain competition and the interstate, its neon relit in 2010.

**Location:** 37.6864, -92.6400

**Categories:** U.S. Route 66 in Missouri, Motels in the United States, Buildings and structures on U.S. Route 66, Hotel buildings completed in 1946, Buildings and structures in Laclede County, Missouri

## Introduction

The name on the sign belongs to a couple. The Munger-Moss Sandwich Shop was named for its original owner, Nelle Munger, and her second husband, Emmett Moss, and by around 1936 it was feeding travelers on U.S. Route 66 beside the Big Piney River at Devil's Elbow, Missouri. The business was sold in 1945, and the restaurant and filling station that once anchored it are gone. Yet their joined surnames still hang in neon over a motel in Lebanon, Missouri, a place that rode out every upheaval Route 66 threw at it.

## Following the Traffic

A new highway moved the Munger Moss. Construction of a four-lane bypass of Route 66 to Fort Leonard Wood drew traffic away from the original barbecue site at Devil's Elbow, where the Elbow Inn stands today. After World War II, in 1945, the business was sold to Pete and Jesse Hudson, who kept the name and in September of that year relocated it to the former Chicken Shanty site in Lebanon. In 1946 they expanded with tourist cabins. The lodgings began as a 14-room cabin court, an early motel-like form in which each pair of rooms stood as its own free-standing building, with carports in the middle. It was a design of its moment. Just as cabins and motor courts had replaced the campgrounds that served Depression-era motorists, motels with many rooms under one roof would replace individual cabins in the 1950s, and the carports and open ground between cabins would soon fill with additional rooms.

## Neon Against the Chains

The postwar years brought a huge increase in tourism, as automakers returned to civilian production and wartime rationing of fuel and tires ended. The 1950s also brought competition, both from other independents and from chains such as Holiday Inn, founded in 1952 in Memphis, Tennessee. The Munger Moss answered with elaborate neon signage to rival the chain's Great Sign, along with in-room televisions, air conditioning and an outdoor swimming pool. Then came the interstate, and here the Munger Moss proved fortunate. Interstate 44 closely parallels old 66 at Lebanon, and the nearest off-ramp lies within half a mile of the motel. Freeway construction meant disruption, but the Munger Moss stayed open as an independent local motel and even added 26 more units in 1961. In June 1971, it passed to new owners.

## Relighting the Sign

The Munger Moss grew well beyond its fourteen original rooms, to 44 rooms and 16 efficiencies. The Lehman family, who bought it in 1971, ran it for more than fifty years; after Ramona Lehman's death in 2023, a company bought the motel in October 2024, and in 2025 the city of Lebanon took over conservation of the sign. Its signature, fittingly, is the neon. In 2010, the motel's neon signage was restored with the help of an $11,300 National Park Service cost-share grant, part of larger efforts to preserve the U.S. Route 66 historic corridor. At dusk the tubes still spell out two surnames first joined at a sandwich shop on a bend of the Big Piney River, a reminder that on Route 66, a business survived by knowing when to follow the road and when to hold its ground.

## Flight Context

The motel is at approximately 37.69°N, 92.64°W in Lebanon, Laclede County, Missouri, on the old Route 66 corridor close to Interstate 44. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, use I-44 as your reference; old Route 66 runs close alongside it here. The motel itself is a low building that is hard to pick out, but the highway corridor through town is easy to follow. Floyd W.
