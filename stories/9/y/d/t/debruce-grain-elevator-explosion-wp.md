# DeBruce Grain Elevator explosion

On June 8, 1998, a single seized bearing ignited grain dust inside what was then the world's largest grain elevator, outside Wichita, and the explosions that raced through its galleries killed seven men and changed how America guards against dust.

**Location:** 37.5878, -97.4137

**Categories:** Industrial fires and explosions in the United States, Dust explosions, 1998 industrial disasters, Disasters in Kansas, History of Wichita, Kansas, Occupational safety and health

## Introduction

It began, investigators believe, with a bearing that stopped turning. Inside the DeBruce Grain Elevator, southwest of Wichita, Kansas, a roller bearing starved of lubrication seized, locking its roller in place while a conveyor belt kept running over it. The friction heated the roller until the grain dust packed inside it caught fire. At 9:18 on the morning of June 8, 1998, that small fire became a series of dust explosions that tore through the complex. Seven workers died and ten others were trapped. What happened next led to significant changes in agricultural safety practices across the United States.

## Half a Mile of Silos

The elevator was built between 1953 and 1955 by Chalmers & Borton for the Garvey Grain Company, about 4 miles southwest of the city. It was immense. At 2,716 feet long, more than half a mile, with hundreds of storage silos, it was reported at the time of the accident, in the Guinness Book of Records, as the world's largest grain elevator. By 1998 it belonged to the DeBruce Grain Company, and on the morning of the accident its employees shared the site with workers from several contractors, among them Labor Source Incorporated, Industrial Maintenance Inc. and two trucking firms. A structure built to hold that much grain carries a hazard in its very purpose: grain dust, allowed to build up in enclosed spaces, can burn with explosive speed once something ignites it.

## The Path of the Fire

Unsafe amounts of grain dust had accumulated throughout the elevator, and that gave the first blast a fuse to follow. The explosion traveled to the head house, the tall working tower at the heart of the complex, then shot in both directions along the north and south galleries. What happened next depended on housekeeping. The north gallery had recently been cleaned, and the damage there was minimal. In the south gallery, the destruction was catastrophic, with the explosion driving on through empty silos and down into the basement. Every casualty occurred in the south array and the head house. Four men died in the west tunnel of the south array, one outside it, one outside the east face of the head house and one inside the head house itself.

## Seven Men

The seven men who died worked for two companies. From DeBruce Grain came Howard Goin, 65; Jose Luis Duarte, 41; and Lanny Owen, 43. From Labor Source Incorporated came four young men in their twenties: Jose Prajedes Ortiz, 24; Noel Najera, 25; Raymundo Diaz-Vela, 23; and Victor Manuel Castaneda, 26. They ranged in age from 23 to 65, and they had come to work on a Monday morning like any other. For one family, the wait for news lasted more than a month: the last missing worker was not recovered until five weeks after the explosion.

## Down From the Silo Tops

First responders arrived within ten minutes. Survivors climbed, or were helped, onto the tops of the silos, and Sedgwick County fire rescue crews used a crane borrowed from a local company to lower them to the ground. At one point a U.S. Army helicopter from Fort Riley lifted an injured worker off the gallery roof of the south array. The first day's rescues took about four hours. With workers still missing the next day, President Bill Clinton declared the accident a federal emergency, clearing the way for FEMA to join the rescue and recovery effort. The agency sent 20 trained searchers and 42 support personnel to work alongside the local crews searching the wrecked structure.

## What the Dust Taught

The disaster brought a new investigative body into being, the Grain Elevator Explosion Investigation Team, an independent group of engineers sponsored by OSHA, and its investigators arrived two weeks after the accident to lead the inquiry. In February 2001, DeBruce Grain settled the OSHA citations, agreeing to pay $685,000 in penalties. In 2010 the Omaha-based grain company Gavilon bought DeBruce Grain's grain-handling facilities and other assets, later accounts name Viterra as the owner, and the elevator itself is still in operation. The clearest lesson of June 8 was written in the damage itself. The gallery that had recently been cleaned came through with minimal harm, while the dust elsewhere carried the blast to where the men were working.

## Flight Context

The DeBruce elevator stands at approximately 37.59°N, 97.41°W, about 4 miles southwest of Wichita, Kansas. Its enormous length, more than half a mile of silos, makes it one of the most distinctive structures in the area from 2,000-3,000 ft AGL. It lies very close to Wichita Dwight D. Eisenhower National Airport (KICT), about 4 nm to the north, so expect to be inside Wichita's terminal airspace and in contact with approach. McConnell Air Force Base (KIAB) is about 7 nm east-northeast, and Westport Airport (FAA identifier 71K, no ICAO code) is about 4 nm north-northeast. Flat terrain and long summer visibility are typical, with afternoon thunderstorms in the warm months.
