# Joyland Amusement Park (Wichita, Kansas)

Founded in 1949 around a miniature steam train, Wichita's Joyland grew into what was reportedly central Kansas's largest theme park, with a classic wooden coaster, before closing in 2006 and slowly vanishing piece by piece.

**Location:** 37.6412, -97.3039

**Categories:** Defunct amusement parks in the United States, Amusement parks in Kansas, Buildings and structures in Wichita, Kansas, Demolished buildings and structures in Kansas, Roller coasters

## Introduction

For more than a decade, nobody could say where Louie the Clown had gone. The animatronic clown had once played the Wurlitzer organ at Joyland Amusement Park in Wichita, Kansas, and on February 19, 2015, the Wichita Police Department announced that he was coming home. By then there was not much of a home left. Joyland, which opened in 1949 and was reportedly once the largest theme park in central Kansas, had closed for good in 2006, and its rides and buildings had been worn down by vandalism, looting, fire and weather. Within months of Louie's return, the park's wooden roller coaster would be gone too.

## A Home for a Little Train

Joyland began with a locomotive. Lester Ottaway founded the park on June 12, 1949, to house a miniature steam engine that ran on 12-inch-gauge track. The first site was at 1515 East Central, but the park soon moved to its lasting home at 2801 South Hillside. After Ottaway died in the mid-1950s, his three sons, Herbert, Harold and Eddie, kept it running. When the brothers retired from the business in the early 1970s, they sold Joyland to Stanley and Margaret Nelson, who would own it for more than thirty years. The original train retired with the Ottaways. In its place came the first C. P. Huntington miniature train ever built by Chance Rides, and under the Nelsons a large share of the park's rides were added.

## The Coaster Classic

The heart of Joyland was its 1949 wooden roller coaster, built by the Philadelphia Toboggan Company and designed by Herbert Paul Schmeck. For most of its life it was called simply "Roller Coaster." Its track ran 2,600 feet, with an 80-foot drop and a top speed of 50 miles per hour, and it was one of the last surviving original wooden coasters. By one count, the American Coaster Enthusiasts had designated 44 rides as Coaster Classics, and Joyland's was one of 33 still standing. It held one more reported distinction: it was said to be the only remaining coaster in North America still running vintage rolling stock with fixed lap bars.

## The Whacky Shack and Friends

Around the coaster, Joyland offered a go-cart track and fifteen other rides, among them Dodgems bumper cars, a Ferris wheel, a giant slide, the Log Jam log flume, a Paratrooper, a Scrambler, a Skycoaster, a Tilt-A-Whirl, the Zumur swing ride by Chance Rides and the Round Up, a 1960s-era Hrubetz spinning ride. The Whacky Shack, a dark ride designed by Bill Tracy, was one of many attractions added under the Nelsons, and it carried a bittersweet footnote. It was Tracy's last project. He died in August 1974, only a few months after the ride was finished.

## The Gates Close

In April 2004, a 13-year-old girl fell 30 feet from the Ferris wheel and was seriously injured, and the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission investigated. Amid economic troubles and safety concerns, the park stayed closed through the 2004 and 2005 seasons. Hope returned in 2006, when the Seattle-based T-Rex Group leased Joyland to restore and reopen parts of it. The coaster received $10,000 in wood repairs and a new name, "The Nightmare," and the Log Jam got new pumps and a fresh coat of paint. A group called Restore Hope joined in, aiming to rebuild the park through community effort. It was not enough. After financial troubles during the 2006 season, Joyland closed permanently, and vandalism and looting followed. "We're sick. Our hearts are just sick," owner Margaret Nelson said. "It's not easy, not easy." Stanley Nelson died in 2010 at 87.

## Piece by Piece

The park came apart slowly. In August 2012, a former maintenance building burned; the rides were spared, and police suspected arson. In 2014, Margaret Nelson Spear donated the carousel to Wichita's Botanica gardens, where it was fully restored, and the park's parking-lot sign and marquee were sold to the Historic Preservation Alliance of Wichita and Sedgwick County, which later bought the Whacky Shack and a horse-and-buggy ride as well. On the night of April 2, 2015, a windstorm damaged the coaster's elevated track, and by mid-April what remained of the coaster was being dismantled. A fire destroyed the Whacky Shack in August 2018. That November, the 57-acre site sold at auction for $198,000 to a buyer who asked to stay anonymous and was later identified as Greg Dunnegan; in 2019 he began clearing the grounds for what he called the Joyland Event Complex. Joyland survives in other forms: on the cover of Andy McKee's album Joyland, and in the 2018 PBS documentary Joyland: Reliving the Memories.
