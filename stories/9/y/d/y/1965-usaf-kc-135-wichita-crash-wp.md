# 1965 USAF KC-135 Wichita crash

On January 16, 1965, a fuel-laden KC-135 tanker lost control three minutes after leaving McConnell Air Force Base and crashed into a northeast Wichita neighborhood, killing seven airmen and twenty-three people on the ground in the deadliest aviation disaster in Kansas.

## Introduction

Piatt Memorial Park is easy to overlook, a small patch of green on a residential street in northeast Wichita. Its monument is not. Cut from black granite in the shape of an airplane, it carries thirty names. The park exists because of three minutes on the morning of January 16, 1965, when a U.S. Air Force KC-135 Stratotanker heavy with jet fuel lifted off from McConnell Air Force Base, lost control, and fell onto the neighborhood at 20th and Piatt. Seven airmen and twenty-three people on the ground were killed. It is the deadliest aviation disaster ever to occur in Kansas.

## Three Minutes After Takeoff

The tanker, a KC-135A with the serial number 57-1442, had a routine-sounding job that Saturday: to refuel, in midair, a modified B-52 bomber that was being tested. It took off from McConnell shortly before 9:30 a.m. Central time and never got more than a few hundred feet off the ground. As it turned northwest over Oliver Street, the jet began to yaw, swinging unstably from side to side. The crew reportedly tried to bring it back to the base. Over Wichita State University they began dumping fuel from the aircraft's massive refueling tanks, an attempt to lighten the struggling aircraft. Then the tanker banked hard to the left and entered a roll the crew could not recover from. It came down at the intersection of Piatt and 20th Street, just three minutes after leaving the runway.

## Fire on Piatt Street

The KC-135 carried a large load of jet fuel, up to 31,000 gallons by some accounts, and the crash set off a massive explosion and fire. It struck a working-class, mostly African American neighborhood where families were just beginning their Saturday. Fire engulfed the houses around the impact, and a dozen homes were destroyed. All seven crew members aboard the tanker died. On the ground, twenty-three more people were killed, residents who had been at home on an ordinary winter morning. Each of them had a name, a family and a place on those blocks, and neighbors who survived lost homes and loved ones in the same instant. Only one aircraft accident in the United States has killed more people on the ground: the 1994 Green Ramp disaster, in which twenty-four people on the ground died.

## A Failure No Crew Could Fix

The Air Force investigation traced the disaster to a malfunction in the tanker's rudder control system, a failure it described as impossible for the crew to overcome. Seen in that light, the final minutes read differently. The yaw that set in after takeoff, the turn back toward McConnell, the fuel pouring from the tanks over the university were all signs of a crew fighting an airplane that would no longer obey. The rudder problem left them no way out, and in the space of three minutes the emergency overhead became a catastrophe on the ground. The seven airmen who died that morning had been working the problem until the end.

## Names in Black Granite

Wichita earned high marks for its immediate response to the disaster. But displaced residents later said that in the days and weeks that followed, the help began to fade. In 1971 the city opened Piatt Memorial Park on a small parcel at the crash site, at 2037 North Piatt. The monument came much later. On July 14, 2007, more than forty-two years after the crash, an airplane-shaped monument of Imperial Black Granite, twelve feet by twenty-two feet, was unveiled at a ceremony. Inscribed on it are the names of all thirty people who died: the seven members of the crew and the twenty-three neighbors who were simply at home. It took decades, but every one of them is now remembered together.

## Flight Context

The crash site, now Piatt Memorial Park, lies at 37.7202°N, 97.3131°W near 20th Street and Piatt in northeast Wichita, Kansas. From the air, use the Wichita State University campus about a mile to the east and downtown Wichita's skyline to the south-southwest as references; the park itself is a small green lot in a grid of residential streets. McConnell Air Force Base (KIAB), where the tanker took off, is about 7 miles to the south; Wichita Dwight D. Eisenhower National Airport (KICT) is about 8 miles to the southwest; and Colonel James Jabara Airport (KAAO) is about 5 miles to the east-northeast. The area lies within Wichita's Class C airspace, with military traffic from McConnell to the south, so contact Wichita Approach and view the site from around 2,000–2,500 feet AGL. Winter brings strong northerly winds; spring and early summer bring severe thunderstorms.
