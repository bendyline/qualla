# Dockum Drug Store sit-in

In the summer of 1958, young members of Wichita's NAACP Youth Council sat in silent, disciplined protest at the Dockum Drug Store lunch counter until the owner agreed to serve them, one of the earliest organized sit-ins in the nation.

**Location:** 37.6860, -97.3355

**Categories:** Sit-in movement, July 1958 in the United States, History of Wichita, Kansas, 1958 in Kansas, 1958 protests, Lunch counters, August 1958 in the United States, Protests in Kansas

## Introduction

They dressed as if for church. On July 19, 1958, ten students took seats at the segregated lunch counter of the Dockum Drug Store in downtown Wichita and began a protest of remarkable discipline. They sat facing forward without leaning on the counter. They did not read or distract themselves. They spoke only when employees spoke to them. The protest stretched on for more than three weeks, and when it ended on August 11, the store's owner had ordered his manager to serve them. It happened a year and a half before the Greensboro sit-ins made the tactic famous across the nation.

## A Youth Council Takes the Lead

The protest was organized by twenty-year-old Ron Walters, president of the local NAACP Youth Council, together with his nineteen-year-old cousin Carol Parks-Hahn, the council's treasurer and daughter of the local NAACP secretary. Their mentor was Chester I. Lewis, president of the local NAACP branch, who provided guidance to the young organizers. Wichita at the time was a midsize city of more than 150,000 people, about 10,000 of them Black, and segregated lunch counters like Dockum's were a fact of daily life for its Black residents. The national NAACP was not ready to back them. It sent a telegram saying it could not support the students "legally or otherwise." The young people went ahead anyway, relying on their own organization, their mentor's counsel and their willingness to be seen.

## The Discipline of Sitting Still

What made the Dockum sit-in so powerful was its restraint. The students wore clothing usually reserved for church. They sat upright, facing forward the entire time, without leaning on the counter, reading or otherwise occupying themselves, and they spoke only in response to the store's employees. There was nothing for anyone to object to except their presence, which was precisely the point. Ten students began the protest, and as it continued they recruited others from the local high school and university. White students from the local university joined them at the counter as well. As the weeks passed, the stools at the segregated counter stayed occupied by young people who would not leave and would not give anyone an excuse to throw them out.

## The Counter Opens

In the end, it was the cash register that broke first. The sit-in was costing the business money, and the store's owner finally gave in to the students' demands. Citing the financial burden the protest had placed on the store, he ordered the manager to serve them. On August 11, 1958, after more than three weeks of quiet persistence, the Dockum lunch counter was desegregated. The students had won without violence and without the backing of the national organization that had declined to support them. They had also demonstrated something important about the tactic: a sit-in did not need to be loud to work. It needed patience, discipline and enough people willing to keep coming back.

## Overshadowed, Then Remembered

Dockum attracted little media attention at the time. About a week after it ended, on August 19, 1958, a sit-in at the Katz Drug Store lunch counter in Oklahoma City drew national notice. It was led by NAACP Youth Council leader Clara Luper, a high school teacher, together with local students, including Luper's eight-year-old daughter, who had suggested the protest. They quickly desegregated the Katz lunch counters, and afterward the tactic of nonviolent student sit-ins spread. On February 1, 1960, students began the widely publicized sit-ins at a Woolworth's in Greensboro, North Carolina, launching a wave of protests across the South. Wichita has since moved to remember its own place in that history. On September 16, 2023, the city opened Chester I. Lewis Reflection Square Park on Douglas Avenue, honoring the mentor who guided what local accounts call the first successful student-led lunch counter sit-in in America, at the Dockum Drug Store. A bronze lunch counter sculpture by Georgia Gerber that stood on the site was set to be moved to Finlay Ross Park, across from Century II.

## Flight Context

The Dockum Drug Store stood at the corner of Douglas Avenue and Broadway in downtown Wichita, Kansas, at about 37.686°N, 97.335°W. From the air, find downtown's cluster of towers and follow Douglas Avenue, the main east-west street, toward the Arkansas River about a mile to the west, where the round, blue-domed Century II convention center and the Keeper of the Plains statue at the river confluence make excellent references. Wichita Dwight D. Eisenhower National Airport (KICT) is about 6 miles to the west-southwest, McConnell Air Force Base (KIAB) about 6 miles to the southeast, and Colonel James Jabara Airport (KAAO) about 7 miles to the northeast. Downtown lies inside Wichita's Class C airspace, so contact Wichita Approach; 2,000–2,500 feet AGL gives a good view. Expect summer thunderstorms and gusty south winds.
