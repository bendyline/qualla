# Chilocco Indian Agricultural School

For nearly a century, the Chilocco Indian School on the Oklahoma-Kansas line took in Native American children from more than 120 tribes to be assimilated through drill and labor, and its former students remember it as both haven and prison.

**Location:** 36.9850, -97.0625

**Categories:** Native American boarding schools in Oklahoma, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, National Register of Historic Places in Kay County, Oklahoma, Defunct schools in Oklahoma, 1884 establishments in Indian Territory, Native American history

## Introduction

Twenty-two bugle calls a day. That is what former students of the Chilocco Indian School remember, along with government-issue uniforms, scanty meals, inadequate health care and a paucity of individual attention. For some the school was "home and haven," in the words of one account; for others it was "reformatory and prison." From 1884 to 1980, Chilocco operated as an agricultural boarding school for Native Americans on reserved land in north-central Oklahoma, about 20 miles north of Ponca City and seven miles north of Newkirk, close to the Kansas border. Nearly 18,000 students from more than 120 tribes passed through it, and even the name has a curious history: it apparently comes from a Creek phrase, literally "big deer," that usually meant a horse.

## Land Set Aside

Chilocco was one of the federal off-reservation boarding schools for Native American children of the era, alongside Carlisle in Pennsylvania, Haskell in Kansas and Chemawa in Oregon. Major James M. Haworth, the first Superintendent of Indian Schools, chose a site along Chilocco Creek, and in 1884 an executive order signed by President Chester Arthur set the land aside. It lay in the Cherokee Outlet, also called the Cherokee Strip, and the Cherokee provided 8,640 acres so the school could fulfill its mandate for agricultural education. Even the school's legal status was contested. In 1912, a court case over an election dispute involving whisky turned on whether the Chilocco land belonged to Kay County and the state of Oklahoma or remained Indian Territory. The ruling held that it was not an Indian reservation at all, but an off-reservation school.

## Assimilation by Design

Chilocco took students from tribes across the United States, and like other American Indian residential schools, its purpose was to integrate and assimilate them into the mainstream of American life. Until the 1930s it ran on a highly structured, strict military regime. Instruction leaned heavily toward vocational training rather than academic subjects, and students had to perform manual and domestic labor, known as "actual work." What schooling they did receive was often rudimentary compared with the education of their white contemporaries. Girls were trained not to work in their own homes but to serve as employees of white women or of the boarding schools themselves, and one assessment of the school concludes that it aimed to teach Native girls subservience rather than to educate them. Attendance at Christian religious services was required once a week. Behind the policy language were children, sent away to school to be remade according to someone else's plan.

## Fed, Clothed and Sheltered

Enrollment fell in the 1920s, and the elementary school closed. Then the Great Depression arrived in 1929, poverty deepened across Native communities, and enrollment climbed again. One graduate described the calculation plainly: "It wasn't a matter of enjoying [Chilocco], it meant we were educated, clothed, fed, and had a roof over our head." In 1949 the school began a special program for Navajo youth, and in the 1950s enrollment peaked at about 1,300. One object on campus carried a history of its own. Chief Joseph gave his hunting rifle to Chilocco in 1885, and it stayed there for nearly a century before it was returned to the Nez Percé when the school closed.

## Eighteen Thousand Students

After its peak, enrollment declined as more Native families gained access to public schools and a boarding school was no longer essential. In the 1970s, activists from American Indian organizations criticized abuses at the school, and in 1972 the National Indian Youth Council staged a sit-in there. By the late 1970s only about 100 students still lived on campus, and in 1980 Chilocco closed. Over its lifetime, nearly 18,000 students from more than 120 tribes attended, and 5,542 earned high school diplomas: 2,741 women and 2,801 men. Among the graduates were 688 Cherokee, 573 Choctaw, 545 Navajo and 452 Creek students. Former students include the Cherokee actor Wes Studi, the artist Johnny Tiger Jr., and Moses J. Yellow Horse, a Pawnee baseball pitcher.

## What the Campus Holds Now

After closure, the land was granted to five nearby tribes, the Kaw Nation, the Otoe-Missouria Tribe, the Pawnee Nation, the Ponca Nation and the Tonkawa Tribe, as the Chilocco Development Authority, with the Cherokee Nation holding a half mineral interest. From 1989 to 2001 the property was leased to Narconon, which ran a substance abuse rehabilitation center there. In 2000, the Chilocco Treaty between the United States and the five nations created the Council of Confederate Chilocco Tribes, giving them joint authority over the campus. In 2011 the campus was closed to the public and put to use as a training facility for federal law enforcement. In 2018, with the council's permission, the Department of Homeland Security released particles onto campus buildings to test how well biological agents penetrate homes; the agency said the materials were nontoxic, though many in Newkirk were skeptical. And in 2021, after the reported discovery of hundreds of graves at Canadian residential schools, the Chilocco Alumni Association called for ground-penetrating radar to search beyond the school cemetery for unmarked graves.

## Flight Context

The former Chilocco campus lies at approximately 36.99°N, 97.06°W in Kay County, Oklahoma, about 7 miles north of Newkirk and just south of the Kansas state line. From 2,500-3,500 ft AGL, look for the cluster of old campus buildings and grounds standing alone amid open farmland, with Chilocco Creek nearby. The campus is closed to the public and used for federal training, so view it from the air only and do not plan to land nearby. Nearest airports: Strother Field (KWLD), serving Winfield and Arkansas City, about 11 nm north; Ponca City Regional (KPNC), about 15 nm south.
