# E. W. Marland Mansion

An oilman who once controlled a tenth of the world's oil built this $5.5 million "Palace on the Prairie" in Ponca City, lived in it barely three years, and sold it for $66,000.

**Location:** 36.7169, -97.0606

**Categories:** Historic house museums in Oklahoma, National Historic Landmarks in Oklahoma, Mediterranean Revival architecture in Oklahoma, Ponca City, Oklahoma, Oil industry history

## Introduction

E.W. Marland spent $5.5 million building his house and sold it for $66,000. Between those two numbers lies one of the great rise-and-fall stories of the American oil boom. At his peak in the 1920s, Marland was said to control one tenth of the world's oil reserves, and in Ponca City, Oklahoma, he built a home to match: a 43,561-square-foot Mediterranean Revival mansion, one of the largest residences in the southwestern United States, known ever since as the "Palace on the Prairie." He was able to live in it for only about three years.

## A Palazzo from a Quarry

Marland had admired the Palazzo Davanzati in Florence while traveling in Europe, and its influence shows in the design by the prominent Tulsa architect John Duncan Forsyth. Construction ran from 1925 to 1928 and employed dozens of European craftsmen. The site was an abandoned stone quarry on the side of a small hill, and the builders put it to use: the old excavations became the swimming pool, and the light-colored, rusticated limestone blocks were quarried right on the property, set in concrete on a steel frame. The roughly U-shaped house measures 78 by 184 feet. Guests arriving on the west front passed under an arched porte-cochère to reach 11-panel double doors custom-made in New York, while at the four inside corners sat sculptures of Marland's four hunting dogs, carved by the Italian sculptor Ernest G. Pellegrini.

## Buffalo Leather and Seashell Shelves

Even the plumbing shows off. On the south terrace, the exposed drainage system displays Marland's monogram and the date 1927, and the water drains through a carved figure of Pan into a water feature set in a niche of the terrace wall. The family's private quarters on the third floor are reached by a stone staircase or by the original Otis elevator, lined with buffalo leather and one of the first installed in Oklahoma. Marland's Tudor-style bedroom has a carved mantel featuring two of his favorite polo horses, and the adjoining bathroom holds an electric sauna believed to be one of the first in the United States. His library, whose bookshelves are topped with giant seashell designs copied from those in the White House Oval Office, was both his private study and the headquarters of his campaign for governor. Lydie Marland's Louis XV-style bedroom is lined with hand-carved lime-wood panels imported for the room, around a fireplace of Italian pink marble. One guest room is named for Will Rogers, who is believed to have stayed there.

## Five Lakes and a Derrick's Beams

Marland called his 2,500-acre estate The Refuge, and he opened it on June 2, 1928, with a luncheon for invited friends and family and an afternoon of polo and fox hunting, sports he had introduced to Oklahoma. The grounds once held five lakes and a T-shaped, Olympic-sized pool. A later owner filled in four of the lakes and the pool, leaving only Lake Whitemarsh, named for Marland's yacht, which was itself named for a lake in his hometown of Pittsburgh. The Boat House conceals a large underground wine cellar behind a safe door, and a tunnel running through it connects the mansion to the Artist Studio, built for Jo Davidson, sculptor of the family's statues. The studio's beams were recycled from one of Marland's first oil derricks. Today it houses an oil museum and a gallery of 44 works by Bryant Baker, who sculpted the Pioneer Woman statue that Marland commissioned for Ponca City.

## The Fall of an Oil King

The palace was barely finished when the empire behind it began to slip. In 1928, J.P. Morgan Jr. took over Marland's company in a hostile takeover, and the cost of running the enormous house drained the family's much-reduced finances. By 1931 the Marlands had moved out of the mansion and into the Artist Studio, opening the big house only for special occasions. The grandest came in 1935, when an Inaugural Ball celebrated Marland becoming Oklahoma's 10th governor. In 1941 he sold the estate to the Discalced Carmelite Fathers for $66,000, keeping only the former chauffeur's cottage and the land around it. Forsyth remodeled the cottage for the couple. Marland died there of a heart condition about six months after the sale.

## Lydie's Long Return

The cottage passed to Lydie, whose life was as unusual as the house. She and her brother had been adopted as teenagers by their aunt and uncle, Virginia and E.W. Marland; after Virginia died in 1926, Marland had the adoption annulled, and he married Lydie in 1928. In 1953 she left Ponca City and spent 22 years away, mostly out of sight, before returning in 1975. The estate had moved on without her. The Sisters of St. Felix bought it in 1948 and ran a private high school there, and when they left for New Mexico in 1975 the City of Ponca City and Continental Oil Company bought it for about $1.4 million, with the company later transferring its share to the city. The mansion opened to the public the following year and became a National Historic Landmark in 1977. Lydie lived in her cottage, off and on, until her death in 1987. It now holds the Marland Family Exhibit.

## Flight Context

The Marland Mansion sits at 36.717°N, 97.061°W on the edge of Ponca City in north-central Oklahoma. From 1,500-3,000 feet above ground, look for the long, pale limestone house with its red clay-tile roof and Lake Whitemarsh on the grounds, set among trees on the city's outskirts; the Pioneer Woman statue and museum are also in Ponca City. Ponca City Regional Airport (KPNC) serves the city, and Enid Woodring Regional Airport (KWDG) is farther west. North-central Oklahoma generally offers good visibility, but spring and early-summer thunderstorms can build rapidly.
