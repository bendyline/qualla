# Pioneer Woman

Ponca City's 17-foot bronze Pioneer Woman was chosen by a public vote after twelve sculptors' models toured the nation, and was unveiled in 1930 before an estimated 40,000 people and a coast-to-coast radio audience.

**Location:** 36.7100, -97.0653

**Categories:** Bronze sculptures in Oklahoma, Monuments and memorials in Oklahoma, Women's museums in the United States, 1930 sculptures, Ponca City, Oklahoma

## Introduction

What should a pioneer woman look like? In 1927, E.W. Marland let the American public decide. The Oklahoma oilman paid twelve prominent sculptors to make bronze models, sent them on tour from New York to Texas, and asked visitors in city after city to vote. The winner, by Bryant Baker, now stands 17 feet tall and weighs 12,000 pounds in Ponca City: a sunbonneted woman leading a child by the hand, dedicated on April 22, 1930. The contest that produced her is almost as memorable as the statue.

## An Oilman's Cherished Dream

Around 1925, Marland sketched out an ambitious program of statues on the settling of the American West and tried to hand it to the sculptor Jo Davidson. Davidson declined, and when Marland replied that he could pay for it, Davidson answered, "I don't doubt it for a minute, but I don't see myself working for you for the rest of my life." He did agree to come to Ponca City and make statues of Marland and of his adopted children, Lydie and George. While he worked, Marland confided his "most cherished dream," as Davidson later wrote: a twenty-five-foot figure on a hill where it could be seen for miles. Davidson recalled that Marland brought friends to watch and "acted as if he was the sculptor," saying he was doing the figure and Davidson was merely his hands.

## Twelve Sculptors and a Sunbonnet

Marland then invited many of America's leading sculptors to make a bronze model roughly three feet tall, offering each a $10,000 honorarium. Daniel Chester French, George Grey Barnard and Paul Manship said no, as did the only two women invited, Gertrude Vanderbilt Whitney and Anna Hyatt Huntington. That left twelve men, among them Baker, A. Stirling Calder, Davidson, James Earle Fraser, John Gregory, Mahonri Young and Wheeler Williams. Marland pictured a woman in pioneer dress with a child, and he sent every sculptor a sunbonnet. Nine of the twelve models wore one, and all but Davidson's included a child. Young took a grander route, with a mother holding her child in the embrace of a Renaissance Madonna and a pair of spirited bison guarding the stairs to the base. It fared poorly with voters, and Young later called the loss the worst disappointment of his career.

## A Nation Casts Its Ballots

The models were unveiled at the Reinhardt Galleries in New York on February 26, 1927, and stayed until March 19 before setting out on tour. Stops included Boston, Pittsburgh, Chicago, Minneapolis, Kansas City, Dallas, Oklahoma City and Ponca City, and at each one visitors voted for their three favorites. Critics argued the merits, almost universally mocking the sunbonnets as "terrible headwear," and elementary school art classes even wrote assignments about the models. The critic Helen Appleton Read complained that most competitors "failed to produce anything monumental." On December 20, 1927, Marland's son George announced the winner. Baker's model had drawn the most votes in 11 cities and Gregory's in three, and in the final count Baker out-balloted Gregory 42,478 to 37,782.

## Whose Hands?

Victory came with an asterisk. Baker was the last sculptor to enter and had only a month to prepare. He claimed the pose came from an eight-to-ten-inch sketch he made within hours of hearing about the competition, but according to the biographer of his chief assistant, Donald De Lue, it was De Lue who executed the 33-inch competition model in 1927, with Baker supervising and finishing the face. De Lue also modeled the full-size figure in 1928 and 1929 in Baker's Brooklyn studio, and remembered telling Baker to leave when he tried to help. One writer has noted that Baker used a professional actress as his model, producing a glamorous figure that represented Western myth more than reality. The runners-up were not all forgotten: Wheeler Williams' model was rediscovered years later, enlarged, cast and placed in front of the public library in Liberal, Kansas.

## Forty Thousand at the Unveiling

The project cost an estimated $300,000 to $350,000, depending on the account, and by the end Marland's fortunes were in decline. Private fundraising helped, and his longtime business rival Lew Wentz supplied $25,000, later lending Marland money for the final payments, a loan Wentz sued to collect in 1936. The statue rose on land Marland had bought for it, and the governor declared the dedication day, the 41st anniversary of the Land Run of 1889, a state holiday. An estimated 40,000 to 44,000 people came for a parade and a 19-gun salute. President Herbert Hoover opened a 90-minute national radio broadcast from the White House, and Will Rogers closed the ceremonies. The base reads, in part, that the monument honors "the heroic character of the woman who braved the dangers and endured the hardships" of pioneer life. On September 16, 1958, the 65th anniversary of the opening of the Cherokee Outlet, the state opened the Pioneer Woman Museum.

## Flight Context

The Pioneer Woman statue stands at 36.710°N, 97.065°W, just off U.S. Route 77 about a mile northeast of the center of Ponca City, Oklahoma. The 17-foot bronze is small from the air, so use the museum grounds and U.S. 77 as references from 1,500-2,500 feet above ground; the E.W. Marland Mansion lies little more than half a mile to the northeast. Ponca City Regional Airport (KPNC) serves the city, and Enid Woodring Regional Airport (KWDG) lies farther west. North-central Oklahoma typically has good visibility, with strong winds and spring thunderstorms to watch for.
