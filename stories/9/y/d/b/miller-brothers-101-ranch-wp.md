# Miller Brothers 101 Ranch

A 110,000-acre Oklahoma cattle ranch launched a Wild West show that starred Bill Pickett and Tom Mix, sparked an oil company that became Conoco, and then lost almost everything.

**Location:** 36.6150, -97.1417

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Oklahoma, Ranches on the National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Wild West shows, Cowboys, Oil industry history

## Introduction

The well that helped launch the company later known as Conoco was called Willie-Cries-for-War. It came in during 1911 on land belonging to the Miller Brothers 101 Ranch, a 110,000-acre cattle empire near what is now Ponca City, Oklahoma, and it was only one of the ranch's claims to fame. The 101 was, in its day, the largest diversified farm and ranch in America. It gave birth to a Wild West show that toured two continents. And within a few decades, almost all of it was gone.

## An Empire Before Statehood

Colonel George Washington Miller, a veteran of the Confederate Army, founded the ranch in 1893, before Oklahoma became a state. When he died in 1903, his three sons, Joseph, George Jr. and Zack, took over the whole 110,000 acres. The family came to own most of the land outright, and they also leased land from the Ponca, Pawnee and Otoe peoples across Kay, Noble, Osage and Pawnee counties. For nearly four decades the ranch stayed in Miller hands, raising cattle and crops on a scale few American operations could match. Around 1924 it reportedly kept a herd of about 500 American bison, which appeared in the film North of 36.

## Taking the Ranch on the Road

The Millers' neighbor, Major Gordon W. Lillie, performed as Pawnee Bill, and his example pushed the brothers to stage a Wild West show of their own. After years of local shows, they reached the national stage in 1907 at the Jamestown Exposition in Virginia, and later that year the Miller Brothers 101 Ranch Wild West Show began touring from Brighton Beach, New York. Joe, the eldest, was an exceptional rider and a star in his own right. Over the years the cast included Lillian Smith, Bee Ho Gray, Tom Mix, Jack Hoxie and, late in his life, Buffalo Bill himself. Its most influential performer was Bill Pickett, the African American cowboy who invented bulldogging, wrestling a running steer to the ground, which the 101 show introduced to audiences and which lives on today as rodeo steer wrestling. Still, the Millers had entered the business late. The first year brought a serious railroad accident, cast members later contracted typhoid fever, and motion pictures were already drawing crowds away from live Wild West shows.

## Stranded Abroad

The show's darkest chapter came overseas. As tensions built toward World War I, the British military confiscated most of the 101's horses, stagecoaches and automobiles in England. In the German Empire, authorities reportedly arrested some of the show's Oglala Sioux performers on suspicion of being Serbian spies, and by one account they were never seen again. Zack Miller frantically moved the rest of the cast out of Germany through Norway to England, then struggled in London to find steamship passage before securing places on an American ship. The Native performers who made it home had endured all of that, and when they returned to Oklahoma, Joe Miller refused to pay them overtime. The entire Native cast quit. By 1916 his younger brothers had given up working with him. Joe hired an aging, ill Buffalo Bill to headline a World War I recruitment show called "Pageant of Preparedness," but Cody soon quit and died within a year.

## Oil Beneath the Pasture

Cattle and showmanship were not the ranch's only fortunes. In 1908 the Millers entered a leasing arrangement with E.W. Marland, who formed the 101 Ranch Oil Company, and the 1911 strike at Willie-Cries-for-War made the ranch one of the early focal points of the oil rush in northeastern Oklahoma. The discovery led to the founding of the Marland Oil Company, which merged with Continental Oil in 1929 under the Continental name and eventually became part of ConocoPhillips. Marland became a millionaire, then a U.S. congressman, and finally governor of Oklahoma. The Millers' own story went the other way.

## The Last Brother Standing

In 1927 Joe tried and failed to sell the show to the American Circus Corporation. On October 21 that year, a neighbor found him dead in the ranch garage with his car running; the family physician ruled the death accidental. George Jr. died in a car accident in 1929. Zack tried to carry on alone, but the ranch went into receivership in 1931 and, in 1932, in the depths of the Great Depression, he filed for bankruptcy, and the federal government seized the show's remaining assets and bought 8,000 acres of the ranch. The Farm Security Administration divided the remaining lands and sold them to individuals. Zack died of cancer in 1952. The ranch house and most other buildings were torn down, and the old 101 Ranch store burned in a fire of unknown origin on September 22, 1987. A small portion of the property became a National Historic Landmark in 1975, and in 1990 Oklahoma named State Highway 156 the 101 Ranch Memorial Road. A historical marker stands beside it about 13 miles southwest of Ponca City.

## Flight Context

The 101 Ranch site lies near 36.615°N, 97.142°W, about 13 miles southwest of Ponca City, Oklahoma, along State Highway 156, the 101 Ranch Memorial Road, which makes a useful line to follow. Few buildings survive, so from 2,000-4,000 feet the site reads mainly as open ranch and farm country; Ponca City to the northeast is the obvious reference point. Ponca City Regional Airport (KPNC) is the closest airport, with Enid Woodring Regional Airport (KWDG) farther west. Expect good visibility most days, with gusty winds and spring thunderstorms common across north-central Oklahoma.
