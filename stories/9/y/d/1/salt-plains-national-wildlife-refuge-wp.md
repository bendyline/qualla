# Salt Plains National Wildlife Refuge

On an Oklahoma salt flat where the Osage once gathered salt and wartime bombers practiced, whooping cranes rest and diggers unearth selenite crystals with hourglasses of sand inside.

**Location:** 36.7503, -98.2243

**Categories:** National Wildlife Refuges in Oklahoma, National Natural Landmarks in Oklahoma, Wetlands of Oklahoma, Protected areas of Alfalfa County, Oklahoma, Salt flats

## Introduction

Beneath the salt flats of northern Oklahoma, selenite crystals form around a small secret: an hourglass-shaped inclusion of sand, a feature not known in selenite found anywhere else in the world. Visitors are allowed to dig for them, but only between April 1 and October 15, because the same ground is critical habitat for the endangered whooping crane. People and wildlife have been drawn to this salt for centuries, and the Salt Plains National Wildlife Refuge, spread along Great Salt Plains Lake just north of the small town of Jet, is the place where their claims on it are balanced.

## An Oasis Built on Salt

President Herbert Hoover established the refuge by executive order on March 26, 1930, and today it protects 32,197 acres, where 315 species of birds have been recorded alongside about 30 species of mammals. It was designated a National Natural Landmark in June 1983. The land divides into almost equal parts: bare salt flat, open water and vegetated ground, with marshes, woods, grasslands and croplands scattered through it. Some 11,000 to 12,000 acres are salt flats, and within them a designated area has gypsum concentrations high enough to grow selenite. The lake itself is formed by a dam on the Salt Fork of the Arkansas River. Long before the dam, the heavily wooded, well-watered bottomland along the Salt Fork was like an oasis in the surrounding grassland, and bison and other game came to eat the salt.

## Salt Worth Controlling

That salt made the plains a favorite hunting ground for Native peoples, and a prize. According to historian Louis F. Burns, the Osage used salt from here to bleach deerskins and had enough surplus to trade with other tribes, and by 1760 they were firmly in control of the plains. In May 1811, George Champlin Sibley, head trader at Fort Osage in Missouri, set out to examine the Osage hunting grounds, guided by an Osage man named Sans Orielle. His party was said to be the first white men to visit the plains. Sibley named the area the Grand Saline, cut his trip short on reports that Comanche bands were nearby, and returned on July 11, 1811, predicting that the salt would one day prove commercially profitable. The plains hosted many councils of war and peace, and when the 1835 Treaty of New Echota gave the Cherokee Nation the Cherokee Outlet, the United States reserved the right to let other tribes, "red men" in the treaty's language, get salt here as well.

## Pelicans, Cranes and an Island Rookery

The refuge was created as a resting and breeding ground for migratory waterfowl, especially in fall and spring, and it ranks among the most important shorebird habitats in the Western Hemisphere. Whooping cranes are mostly seen during fall migration. Endangered least terns, threatened snowy plovers and peregrine falcons all make a home here, and large flocks of American white pelicans stop on the reservoir between August and September. The heart of the nesting season is Ralstin Island, a rookery in the lake that is closed to the public. Originally 25 acres, it has eroded to about six, yet it still hosts more than 30,000 birds during breeding season. More than 30 species nest in the colony, including white-faced ibis, great blue herons and, most notably, tricolored herons.

## What the Diggers Found

Not everything buried in the flats is natural. During World War II the salt plains served as a bombing and strafing range, and remnants of that use are still scattered across them. In 2007 a Boy Scout digging for crystals uncovered something far stranger: 7.5-inch vials holding chemical agents including mustard gas, lewisite, chloropicrin and phosgene. They came from World War II-era military chemical agent identification sets, which were routinely disposed of by burial at the time, although no record of a disposal site at the salt plains had ever been reported. Crystal digging was halted from April 2007 to April 2009 while the risks were assessed, and workers eventually retrieved 171 vials and at least a dozen incendiary devices.

## Walking the Edge of the Flats

Today the refuge invites slow exploration. The 2.5-mile Harold F. Miller Auto Tour begins near the nature center and follows a dirt road past ponds and fields planted with crops for wildlife, and a quarter-mile trail leads to Casey Marsh Tower, where bald eagles can be seen in fall and winter. The Sandpiper Trail runs 0.35 miles to the edge of the salt flats and a birding platform, while the 1.25-mile Eagle Roost Nature Trail loops beneath towering cottonwoods to a waterfowl-watching shore on Sand Creek Bay. The lake has had hard years. The extreme drought of 2011 and 2012 caused very large fish kills and almost ended fishing at the refuge.

## Flight Context

The refuge centers near 36.750°N, 98.224°W in Alfalfa County, northern Oklahoma, just north of Jet. Great Salt Plains Lake, impounded on the Salt Fork of the Arkansas River, and the brilliant white expanse of roughly 11,000 to 12,000 acres of salt flat beside it make one of the most distinctive landmarks in the region; both are easy to identify from 4,000-8,000 feet. Nearby airports include Alva Regional Airport (KAVK) to the west and Enid Woodring Regional Airport (KWDG) to the southeast. Glare off the salt flats can be intense on sunny days. Remember that the refuge is prime habitat for large birds, including pelicans and cranes during migration, so maintain a generous altitude, and expect strong winds and summer thunderstorms typical of the southern plains.
