# King Opera House

A Van Buren saloon-and-billiards block turned opera house, movie palace and restored stage, the King Opera House has survived a fire, a string of new names and a ghost story that begins with gunshots at the train depot.

**Location:** 35.4365, -94.3414

**Categories:** Opera houses in Arkansas, Buildings and structures in Van Buren, Arkansas, Performing arts centers in Arkansas, Reportedly haunted locations, National Register of Historic Places in Crawford County, Arkansas

## Introduction

The curtain first rose on Faust. On October 18, 1901, the King Opera House on Van Buren's Main Street opened with the tale of a man who trades his soul for a second chance, a fitting choice for a building already on its second life. Ten years earlier the same Victorian walls had held a billiard parlor, a saloon and the local newspaper. In the century that followed they would burn, go dark, flicker with moving pictures, and return, restored, to the name of the colonel who owned it when the first stage went in.

## Billiards, Whiskey and the Wallace Block

When the structure opened in 1891, nobody called it an opera house. It was the "Wallace Block," and its ground floor was split between the Willard Billiard Parlor and the Wallace Saloon and Restaurant, while the Van Buren Press newspaper worked upstairs. In 1898 Col. Henry P. King bought the building, and on February 3, 1901, came the announcement that it would become an opera house. Small storefronts stayed at the front, but the theater behind them was finished that same year, and Faust took the stage that October. For more than a decade the house stayed busy, hosting many kinds of acts and even sermons.

## Fire in the Cleaners

On March 14, 1914, a fire started in a dry-cleaning storefront and spread into the opera house, damaging the entire interior. The show, in a sense, went on anyway: vaudeville acts stayed booked despite the damage. That November came word of a new direction: the building would become a moving picture house. The remodel took until February 1919, when the doors opened as the Van Buren Theater. Joe Huff bought the building on June 3 of that year and, in April 1920, announced yet another remodel. By the 1930s the theater had taken the name of the actor and radio star Bob Burns, who grew up in Van Buren, and became the Bob Burns Theater, with a storefront out front and an apartment on the top floor. Malco Theaters bought the property in the 1960s and kept the name, but the theater closed in 1974.

## Bringing Back the King

In 1979 the City of Van Buren Community Development Agency bought the building and began restoring it as the King Opera House. Many parts of the interior, including structural beams, still bore damage from the 1914 fire. The work stretched until 1991, and much of it remains visible today. The city ran the building until 2022, when it handed management to Arts on Main, the arts nonprofit next door. Inside, the auditorium holds 218 seats on the ground level, each with an intricate, period-correct design, and about 96 more in the balcony. The stage measures 28 feet wide from curtain to curtain and 26.5 feet deep, with a 20-foot baton height, generous room for a big production. Below it is another matter: the basement offers two small dressing rooms and two closets, and a large cast can find the green room very cramped.

## The Actor at the Depot

Staff and many Van Buren residents say the building is haunted, and the ghost most often named is Charles C. Tolson. Born in Mississippi on Christmas Day, 1868, Tolson managed a stock company and worked as a vaudeville actor, and he performed at the King Opera House. After one show, the story goes, the daughter of Dr. William Parchman, a local practitioner, went up to talk with Tolson about the performance. A friend told Parchman that the two had been flirting. In September 1903, as Tolson waited for his train at the depot, Parchman shot him; Tolson died of his wounds, and Parchman was later acquitted of murder. The tale has outlived everyone in it, and it still hangs over the old stage where Tolson once performed.
