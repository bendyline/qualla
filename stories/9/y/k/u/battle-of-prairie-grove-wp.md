# Battle of Prairie Grove

On December 7, 1862, a Confederate army tried to crush two separated Union forces one at a time on the high ground at Prairie Grove, Arkansas, and the bloody, inconclusive fight that followed left the Union in control of northwest Arkansas.

**Location:** 35.9831, -94.3106

**Categories:** civil-war, battlefields, arkansas, state-parks, military-history

## Introduction

Francis Herron's soldiers had marched through intensely cold weather over rough roads, averaging 30 miles a day, when they reached the Illinois River in northwest Arkansas on the morning of December 7, 1862. Since the afternoon of December 3, one of his two divisions had covered 120 miles and the other 105. They had come because James Blunt's Union division, isolated near Cane Hill, had telegraphed for help. So exhausting was the march that only about 3,500 men, half the strength of the two divisions, were fit to fight when they arrived. Ahead of them, Thomas C. Hindman's Confederate army was converging on a ridge called Prairie Grove.

## A Trap Reversed

Blunt had beaten John S. Marmaduke's 2,000 Confederates at Cane Hill on November 28, leaving his division far from the rest of the Army of the Frontier. Hindman, commanding the Confederate First Corps of the Trans-Mississippi Army, saw a chance to destroy it. Hindman had a forceful personality, and when his superior, Theophilus Holmes, tried to talk him out of the attack, Hindman won their argument over the telegraph. By December 1 he had gathered between 11,000 and 12,000 troops near Van Buren. But Blunt's intelligence detected the preparations, and his call for reinforcements set Herron's divisions marching. When Hindman learned Herron was close, he changed plans: he would slip north on Cove Creek Road, crush Herron first, then turn on Blunt. The danger was being caught between the two.

## Blue Coats and Broken Lines

The morning began well for the Confederates. Joseph O. Shelby's cavalry, many of them wearing captured blue uniforms, surprised and routed several Union cavalry regiments, including a regiment of Arkansas cavalrymen who had stayed loyal to the Union. Shelby himself was briefly captured and then rescued in the melee. But Herron's infantry ignored the fleeing horsemen and pressed on, and its bold front made the Confederate cavalry fall back across the river. Then Hindman hesitated. Rather than strike Herron's weakened force at once, he set up a defensive line on the high ground and waited. Herron's artillery soon found it. The Confederate cannons were of inferior quality, lacked range and were short of ammunition, and the Union gunners wreaked havoc along the ridge.

## The Borden Farm

Emboldened, Herron sent two brigades up the slope near a farm owned by Archibald Borden. The 20th Wisconsin and 19th Iowa overran a Confederate battery, but a counterattack by Arkansas brigades under James F. Fagan and Dandridge McRae drove the Wisconsin regiment back, and brigade commander Henry Bertram was wounded. Herron attacked twice, and both assaults were beaten off with heavy casualties. Each time, Hindman answered with counterattacks of his own, and each of those failed too. The hillside became a place of charge and countercharge, with neither side able to hold what it briefly won.

## Blunt Follows the Guns

Blunt had been fooled. Hindman left 400 horsemen at Reed's Mountain to keep him in place, and a report of the Confederate march went unforwarded for two hours. By 10:00 a.m., realizing the enemy was gone, Blunt had his division marching north. His lead cavalry took a wrong turn toward Rhea's Mill, a mistake that turned out to be a gift, since it gave the division the least obstructed route. From Rhea's Mill the troops could hear the thunder of cannon, and on the flat ground regiments spontaneously left the road and set out cross-country. Blunt's division, the "Kansas Division," included the 1st and 3rd Indian Home Guard, regiments of Creek, Seminole and Cherokee soldiers, and by some accounts a number of Black soldiers, making it unusual among Union units in 1862. In mid-afternoon, at a time that accounts place between about 2:00 and 3:15 p.m., Blunt reached the field with 30 cannons and struck Hindman's flank.

## The Cost of a Ridge

As darkness fell, both sides gradually disengaged. The Union reported 1,251 casualties, including 175 dead; the Confederates reported 1,317, with between 164 and 204 dead, totals that may be too low because lightly wounded men were often not counted. Short of reinforcements and ammunition, Hindman withdrew, and the Confederates had to leave many of their dead on the field, piled together behind makeshift barriers to keep feral pigs away. Many demoralized conscripts deserted. Tactically the battle was a draw, but it gave the Union control of northwest Arkansas, and on December 29 Blunt and Herron drove Hindman's army from Van Buren in disarray. The battlefield was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1970, and much of it is now protected in Prairie Grove Battlefield State Park, which Arkansas State Parks says covers more than 1,000 acres.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lies at approximately 35.98°N, 94.31°W at the town of Prairie Grove, about ten miles southwest of Fayetteville, Arkansas. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, look for the open ridge and fields of Prairie Grove Battlefield State Park, with the Illinois River's upper valley to the east. Drake Field (KFYV) in Fayetteville is about 7 nm to the east, and Northwest Arkansas National Airport (KXNA) lies about 18 nm to the north; be aware of its Class C airspace. The Boston Mountains rise to the south. Winter fronts can bring low ceilings, and summer afternoons often bring haze and thunderstorms.
