# SEFOR

Near the tiny community of Strickler in northwest Arkansas, the SEFOR reactor spent three years proving that a sodium-cooled fast reactor could steady itself in an accident, then waited nearly half a century to be taken apart.

**Location:** 35.8334, -94.2814

**Categories:** nuclear-history, science-and-technology, arkansas, decommissioned-sites

## Introduction

SEFOR was built to find out what a reactor core would do when something went wrong. From 1969 to 1972, in Cove Creek Township near the small community of Strickler, about 18 miles southwest of Fayetteville, Arkansas, the Southwest Experimental Fast Oxide Reactor generated 20 megawatts of heat and not a single watt of electricity. Electricity was never the point. The reactor existed to test an idea about safety, and it succeeded. What came after, a decades-long struggle over who would pay to take it apart, turned out to be the harder experiment.

## Built to Be Tested

SEFOR was an experimental fast breeder reactor fueled with mixed oxide, or MOX, fuel and cooled by liquid sodium. It was built specifically to test the suggested inherent safety features of that combination, in particular whether the Doppler effect, a drop in the core's reactivity as its fuel heats up, would shut the reactor down during a power spike, including in an accident. The theory held that as the core heated, the Doppler effect would help stabilize it. SEFOR confirmed that belief. Its data was later used in the safety analysis of large fast breeder reactors.

## An Unusual Partnership

The site itself was modest: a 20-megawatt thermal test reactor, a shop building, an operations building, a maintenance shed and an electrical transformer yard. General Electric operated it privately, while funding came from the United States government through the Southwest Atomic Energy Associates, a nonprofit consortium formed by 17 power companies of the Southwest Power Pool together with European nuclear agencies. The program ran until 1972, when it was completed as planned. A proposal to extend operations to 1977 was turned down. Later that year the fuel and irradiated sodium coolant were removed and taken offsite, and the facility was placed in safe storage.

## A Landmark With a Caretaker

In 1975 the University of Arkansas acquired the reactor, hoping to use it for research. For a time it served to calibrate equipment and as a research tool for graduate students. In October 1986, SEFOR was designated a Nuclear Historic Landmark, and that same year the university stopped using it. The building did not simply sit empty, though. The university kept up surveillance through maintenance and periodic visits, and a caretaker lived on site in the former Visitors' Center, keeping watch over a reactor that no longer ran.

## Paying for the Cleanup

By the early 2000s the site was considered contaminated, and in 2005 the Energy Policy Act, which President George W. Bush signed, authorized $16 million to decontaminate it. Senator Blanche Lincoln had been pushing for cleanup money since 1999 and introduced the 2005 legislation. No money was appropriated. In 2008 Lincoln requested federal funding to start the dismantling, and in 2009 the Arkansas congressional delegation secured $1.9 million in an omnibus appropriations act for a study of what a cleanup would require; that November, a team of eight from the nuclear services company EnergySolutions began planning work at the site, and a complete cleanup was estimated at around $20 million. The breakthrough came in September 2016, with a $10.5 million Department of Energy grant to fund the next phase of decommissioning and dismantling the site, and another $10 million followed in 2018 to complete the cleanup.

## Eighty-Four Thousand Pounds

Before the heavy work began, the university opened the site for public tours on January 19, 2017. Then, on October 16, 2018, crews lifted out the 84,000-pound radioactive core by crane, and set it in a carbon steel containment vessel, a removal that took about 18 minutes. The vessel was then welded shut and shipped out of state. In April 2019, EnergySolutions announced at a community meeting in the Strickler Fire Department Community Center that the cleanup was complete, pending final reports from state and federal agencies declaring that no further action was required. Nearly half a century after it last ran, the reactor that proved a point about safety was finally gone.

## Flight Context

The former SEFOR site lies at approximately 35.83°N, 94.28°W near Strickler in Cove Creek Township, Washington County, about 18 miles southwest of Fayetteville in the hills of northwest Arkansas. The reactor has been dismantled, so little remains to identify from the air; use Fayetteville and the surrounding rural roads as references. A viewing altitude of 3,500-4,500 feet MSL keeps clear of the hilly terrain. Drake Field (KFYV) in Fayetteville lies about 12 nm to the north-northeast, and Northwest Arkansas National Airport (KXNA) is about 27 nm to the north; watch for its Class C airspace. Morning fog can linger in the valleys.
