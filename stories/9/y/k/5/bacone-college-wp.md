# Bacone College

Founded in 1880 as Indian University and later rooted on land granted by the Muscogee Creek Nation, Bacone College in Muskogee nurtured a celebrated school of Native American painting before financial collapse sent it into bankruptcy and liquidation.

**Location:** 35.7769, -95.3347

**Categories:** colleges, native-american-history, native-american-art, oklahoma, historic-districts

## Introduction

The fireplace in Ataloa Lodge is built of stones that Native communities sent to the college from many places. Among them are rocks from the grave of Sitting Bull and from the field where Custer made his last stand. The lodge, long home to an art museum, stands on the campus of Bacone College in Muskogee, Oklahoma, a school that for more than a century tied its identity to Native American students, Native American art and the tribal nations that helped it survive. Its story runs from a mission classroom with three students to a nationally known art program, and finally to bankruptcy.

## Three Students and a Missionary

The college was founded in 1880 as Indian University by Almon C. Bacone, a missionary teacher in the Cherokee Nation who had taught at the Cherokee Male Seminary. It was affiliated with the mission arm of what is now American Baptist Churches USA. Historian John Bartlett Meserve traced its roots even further, to a Baptist mission school at Valley Town in western North Carolina, part of the Cherokee homeland. After the United States forcibly removed most Cherokee to Indian Territory in the late 1830s, that school moved west to a site near present-day Westville, Oklahoma, and in 1867 John B. Jones, son of its missionary leader Evan Jones, moved it to Tahlequah, the Cherokee capital. When Indian University opened, Bacone was its only teacher and it had three students. By the end of its first year, 56 students were enrolled and the faculty numbered three.

## A Gift of Land

Bacone asked the Muscogee Creek Nation's Tribal Council to donate a quarter section of land, 160 acres, near Muskogee, a town in the Creek Nation that became known as "The Indian Capital" once the Dawes Commission and the Union Agency to the Five Tribes were based there. The Nation granted it, and in 1885 Indian University moved into a new building on the site. A stone bible sculpture marks the spot where Bacone and two Baptist missionaries and trustees, Joseph Samuel Murrow and Daniel Rogers, knelt in prayer to dedicate the college, and the names of all its presidents are inscribed on its surface. One of the first buildings was Rockefeller Hall, made possible by a $10,000 gift from John D. Rockefeller. "Old Rock" served as classroom, dormitory, dining hall, chapel and offices until it was razed in 1938. In 1910 the school was renamed Bacone College after its founder.

## The Bacone Style

Bacone became nationally known for its art. After Mary "Ataloa" Stone McLendon, a Chickasaw educator, storyteller and musician, arrived in 1927, she built the Indian art program and became the first director of its art department. It grew nationally known through artists such as Acee Blue Eagle (Muscogee), Woody Crumbo (Citizen Potawatomi) and Richard "Dick" West (Southern Cheyenne), who directed the department from 1947 to 1970. Together they helped develop the modernist Flatstyle painting movement known as the Bacone school. The McCombs Gallery held a wide cross-section of Native American art, and the Indian Room at the library kept many of Almon Bacone's papers. The campus even played a role on screen, standing in for the Carlisle Indian School in the Jim Thorpe biopic Jim Thorpe - All-American.

## Small School, Long Shadows

In 1967, Bacone's baseball team, from a school of about 250 students, won the Junior College World Series against schools with more than 20,000. Their coach, Enos Semore, went on to coach at the University of Oklahoma for more than two decades. The alumni list reaches far beyond the ballfield: the Muscogee writer and humorist Alexander Posey; Crow tribal historian and war chief Joseph Medicine Crow; Hopi traditionalist Thomas Banyacya; Navajo code talker and linguist Adee Dodge; and Cherokee soldier Jack C. Montgomery, who received the Medal of Honor in World War II. In 2014, the Bacone College Historic District was listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## The Last Semester

By spring 2018 the college was in severe financial trouble, saying it needed an immediate $2 million to keep operating. It survived by cutting programs and faculty and selling property. Tribal nations then tried to take over the college as a consortium and charter it as a tribal college, hoping to control the education of their own students and secure federal support under the government's treaty obligations. The United Keetoowah Band of Cherokee Indians, the Osage Nation, the Otoe-Missouria Tribe, the Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes and the Kiowa Tribe approved charters, but the Bureau of Indian Affairs did not approve the conversion. In 2023 the campus was set for a sheriff's sale to satisfy a judgment of roughly $1 million owed to a contractor, though the sales scheduled for April and December were both called off. Most students were sent home after the fall 2023 semester, the last nine graduated in spring 2024, and in June 2024 the college filed for bankruptcy and soon lost its accreditation. Its president hoped to bring a limited number of students back in fall 2025, but in May 2025 a federal judge converted the case to a Chapter 7 liquidation at a U.S. trustee's request, and in October 2025 the college's furniture, vehicles and other contents were auctioned in Oklahoma City. The trustee said the best hope for the college's creditors was an investor buying its real estate. At its closing, Bacone was described as the oldest continuously operated institution of higher education in Oklahoma, a title Northeastern State University, with roots in the Cherokee seminaries of 1851, also claims.

## Flight Context

Bacone College sits at approximately 35.78°N, 95.33°W on the northeast side of Muskogee, Oklahoma, in the Arkansas River valley. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, look for the campus buildings and grounds at the city's northeastern edge. Muskogee-Davis Regional Airport (KMKO) lies about 7 nm to the south. Tulsa International Airport (KTUL) is roughly 37 nm to the northwest; stay aware of Tulsa's busier airspace. The flat river country offers good visibility most of the year, but spring storms in eastern Oklahoma can be severe.
