# Ned Christie

Cherokee senator, blacksmith and Keetoowah traditionalist Ned Christie was branded an outlaw for a killing an eyewitness later pinned on another man, held off federal lawmen for five years, and was killed in 1892; he lies at his father's cemetery in Wauhillau.

**Location:** 35.8555, -94.7743

**Categories:** Cherokee Nation history, Native American history, People of the American Old West, Adair County, Oklahoma, Cherokee Nation politicians (1794–1907)

## Introduction

For five years the federal court at Fort Smith called Ned Christie an outlaw, and newspapers and dime novels sold his story as Wild West entertainment. Today a plaque at the Cherokee Court House in Tahlequah tells it differently: Christie, it reads, was "assassinated by U. S. Marshals in 1892." The distance between those two versions leads back to Wauhillau, in present-day Adair County, Oklahoma, where he was born and where his family finally laid him to rest.

## Fiddler, Smith, Senator

Christie was born on December 14, 1852, at Wauhillau in the Goingsnake District of the Cherokee Nation. His parents, Watt and Lydia Christie, had survived the Trail of Tears, the U.S. government's forced removal of the Cherokee from the Southeast to Indian Territory, and the family belonged to the Keetoowah Society, among the most traditional circles of Cherokee life. His father and grandfather were chiefs of the ceremonial ground near the family home. As a boy and young man Christie was a marble champion, a stickball player and a popular fiddler. He grew to 6 feet 4 inches, worked as a blacksmith and gunsmith, and learned to read and write in both Cherokee and English. In 1885 he was elected to the tribal Senate and served as an adviser to the Principal Chief. He was also said to have a hot temper when drinking; charged with manslaughter in an 1885 death, he was tried in the tribal court at Tahlequah and found not guilty.

## A Voice Against the Railroads

Christie's politics made him conspicuous. He strongly opposed granting the federal government land for railroads through Cherokee territory, and he resisted the push, formalized in the Dawes Act of February 1887, to break up communal tribal land into individual allotments. Those policies had powerful white backers in the region and in the East, and as Christie became known as a leader among his people, he made enemies. In early May 1887 (sources give May 3 to May 5), Deputy U.S. Marshal Daniel Maples was shot from ambush near Spring Branch Creek outside Tahlequah while hunting whiskey sellers, and died of his wounds. A principal suspect had been Bud Trainer, a whiskey seller known for violence. But when an arrested associate, John Parris, told authorities that Christie had fired the shot, a federal grand jury indicted him, though Christie was said to have been drinking with friends at the time.

## Double Walls of Log and Sand

Fearing a trial before white people in a U.S. court, Christie fortified his home in the Rabbit Trap community. He reportedly wrote to Judge Isaac C. Parker offering to surrender if he could be released on bail to gather evidence of his innocence; Parker never answered. In 1889 a posse led by Heck Thomas, and including Bud Trainer himself, set fire to Christie's blacksmith shop, and the flames took the cabin. Christie was wounded, as was 18-year-old Little Arch Wolf, described as his nephew in some accounts and his cousin in others. His wife Nancy found him alive in the ruins, and a local doctor and a Cherokee medicine man saved both men. Christie and his friends then built a new fort, double-walled with logs, sand packed between the walls, and gun ports for rifles. The reward on his head rose from $500 to $1,000.

## November 3, 1892

After a first posse withdrew in October 1892 with two men wounded, Deputy Gideon S. "Cap" White surrounded the fort on the morning of November 3 with a hired guide, Gus York, 14 other men, and a cannon borrowed from an army post in Kansas. Inside were Christie's family and Charles Hair, a 12-year-old Cherokee boy; the women were allowed to leave after the first exchange of fire. When a double charge of powder blew up the cannon's barrel, the lawmen pushed a wagonload of dynamite against a wall and detonated it. Christie was fatally shot as he escaped the burning ruins. His body was tied to a door, carried by train and photographed with the lawmen as a trophy, as was common then, and a studio sold the picture as postcards. Hair and Arch Wolfe were both convicted of assault with intent to kill a lawman. Hair, because of his age, was sent for three years to a reformatory in Illinois, far from his people; Wolfe was held for years and died in 1912 in a South Dakota asylum that refused him contact with his family.

## A Witness Speaks Too Late

In 1918 Dick Humphreys, a freedman, told the Daily Oklahoman that he had witnessed the shooting of Marshal Maples and that Bud Trainer, not Christie, fired the shot; he had kept silent for fear of Trainer. Some researchers believe his opposition to railroads, allotment and statehood may have contributed to his being accused, though whether he fired the shot has never been settled; Mihesuah's biography concludes that we may never know the full story. His family brought his body home to Wauhillau, where it lies under a tombstone in his father's Watt Christie Cemetery, and the Fort Smith National Historic Site now also has material recognizing his death as an assassination. He became a folk hero among the Cherokee, and his life has since drawn novelists such as the Cherokee author Robert J. Conley, whose Ned Christie's War appeared in 1991, and historians including Devon A. Mihesuah, whose 2018 biography describes the century of sensational press and pulp-fiction accounts as "largely fictitious."

## Flight Context

Wauhillau lies at about 35.856°N, 94.774°W in the wooded hills of Adair County, eastern Oklahoma, between Tahlequah to the west and Stilwell to the southeast. This is rural ridge-and-hollow country with few distinct landmarks, so navigate by the towns and the Illinois River corridor near Tahlequah; 2,500-3,500 ft AGL keeps the hills in view. Nearby airports: Tahlequah Municipal (KTQH, FAA identifier TQH), about 12 nm west-northwest; Drake Field at Fayetteville, Arkansas (KFYV), about 31 nm east-northeast. Fog can linger in the hollows on cool mornings.
