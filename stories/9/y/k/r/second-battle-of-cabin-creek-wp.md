# Second Battle of Cabin Creek

In September 1864, Stand Watie and Richard Gano's Confederates captured a Union supply train meant for refugees near Fort Gibson, a celebrated raid that changed nothing about the war in Indian Territory.

**Location:** 36.4890, -95.1229

**Categories:** American Civil War, Military history, Indian Territory, Native American history, Mayes County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

At one o'clock in the morning on September 19, 1864, Confederate troops moved against a Union wagon train at Cabin Creek in Indian Territory. When the Union soldiers opened fire, Confederate artillery answered, and the barrage sent the train's mules into a panic. Some bolted with their wagons still attached. Some, terrified, fell off the bluff into Cabin Creek. By the end of the fight the Confederates held 740 mules and 130 wagons, a haul worth about $1 million, and yet the victory would change almost nothing.

## A Plan to Strike Kansas

The raid grew out of a larger ambition. Stand Watie, the Cherokee officer promoted from colonel to brigadier general in 1864, conceived a plan for a Confederate force to attack central Kansas from Indian Territory. It would raid Union Army facilities and encourage Indian tribes in western Kansas to join an attack on the eastern part of the state. Watie presented the idea to his superior, General S. B. Maxey, on February 5, 1864. Maxey approved it on one condition: the attack had to begin by October 1, to coincide with an assault on Missouri already planned by General Sterling Price.

## Deference at Camp Pike

On September 13, 1864, Watie met Brigadier General Richard M. Gano at Camp Pike in the Choctaw Nation to plan the expedition. Gano, commanding several Texas Confederate units, had agreed to serve as co-leader. Watie knew what he was up against: many of the Texans looked down on Indian troops, even their own allies, and resented his promotion. According to one account, Colonel Charles DeMorse of the 29th Texas Cavalry Regiment refused to serve under him. Watie deferred to Gano as commander of the expedition, a decision both Gano and Maxey commended as an act of solidarity, and Gano also held more seniority as a Confederate general. Watie kept command of the Indian Brigade, about 800 men, while Gano's brigade of Texas cavalry and artillery numbered about 1,200.

## The Hay Camp at Flat Rock

Three days later, while waiting for the supply train, the Confederates came upon a detachment of Black Union soldiers cutting hay at Flat Rock, near where Flat Rock Creek meets the Grand River, about 15 miles northwest of Fort Gibson. Captain E. A. Barker led the guard, a small group from the 2nd Kansas Cavalry and a detachment of the 1st Kansas Colored Infantry. They were surrounded and attacked from all sides. In desperation, Barker ordered his mounted men to try to break out, and only 15 of the 65 who attempted it reached the fort. The Union lost all its haying equipment, several hundred tons of hay and more than 100 men as casualties, including prisoners. Watie's own report counted 85 prisoners and "about as many killed." There were unconfirmed reports that many of the Black soldiers were killed by the Texans.

## Mules in the Dark

The prize they had been waiting for had left Fort Scott, Kansas, on September 12. Led by Major Henry Hopkins and escorted by men of the 2nd, 6th and 14th Kansas Cavalry, the train carried supplies and provisions for Native Americans who had fled their homes and were camped near Fort Gibson. At Cabin Creek, the Texans covered the Confederate left flank and the Indian Brigade the right. Once the artillery set the mules stampeding, the teamsters cut many of them loose from their traces, and men jumped on the animals and rode across the ford to safer ground. Hopkins escaped to Fort Gibson. The Confederates captured about $1 million worth of wagons, mules and commissary supplies. Commonly cited figures put Confederate losses at 9 dead and 38 wounded and Union losses at 35 killed, wounded or missing, though sources differ.

## A Victory That Changed Little

Watie and his troops were commended for their success by Confederate President Jefferson Davis and the Confederate Congress. The raid was bold, and its haul enormous. Yet the Second Battle of Cabin Creek had no significant impact on the outcome of the Civil War in Indian Territory. Behind the commendations stood people the raid left worse off: the displaced Native Americans near Fort Gibson for whom the captured supplies had been intended, and the Black soldiers at Flat Rock, whose fate remains clouded by those unconfirmed reports.
