# Mary Golda Ross

Born in Park Hill, Oklahoma, a descendant of Cherokee Principal Chief John Ross became the only woman engineer on Lockheed's founding Skunk Works team and contributed to the Agena rocket that served as a docking target for NASA's Gemini missions.

**Location:** 35.8512, -94.9478

**Categories:** Women aerospace engineers, American aerospace engineers, Cherokee Nation, 20th-century American mathematicians, Engineers from Oklahoma, Space program

## Introduction

Turn over a 2019 Native American dollar coin and you will find Mary Golda Ross. The U.S. Mint chose her in 2018, ten years after her death, for a coin celebrating Native Americans in the space program. It was a long way from Park Hill, Oklahoma, the small town where she was born on August 9, 1908. In between lay a career that took her from rural Oklahoma classrooms to a highly confidential Lockheed engineering team, and on to rocket work for the space program.

## A Chief's Descendant

Ross was the second of five children of William Wallace Ross Jr. and Mary Henrietta Moore Ross, and a descendant of Cherokee Principal Chief John Ross; sources differ on whether he was her great-grandfather or great-great-grandfather. John Ross had been influential in creating the new Cherokee settlement in Oklahoma after the U.S. government, under President Andrew Jackson, forced the Cherokee and other Native nations from their homelands, a settlement complete with a school and a government. She was a talented child, and her family sent her to live with her grandparents in Tahlequah, the Cherokee Nation capital, so she could attend primary and secondary school. At 16 she enrolled at Northeastern State Teachers' College in Tahlequah, and in 1928, at 20, she earned a bachelor's degree in mathematics.

## Chalkboards and Stars

Ross began her career teaching math and science in rural Oklahoma schools for nine years. In 1936 she took the civil service examination and went to work for the Bureau of Indian Affairs in Washington, D.C., as a statistical clerk, and in 1937 she was reassigned as an advisor to girls at the Santa Fe Indian School. All the while she was working toward a master's degree in mathematics, which she received in 1938 from Colorado State Teachers College, now the University of Northern Colorado, in Greeley. Space fascinated her. While earning that degree, she took as many astronomy classes as were offered, feeding an intense fascination with space that would soon find a practical outlet.

## The Only Woman in the Room

In 1942 Lockheed hired Ross as a mathematician working on fighter planes, and one of her first tasks was analyzing the effects of pressure on the Lockheed P-38 Lightning. She helped solve design problems involved with high-speed flight and aeroelasticity for fighter jets. Lockheed sent her to UCLA, where she obtained a professional certification in engineering, widely described as the first Native American woman to do so. In the early 1950s she joined Lockheed's Advanced Development Program, known as Skunk Works, as one of its founding engineers. She was one of only forty people on that project and the only woman engineer on the team. The work was highly confidential, and most of its specifics are still classified. She played an important role in researching and evaluating ballistic missiles and other new defense technologies, and her work fed into the Polaris program.

## Agena and the Planets

Another of Ross's major contributions at Lockheed was the Agena rocket project. The Agena served as a rendezvous and docking target for the Gemini program, and it flew a successful mission in 1966. Her work reached further still, into preliminary design concepts for interplanetary space travel, crewed and uncrewed Earth-orbiting flights, and some of the earliest studies of orbiting satellites for both defense and civilian purposes. She also joined the team working on the Poseidon and Trident missiles, and she contributed to the NASA Planetary Flight Handbook Vol. 3. For a woman who had once filled her graduate schedule with astronomy courses, the work turned a private fascination into hardware and mathematics that helped Americans reach space. She stayed with Lockheed until she retired in 1973.

## Holding the Door Open

Ross spent much of her life making room for others. Since the 1950s she had been a charter member of the Los Angeles chapter of the Society of Women Engineers, which supports young women pursuing engineering through mentoring, scholarships and community, and she became a fellow and life member. She worked to recruit young women and Native American youth into engineering careers. At 96, wearing her "first traditional Cherokee dress," made of green calico by her niece, she took part in the opening ceremonies of the National Museum of the American Indian, and when she died in Los Altos, California, on April 29, 2008, she left the museum a $400,000 endowment. Honors followed her, including a Woman of Achievement Award in 1961 and a Google Doodle on August 9, 2018, her 110th birthday.

## Flight Context

Park Hill, Mary Golda Ross's birthplace, lies in northeastern Oklahoma at approximately 35.851°N, 94.948°W, a few miles southeast of Tahlequah, the Cherokee Nation capital where she went to school. From about 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, Tahlequah makes the easiest reference to the northwest. Tahlequah Municipal Airport is about 5.5 nm north-northwest (FAA identifier TQH), and Muskogee-Davis Regional Airport (KMKO) about 23 nm southwest. Northwest Arkansas National Airport (KXNA) is about 40 nm northeast for larger services. Check local weather for valley fog in the morning and thunderstorms on summer afternoons.
