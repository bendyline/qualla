# Sequoyah's Cabin

Near Akins, Oklahoma, a log cabin sheltered inside a Depression-era building was home for some thirteen years to Sequoyah, the Cherokee man who gave his people a written language that is still in use today.

## Introduction

Twelve years of work produced eighty-five characters. Sequoyah began in 1809, a Cherokee man with no schooling beyond the ways of his own people, and by 1821 the Cherokee Nation had a written language of its own. That writing system, the Cherokee syllabary, is still used today. East of the small community of Akins in eastern Oklahoma, off State Highway 101, stands the log cabin that was his home from 1829 until he left around 1842 on the journey to Mexico from which he never returned, now protected, furnished and owned by the nation whose words he taught to take shape on paper.

## Twelve Years, Eighty-Five Characters

Sequoyah, who was also known as George Gist, was born sometime between about 1765 and 1776, accounts differ, on the ancestral lands of the Cherokee in the southeastern United States. His mother was Cherokee, and his father was white or half-white. He never went to school, but he came to understand the value of writing, especially in dealing with the British settlers who lived alongside Cherokee communities. In 1809 he started work on a writing system for Cherokee, and he kept at it until 1821. The result was a syllabary of 85 characters (his first version had 86), each representing a syllable of spoken Cherokee rather than a single sound, and it remains in use two centuries later.

## Carrying Writing West

In the 1820s Sequoyah moved west to teach the syllabary to the western Cherokees, carrying his invention to Cherokee communities far from where it was born. During this period, in 1829, the cabin that bears his name was built. It would be his home for some thirteen years, until he set out for Mexico around 1842 in search of a band of Cherokees; he died there in August 1843. The setting was modest: a single-story log house with a gabled roof, sitting in a park-like setting on a property that now covers more than 100 acres, of which the landmark itself is about ten. Here, far from the mountains and rivers of the Cherokee homeland, the man who had given his people their written words made his home, in the country that is now Sequoyah County.

## A Cabin Within a Shelter

The cabin survives because someone decided, during the Great Depression, that it needed protecting. In 1936 the Works Progress Administration, the New Deal program that put unemployed Americans to work on public projects, raised a building around it to shield the logs from weather and decay. Visitors today step through that outer structure to find the older home inside, furnished to look as it might have when Sequoyah lived there. Relics and documents associated with his life are on display, and a bronze statue of Sequoyah stands outside. The site lies east of Akins on the east side of State Highway 101, at a point where the road makes a northward jog, easy to miss if you are not watching for it and worth the stop if you are.

## Returned to the Cherokee Nation

The cabin and its surrounding park were declared a National Historic Landmark in 1965. For decades the site was in state hands. Then state budget cuts put its future in question, and in 2016 the Cherokee Nation stepped in, formalizing its ownership on November 9 of that year. The nation now operates the cabin as a museum that tells Cherokee history and introduces visitors to the basics of the syllabary. There is a quiet fittingness to that outcome. The writing system Sequoyah spent twelve years building is still read and written by Cherokee people, and the home of his later years now belongs to the nation that inherited his work.

## Flight Context

Sequoyah's Cabin lies at 35.5142°N, 94.6519°W, east of Akins in Sequoyah County, Oklahoma, just off State Highway 101. The cabin and its shelter are small, so fly at about 1,500-2,500 feet AGL and use the town of Sallisaw and Interstate 40, several miles to the south and southwest, as your primary references.
