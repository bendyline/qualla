# Dwight Presbyterian Mission

Founded at a Cherokee chief's request in 1820 and moved west with the Cherokee in 1829, Dwight Mission survived removal, civil war and a deadly 1918 fire before returning to Cherokee Nation hands in 2021.

**Location:** 35.5475, -94.8525

**Categories:** Native American history, Cherokee Nation, Missions, Native American boarding schools, National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma

## Introduction

In 1818, Tahlonteeskee, Principal Chief of the Western Cherokees, visited Brainerd Mission and came away wanting a mission for his own people. He did not live to see it. He died in the spring of 1819, and his brother John Jolly became the mission's supporter. In August 1820, Cephas Washburn founded Dwight Mission on Illinois Bayou, near present-day Russellville, Arkansas, to serve the Arkansas Cherokees. It became one of the first American missions to Native Americans, and it would follow the Cherokee west into what is now Oklahoma.

## A Town Built for Learning

The mission took its name from the Rev. Timothy Dwight, president of Yale College and a corporate member of the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions. By 1824 it had grown into a self-contained small town on the frontier, with at least 24 buildings. There were residences for missionaries, students, staff and visitors, and a single building that served as library, post office and pharmacy. A dining hall with a kitchen stood alongside a storehouse, a blacksmith shop, a lathe and carpenter shop, a stable and a barn. Then an 1828 treaty forced the Arkansas Cherokees to give up their lands and move to Indian Territory, and the mission moved with them. In May 1829 it was reestablished on Sallisaw Creek, near present-day Marble City, Oklahoma, and its school opened on May 1, 1830.

## Printing Press and the Trail of Tears

In the late 1830s, a Cherokee-language printing press, reported to be the first in what was then Indian Territory, was moved from Union Mission to Dwight Mission, bringing the written Cherokee word to this valley. In 1839 the majority of the Cherokee Nation arrived in the area after the U.S. government and army forced them from the Southeast on the Trail of Tears. The Western Cherokees who had first asked for Dwight Mission were now joined by the survivors of that forced removal. The mission that had once followed the Cherokee west now stood among a far larger Cherokee community, one rebuilding its life in a new land after enormous loss.

## Burned, Abandoned, Reborn

The Civil War reached the mission directly. Many of its buildings were burned in fighting between pro-Confederate and pro-Union forces, and the last religious service was held in November 1862 before the site was abandoned. Private owners took over the property, and by 1884 only two of the original buildings remained. The Cherokee National Council decided to reopen the school in the 1880s. The Presbyterian Women's Board of Home Missions supplied the funds, and a large building was constructed to serve as a boarding school for Cherokee girls. It later spent time as a day school, and in 1900 it returned to boarding, now serving both boys and girls.

## Thirteen Boys

On January 12, 1918, fire swept through the Dwight Mission School dormitory, destroying the building and killing thirteen students. The boys, aged 9 to 17, were trapped on a screened porch. They came from several nations: George Wickett, Nighthawk Mclemore, Phillip Correll, Rufus Young, J. P. Chandler Jr., Kenneth Crutchfield and Delbert Barnes were Cherokee; Wilson Beaver, George Tiger, Robert Daniels and Solomon Bruner were Creek; Newton Goins was Choctaw; and Gilbert Grants was Omaha. Accounts differ on where they were laid to rest: some say their bodies were returned to their hometowns, while another says the victims were buried together in the mission cemetery. A memorial to the thirteen boys was erected in the Dwight Mission Cemetery, keeping their names at the school where they had come to learn.

## Back to the Cherokee Nation

The school carried on. By 1944 it was called the Dwight Indian Training School, operated by the Board of National Missions of the Presbyterian Church USA, and it served seventy-one students, most of them Cherokee and Choctaw. It closed in 1948. The grounds between Marble City and Sallisaw went on to serve as a Presbyterian camp, retreat and conference center, and the mission is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, while historical markers in Arkansas identify its first site. In June 2021, the Cherokee Nation acquired Dwight Mission. Two centuries after a Cherokee chief asked for it, the place returned to the nation it was founded to serve.
