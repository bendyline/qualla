# Skullyville, Oklahoma

Named for the Choctaw word for money, Skullyville was once a district capital and meeting place for Choctaw Nation leaders and a Butterfield stage stop before war, a bypassing railroad and a closed post office left little more than its cemetery.

**Location:** 35.2542, -94.5931

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Oklahoma, Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma, Le Flore County, Oklahoma, Butterfield Overland Mail, Native American history

## Introduction

Ask what Skullyville means and the answer is money. The name comes from iskuli, or iskulli, the Choctaw word for it, and the author W. B. Morrison rendered the English sense as "Moneytown." The name was practical rather than poetic: this was where Choctaw families came to collect the annuity payments owed to them at the Choctaw Agency. Today the place sits about a mile east of Spiro, in Le Flore County, Oklahoma, some 15 miles southwest of Fort Smith, Arkansas, and it is considered a ghost town. For a few decades in the middle of the nineteenth century, though, the road to the Choctaw Nation's government ran through here.

## Payday at the Agency

The story begins in 1832, after the federal government forced the Choctaw out of their homelands and into Indian Territory. On this site it built the Choctaw Indian agency, where the US Indian agent handed out annuity payments. A trading and business community grew up around the post, and until about 1860 the settlement was officially called simply "Choctaw Agency." Its first agent, Major F. W. Armstrong, served until his death in 1835, when his brother, William Armstrong, succeeded him. In 1834 the US Army added Fort Coffee at Swallow Rock, about three miles north of the agency. The garrison stayed only four years before the Army decided it was no longer needed and handed the facility to Methodist missionaries, who turned it into a boys' school, Fort Coffee Academy.

## Oak Lodge Becomes a Capital

The Choctaw made the settlement, which also went by the name "Oak Lodge," the county seat of Skullyville County and the capital of the Moshulatubbee District, and for a time a meeting place for the Choctaw Nation's national leaders. Through the 1840s it grew into a political, educational and social center. In the middle of that decade the Methodist Church opened New Hope Seminary, a school for girls that drew students from across the Choctaw Nation. Traffic passed through as well, since the town was a stop on the California Road. Politics did not stay calm. After the Nation's council began gathering at Doaksville in 1850, tensions among factions flared into renewed strife. In January 1857 a small group of prominent Choctaw leaders met in Skullyville and drafted the Skullyville Constitution, which only a minority of Choctaw citizens approved. It replaced the district chiefs with a single leader, called the governor.

## Tandy Walker's Station

In 1858, Tandy Walker, a member of a prominent Choctaw family, was appointed supervisor of the Butterfield stage stop and moved into the old agency building. He also became governor of the Choctaw Nation that year, succeeding Alfred Wade, who had resigned. Walker made the building do double duty as his residence and as the office of the Butterfield Overland Mail, whose stop in Skullyville was known as Walker's Station. He lived there until he died in 1877. The building outlasted him: Morrison reported that the old agency still stood in the 1930s, and the structure is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. One more relic of the county-seat years has also survived, the Skullyville County Jail, an unusual holdover among the Choctaw Nation's county governments.

## How Moneytown Emptied

The Civil War began the unraveling. The Choctaw allied with the Confederacy and served in its army, and the fighting brought serious damage to the town. Fort Coffee Academy and other buildings burned; the boys' school never reopened. New Hope Seminary closed during the war but opened again afterward, carrying on until its building burned down in 1896. By then commerce had already made its choice. In 1895 the Kansas City Southern Railway laid its line past Skullyville rather than through it, and local businessmen moved to Spiro, the nearest station, judging rail service essential for trading and getting goods to market. When the post office closed in 1917, that closure marked the town's final decline. The notable names tied to Skullyville include Frank Crawford Armstrong, a Confederate general born there, along with Green McCurtain, a Choctaw principal chief, and Douglas H. Johnston, governor of the Chickasaw Nation. What remains of the town itself is little more than the cemetery.

## Flight Context

Skullyville lies at 35.254°N, 94.593°W, about a mile east of the town of Spiro in the Arkansas River valley of eastern Oklahoma. Little of the old town is visible from the air, so use Spiro as the reference and look just east of it at 2,000-3,000 ft AGL. Fort Smith, Arkansas, the nearest city, lies roughly 12 nm to the east-northeast. Nearby airports: Fort Smith Regional (KFSM), about 12 nm east-northeast; Robert S. Kerr Airport at Poteau (KRKR), about 14 nm south. The valley is low and open; spring thunderstorms are common in this part of Oklahoma and Arkansas.
