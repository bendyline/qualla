# Trail of Tears

The death march of a nation - where 16,000 Cherokee were forced from their homeland at gunpoint in 1838, marched a thousand miles to Oklahoma, with 4,000 dying along the way.

**Location:** 35.9131, -94.9697

**Categories:** oklahoma, cherokee, trail-of-tears, 1838, native-american

## Introduction

In the fall and winter of 1838-39, the United States government forcibly removed approximately 16,000 Cherokee people from their ancestral homeland in the southeastern United States and marched them to Indian Territory in present-day Oklahoma. The Cherokee called it Nunna daul Tsuny - 'The Trail Where They Cried.' The forced march, conducted in brutal conditions with inadequate food, shelter, and clothing, killed an estimated 4,000 people - roughly one quarter of the Cherokee Nation. The Trail of Tears was not unique: the Choctaw, Creek, Chickasaw, and Seminole were similarly expelled under the Indian Removal Act of 1830. But the Cherokee removal became the defining symbol of American betrayal of Native peoples - a nation that had adopted American customs, written its own constitution, and won a Supreme Court case, only to be expelled at gunpoint when Georgia wanted their land.

## The Cherokee Nation

By the early nineteenth century, the Cherokee had transformed their society to resemble their American neighbors. Sequoyah developed a written Cherokee syllabary. The Cherokee published a newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix. They adopted a written constitution modeled on the U.S. Constitution. Many Cherokee farmed, owned property, and practiced Christianity. They believed that if they became 'civilized' by American standards, they could keep their land. It was a miscalculation. Georgia wanted Cherokee land for cotton cultivation. When gold was discovered on Cherokee territory in 1828, pressure intensified. Georgia extended its laws over Cherokee territory and nullified Cherokee governance. The Cherokee sued.

## The Betrayal

In Worcester v. Georgia (1832), the Supreme Court ruled that states had no authority over Indian nations. Chief Justice John Marshall declared that the Cherokee Nation was a 'distinct community' with rights to its territory. President Andrew Jackson reportedly responded: 'John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it.' The federal government refused to enforce the ruling. Instead, it negotiated the Treaty of New Echota with a minority faction of Cherokee, exchanging all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi for territory in Indian Territory and $5 million. The treaty was signed by about 100 Cherokee, rejected by 16,000. It didn't matter. The treaty was ratified. The Cherokee were given two years to leave voluntarily.

## The Removal

Most Cherokee refused to leave, hoping the treaty would be overturned. In May 1838, 7,000 federal troops began rounding them up at bayonet point. Families were taken from their homes without time to gather belongings. They were held in stockades through the summer, where disease spread rapidly. The first groups were marched west in summer heat; many died. The bulk of the Cherokee left in the fall and winter of 1838-39, traveling by foot, wagon, and boat across a thousand miles. Winter conditions were brutal. Food was scarce. Blankets were insufficient. The sick and elderly couldn't keep up. An estimated 4,000 Cherokee died during the removal - in the stockades, on the trail, and in the chaos of arrival.

## The Legacy

The Trail of Tears shattered the Cherokee Nation but didn't destroy it. The Cherokee rebuilt in Indian Territory, establishing farms, schools, and a new government. The Eastern Band of Cherokee - those who escaped removal by hiding in the mountains or through legal exemptions - remained in North Carolina. Today, the Cherokee Nation is the largest federally recognized tribe, with over 300,000 citizens. The Trail of Tears National Historic Trail, established in 1987, follows the routes of removal across nine states. The story serves as a reminder of what the United States did to secure the land it now occupies - and of the resilience of peoples who survived.

## Visiting Trail of Tears Sites

The Trail of Tears National Historic Trail spans approximately 2,200 miles across nine states. Key sites include: New Echota, Georgia, the Cherokee capital where the fraudulent treaty was signed, now a state historic site with reconstructed buildings; Red Clay, Tennessee, the last seat of Cherokee government before removal; Fort Payne, Alabama, where Cherokee were held in a stockade; and the Cherokee Heritage Center near Tahlequah, Oklahoma, where the Trail of Tears ended and the Cherokee Nation is headquartered. Multiple interpretive sites along the route tell the removal story. The experience is necessarily dispersed - the Trail of Tears was a journey of suffering across hundreds of miles. Visiting multiple sites helps convey its scale. Tahlequah, the Cherokee Nation capital, is 80 miles east of Tulsa.

## Flight Context

The Trail of Tears crossed approximately 2,200 miles from the Cherokee homeland in Georgia, Tennessee, North Carolina, and Alabama to Indian Territory in present-day Oklahoma. From altitude, the route traced multiple paths - northern, southern, and water routes - across mountains, rivers, and plains. New Echota (34.49°N, 84.93°W) marked the start; Tahlequah (35.91°N, 94.97°W) marked the end. The terrain the Cherokee crossed - Appalachian mountains, Mississippi River, Ozark foothills - remains visible.
