# Cherokee Female Seminary

Rebuilt by the Cherokee Nation in 1889 after fire destroyed the first school, the Cherokee Female Seminary educated generations of Cherokee women and survives as Seminary Hall, the oldest building at Northeastern State University in Tahlequah.

**Location:** 35.9203, -94.9700

**Categories:** Cherokee Nation history, Female seminaries in the United States, Northeastern State University, Buildings and structures in Tahlequah, Oklahoma, National Register of Historic Places in Cherokee County, Oklahoma, Women in Oklahoma

## Introduction

Rachel Caroline Eaton began her schooling at the first Cherokee Female Seminary, before it burned. She went on to further education and is believed to be the first Indigenous woman from Oklahoma to earn a Ph.D., and then she came back to Tahlequah to teach at the new seminary the Cherokee Nation had raised on a hill north of town. Her path, from student to scholar to teacher, is the story of the place in miniature: a school the Cherokee built for their daughters, lost, rebuilt, and handed down.

## A Nation Invests in Its Daughters

The Cherokee set aside proceeds from land cessions to the United States in 1819 to invest in their children's education. In November 1846, at the annual session of the Cherokee National Council, they established two seminaries, one for young men and one for young women, and the Female Seminary opened to students on May 7, 1851, the same year as the Male Seminary. The model came from Massachusetts. The school was designed after Mount Holyoke, and its first two teachers, Ellen Whitmore and Sarah Worcester, were Mount Holyoke alumnae. Early graduates carried the education outward: Carrie Bushyhead Quarles, valedictorian of the class of 1855, taught in the Cherokee school system for almost forty years. Across the United States, female seminaries were part of a broad mid-nineteenth-century movement, and the Cherokee Nation's decision to run both a male and a female seminary showed how much weight it placed on education.

## Easter Fire, Mile-Long Welcome

On Easter Sunday in 1887 the original seminary burned down. The Cherokee Council chose to rebuild on a 40-acre site north of Tahlequah, near Hendricks Spring, and on May 7, 1889, it dedicated the new building. According to the Cherokee Advocate, up to three thousand people came to the celebrations, in a procession a mile long. What they saw was modern for its day. The large building on the hill had electric lights, steam heating and water from a local spring, and it held a parlor, a library, a kitchen, a chapel and bedrooms for up to 175 students plus staff and stewards. The staff included a medical superintendent, reflecting a growing Cherokee emphasis on healthcare alongside schooling. St. Louis architect Charles E. Illsley designed it in the Romanesque Revival style, with fortress-like turrets flanking the main entrance and a clock tower, shaped like a church steeple, rising two stories above the roof line.

## Latin, Chemistry and Chapel

Florence Wilson served as principal at both the original and the rebuilt school from 1875 to 1901, and many teachers and alumni regarded her as the most influential figure in its history. The seminary began with four high school years and eventually grew to take in grades one through twelve. High school students worked through English, U.S. history, physiology, arithmetic, Latin, botany, algebra, general history, geometry, physics, civics and chemistry, with instrumental and vocal music available for an extra charge. Christianity ran through the curriculum and church attendance was mandatory, and students also learned domestic skills such as sewing, as the federal government endorsed. Graduates went on to teach, to study medicine and to march: Belle Cobb returned to teach before studying medicine, Carlotta Archer taught music at the new seminary from 1894 to 1902, and Bula Benson Edmondson, class of 1902, joined suffrage parades.

## From Seminary to University

The Cherokee Nation owned and ran the seminary until March 6, 1909. Oklahoma had become a state in 1907 and tribal land claims had been extinguished, and the new State Legislature created Northeastern State Normal School at Tahlequah, authorizing the purchase of the seminary's building, land and equipment from the Cherokee tribal government. At the start of the next academic year, on September 14, classes began, mainly training teachers for the elementary grades. The school has grown into Northeastern State University, and the old seminary, now Seminary Hall, is the oldest building on campus. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973 and fully restored in 1994. A renovation that began in 2020, aided by a $4 million gift from the Cherokee Nation announced in 2015, used salvaged nineteenth-century wood and brick, restored period wood window frames, and added half-octagon dormers drawn in the original plans; NSU marked its completion, along with a new museum on the building's history, in May 2023. At the main entrance hang three 1930s WPA murals by the Kiowa artists Stephen Mopope and Jack Hokeah and the Pawnee artist Albin Jake.

## Flight Context

Seminary Hall stands on the Northeastern State University campus on the north side of Tahlequah, Oklahoma, at 35.920°N, 94.970°W. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL look for the campus buildings on the hill north of downtown; the Illinois River valley lies east of town. Nearby airports: Tahlequah Municipal (KTQH, FAA identifier TQH), about 2 nm west-northwest of the campus; Muskogee-Davis Regional (KMKO), about 25 nm southwest. Expect hilly, wooded terrain and occasional valley fog on calm mornings.
