# Cherokee National Prison Museum

Built of sandstone in 1874 for the Cherokee Nation's own system of justice, the prison in Tahlequah was the only penitentiary in Indian Territory for more than a quarter century and now tells the story of Cherokee law, outlaws and patriots.

**Location:** 35.9119, -94.9672

## Introduction

For twenty-six years, a stout sandstone building a few blocks from the Cherokee capitol in Tahlequah was the only penitentiary in all of Indian Territory. The Cherokee Nation built it in 1874 as part of its governmental complex, the physical proof that this was a nation with its own laws, its own courts and its own means of enforcing them. Today the Cherokee National Prison Museum, known in Cherokee as Ꮳꮃꭹ Ꭼꮎꮥꮎ Ꮧꮣꮝꮪꭹ, invites visitors inside those thick walls to hear how justice worked when the Cherokee Nation administered it for itself.

## A Nation's Own Justice

The prison cost $6,000 to build, and it went up alongside the other institutions of Cherokee government in the nation's capital. It was meant for more than confinement. The Cherokee Nation intended it as a place of reformation as well as punishment, and it was built to hold the most hardened and dangerous offenders. From 1875 to 1901 it served as the only prison in Indian Territory, a span of years when the territory had no other penitentiary building at all. Its existence said something about Cherokee self-government. Just down the street, the capitol housed the nation's legislature and courts, and here the sentences those courts handed down were carried out by the nation's own officials.

## Sandstone and a Sheriff Upstairs

The building is made of sandstone and measures 48 feet by 35 feet. As first built, it had a basement and two stories, with two sandstone porches on the main level, one at the front and one at the back, each under a hipped roof. The upper floor had an unusual resident. According to the Cherokee Nation's cultural tourism staff, it housed the high sheriff and his family, and the only way up was an exterior ladder. That floor is gone now. It was removed and replaced with a flat roof, and stones from it were later used to reconstruct a wall in the museum's interpretive center, so that pieces of the lost story still stand.

## From Nation to County

The prison served the Cherokee Nation until it was sold to Cherokee County, Oklahoma. The county kept using it as a jail into the 1970s, so that for decades the former penitentiary of a sovereign nation served as an ordinary county jail. The Cherokee National Jail, as it was then known, was placed on the National Register of Historic Places on June 28, 1974. A new chapter opened on May 1, 2012, when Cherokee Nation officials cut the ribbon on the Cherokee National Prison Museum at 124 East Choctaw Street. It was the third museum the nation had opened and wholly owned and operated through its cultural tourism program.

## Outlaws and Patriots

The museum is a two-building interpretive site that explores Cherokee crime and punishment, law enforcement, daily life at the prison and the famous outlaws who operated in the area. It also tells the stories of incarcerated women, and it draws a careful line between outlaws and those the museum presents as Cherokee patriots. Among the figures it covers are Ned Christie, who the museum says was falsely accused of murder in 1887, along with Henry Starr, Belle Starr and the Wickliffe brothers. A working blacksmith area is also part of the site. A reproduction gallows stands on the grounds as a sober reminder of the ultimate punishment, and of the real people whose lives ended under the laws of that era.

## Flight Context

The Cherokee National Prison Museum is at 35.9119°N, 94.9672°W in downtown Tahlequah, Oklahoma, a few blocks east of the Cherokee National Capitol on Courthouse Square. The building is small, so locate the downtown grid and the capitol square from about 1,500-2,000 feet AGL. The Illinois River runs past Tahlequah and Lake Tenkiller lies to the south. Tahlequah Municipal Airport (KTQH) is about 2 nm northwest of downtown, and Davis Field (KMKO) in Muskogee is about 24 nm southwest.
