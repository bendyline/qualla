# Cherokee National Capitol

Rebuilt in red brick after the Civil War left Tahlequah's log government buildings in ashes, the Cherokee National Capitol has served as the seat of a nation, a county courthouse and a tribal court, and now tells the Cherokee story as a museum.

**Location:** 35.9124, -94.9705

## Introduction

The first government buildings on this square in Tahlequah were made of logs, and they burned. The Cherokee Nation had chosen the town as its capital in 1839, after the U.S. government forced its people from their homelands in the Southeast, and during the Civil War those early structures were destroyed. What rose in their place, completed in 1869, was built to last: a two-story capitol of red brick and white-painted wood that still stands at the center of Courthouse Square. Its name, written in the syllabary Sequoyah created for the Cherokee language, is ᏣᎳᎩ ᎠᏰᎵ ᏧᏂᎳᏫᎢᏍᏗ ᎠᏓᏁᎸ.

## Rebuilding After the War

The Civil War tore through the Cherokee Nation as it did through the states around it. The log buildings that had served as the nation's seat in Tahlequah were destroyed during the conflict, when the rival Cherokee factions on opposite sides of the war wrecked most of the capital complex. When peace came, the nation rebuilt. Work on a new capitol began in 1867 and was completed in 1869. The building gave the Cherokee government a permanent, dignified home, with offices for the nation's executive, judicial and legislative branches under a single roof. For nearly four decades it was the political heart of a sovereign nation, the place where Cherokee laws were debated and made, until Oklahoma statehood in 1907 brought that era to an end.

## Brick, Arches and Pediments

The capitol is a two-story masonry building in a neoclassical Italianate style, its red brick walls trimmed in white-painted wood. It is five bays wide and seven bays deep, and on each side the center three bays project slightly forward. Each of those projecting sections is crowned by a pedimented gable with a dentillated cornice, a row of small, tooth-like blocks catching the light beneath the roofline. Piers divide the wall bays, and the windows change shape as they rise: segmented arches on the ground floor, rounded arches on the second. There are entrances on the east and west ends, with the main entrance on the west side sheltered by a brick vestibule added in the 20th century. The square around it is bounded by East Delaware Street, South Water Avenue, East Keetoowah Street and South Muskogee Avenue.

## Courthouse, Then Courtroom

Statehood cost the Cherokee Nation more than its government's authority. It also cost the nation its capitol. For decades after 1907, Cherokee County used the building as its courthouse, a county government operating out of rooms built for a nation. Not until the 1970s, by the Oklahoma Historical Society's account, was the building returned to the Cherokee Nation. In 1991 the Tribal Council re-established the nation's District Court there, so that Cherokee justice was once again dispensed within the walls built for Cherokee self-government. The court remained until early 2018, when it outgrew the building and moved to the nation's W.W. Keeler Tribal Complex. Before that, in 2013, the nation had launched a restoration aimed at returning the capitol to its original appearance.

## A Museum of a Nation

The restoration touched the building from the top down. Roof repairs used shingles of a kind appropriate to the building's era, laid over new decking. New doors and windows went in, a cupola was added to the roof, and plans included a new back porch. In 2019 the old capitol reopened as the Cherokee National History Museum, with permanent exhibits covering Cherokee history and culture alongside rotating exhibitions. It is a fitting use for a building with this particular past. A structure raised by a nation that had survived removal and war, then taken away at statehood and finally given back, now tells that whole story to visitors from the rooms where much of it happened. The capitol is also a National Historic Landmark.

## Flight Context

The Cherokee National Capitol stands in the center of Courthouse Square in downtown Tahlequah, Oklahoma, at 35.9124°N, 94.9705°W. Look for the square in the middle of the downtown grid from about 1,500-2,000 feet AGL. The Illinois River runs past Tahlequah to the east, and Lake Tenkiller lies to the south, both useful references in the green hills of northeastern Oklahoma. Tahlequah Municipal Airport (KTQH) is about 2 nm northwest of downtown, and Davis Field (KMKO) in Muskogee is about 24 nm southwest. The Cherokee National Prison Museum is a short walk away in downtown Tahlequah.
