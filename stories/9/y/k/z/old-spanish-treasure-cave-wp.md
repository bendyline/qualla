# Old Spanish Treasure Cave

A limestone maze cave in Arkansas's northwest corner, the Old Spanish Treasure Cave has drawn visitors since at least 1908 with competing legends of hidden Spanish gold that nobody has ever found.

**Location:** 36.4822, -94.4603

**Categories:** Caves of Arkansas, Show caves in the United States, Legends and folklore, Benton County, Arkansas, Tourist attractions in Benton County, Arkansas

## Introduction

According to the story, it began with a bear. A Spaniard named Don Carlos Lavilla, prospecting in the Ozarks in the early 1700s, spotted the tracks of a black bear and followed them to a hole in the hillside. He fought the animal, the legend says, then sketched the tunnels beyond on a sheet of sheepskin before returning to Spain. The cave he supposedly found lies along Highway 59 between Sulphur Springs and Gravette, in the far northwest corner of Arkansas, and it has been drawing visitors since at least 1908.

## A Maze in the Limestone

Strip away the legends and the Old Spanish Treasure Cave is still unusual. It is a karst cave, carved as water carrying carbon dioxide slowly dissolved the limestone, a process geologists are still studying. Unlike most tour caves, this one is a maze cavern: passageways branch throughout, opening into different rooms and still more tunnels. The cave has never been fully explored, and new territory is charted every year. It remains open to the public, with guided tours, and visitors can also try panning for treasure or hunting for fossils.

## The 1908 Telling

Caves across Arkansas and the Ozarks carry tales of hidden gold and silver, often tied to Hernando de Soto's search for gold. Such stories go back at least to the early 1700s, when the French explored the region, and some may echo still older Spanish legends. The 1908 version of the local legend casts Lavilla as one of the Spaniards hunting El Dorado, the lost city of gold. He was prospecting near Sulphur Springs because the area's lead and zinc outcroppings sometimes signal other valuable metals nearby. Many years after his bear fight, the legend continues, his heirs came back, worked a vein of ore, smelted it, and hid bullion worth $3 million in the cave rather than ship it to Spain. In 1900 another descendant arrived with the sheepskin map. The map proved accurate, but local Benton County residents were already digging, and the bullion had been moved.

## Conquistadors in a Blizzard

A later version, recorded in 2018, tells it darker. In this account, conquistadors who had been raiding in Mexico were crossing the Ozarks when a blizzard drove them into the cave. They hid their gold and camped in a chamber called the Council Room, which has a natural chimney. While in the region they destroyed a Native American village, and the few villagers who survived tracked them by their campfire smoke and killed them in revenge. Before the last conquistador died, the story goes, he made two maps, one on parchment and another carved into a limestone rock outside the cave, now partly eroded. In 1885 a man in Madrid supposedly found a map in a family Bible, followed it to the carved rock and the cave, and left, ill, without the gold. Later, in the 1900s, George Dunbar searched while pretending to run a mining company, and is said to have found a few pieces.

## Still Looking

In this telling, the hunt has never stopped. During a recent drought, symbols turned up on a wall that spring water normally keeps out of reach, pointing toward a hidden room with a large pool. Explorers had only begun to investigate when the rains returned and flooded it again. The same telling estimates the treasure at well over $40 million and credits the cavern with artifacts from the conquistadors' era, including helmets, pieces of armor and weapons, and a few gold coins are reportedly among the finds. None of it has settled the question. Whether or not the gold ever existed, the maze keeps its dark rooms and its undiscovered passages, and every year there is a little more of it to map.
