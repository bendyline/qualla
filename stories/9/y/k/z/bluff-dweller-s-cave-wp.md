# Bluff Dweller's Cave

A trapper who felt cold air blowing from a hole on his family's land in 1925 uncovered a Missouri show cave of crystal pools and flowstone, and traces of people who had sheltered there thousands of years before.

**Location:** 36.5188, -94.4870

**Categories:** Caves of Missouri

## Introduction

In 1925, C. Arthur Browning was checking his traps on land he had lived on his entire life when he noticed something odd: cold air blowing out of a small hole in the ground, about a foot across. Browning did not walk on. He found help, and together they began digging out the loose rock and dirt to see what lay beyond. What they opened was Bluff Dweller's Cave, just south of Noel, Missouri, a cave that had been sealed for longer than anyone could remember and that had once sheltered people whose names no one would ever know.

## The People Before

The excavation turned up more than rock. Browning and his helpers found arrowheads, grinding stones and tools made of bone, along with a bed of ashes and a few skeletal remains of early Native American inhabitants, with the oldest of the finds put at about 7,000 years old. These were people who used the cave as a periodic shelter, returning to it again and again in the Ozark hills thousands of years before any written record of the region. The remains found here were those of human beings, members of communities that made their lives in these hills, not curiosities. Then, some 2,000 to 3,000 years ago, a landslide at the entrance sealed the cave off. The passage stayed closed from then until the day Browning felt that breath of cold air.

## Written in Limestone

The cave's story begins far earlier still. It formed in the Pierson Limestone, a fossil-rich rock laid down during the Mississippian period of the Paleozoic Era. The Ozark Uplift raised that solid bed of limestone above sea level, and rainwater began slowly seeping through its cracks, dissolving the soluble rock as it went. Over immense spans of time the cracks widened until groundwater could flow through them, carving out long passageways. The same uplift lifted the rock, and the cave within it, above the water table, letting the passages drain. With air in the cave, a new process took over. Rainwater kept dissolving limestone above and depositing it again when it dripped into the open cave, building the formations visitors see today, grain by mineral grain.

## Crystal Lake and Stone Curtains

The passages of Bluff Dweller's Cave total about 4,000 feet, reached through two entrances beneath a limestone outcrop of the bluff. Inside, the cave is richly decorated with formations: stalactites hanging from the ceiling, stalagmites rising to meet them, knobby cave coral, draperies that fall like stone curtains, and sheets of flowstone and rimstone. The showpiece is a crystal lake held back by a rimstone dam, a natural wall of mineral that is about 2.5 centimeters thick and 30 centimeters high but runs 22.8 meters long, roughly 75 feet. That makes it one of the longest such features known in Missouri. Seen in the cave's lights, the still water behind that thin mineral rim is the kind of sight that justifies every step down.

## A Family Cave

Browning did not keep his discovery to himself for long. Public tours began in 1927, only two years after he first felt the air moving, and the cave has stayed in the family ever since. Today it is owned and operated by the third generation of Brownings. A ticket to the cave also includes the Browning Museum, where visitors can see arrowheads collected from the local area, along with rocks, minerals and fossils gathered from around the country and antiques from the Browning family itself. It is a fitting pairing. Underground lies the long geological story and the evidence of the cave's first human visitors. Above ground, a family's collection tells the shorter story of the people who found the cave again and have looked after it for a century.

## Flight Context

Bluff Dweller's Cave lies just south of Noel, Missouri, at 36.5188°N, 94.4870°W, in the wooded hills of the Missouri Ozarks close to the Oklahoma and Arkansas state lines. The cave itself is invisible from the air, so use the town of Noel and the Elk River valley that runs through it as references from about 2,000-3,000 feet AGL. Northwest Arkansas National Airport (KXNA) is about 17 nm south-southeast, Grove Regional Airport (KGMJ) in Oklahoma is about 13 nm west-northwest, and Joplin Regional Airport (KJLN) is about 38 nm north. Morning fog often fills the river valleys of the Ozarks.
