# Stand Watie

Signer of the treaty that led to Cherokee removal, principal chief of the Confederate Cherokee, the only Native American Confederate general and the last Confederate general to surrender, Stand Watie lies buried in Delaware County, Oklahoma, at the center of a history his own nation still weighs.

**Location:** 36.5256, -94.6360

**Categories:** cherokee-nation, civil-war, native-american-history, biography, oklahoma

## Introduction

His Cherokee name was Degataga, which one biography translates as "standing firm." Born on December 12, 1806, at Oothcaloga in the Cherokee Nation, in what is now Calhoun, Georgia, Stand Watie lived a life that tracked almost every fracture in nineteenth-century Cherokee history: removal, assassination, civil war within a civil war, and a peace that divided his people further. He became the only Native American general officer of the Confederacy and the last Confederate general to surrender. He is buried in Delaware County, Oklahoma, in the old Ridge Cemetery, later called Polson's Cemetery.

## The Treaty and the Blood Law

Watie grew up close to his uncle, Major Ridge, and his cousin, John Ridge, both leaders of the tribe. His father had become a wealthy planter who held enslaved African Americans as laborers. In 1835 Watie and his older brother, Elias Boudinot, were among the Cherokee leaders who signed the Treaty of New Echota, giving up the tribe's eastern lands. Most Cherokee opposed it. In 1838 the U.S. government rounded up the Cherokee who remained in the East and forcibly removed them on the journey that became known as the Trail of Tears, during which an estimated 4,000 people died. Under Cherokee law, giving up tribal land was a capital offense. In June 1839, Watie, Boudinot, Major Ridge, John Ridge and other Treaty Party men were sentenced to death and attacked. Only Stand Watie survived.

## A Cycle of Vengeance

The violence did not end in 1839. Boudinot's orphaned children were sent to their late mother's family in Connecticut for safety and schooling. In 1842 he killed a man named Foreman, one of his uncle's attackers, and in 1845 his brother Thomas was killed in retaliation. Watie was tried in Arkansas for Foreman's death and acquitted on grounds of self-defense. In 1842, the year of Foreman's death, he also married Sarah Bell, from a family long friendly with his own. They had three sons and two daughters, and he would outlive most of them.

## Chief of a Divided Nation

In 1861 Principal Chief John Ross allied the Cherokee Nation with the Confederacy to avoid disunity in Indian Territory, but within a year Ross concluded the alliance had been a disaster and left for Union territory. In August 1862 the remaining Confederate faction elected Stand Watie principal chief; Ross's supporters never recognized the election. Open warfare followed between Union and Confederate Cherokee. Even before his election, at Pea Ridge in March 1862, Watie's troops had captured Union artillery positions and covered the Confederate retreat, and he also led attacks on Unionist Cherokee civilians and farms and fought Creek, Seminole and other Native soldiers who sided with the Union. In February 1863, a special session of the Cherokee National Council voted to emancipate all enslaved people within the Nation's boundaries, while Watie, himself a slaveholder, fought on for the Confederacy.

## Cabin Creek and Its Shadow

Commissioned a brigadier general in May 1864, Watie commanded the First Indian Brigade, and his force reportedly fought in more battles west of the Mississippi than any other unit. On September 19, 1864, he and General Richard Montgomery Gano raided a Federal wagon train at the Second Battle of Cabin Creek, in what is now Mayes County, Oklahoma, capturing wagons, mules and supplies worth about $1 million, a raid considered the most famous Confederate victory in Indian Territory. The same campaign carried an atrocity. Three days earlier, at a hay camp near present-day Wagoner, Gano's Texans and Watie's Indian Brigade attacked soldiers of the 1st Kansas Colored Infantry who were cutting hay under the guard of a 2nd Kansas Cavalry detachment. Gano reported "seventy-three Federals, mostly negroes," dead on the field, a biography sympathetic to Watie says the fight became a massacre, and Union reports suggest that some Black soldiers were killed as they tried to surrender. Those men, killed while cutting hay, belong in this story too.

## The Last to Lay Down Arms

By early 1865 the Confederacy could no longer fight effectively in Indian Territory, and on June 23, 1865, at Doaksville in the Choctaw Nation, near Fort Towson, Watie surrendered, the last Confederate general to do so. That September he went to Texas to see his wife and mourn their son Cumiska, who had died at 15. He pressed Washington to recognize a separate Southern Cherokee Nation but never achieved it. The 1866 treaty required the Cherokee Nation to free the people it had enslaved and granted the Cherokee Freedmen full citizenship rights. After a period of exile in the Choctaw Nation, Watie returned to Honey Creek, tried to rebuild his fortunes and died there on September 9, 1871. His sons Saladin and Solon had died in 1868 and 1869. In 2020, a 1921 monument to him was removed from the Cherokee Capitol grounds in Tahlequah, along with a 1913 fountain honoring Confederate soldiers and Watie, as Principal Chief Chuck Hoskin Jr. looked on; the Daughters of the Confederacy, not the Cherokee Nation, had put them up.

## Flight Context

Stand Watie's grave in Polson's Cemetery lies at approximately 36.53°N, 94.64°W in Delaware County, northeastern Oklahoma, close to where Oklahoma, Missouri and Arkansas meet. The cemetery is small and hard to see from the air; use the town of Grove and the Grand Lake reservoir to the west as references, at 2,500-3,500 feet AGL. Grove Regional Airport (KGMJ) lies about 7 nm to the northwest. Northwest Arkansas National Airport (KXNA) is about 21 nm to the southeast, within Class C airspace. The Old Fort Wayne battlefield, where Watie's regiment was among the Confederate forces in 1862, lies about 10 nm to the south. Expect haze in summer and strong thunderstorms in spring.
