# Battle of Devil's Backbone

On a Ouachita ridge southwest of Greenwood, Arkansas, a Confederate rear guard set a clever ambush on September 1, 1863, then watched most of its own force stampede away, leaving Union troops in command of Fort Smith for the rest of the war.

**Location:** 35.1785, -94.3106

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Arkansas, 1863 in Arkansas, Trans-Mississippi theater of the American Civil War, Ouachita Mountains, Union victories of the American Civil War

## Introduction

At noon on September 1, 1863, the ambush worked exactly as planned. Union Colonel William F. Cloud's advance guard, riding hard down the road toward Waldron, blundered into a Confederate cavalry regiment hidden at the foot of a ridge called Devil's Backbone, and the survivors fell back in confusion as a Confederate battery opened on the rest of the column. It was the high point of the afternoon for Brigadier General William L. Cabell. Within minutes, much of his own command would be running the other way.

## A Retreat That Needed Time

The collision grew out of a Union push into western Arkansas. In late August 1863, after driving other Confederate forces farther south into Indian Territory, Union Major General James G. Blunt turned quickly toward Fort Smith. His troops skirmished with Cabell's brigade southwest of the town on August 31, and that evening Cabell decided to retreat to the southeast, sending his baggage and ordnance wagons ahead. When Blunt discovered the withdrawal the next morning, he took an infantry regiment and captured Fort Smith without incident. Cloud, meanwhile, set off in pursuit with Union cavalry and artillery men, put at about 700 by the Encyclopedia of Arkansas and about 1,500 by other accounts. Cabell's wagons were slow, so he turned his force around to buy them time. Devil's Backbone, a ridge in the Ouachita Mountains about four miles southwest of present-day Greenwood, offered the place to do it.

## An Army With Divided Hearts

On paper Cabell had 1,250 men, though probably not all of them took part in what followed. Morale was his real problem. Some of his troops were described as deserters, conscripts and jayhawkers with little interest in the Confederate cause, and Cabell seems to have known which units he could trust. He hid a reliable cavalry regiment at the base of the ridge, where the road crossed it. Three less dependable cavalry units took position on the hillside beside the road, and an artillery battery and an infantry regiment waited a few hundred yards up the slope behind them. After the ambush, Cloud's gunners deployed across the road while his cavalrymen dismounted, formed on either side, and began to climb. The concealed Confederate regiment pulled back before Cloud's troops had fired a shot.

## The Stampede

What happened next was recorded by an officer in Cabell's infantry regiment. "As the Yankees came in musket range of Cabell's leading regiment," he recalled, "it stampeded without firing a shot and running into and through the two regiments in its rear, stampeded them and the whole combined mass ran through the left wing of Cabell's infantry regiment and swept them off the field." In an instant, Cabell was left with one cavalry regiment, his battery, and part of his infantry. Cloud pressed his dismounted troopers to within a quarter mile of the Confederate line on the summit, and then the fight settled into something less dramatic. For three hours the two sides traded small-arms and artillery fire. Cabell personally directed his battery, and Cloud's men made occasional demonstrations as if to charge, but no assault came; both sides were content to trade fire without closing to close quarters.

## Two Victories, One Field

Both commanders claimed to have won. Cloud reported that the Confederates retreated suddenly during a pause in the Union fire; Cabell, who may have misread that pause, reported that he had repulsed Cloud and then withdrawn. Either way, the Union held the field when the shooting stopped. Cabell reached Waldron the next day, and Cloud returned to Fort Smith. The consequences outlasted the argument. With Fort Smith captured and Cabell beaten back, Federal forces had a foothold in the Arkansas River Valley. From that base they fought Confederate partisans, marched to join the Camden Expedition in April 1864, and supported Union efforts in Indian Territory. The Battle of Massard Prairie in July 1864 showed that Fort Smith could still be threatened, yet it remained a U.S. garrison until the war's end. Today the American Battlefield Trust and its partners have saved more than 10 acres of the battlefield, as of mid-2023.

## Flight Context

Devil's Backbone lies at about 35.179°N, 94.311°W, a wooded Ouachita Mountains ridge roughly four miles southwest of Greenwood, Arkansas. Fort Smith and the broad Arkansas River valley lie to the north; the ridge country rises to the south. A pass at 2,500-3,500 ft AGL gives a good view of the ridge line and the road corridor the armies followed. Nearest public airport: Fort Smith Regional (KFSM), about 10 nm to the north-northwest. Fort Chaffee's military training lands lie several miles to the northeast, so check for active restricted airspace before flying low in the area. Ridges can hold low cloud and fog on humid mornings.
