# Battle of Old Fort Wayne

At dawn on October 22, 1862, James Blunt's Kansas and Cherokee troops broke a Confederate Indian brigade on Beattie's Prairie near an abandoned frontier fort, a half-hour fight in which Cherokee soldiers stood on both sides of the line.

**Location:** 36.3680, -94.6478

**Categories:** civil-war, battlefields, oklahoma, indian-territory, native-american-history, military-history

## Introduction

Union scouts told James G. Blunt that about 7,000 Confederates were waiting for him on the Indian Territory border near Maysville. The real number was roughly 1,500. Blunt did not know that when he set out, and his decision to strike anyway, after a grueling night march, produced a decisive Union victory. On October 22, 1862, on the sweep of grassland called Beattie's Prairie beside the abandoned post of Old Fort Wayne, in what is now Delaware County, Oklahoma, it took about half an hour.

## A Fort Without Soldiers

Old Fort Wayne was an abandoned pre-war Federal garrison on the southern edge of the sprawling Beattie's Prairie. In the fall of 1862, Confederate Colonel Douglas H. Cooper made camp there with a brigade drawn largely from the Indian nations. Its order of battle included a Choctaw and Chickasaw regiment, Cherokee units among them Colonel Stand Watie's 2nd Cherokee Mounted Rifles, a Creek battalion under Lieutenant Colonel Chilly McIntosh, and Howell's Texas Battery with four guns. Cooper had already been pushed out of Newtonia, Missouri, earlier in October. Now he posted pickets four miles north at Maysville, a small village sitting directly on the boundary between Arkansas and Indian Territory, 23 miles west of Bentonville.

## Through the Night

Blunt's First Division of the Army of the Frontier numbered about 3,500 men, smaller than the force he believed he faced but better trained and equipped than many newly raised Confederate units. At 7 p.m. on October 20 he left camp near Pea Ridge with two brigades, marched south through the night and reached Bentonville shortly after sunrise. There he waited until 5 p.m. for his cumbersome supply wagons. Anxious to catch the Confederates by surprise, he then pushed 25 miles west, halting at 2 a.m. to let most of his men sleep. The 2nd Kansas Cavalry kept going. At 5 a.m. it drove in the Confederate pickets at Maysville while the rest of the division was still asleep nearly seven miles behind.

## Half an Hour on the Prairie

The Kansas cavalry followed the pickets into Indian Territory and found Cooper's main line stretched about three-quarters of a mile along an east-west road, facing north with dense woods at its back and the Texas battery in the center. Blunt brought up mountain howitzers to duel with the Confederate guns and sent the 2nd Kansas to clear skirmishers from a ridge in front of the line. When the rest of the division arrived, about 7 a.m., he concentrated his attack on the thinly held center. The howitzers silenced the battery, and Kansas and Cherokee soldiers tore a wide hole in Cooper's line. After roughly thirty minutes of stiff resistance, much of the Confederate force was in full retreat, leaving its artillery behind, and Blunt pursued for nearly seven miles.

## Nations Divided

The fight laid bare how deeply the Civil War split the Indian nations. Blunt's division included Indian Home Guard regiments with Cherokee soldiers, among them the 3rd Indian Home Guard under Colonel William A. Phillips, fighting alongside troops from Kansas and Indiana. Across the field stood Cherokee regiments loyal to the Confederacy, together with Choctaw, Chickasaw and Creek soldiers. Blunt lost 14 men. Cooper lost approximately 150, including a reported 50 dead who were buried on the battlefield. Many of those who fought here, on both sides, belonged to nations forced west only a generation earlier, and now the war had followed them onto their new land.

## Seventy Miles South

The Confederates fell back some 70 miles toward Fort Gibson, and the Union again held undisputed possession of Indian Territory north of the Arkansas River. The defeat was a setback for the broad Confederate offensive of 1862, which stretched from the Tidewater in the East to the plains of Indian Territory. Less than two months later, Blunt would fight Thomas Hindman's army at Prairie Grove, and in 1863 he was appointed a major general of volunteers. The State of Arkansas later placed a commemorative marker at the northwest corner of state routes 43 and 72 in Maysville.
