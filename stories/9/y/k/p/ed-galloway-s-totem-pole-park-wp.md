# Ed Galloway's Totem Pole Park

Near Foyil, Oklahoma, a retired manual arts teacher spent his last decades building the "World's Largest Concrete Totem Pole" and a whimsical sculpture park that volunteers later rescued from ruin.

**Location:** 36.4372, -95.4481

**Categories:** Folk art, Roadside attractions, Route 66, Sculpture parks, National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma

## Introduction

Six tons of steel, 28 tons of cement and 100 tons of sand and rock: that was Ed Galloway's recipe for a retirement. On a small farm near Foyil, Oklahoma, the former manual arts teacher mixed those materials into a concrete tower about 90 feet tall, covered with roughly 200 bas-relief images. Today it is billed as the "World's Largest Concrete Totem Pole," the centerpiece of Ed Galloway's Totem Pole Park, eleven sculptures and one building spread across the park's grounds in Rogers County, a few miles east of historic Route 66.

## From Tree Trunks to Teaching

Galloway was born in 1880 in Springfield, Missouri. He served in the U.S. Army in the Philippines, and when he came home he began carving monumental sculptures out of tree trunks. His work caught the eye of Charles Page, a Tulsa-based philanthropist, in 1914. Page hired him to teach manual arts at the Children's Home orphanage in Sand Springs, Oklahoma, where Galloway spent years showing young people how to make things with their hands. Woodcarver, sculptor and folk artist, he had found a way to earn a living by passing his craft to children who had lost their families.

## A Retirement Poured in Concrete

Galloway retired in 1937 and moved to a small farm near Foyil, about 10 miles northeast of Claremore. He soon started on a totem pole, and instead of wood he used modern building materials. He finished it in 1948. The tower stands about 90 feet tall and 30 feet wide at its base, and roughly 200 bas-relief images cover its surface, including brightly colored Native American portraits, symbols and animal figures. Around it he added four smaller concrete totems, two ornate concrete picnic tables with animal-shaped seats, a barbecue and four sets of animal-form gateposts. He also built the Fiddle House, an eleven-sided building supported by 25 concrete totem poles inside and out, which once displayed hand-carved fiddles, handmade furniture and bas-relief portraits of presidents through John F. Kennedy.

## Why He Built It

Galloway lived at the park and worked on it every day until his death. Accounts of his reasons differ. Some say he hoped to use his work to educate young people about Native Americans. Others say he simply thought the park would be a good place for youngsters, Boy Scouts in particular, to visit. Either way, the man who had spent years teaching orphans to work with their hands built something meant for children to explore. The park is now owned and operated by the Rogers County Historical Society, and it was added to the National Register of Historic Places on March 30, 1999, recognition for a place that began as one man's retirement hobby.

## Bringing Back the Colors

In the decades after Galloway's death, weather and neglect took their toll, and the sculptures began to deteriorate. In the 1990s, the Kansas Grassroots Art Association led an extensive restoration. The outdoor sculptures were restored and repainted, and the Fiddle House, brought back from the brink of collapse, became the Fiddle House Museum and Gift Shop. The great totem needed more. Its lower 20 feet were restored in 2008 and 2009, and work on the upper section began in mid-June 2015 and was completed by mid-August 2020. More than seventy years after Galloway set the last of his concrete, its colors were bright again.
