# Blockhouse on Signal Mountain (Oklahoma)

A fourteen-by-sixteen-foot stone lookout on a Fort Sill summit, where Army signalmen once passed messages by flag, mirror and lamp across contested Indian Territory.

**Location:** 34.6743, -98.4906

**Categories:** National Register of Historic Places in Comanche County, Oklahoma, Military installations established in 1871, 1871 establishments in Indian Territory, American Indian Wars, Fort Sill

## Introduction

Before wire stretched across these hills, a message from Fort Sill could travel by light. From the summit of Signal Mountain, soldiers of the Army Signal Corps worked with semaphore flags, heliographs and signal lamps, sending information along an unbroken line of sight to the next station, which read it and passed it on. The small stone blockhouse that sheltered that work still crowns the mountain at an elevation of about 1,750 feet, reached by Mackenzie Hill Road inside the Fort Sill Military Reservation north of Lawton, Oklahoma. Built around 1870 or 1871, when this was still Indian Territory, it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on November 29, 1978.

## Fourteen Feet by Sixteen

The blockhouse is modest by any measure: four stone walls enclosing a space fourteen feet by sixteen, built from native stone gathered in the Wichita Mountains around it. It belongs to the earliest generation of stone construction at Fort Sill, a post that began as Camp Wichita at Medicine Bluffs and grew into a key Army installation during the American Indian Wars. The blockhouse was established under the framework of the Medicine Lodge Treaty of 1867, three decades before the Kiowa-Comanche-Apache Opening of 1901 transformed the lands around it. Today the structure sits within Fort Sill's West Range, in Comanche County, a small square of frontier masonry on a working military reservation.

## Speaking in Light

The summit served two purposes at once. It was a signal station, and it was a meteorological observatory. From this height, the Army linked Signal Mountain with Medicine Bluffs, Mount Scott and Fort Reno, which lay far to the north beyond the Canadian River. Optical signaling depended entirely on sight lines, and a mountaintop gave the best ones available. Flags, reflected sunlight and lamps carried the traffic until the Signal Corps strung field wire, bringing electric telegraphy to the post. The Fort Sill signal station formally began atmospheric observations and telegraphic communication in the summer of 1875, and its meteorological reports followed that September. The same vantage made the blockhouse a lookout, where soldiers could watch the surrounding country for movement and be ready to send troops out from the post into the rugged terrain of southwestern Indian Territory.

## Contested Ground

The country below the blockhouse was the homeland of the Kiowa, Comanche and Apache, shortgrass prairie and red earth where horse herds and bison hunts had shaped life for generations. United States policy in the 1870s upended that way of life, and resistance followed. The decade saw the Buffalo Hunters' War and the Red River War, and Southern Plains bands raided forts and military supply trains. Comanche and Kiowa fighters skirmished at Camp Supply on June 11, 1870, and struck the Warren Wagon Train in Young County, Texas, on May 18, 1871. The Army answered with a defensive chain of forts across Indian Territory and Texas. In September 1874, Captain Wyllys Lyman's train of thirty-six wagons and about sixty soldiers was besieged by Kiowa and Comanche warriors from September 9 to 14. That fight, known as Lyman's Wagon Train, was one of the major engagements of the Red River War.

## The Vanishing Herds

What the lookout could not watch for was the slower catastrophe unfolding across the plains. During the last quarter of the nineteenth century, hunters killed the North American bison on a scale that devastated the herds. The fur trade and the westward reach of the railroads drove the slaughter, and a lucrative market in buffalo robes and bones fed it. From 1870 to 1890, buffalo rifles such as the Sharps, the Remington Rolling Block, the Springfield Model 1873 and the Winchester Model 1876 were prized equipment on the Great Plains. For the peoples whose lives had centered on the bison, the loss reached far beyond hunting. The stone hut on Signal Mountain outlasted all of it, and it remains a quiet witness to the years when this summit watched over a world being remade.

## Flight Context

The blockhouse sits at 34.67433 N, 98.49058 W on the summit of Signal Mountain in the Fort Sill West Range, Comanche County, Oklahoma, north of Lawton and at the southeastern edge of the Wichita Mountains. Mount Scott rises to the northwest as a prominent reference. Fort Sill's artillery ranges lie beneath restricted airspace, so check current charts and NOTAMs and view the area from outside active restricted areas. Nearest airports: Henry Post Army Airfield (KFSI) on Fort Sill (military use) and Lawton-Fort Sill Regional Airport (KLAW), about two miles south of Lawton. Plains visibility is typically excellent, but spring brings strong thunderstorms.
