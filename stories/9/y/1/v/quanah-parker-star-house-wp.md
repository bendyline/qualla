# Quanah Parker Star House

Cattlemen paid for it, Theodore Roosevelt came to call, and nobody can say for sure why Comanche leader Quanah Parker painted stars across its roof.

**Location:** 34.6344, -98.6417

**Categories:** Comanche, Native American history of Oklahoma, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Historic houses, Comanche County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

Stars are painted across the roof of a two-story clapboard house in Cache, Oklahoma, and nobody can say for certain why they are there. Quanah Parker had them put up, and he took his reasons with him when he died inside these walls on February 23, 1911. What he left behind is a large two-story house with ten-foot ceilings and a picket fence, along with more than a century of competing explanations. The house has stood through a relocation, a storm and decades of decay, and at the latest reports it still waits on a restoration that has been estimated at more than a million dollars.

## From Tipi to Ten Rooms

Quanah was born around 1845, the son of Kwahadi Comanche chief Peta Nocona and Cynthia Ann Parker, an Anglo-American who had been abducted as a child during the 1836 raid on Fort Parker in Texas and raised among the Comanche. He became a war leader of the Kwahadi, the Antelope band. With their food source depleted and the army pressing them constantly, the Kwahadi surrendered in 1875. For many years afterward Quanah lived in a tipi on the reservation. Eventually he decided he needed quarters that matched his status among the Comanche and suited his role as a spokesman for the white cattle owners he dealt with. He also needed space, because the house had to hold his several wives and their children.

## A Cattleman's Gift

Parker asked the United States government for help paying for a house, and the request was denied. His friends in the cattle business stepped in instead, above all Samuel Burk Burnett, owner of the 6666 Ranch. Around 1890 they financed a clapboard home of two stories, with ceilings ten feet high and a picket fence around it, and with ten rooms by one count (the Handbook of Texas says twenty-two). Construction cost slightly over $2,000, or about $48,000 in 2010 dollars. The house did not start out in Cache. It was first built near the Wichita Mountains, north of town, on land that is now Fort Sill's west range.

## Nobody Turned Away

The heart of the house was its formal dining room, papered and warmed by a wood-burning stove. Parker hosted white business associates, celebrities and tribal members at that table. Theodore Roosevelt was the most famous guest. In April 1905 the president visited him at the Star House, and the two went wolf hunting with Burnett near Frederick, Oklahoma. The house drew spiritual visitors as well. Parker was a strong advocate of the peyote religion associated with the Native American Church and conducted peyote ceremonies on the reservation, and practitioners often came to his home to ask his advice. He also fed hungry tribal members there, and people remembered that he never turned anyone away. He died at the Star House in 1911, in his mid-60s. In 1957 his remains were moved to the Fort Sill Post Cemetery.

## Reading the Stars

There are two explanations for the stars on the roof, and neither can be proven. One version says Parker meant them as a mark of rank, the same way stars on a uniform identify a general. The other comes from Parker's descendants, as told to the house's owner, Wayne Gipson, a nephew of later owner Herbert Woesner. In this version Parker had traveled to Washington, D.C., to speak with Theodore Roosevelt and stayed at a "five star hotel." He then had ten stars painted on his own roof so that when Roosevelt arrived, the president would know he was getting better accommodations: ten stars instead of five. It is a good story, but it remains family lore rather than documented fact.

## A House in Peril

After Parker's death his daughter, Laura Neda Parker Birdsong, bought the house. She later moved it from its original site to Cache, and in 1958 she sold it to Herbert Woesner. The Star House was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1970, but a listing did not stop its slow decline. Preservation Oklahoma placed it on the state's list of Most Endangered Historic Places. A storm in 2015 badly damaged the already crumbling structure, and the damage prompted new efforts to save it. A grant from the National Trust for Historic Preservation paid for an assessment of the building's condition and a plan to maintain it. The work is complicated because the house is privately owned, and full restoration has been estimated at more than one million dollars.

## Flight Context

The Star House stands in the small town of Cache at 34.6344°N, 98.6417°W, just west of Lawton in southwestern Oklahoma. The Wichita Mountains rise to the north and make the best visual reference. The peaks are easy to pick out against the surrounding plains, and the house's original site lay near them on what is now Fort Sill's west range. The house is too small to identify from altitude, so a low pass over Cache at about 2,000–3,000 ft AGL is the best way to take in the setting. Lawton–Fort Sill Regional Airport (KLAW) is about 12 nm to the east-southeast. Henry Post Army Airfield (KFSI) at Fort Sill is about 12 nm east and is military. Fort Sill's training ranges border Cache, so check current charts for active military airspace before flying near the mountains. Visibility is usually good over this open country, but watch for thunderstorms building on the southern plains in spring and summer.
