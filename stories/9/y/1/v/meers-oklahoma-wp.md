# Meers, Oklahoma

A 1901 gold-rush camp in the Wichita Mountain foothills that faded to a single building, a celebrated hamburger, and a fault line with a very long memory.

**Location:** 34.7828, -98.5789

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in Comanche County, Oklahoma, National Register of Historic Places in Comanche County, Oklahoma, Gold rush towns, Wichita Mountains, Populated places established in 1901

## Introduction

Meers was founded to chase gold. More than a century later, people still make the drive out State Highway 115 into the foothills of the Wichita Mountains, but what they come for is a hamburger. The gold-rush town that sprang up here in 1901 has almost entirely vanished, leaving one survivor: the Meers Store and Restaurant, a building that has served as the area's post office, its last link to the boom years, and now a hamburger joint that Food Network once named the best in Oklahoma. Beneath all of it runs the Meers Fault, a reminder that this quiet corner of Comanche County has seen far bigger upheavals than a mining bust.

## A Boom Named for a Miner

The town took its name from Col. A. J. Meers, a mine operator who had worked the Wichita Mountains since 1886 (one account traces him to Cherokee County, Georgia, another calls him a Texas miner), and it was built for prospecting. For a few years the camp had real momentum. According to the Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History and Culture, Meers had a smelter and reached a peak population of five hundred before it faded in 1905, following the mining bust. A 1978 National Register nomination put the camp's population at perhaps 3,000 in 1903 and dated the end of the boom to about 1907, so estimates vary. The post office outlasted the miners by a very long time. It opened on March 12, 1902, operating out of the Meers Store; by 1978 it had become a branch of the Lawton post office, and postal service there is reported to have lasted until February 1989. Today the people who live around Meers have Lawton mailing addresses, and the community is unincorporated.

## The Last Building Standing

Of everything the prospectors built, only the Meers Store and Restaurant remains. That survival earned it a place on the National Register of Historic Places in 1978, listed under the name Meers Mining Camp because it was the only relic left from the community's gold-rush days. The store has found a second life that the founders could hardly have imagined. Food Network once named it the best hamburger joint in Oklahoma and one of the best in the United States, largely on the strength of its signature MeersBurger, made with Texas Longhorn beef. The old boomtown's most lasting treasure, it turns out, came off the grill rather than out of the ground.

## A Fault With a Long Memory

Meers is defined by more than its store. The Meers Fault, a planar fracture in the Earth's crust, runs through this part of southwestern Oklahoma, and it has drawn close study from geologists. Geological research indicates that the fault last ruptured roughly 1,100 to 1,300 years ago, in an earthquake estimated at around magnitude 7. The Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History and Culture describes a fault scarp about sixteen miles long left by a very large prehistoric earthquake north of the present town. Modern scientists have kept watch here too. In 1985, to monitor a seismic event, the Oklahoma Geological Survey installed a seismograph inside the Meers Store, so that for a time the same building held burgers and earthquake instruments under one roof.

## Rumors of Spanish Silver

Long before the 1901 prospectors, rumors of lost Spanish gold and silver mines in the Wichita Mountains go back to the early 1600s. A tantalizing clue turned up in 1955, when a primitive mill known as an arrastra was reported south of Meers. It was thought to be of Spanish design and origin, but the arrastras that survive in the Wichitas are generally dated to the 1901 gold rush, and a National Register nomination for one of them found no evidence that Spaniards ever mined here. An arrastra ground ore into fragments small enough to carry on pack animals over long overland routes. Whoever built the mills, they are a reminder of how many people came to these hills hoping the rock would pay.

## Flight Context

Meers lies at 34.7828 N, 98.5789 W on State Highway 115 in the northern foothills of the Wichita Mountains, Comanche County, Oklahoma. From 3,000-5,000 feet AGL, look for the highway threading between low granite hills, with Mount Scott to the southeast and Lake Lawtonka to the east. The Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge lies just to the south, and Fort Sill's restricted artillery ranges are farther south and southeast; check charts and NOTAMs. Nearest airports: Lawton-Fort Sill Regional Airport (KLAW), south of Lawton, and Henry Post Army Airfield (KFSI) on Fort Sill (military use). Spring thunderstorms can build quickly over the plains.
