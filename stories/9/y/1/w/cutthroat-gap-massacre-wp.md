# Cutthroat Gap massacre

In 1833 an Osage war party fell on a Kiowa camp left almost without its fighting men, killing about 150 people and carrying off the sacred Tai-me bundle along with two children.

**Location:** 34.8364, -98.8319

**Categories:** Massacres of Native Americans, Native American history of Oklahoma, Pre-statehood history of Oklahoma, Kiowa, 1833 in North America

## Introduction

A Kiowa boy had taken his family's horse out beyond the camp to graze when he saw a man crouched behind some rocks. The man was an Osage warrior. The boy ran back and raised the alarm, but the warning came too late to save the camp. At the place now called Cutthroat Gap, near present-day Cooperton in Kiowa County, Oklahoma, Osage warriors killed about 150 Kiowa people. Most of the band's fighting men were away, so most of the dead were women, children and elders.

## A Council Scattered

The massacre took place in 1833, which Oklahoma remembers as "The Year the Stars Fell." That was also the year of the great Leonid meteor storm of November 12–13, an event several Native American nations marked in their calendars. A few days before the raid, Kiowa bands from across the Plains had gathered near Rainy Mountain Creek to hold council and plan the annual Sun Dance. Then someone found an Osage arrow. The bands scattered to escape the threat, meaning to reunite before the ceremony. One group, led by a chief named A'date, or Island Man, traveled southwest looking for better grazing and resources. It made camp west of the mountain, believing it was safe. Most of the men then left, some to raid the Utes and others to hunt bison. The Kiowa did not know that Osage warriors from the Three Forks country had been trailing them since the council, looking for horses.

## Courage in the Chaos

The Kiowa who remained were surprised, outnumbered and disorganized, and they could only flee. Mothers searched desperately for their babies as the Osage rode into the camp. They killed women, children and elderly people, beheaded some of the dead and burned the tipis. People fought back anyway. A visiting Pawnee warrior held off the attackers so that some of the women and children could escape. A father is said to have carried his son in his teeth as he ran. He would set the boy down to shoot arrows at the Osage and then pick him up and run again. A young boy stood between the attackers and a group of women and children, firing arrow after arrow. A mother fought off an Osage warrior by herself with a baby in a cradleboard on her back while she held her young daughter's hand. One elder got away and brought word to the nearby camps.

## What the Warriors Found

The Kiowa warriors came back to find the mutilated bodies of their families, and in the Kiowa telling the heads of the victims had been left in cooking pots at the camp. The Kiowa calendar remembers it as the summer they cut off their heads. The Osage had also taken two children, a brother and sister named Thunder and White Weasel. They took the Tai-me as well, the sacred medicine bundle that the Kiowa needed to hold the Sun Dance. Without it the Kiowa could not perform the ceremony, and for two years they did not. The disaster also changed the band's leadership. Island Man was disgraced and replaced by To-hau-san, who took on the job of dealing with the Osage and recovering the Tai-me.

## A Pony for the Tai-me

Peace came slowly. The Osage offered White Weasel back as a gesture of goodwill, and she was returned to her people during the first Dragoon Expedition in 1834. Her brother did not come home, because Thunder had died in captivity. The Osage then let the Kiowa take back the Tai-me in exchange for a single pony, and relations between the two nations began to ease. The Kiowa never went back to Cutthroat Gap. It had been a favorite camping place, but no Kiowa ever camped there again. Some people came to believe that the spirits of the dead still wandered the gap and could be heard.

## Flight Context

Cutthroat Gap lies at about 34.8364°N, 98.8319°W in Kiowa County, Oklahoma, near the small town of Cooperton, about 25 nm northwest of Lawton. Fly over at about 3,000–4,000 ft AGL to see the gap in the context of the surrounding hills and prairie. Lawton and Fort Sill to the southeast make good visual references for finding the area. The nearest airport with an ICAO code is Hobart Regional (KHBR), about 14 nm northwest. Lawton–Fort Sill Regional (KLAW) is about 26 nm southeast. Henry Post Army Airfield (KFSI) at Fort Sill and Altus Air Force Base (KLTS) to the southwest are both military fields. The Fort Sill ranges lie between the site and Lawton, so check current charts for active military airspace. Visibility is usually good over the open plains, but watch for thunderstorms in spring and summer.
