# Babbs Switch fire

On Christmas Eve 1924, a candlelit tree set a crowded one-room Oklahoma schoolhouse ablaze, killing thirty-six people and forcing a state to rewrite how its schools were built.

**Location:** 34.9580, -99.0608

**Categories:** Fires in Oklahoma, 1924 fires in the United States, School fires in the United States, One-room schoolhouses in Oklahoma, Kiowa County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

The tree stood at the front of the room, its branches lit with candles, with presents hung among the needles for the children to open once the program ended. It was Christmas Eve, 1924, and more than 200 people had crowded into the one-room schoolhouse at Babbs Switch, in Kiowa County, Oklahoma. Before the night was over, the party had become one of the deadliest school fires in American history. Thirty-six people died. Whole families were lost, and more than half of the dead were children. The National Fire Protection Association counts it as the sixth-deadliest school fire on record in the United States.

## Candles, Tinsel and One Door

The fire began as a teenage student dressed as Santa Claus was taking gifts from the tree to hand to the children. Flames caught the paper decorations, the tinsel and the dry needles, then spread swiftly to the tree, the stage and the building itself. People rushed for the only usable door. It opened inward, and it was soon jammed with people trying to get out. The windows offered no escape either, because they had been covered with secure metal screens to keep vandals from breaking into the school. One boy got out through a window after someone managed to pry open a corner of a screen. The next day, 32 people were reported dead and 37 injured were being treated in hospitals in nearby Hobart. Four of the injured later died, bringing the toll to thirty-six.

## What the Investigators Found

The National Fire Protection Association and the Oklahoma Inspection Bureau both examined the fire quickly and published their findings in January 1925. The NFPA reported a single door. The state bureau noted a second door, but it opened into a coal bin that had been added after the school was built, so it could never have served as an exit. The danger had not been a secret. In the years before the fire, the Oklahoma State Fire Marshal had tried to identify every one-room, one-door schoolhouse in the state because of their known risks, but county superintendents had not been forthcoming. Amid the grief came small acts of kindness. The day after the fire, 25 children in Muskogee packed up their own new Christmas toys and sent them to the children who had survived.

## A Law Written in Grief

Babbs Switch changed how Oklahoma thought about its schools. Officials and worried citizens began requesting safety inspections on their own, and by February 1925 the fire marshal's office had received more than 400 requests, with over 150 schools already inspected and improved. The legislature then passed the Fawks Bill. Every school now needed at least two doors, and every door had to open outward. Window screens had to be removable from inside, and fire extinguishers had to be kept on the premises. Copies of the law went to every school official in the state so that no one could plead ignorance. By July 1926, assistant fire marshal John Carrol could report that school officials had willingly made the recommended changes. "Schoolhouses of Oklahoma are virtually all in excellent shape now," he said.

## The Child Who Never Came Home

One family's loss carried an especially cruel epilogue. Three-year-old Mary Edens was among those lost, but her body was never recovered, and her parents held on to the hope that she had somehow survived. In 1957 a woman from Barstow, California, came forward claiming to be the missing girl, and she was reunited with the Edens family on the television program Art Linkletter's House Party. A book telling her story, Mary, Child of Tragedy, was published in 1980 as a true account and later discredited. A local newspaper editor had known the claim was false. The editor kept that knowledge private until 1999, at the request of Mary's father, who believed his wife could not endure losing her child a second time. Today a stone monument stands on the site of the former school, bearing a short account of the fire and the names of the dead.

## Flight Context

The site of the Babbs Switch school lies at 34.958 N, 99.0608 W in the farmland of Kiowa County, southwestern Oklahoma, a few miles south of Hobart. There is little to see from the air beyond the section-line roads and fields that surround the memorial; the best reference is the town of Hobart just to the north. A low pass at 2,000-3,000 feet AGL offers the clearest view of the rural setting. Nearest airport: Hobart Regional Airport (KHBR), about three miles southeast of Hobart and very close to the site. Plains winds are often strong, and spring brings severe thunderstorms.
