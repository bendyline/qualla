# Antelope Hills expedition

In 1858 Texas Rangers and their Tonkawa allies crossed into Indian Territory in open defiance of federal law and struck Comanche villages beneath the Antelope Hills - a raid whose methods the U.S. Army would later adopt across the Plains.

**Location:** 35.9042, -99.8844

**Categories:** Texas-Indian Wars, Battles involving the Comanche, Massacres of Native Americans, Native American history of Oklahoma, Indian Territory, Conflicts in 1858

## Introduction

His job, Captain John S. "Rip" Ford said afterward, was to "find and fight Indians, not to learn geography." It was a blunt line from a man who had knowingly marched his force across the Red River into Indian Territory, where federal law and numerous treaties forbade exactly what he intended to do. The campaign he led, from January to May 1858, ended on May 12 in a series of fights among the Comanche villages along the Canadian River in what is now western Oklahoma, beside the low rise of the Antelope Hills near Little Robe Creek. The day is often called the Battle of Little Robe Creek. For the Comanche and Kiowa, it marked the start of a long unraveling.

## Pressure on the Comancheria

The years from 1856 to 1858 were particularly vicious and bloody on the Texas frontier. Settlers kept pushing into the Comancheria, the Comanche homeland, plowing under valuable hunting grounds and taking the grazing land the Comanche needed for their horse herds. The United States had also done much to block the traditional Comanche raids into Mexico. The Comanche struck back with a series of ferocious, bloody raids on the settlers. The U.S. Army could not stop the violence. Units kept being transferred away, and as the Civil War approached, the 2nd Cavalry was transferred from Texas to Utah. Federal law and treaties also barred the Army from attacking Native people inside Indian Territory, where the Comanche and Kiowa still lived in their traditional lands while raiding south into Texas.

## An Alliance of Old Enemies

Ford looked for allies among the Comanche's traditional enemies. The Tonkawa were then living on the Brazos Reservation in Texas, and on March 19, 1858, Ford went there to recruit them. The reservation's Indian agent called the Tonkawa leader, Chief Placido, to a war council and stirred his anger against their common foe. About 120 Native fighters joined the campaign, most of them Tonkawa under Placido, with Anadarko and Shawnee men among the rest. Placido was hailed as the "faithful and implicitly trusted friend of the whites." Roughly as many Texas Rangers rode alongside them. Ford's orders from Governor Hardin Runnels were brutal: follow every trail of hostile or suspected hostile Indians, inflict the most severe punishment, which meant killing warriors and their families and destroying homes and food, and allow no interference from "any source." That phrase was aimed at the United States itself, whose soldiers and agents might try to enforce federal law.

## Across the Red River

Scouts fanned out to find Comanche camps north of the Red River. In April 1858, Ford set up Camp Runnels near the former town of Belknap. He and Placido meant to follow the Comanche and Kiowa to their strongholds among the hills of the Canadian River and on into the Wichita Mountains, to kill their warriors, destroy their food, strike their homes and families, and break their ability to make war. On April 29 the Rangers and their allies from the Brazos Reservation crossed the Red River into Indian Territory. Ford knew he was violating federal law and treaties by doing so.

## Dawn in the Canadian Valley

At dawn on May 12, Ford's force attacked a small Comanche village in the Canadian River valley, flanked by the Antelope Hills. Later that day they struck a second village, which fought back hard until its chief, Iron Jacket, was killed. His name probably came from the Spanish coat of scale mail he wore into battle. Iron Jacket's son, Peta Nocona, then arrived with reinforcements, setting off a third clash. By day's end the Rangers and their allies withdrew toward Texas, while more Comanche, along with Kiowa and Kiowa Apache allies, were arriving. Accounts of Ford's own losses vary, but they were light: most sources count two men killed, a Ranger and an ally from the Brazos Reservation, and several more wounded. It reportedly killed 76 Comanche and took 16 prisoners and 300 horses, and it burned both Iron Jacket's village and the first village it had attacked. These were villages, not forts, and the attack fell on families as well as warriors.

## A Blueprint for What Followed

Ford returned to Texas eager to raise more Rangers and ride straight back into the heart of the Comancheria. The governor, however, had spent the state's entire defense budget for the year, and the Rangers were disbanded. The campaign still changed warfare on the Plains. For the first time, Texan or American forces had reached the core of the Comancheria, attacked Comanche villages and returned home. Only the Civil War delayed what came next. After the war, the U.S. Army adopted many of Ford's tactics against the Plains tribes, including attacks on women and children as well as warriors and the destruction of their food supply, the buffalo.

## Flight Context

The Antelope Hills stand at about 35.9042 N, 99.8844 W in a bend of the Canadian River in northwestern Roger Mills County, Oklahoma, near the Texas Panhandle line. From 3,000-4,500 feet AGL, the low, isolated hills are easy to pick out against the surrounding plains, with the braided, sandy channel of the Canadian River curving around them. Nearest airports: Gage Airport (KGAG) in Ellis County to the north, and the small municipal airport at Cheyenne, Oklahoma, to the south. Expect gusty winds and fast-building spring and summer thunderstorms.
