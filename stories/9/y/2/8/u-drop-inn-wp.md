# U-Drop Inn

Sketched in the dirt with an old nail, Shamrock's Art Deco gas station and cafe rose to glory on Route 66, faded when the interstate passed it by, and came back as a landmark and a movie star.

**Location:** 35.2268, -100.2487

**Categories:** U.S. Route 66 in Texas, Art Deco architecture in Texas, Tourist attractions along U.S. Route 66, National Register of Historic Places in Texas, Restaurants in Texas, Wheeler County, Texas

## Introduction

The design began with an old nail and a patch of dirt. John Nunn scratched his idea onto the ground in the driveway of a motel in Shamrock, Texas, and from that rough sketch rose one of the most striking buildings on Route 66: a gas station and cafe dressed in Art Deco, with two flared towers, geometric detailing, curving walls of glazed ceramic tile and bright neon accents. The U-Drop Inn opened on April 1, 1936, at the corner where Route 66 met U.S. Highway 83 in Wheeler County. It has outlived the highway that made it famous, and a whole new generation has met it on the movie screen.

## A Sketch in the Dirt

J. M. Tindall built the U-Drop Inn using the design created by his friend Nunn, and the National Park Service credits Tindall and R. C. Lewis with the construction, at a cost of $23,000. Architect J. C. Berry drew up the plans. The building went up in structural clay tile faced with terra-cotta glazed brick, and its zigzag motifs, strong vertical lines and decorative metal "tulip" made it an excellent example of Art Deco, a style far more common on big-city office towers and public buildings than on a roadside filling station. It held two businesses side by side. Tower Station, on the western end, sold Conoco fuel, while the U-Drop Inn cafe filled the eastern side. A third space intended as a store never opened as one; it became a ballroom and overflow dining room instead. Even the cafe's name had a local story behind it: according to the Park Service, it came from a schoolboy's winning entry in a naming contest.

## The Swankiest of the Swank

The local newspaper greeted the opening with superlatives, calling the building "the most up-to-date edifice of its kind on U.S. Highway 66 between Oklahoma City and Amarillo," and it described the cafe as "the swankiest of the swank eating places." At the time it opened, the U-Drop was the only cafe within 100 miles of Shamrock, and business was brisk. Nunn sold the inn after several years but bought it back in 1950, renaming it Nunn's Cafe. After his death in 1957, his wife Bebe sold it to Grace Brunner, who renamed it again as the Tower Cafe and added a Greyhound bus station. Through all its changes of name and owner, the cafe kept earning praise for the same thing: low-priced, tasty home cooking. Local tradition holds that one favorite sitting area in the cafe is where Elvis Presley sat during his time in Shamrock.

## When the Traffic Moved On

The Federal Aid Highway Act of 1956 set Route 66 on the path to decline, as turnpikes and newer highways bypassed its segments one by one. When Interstate 40 opened, Shamrock became one of the bypassed towns, and the U-Drop Inn, which had once served thousands of travelers, slid into disrepair. In 1984 the American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials decommissioned Route 66, which seemed to seal the fate of both the town and its famous stop. The business closed in the late 1990s. Yet preservationists had already begun paying attention. Starting in 1990, state and local historical groups worked to save landmarks along the old road, and on September 18, 1997, the U-Drop Inn was added to the National Register of Historic Places.

## Neon Returns to Shamrock

Rescue came in stages. In May 1999 the First National Bank of Shamrock bought the building and gave it to the city. Beginning in 2000, and funded primarily by a $1.7 million federal grant, Shamrock hired Phillips Swager Associates of Dallas and the preservation firm Architexas to restore it to its original glory as a museum, visitors' center, gift shop and home of the city's chamber of commerce. In 2006 the building found an unexpected new audience. The animated film Cars drew its fictional town of Radiator Springs from real Route 66 landmarks, and the U-Drop Inn's distinctive architecture appears as Ramone's body and paint shop. Weather tested the restoration in May 2013, when a hailstorm damaged the neon; the broken elements were replaced with LEDs in February 2014. On February 15, 2025, the cafe opened again under new ownership, serving classic American food beneath the towers once more.

## Flight Context

The U-Drop Inn stands at 35.2268 N, 100.2487 W in Shamrock, Texas, at the intersection of historic Route 66 and U.S. Highway 83, just south of Interstate 40 in the eastern Texas Panhandle. From 1,500-3,000 feet AGL, find the crossing of US 83 and the old Route 66 corridor through town; the building's twin towers stand out on the corner. Nearest airport: Shamrock Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 2F1, no ICAO code), about three miles from town, an unattended strip with limited services. Panhandle winds are often strong and gusty.
