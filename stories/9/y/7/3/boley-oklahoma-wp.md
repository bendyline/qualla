# Boley, Oklahoma

Founded in 1903 on a Creek Freedwoman's land, Boley became one of the most celebrated Black towns in America, and it is one of only 13 of Oklahoma's historic Black towns still standing.

**Location:** 35.4928, -96.4817

**Categories:** African-American history of Oklahoma, Freedmen's towns, Muscogee Nation, Towns in Okfuskee County, Oklahoma, National Historic Landmarks

## Introduction

"All Men Up, Not Some Down." Under editor Oniel H. Bradley, that was the motto of the Boley Progress, the newspaper of a town built on a premise its own railroad had to be convinced of: that Black citizens could govern themselves. Boley opened for settlement in the Creek Nation of Indian Territory on September 26, 1903, and was incorporated in 1905. Of the 50 identifiable Black towns established in Oklahoma between 1865 and 1920, it is one of only 13 still in existence. Its rodeo has run since the town's first year, and the 2020 census counted 1,091 people there, a figure that includes the inmates of a state prison.

## A Freedwoman's Land

Boley's roots run through the history of the Muscogee (Creek) Nation, which had held Black people in slavery. Their descendants, known as the Creek Freedmen, were each allotted 160 acres through the Dawes Commission, and some of them set up independent townships. Boley rose on land owned by Abigail Barnett, the daughter of a Creek Freedman. Thomas M. Haynes, the principal founder, served as first townsite manager, while Lake Moore, a white speculator, put up the $500 needed to lease Barnett's land for five years. The name came from J. B. Boley, a white roadmaster for the Fort Smith & Western Railroad who oversaw a depot in the middle of town. According to the Oklahoma Historical Society's marker, he convinced the railroad that Black citizens could govern themselves, and that idea soon boosted the town's population to 4,200.

## The Crown Jewel

What those settlers built drew national attention. Boley's businesses came to include what is believed to be the first nationally chartered Black-owned bank in the country, three cotton gins, a movie theater, hotels, and several law and dental practices. David J. Turner embodied the town's drive, serving repeatedly as mayor and councilman while also working as a pharmacist and bank president. Bradley's Progress promoted an "emancipationist vision" of Boley as a place where Black southerners could escape discrimination and live as "business men, farmers, merchants, and wage workers," free from the antagonism of hostile white mobs. Bradley also worried, though, that separation from whites carried to an extreme could harm Black communities in the long run. Booker T. Washington visited twice, and according to historian Currie Ballard, he deemed Boley the finest Black town in the world. Ballard himself called it "the crown jewel of all the black towns in Oklahoma."

## Statehood and the Color Line

Oklahoma became the 46th state on November 16, 1907, and statehood brought Jim Crow segregation laws, pushed largely by the white Democrat William H. Murray. Disenfranchisement followed. A "grandfather clause" imposed a literacy test on anyone whose ancestors had lacked the right to vote before 1866, a rule that in practice targeted the descendants of enslaved people. In 1925 the state located an institution in Boley under the harsh name of its era, the "State Training School for Incorrigible Negro Boys"; it would later become the John Lilley Correctional Center. The Boley Progress ceased publication in 1926, and in 1939 both the Fort Smith & Western Railroad and the town itself went bankrupt.

## The Day the Town Stood Up

Boley's most famous day came on November 23, 1932, when three members of Pretty Boy Floyd's gang tried to rob the Farmers and Merchants Bank. Floyd had warned his men not to attack Boley because its residents were well armed, but the interracial trio ignored him. While George Birdwell and C. C. Patterson emptied the register, bank president David Turner, on duty that morning, sounded the alarm to alert the town. Birdwell shot and killed him. As Patterson tried to reach the getaway car, he came face to face with Boley's citizens, who had grabbed whatever they could find: guns, screwdrivers, tools. When it was over, Birdwell and a second robber, Charles Glass, were dead, Patterson was seriously wounded, and the money had been recovered. Turner, who had served Boley as mayor, pharmacist and banker, died defending its bank.

## Rodeo Dust and Barbecue Smoke

Boley endures. The Boley Historic District was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1975, and the Boley Rodeo, a tradition since 1903, is the longest-running Black rodeo in the United States; today the town hosts the Annual Boley Rodeo & Bar-B-Que Festival. In 1922, rodeo legend Bill Pickett, credited as the inventor of bulldogging, shot his films The Bull-Dogger and The Crimson Skull here. Hard times have not disappeared. The high school closed in 2007 and the elementary school in 2010 because of declining enrollment. The 2020 census counted 1,091 people, up from 423 in 1980, though the later figure includes inmates of the John Lilley Correctional Center.

## Flight Context

Boley sits at approximately 35.49°N, 96.48°W on US Highway 62 in Okfuskee County, about 13 miles east of Prague and 11 miles west of Okemah. From 2,000-3,500 feet AGL, follow US-62 to find the small town grid. Okemah Municipal Airport (FAA identifier F81) is near Okemah, about 11 miles east, and Shawnee Regional Airport (KSNL) is roughly 28 miles west-southwest. Central Oklahoma sees frequent spring thunderstorms; fall usually brings the clearest skies.
