# Battle of Ingalls

On September 1, 1893, fourteen deputy marshals rode into the Oklahoma Territory town of Ingalls to capture the Doolin-Dalton Gang, and the gunfight that followed killed three lawmen and at least one bystander while most of the outlaws escaped.

**Location:** 36.1019, -96.8817

**Categories:** American Old West gunfights, American frontier, 1893 in Oklahoma Territory, Lawmen of the American Old West, Payne County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

Ingalls was named for a United States senator, John J. Ingalls of Kansas. The town grew out of the 1889 land run into the Unassigned Lands and got its own post office in 1890. By 1893 it had also become a refuge for the Doolin-Dalton Gang, a band of train and bank robbers who had been at work since around 1891. Ingalls harbored them unwittingly, as it did many outlaws in those years. On September 1, 1893, a posse of fourteen men carrying deputy U.S. marshals' commissions rode in to end the arrangement. The gunfight that followed came in the closing years of the Old West era.

## The Posse Rides In

The posse was organized by the new U.S. Marshal, Evett Dumas "E.D." Nix, and it was led into town by Deputy Marshal John Hixon. The shooting began when the lawmen confronted George "Bittercreek" Newcomb, who was badly wounded after firing no more than two rounds. According to Nix's own later account, a large group of outlaws then opened fire from a saloon. The marshals fired back, killing a horse and driving the gang out a side door and into a large stable. The saloon's owner, known only as Murray, joined the fight from his front doorway and was shot in the ribs and arm. Murray survived and served time in prison. Two years later he sued the government over his wounds, but he lost, in large part because Nix firmly defended his deputies.

## A Rifle in the Hotel Window

Then "Arkansas Tom" Jones opened fire with a rifle from the top floor of the Pierce O.K. Hotel. From that high vantage point he pinned the marshals behind cover and shot Deputy Marshal Thomas Hueston, who died the next day. Less than a year earlier, on November 29, 1892, Hueston and Ford County, Kansas, Sheriff Chalkey Beeson had shot and killed gang member Oliver Yantis. The gunfire caught townspeople too. A bystander named Young Simmons was hit by a stray round and killed as he tried to take cover inside Vaughn's Saloon, and another man, remembered as Old Man Ransom, was shot in the leg.

## Speed, Shadley and the Dynamite

Bill Doolin, one of the gang's two leaders, shot and killed Special Deputy Marshal Richard Speed. The other leader, Bill Dalton, was brought down when Deputy Marshal Lafayette Shadley fired and broke the leg of Dalton's horse. Dalton then shot Shadley, who died two days later. Dan "Dynamite Dick" Clifton was hit in the neck but could still ride. The standoff with Jones ended when Deputy Marshal Jim Masterson threw dynamite into his hiding place. The blast stunned him, and he was captured. By the time the shooting stopped, three lawmen had been fatally wounded, at least one bystander was dead, and most of the gang had escaped. Newcomb got away badly hurt, and Charley Pierce, who was later said to have been wounded too, hid out with him. Both were cared for by Newcomb's girlfriend, Rose Dunn.

## The Long Reckoning

Ingalls did not end the Doolin-Dalton Gang, but the reckoning came in time. Over the following years the gang was wiped out, a few members by capture and most killed by deputy marshals. Most were brought down by the Three Guardsmen, Heck Thomas, Bill Tilghman and Chris Madsen. Newcomb and Pierce were killed by the Dunn brothers, who were bounty hunters. In 1896 Bill Doolin was killed in a confrontation with Heck Thomas, and in 1898 Madsen killed Richard "Little Dick" West. The last of the gang, Roy Daugherty, lived until August 16, 1924, when he died in a shootout with police in Joplin, Missouri. Ingalls lost its post office in 1907 and today is a small community about nine miles east of Stillwater. A stone monument on Ash Street honors the marshals who fought there.

## Flight Context

Ingalls lies in eastern Payne County at about 36.1019°N, 96.8817°W, roughly nine miles east of Stillwater. Stillwater and the Oklahoma State University campus make an easy starting point, and from there it is a short hop east to the small community. A pass at 2,000–2,500 ft AGL is enough to see the town's layout, although the monument itself will be too small to spot. Stillwater Regional Airport (KSWO) is about 10 nm to the west-northwest. Visibility is usually good, but spring and summer thunderstorms can build rapidly over north-central Oklahoma.
