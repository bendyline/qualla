# Aerlex Fireworks plant explosion

On June 25, 1985, a spark while unloading flash powder set off an explosion that leveled the Aerlex Fireworks plant in tiny Hallett, Oklahoma, killing twenty-one people in the deadliest Oklahoma disaster of the 1980s.

**Location:** 36.2078, -96.5386

**Categories:** Explosions in 1985, Disasters in Oklahoma, Fires in Oklahoma, Explosions in the United States, Industrial fires and explosions in the United States, 1985 industrial disasters, 1985 in Oklahoma, June 1985 in the United States

## Introduction

On the morning of June 25, 1985, two workers at the Aerlex Fireworks plant in Hallett, Oklahoma, were unloading a metal container from the back of a pickup truck. Inside, according to later accounts, was flash powder, a volatile mixture of potassium perchlorate and powdered aluminum. The Fourth of July was nine days away, and the plant was working full-tilt. Within moments, most of it was gone. The explosion killed twenty-one people and injured five others, and those five were the only survivors. It was the deadliest disaster in Oklahoma during the 1980s.

## A Busy Season in a Small Town

Hallett was a town of about 120 people, roughly 30 miles west of Tulsa in northeastern Oklahoma. The Aerlex plant there manufactured Class B explosives, the kind used in fireworks displays and shows, and in late June its crews were busy. Twenty-six people were at the plant that morning. The facility's production buildings stood close together, a detail that would matter terribly once something went wrong. Fireworks manufacturing leaves little room for mistakes: the same compounds that paint the night sky in bright colors will tear apart anything around them if they ignite in the wrong place. At Aerlex, the most dangerous material on the property was about to be moved in a way that left no room for error.

## The Spark

The material was being carried in a metal container in the rear of a pickup truck. As the two workers unloaded it, investigators believe, metal scraped against metal between the container and the truck bed, and the friction produced a spark. That spark touched off the initial explosion. The Oklahoma State Fire Marshal's Office later concluded that the disaster most likely resulted from careless unloading of pyrotechnic materials from the truck beside an assembly building. Because the buildings used for fireworks production stood so close to one another, the blast did not stay contained. It spread through the facility almost instantly, with one detonation feeding the next. By the time it was over, the explosion had leveled most of the plant, leaving only a few parts of the factory standing. The force threw several employees considerable distances, and some were later found in a pond south of the loading dock area.

## Heard in Two Cities

The sound carried across a huge swath of the state. People heard the detonations as far away as Oklahoma City and Tulsa, and the blast rattled windows in Cleveland, about ten miles from Hallett. Above the ruined plant, a large mushroom cloud rose into the summer sky. On the ground, the work of recovery was grim. The Oklahoma State Bureau of Investigation was called in to help identify victims, some of whom had been thrown as much as 200 feet from the building. Each of the twenty-one people who died had come to work that morning for what should have been an ordinary shift. Their loss fell on a rural corner of Pawnee County where a town of barely more than a hundred residents now had to absorb a loss of staggering scale.

## Questions Without Answers

Oklahoma's state fire marshal, Fred Rucker, led the investigation. He later concluded that the first blast may have occurred where the pickup had been parked, noting that "the initial blast appeared to have been in a smaller building near where the burned-out shell of the truck was found." Investigators focused on the actions of the two workers who had been unloading the ingredients. But both men died in the primary explosion, and the five survivors could add nothing that would either support or refute the fire marshal's conclusions. The moment that mattered most had passed with no surviving witness to explain it.

## A State in Mourning

Governor George Nigh released a statement declaring that "all Oklahoma is grieved by this tragedy," and he ordered state flags flown at half-staff through June 30. He also sent members of the National Guard to assist officials at the site. Hallett's disaster did not happen in isolation. It was one of a series of unrelated fireworks accidents that struck other manufacturers around the same time, including an explosion in Ohio only thirty-six days earlier. Nine days after the blast, the Fourth of July arrived as it does every year, with fireworks blooming over towns across the country. Around Hallett, the holiday came in the shadow of twenty-one lost lives.

## Flight Context

The Aerlex plant site lies at 36.2078°N, 96.5386°W near the small town of Hallett in Pawnee County, northeastern Oklahoma, between Terlton and Jennings and roughly 30 miles west of Tulsa. Little of the plant remains to see, so this is a place to reflect rather than sightsee; 2,500–3,500 feet AGL gives a good view of the rolling, lightly wooded farmland. The town of Cleveland lies about 8–10 miles to the northeast, and the western arms of Keystone Lake spread out to the southeast. Cushing Municipal Airport (KCUH) is about 22 miles to the southwest, Stillwater Regional (KSWO) about 30 miles to the west, and Tulsa's Richard Lloyd Jones Jr. Airport (KRVS) and Tulsa International (KTUL) are roughly 33–36 miles to the east. Spring and early summer bring frequent thunderstorms to the area.
