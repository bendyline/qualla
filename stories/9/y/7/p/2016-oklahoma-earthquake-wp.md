# 2016 Oklahoma earthquake

On September 3, 2016, a fault no one had mapped slipped beneath Pawnee County, producing a magnitude 5.8 earthquake, the strongest in Oklahoma's recorded history, and a reckoning over the wastewater wells that ring it.

**Location:** 36.4300, -96.9320

**Categories:** Earthquakes in the United States, Natural disasters in Oklahoma, Pawnee County, Oklahoma, Induced seismicity, 2016 disasters in the United States

## Introduction

At 7:02 on a Saturday morning, September 3, 2016, the ground beneath Pawnee County began to move along a fault that appeared on no map. The shaking measured 5.8 on the moment magnitude scale, making it the strongest earthquake in Oklahoma's recorded history and a match for the 2011 Virginia quake, which was judged after it struck to be the most powerful in the eastern United States in the preceding seventy years. Some news reports said it was felt as far south as San Antonio, as far north as Fargo, as far east as Memphis and as far west as Gilbert, Arizona. What followed was less a cleanup than a reckoning with what lay underground.

## A Morning in Pawnee

The worst of it landed on Pawnee, near the epicenter, where various buildings were damaged and the destruction ranged from moderate to severe. The rupture was shallow, about 5.6 kilometers (3.5 miles) down, and it rated VII, or "very strong," on the Modified Mercalli intensity scale. Only one person was hurt: a man in Pawnee struck by a falling chimney. Within three and a half hours, nine local aftershocks followed, ranging from magnitude 2.6 to 3.6. The reach of the main shock was harder to believe. Some 225 miles away, in the Kansas City area, the Wyandotte County courthouse in Kansas was left with a crack running from its roof to the ground.

## The Fault Nobody Had Drawn

Geologists traced the quake to a previously unmapped, buried strike-slip fault, the kind along which two blocks of rock slide horizontally past each other. Its epicenter sat near the junction of two faults that had already been charted, the Watchorn and the Labette. The shaking also liquefied the ground in places, turning waterlogged soil briefly unstable, and here the pattern was revealing. The liquefaction damage did not follow the fault that ruptured at all. Instead it showed up where Quaternary alluvial deposits dominate, the loose sediment laid down by rivers. In other words, the ground's own makeup decided where it failed as much as the fault did. The quake edged past the magnitude 5.7 event that struck near Prague, Oklahoma, in 2011.

## Two Hundred Times the Old Rhythm

Pawnee's earthquake did not arrive out of a quiet sky. For the seven years before it, the central and eastern United States had seen a significant increase in induced earthquakes, tremors triggered by human activity rather than by nature alone. Oklahoma was the center of that change. Between 1975 and 2008 the state averaged one to three earthquakes a year. Between 2009 and 2016, its earthquake rate rose more than two hundredfold. In 2013, Oklahoma recorded more than 100 quakes of magnitude 3 or larger; in 2014, it recorded 585. That was more than three times as many as seismically active California experienced in the same year. Against that backdrop, a 5.8 was less a bolt from the blue than the loudest note in a rising chorus.

## Closing the Wells

The response came quickly and pointed underground. The Pawnee Nation declared a state of emergency and closed several of its buildings until the damage could be examined. Governor Mary Fallin declared a state of emergency for Pawnee County, where the worst damage lay. The Oklahoma Corporation Commission ordered more than 35 wastewater disposal wells within 725 square miles around the quake to be shut down rapidly, and days later Oklahoma regulators and the Environmental Protection Agency shut down 32 more in a newly identified earthquake-prone area. Then, in March 2017, the Pawnee Nation filed a lawsuit alleging that a group of oil companies injecting wastewater underground were responsible for causing the earthquake. The tribe filed it in its own tribal court, and one of its lawyers said the choice of venue was a way of stressing the nation's sovereignty. The shaking was over quickly. The argument over responsibility would not be.
