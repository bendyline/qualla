# Pawnee Bill Ranch

Wild West showman Gordon "Pawnee Bill" Lillie and his sharpshooting wife May built their stone Arts and Crafts home on Blue Hawk Peak in 1910, and the ranch still keeps the bison herd he championed.

**Location:** 36.3258, -96.8125

**Categories:** Oklahoma Historical Society, Ranches on the National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Houses in Pawnee County, Oklahoma, Museums in Pawnee County, Oklahoma, Historic house museums in Oklahoma, Biographical museums in Oklahoma, American West museums, Pawnee, Oklahoma, National Register of Historic Places in Pawnee County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

For years, Gordon W. Lillie made his living selling the American West to crowds who had never seen it. As "Pawnee Bill," he toured the country at the head of his own Wild West show, and in 1908 he joined forces with Buffalo Bill Cody himself. Yet the place he and his wife, May, dreamed about was not an arena. It was a hilltop in Pawnee, Oklahoma, overlooking a bend in the Black Bear River. In December 1910 that dream became a house of native stone, and more than a century later, bison still graze on the ranch below it, just as the showman hoped they always would.

## The Man Called Pawnee Bill

Lillie was born in Bloomington, Illinois, on February 14, 1860, but his stage name reflected the years he spent working with the Pawnee. He served as a teacher at the Pawnee agency and was appointed interpreter and secretary to Maj. Edward Bowman. That experience became the foundation of his career. In 1888 he began touring the country with "Pawnee Bill's Wild West," a venture that, in its later form, starred May Lillie as the "Champion Horseback Shot of the West." Like every Wild West show of its day, it packaged the frontier as entertainment, turning a period of conquest and upheaval for Native nations into spectacle for paying audiences. In 1908, Lillie merged his operation with Buffalo Bill Cody's, forming "Buffalo Bill's Wild West and Pawnee Bill's Great Far East." Two of the era's showmen now shared a single marquee.

## A House Quarried From the Hill

The house on the hill took nearly a year to build and was completed in December 1910. James Hamilton, an architect from Chester, Pennsylvania, designed it with input from both Lillies, producing a Tudor-style residence in the Arts and Crafts manner that was comfortable and modern for its time. Area laborers built the nearly 5,300-square-foot home from native stone quarried right on the ranch grounds, so the walls came from the same hill they stand on. The house is fully furnished as a historic home, offering a look at how a couple who had spent so many seasons on tour chose to live away from the arena, though in 2026 the Oklahoma Historical Society lists the ranch and museum as temporarily closed for restoration and construction work. Its rooms tell a quieter story than the show posters did, one about a husband and wife who wanted a real home at the end of the trail.

## Keeping the Bison

Pawnee Bill's attachment to the West was not only theatrical. He believed strongly that the bison was essential to the history of the American West and to Plains Indian culture, and he wanted to see the animal perpetuated rather than lost. He lobbied Congress to pass legislation protecting it and established a herd on his ranch near Pawnee. That herd marked the beginning of the ranch's long role in bison preservation, a role it still plays. The Pawnee Bill Ranch remains an active member of the Oklahoma Bison Association, and its pastures hold bison along with Longhorn cattle and horses. For visitors, the sight of those shaggy animals moving across the grass below the stone house is the clearest link between the showman's ambitions and the land he chose.

## Blue Hawk Peak Today

Pawnee Bill died in his sleep on February 3, 1942. The ranch, also known as Blue Hawk Peak, is now owned and operated by the Oklahoma Historical Society, which preserves 500 of the original 2,000 acres. Alongside the furnished home, the site includes original outbuildings and a modern museum. The ranch has also built a long relationship with the Boy Scouts, hosting numerous Eagle Projects and annual campouts for local and council groups. For many years, a Pawnee Bill District in the Will Rogers Council, and later the Cimarron Council, carried his name. It is a fitting afterlife for a man who spent his career performing the West: the hilltop he built on has become a classroom for it.

## Flight Context

The Pawnee Bill Ranch sits at 36.3258°N, 96.8125°W on a hilltop at the western edge of Pawnee, Oklahoma, above a bend in the Black Bear River. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, look for the small grid of Pawnee's streets, then the river bend and open pastures just west of town where the bison and Longhorn herds graze. Stillwater Regional Airport (KSWO) is about 19 miles to the southwest, Ponca City Regional (KPNC) roughly 32 miles to the north, and Tulsa International (KTUL) about 50 miles to the east.
