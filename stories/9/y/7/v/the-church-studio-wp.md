# The Church Studio

A 1915 stone Methodist church in Tulsa's Pearl District became Leon Russell's recording studio in 1972, the heart of the Tulsa Sound, and reopened exactly fifty years later after a long restoration.

**Location:** 36.1565, -95.9696

**Categories:** Recording studios in the United States, Culture of Tulsa, Oklahoma, National Register of Historic Places in Tulsa, Oklahoma, Churches on the National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Music history

## Introduction

When Leon Russell died in 2016, his fans knew where to go. They came to a stone church on South Trenton Avenue in Tulsa and covered its steps with flowers, paintings, memorabilia and handwritten notes. The building had not held a congregation in decades. It had held something else: the recording studio Russell opened there in 1972, a place since cited as the heart of the Tulsa Sound. The musician, songwriter and producer, who would be inducted into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame, the Songwriters Hall of Fame and the Oklahoma Music Hall of Fame, had turned a sanctuary into a workshop, and Tulsa had come to treat it as a shrine.

## From Grace to Shelter

The building went up in 1915 as Grace Methodist Episcopal Church, a stone structure at 304 South Trenton Avenue in what is now Tulsa's Pearl District. In 1972 Leon Russell bought it, along with adjoining properties, to house his wide-ranging recording activities and to serve as a home for Shelter Records, the label he had started with his partner Denny Cordell. The sanctuary became a working studio. Russell eventually sold the building, but his name never left it. In 2010, the Pearl District Association and the city of Tulsa renamed the stretch of East Third Street where the church stands "Leon Russell Road."

## Caretakers Between the Eras

A studio this storied needed a series of stewards. In 1987 Tulsa musician Steve Ripley, leader of The Tractors, bought the building with partners and kept it until 2006. In 2009 new owners Jakob and Randy Miller announced their intention to revive it as a music facility. Then, in 2016, Tulsa entrepreneur Teresa Knox and her husband, Ivan Acosta, bought the church with a larger plan: renovate it, seek a place on the National Register of Historic Places, and run it as both a recording studio and a community facility. The renovation was expected to take two years. It took more than five. Along the way, in 2017, the building was listed on the National Register for its significance to American music culture and as the heart of the Tulsa Sound.

## Gear With a Pedigree

Inside, the equipment carries its own history. The studio holds a vast collection of vintage microphones and tape machines, a Fairchild 660, and a Neve 8068 mixing console bought from the producer Daniel Lanois, the same console pictured on the cover of Bob Dylan's album Time Out of Mind. Dan Fogelberg's Yamaha C-7 grand piano lives here, and so does the Sakae drum kit of Jamie Oldaker, the drummer for Eric Clapton. The studio offers both analog and digital recording, so an artist can cut a track the way Russell's generation did or the way today's engineers do.

## Fifty Years to the Day

The Church Studio reopened to the public on March 1, 2022, fifty years after Leon Russell bought the building. It now works as a tourist attraction, a recording studio, a concert and event venue, the home of The Church Studio Archive and an audio engineering school. Kenny Loggins and Bill Champlin played in its curated concert series, and a long roster of visitors has come to see or record, from Joe Bonamassa, Rosanne Cash and Nick Mason to Lainey Wilson, The Mountain Goats, T Bone Burnett and Turnpike Troubadours. In 2022 Dropkick Murphys recorded This Machine Still Kills Fascists there, an album built on lyrics by Oklahoma native Woody Guthrie. Taj Mahal co-produced and recorded Swingin' Live at the Church in Tulsa, which won the 2025 Grammy Award for Best Traditional Blues Album, and the Grammy Awards have listed the Church Studio among ten top studios that take fans behind the scenes.

## The Man in the Top Hat

At the new entrance stands Russell himself, cast in bronze at life size as he looked in 1972, the year he bought the church. The sculptor, Jim Franklin of Perry, Oklahoma, spent three years on the statue, commissioned by Teresa Knox. Every detail of that moment is there: the Wrangler denim jacket, the snakeskin boots, the cross necklace and the signature top hat. It greets visitors at the door of the place he made famous.
