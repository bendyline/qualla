# Tulsa: The Oil Capital's Art Deco Treasure

The Oklahoma city where oil barons built an Art Deco skyline, where the 1921 Tulsa Race Massacre destroyed 'Black Wall Street,' and where Route 66 passes through a city that turned petroleum wealth into world-class art museums.

**Location:** 36.1314, -95.9372

**Categories:** oklahoma, tulsa, city, art-deco, oil

## Introduction

The Creek Nation settled this bend of the Arkansas River in the 1830s, calling their council oak 'Tulsey Town.' When oil gushed from the Glenn Pool in 1905, everything changed. Overnight, Tulsa became the Oil Capital of the World - a boomtown where fortunes materialized and petroleum barons built skyscrapers. The wealth shows in the architecture: one of America's finest concentrations of Art Deco, ranking ninth nationally. But Tulsa's oil wealth came with terrible violence. In 1921, a white mob destroyed the prosperous Black neighborhood of Greenwood - 'Black Wall Street' - killing hundreds and leaving 10,000 homeless. The massacre was suppressed from official history for decades. Modern Tulsa reckons with both legacies: the Art Deco splendor of oil money and the horror of what happened on Greenwood Avenue.

## Art Deco Capital

When oil money flowed through Tulsa in the 1920s and 30s, it built in the style of the moment: Art Deco. The Boston Avenue Methodist Church, designed by Adah Robinson and Bruce Goff, is considered one of the finest Art Deco buildings in America - its tower rising 280 feet with terra cotta ornamentation depicting praying hands and Native American motifs. The Philbrook Museum of Art occupies an Italian Renaissance villa built by oilman Waite Phillips; its 23 acres of gardens rival the art inside. The Gilcrease Museum holds Thomas Moran's massive paintings of the American West alongside the world's largest collection of Western art. Downtown's zigzag towers and geometric ornamentation make walking the streets an architectural tour of Depression-era optimism.

## The Greenwood Massacre

In 1921, the Greenwood District was known as 'Black Wall Street' - a prosperous African American community with hotels, theaters, and hundreds of businesses. On May 31, a young Black man named Dick Rowland was accused of assaulting a white woman in an elevator; the accusation was almost certainly false. White mobs invaded Greenwood the next morning, looting and burning while airplanes dropped incendiary devices. Between 100 and 300 people were killed; 10,000 left homeless; 35 blocks burned. The massacre was erased from textbooks for decades; Tulsa's history books simply skipped from 1920 to 1922. The Greenwood Rising museum now tells the story the city suppressed for generations.

## Route 66 Runs Through

Route 66 enters Tulsa from the northeast, runs through downtown on 11th Street, and exits toward Oklahoma City - one of the best-preserved urban sections of the Mother Road. The neon signs that once guided travelers still glow: the Blue Whale of Catoosa, the Admiral Twin Drive-In, the Circle Cinema (Oklahoma's oldest movie theater). The Route 66 experience through Tulsa mixes roadside Americana with the unexpected sophistication of oil wealth - hamburger joints and art museums, motor courts and Art Deco towers. The road that connected Chicago to Los Angeles passed through a city that got rich pumping the petroleum that made America's automobile culture possible.

## Green Country

Tulsa breaks every Oklahoma stereotype. The city sits where the Ozark foothills meet the prairie, receiving 40 inches of rain annually - green country, not dust bowl. The Arkansas River flows through town, its banks lined with parks and bike trails. Turkey Mountain provides hiking minutes from downtown. Gathering Place, a 66-acre riverside park that opened in 2018, offers destination playgrounds, splash pads, and gardens that draw families from across the region. Tulsa's climate grows things; its rolling hills and forests surprise visitors expecting flat, brown prairie. The oil came from underground; above ground, Tulsa is surprisingly lush.

## T-Town

Tulsa International Airport (TUL) offers regional connections; Oklahoma City's airport is 100 miles southwest. Route 66 runs through town; I-44 connects to Oklahoma City and St. Louis. The downtown grid follows original railroad tracks at 25 degrees off true north. Cherry Street and Brookside provide walkable dining and shopping districts. From altitude, Tulsa appears as a city in green hill country - the Arkansas River curving through, the downtown Art Deco towers clustered against the riverbank, the forested terrain extending toward the Ozarks. What appears from the air as Oklahoma's second city was the Oil Capital of the World, where petroleum wealth built an Art Deco skyline and where the Greenwood massacre teaches what America tried to forget.
