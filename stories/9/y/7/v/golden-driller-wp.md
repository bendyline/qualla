# Golden Driller

Built as a temporary prop for Tulsa's International Petroleum Exposition, the 76-foot Golden Driller has stood outside the Tulsa Expo Center since 1966 and became Oklahoma's official state monument in 1979.

## Introduction

The numbers sound like a tall tale: a belt 48 feet around, a shoe in size 393DDD, a hard hat in size 112. Those are the reported measurements of the Golden Driller, a 76-foot, 43,500-pound oil worker who has stood in front of the Tulsa Expo Center since 1966, his right hand resting on a real oil derrick. He is built of concrete and plaster over a steel frame, and he began life as a temporary trade-show attraction. Instead of vanishing when the exhibits were packed up, he outlasted the show that created him and became Oklahoma's official state monument.

## Built for the Oil Capital's Big Show

The Golden Driller was born to sell. Tulsa, long known as the "Oil Capital of the World," had hosted the International Petroleum Exposition since October 1923, a specialized trade fair for the oil industry. In 1953 the Mid-Continent Supply Company of Fort Worth built a version of the giant driller as a temporary feature of the exposition. It worked. Six years later, the company erected one again for the 1959 show, and the towering figure drew so much positive attention that the company donated the statue to the Tulsa County Fairgrounds Trust Authority, and a figure conceived as a piece of marketing began its transformation into a piece of civic identity.

## Remade to Stay

Before he could take up permanent residence, the Driller got a makeover. The Fairgrounds Trust Authority had him anatomically redesigned, then installed him in front of the Tulsa Expo Center for the 1966 International Petroleum Exposition. That same year, the old exposition buildings were demolished and replaced by a ten-acre exhibition hall, with the eight-story-tall Golden Driller planted at its entrance. His right hand rests on a genuine oil derrick, moved to Tulsa from a depleted oil field in Seminole, Oklahoma, so the steel he leans on once did real work. At his base, an inscription spells out his purpose: "The Golden Driller, a symbol of the International Petroleum Exposition. Dedicated to the men of the petroleum industry who by their vision and daring have created from God's abundance a better life for mankind."

## The Year the Show Ended

In 1979 two stories converged. The International Petroleum Exposition drew a dismal twenty-six thousand people that year, and the long-running show was canceled for good. The statue built to advertise it might easily have become an orphan. Instead, that same year the Oklahoma Legislature adopted the Golden Driller as the state monument. His wardrobe changed to match. The belt buckle that had originally read "MID-CONTINENT," advertising the supply company that built him, was replaced in 1979 with the one he wears today, which reads simply "TULSA." The shift was more than cosmetic. A company mascot had become the emblem of a city and a state, a gold-colored tribute to "the men of the petroleum industry," as his inscription puts it, standing watch long after the exposition that gave him his reason to exist.

## Quirky, Colossal and Beloved

The Golden Driller is one of the tallest statues in the United States, and few roadside attractions lean into their oversized charm quite so cheerfully. In October 2006, as part of an online promotional contest sponsored by Kimberly-Clark, he was named the grand prize winner among a top ten of America's "quirkiest destinations," earning the person who nominated him a $90,000 international vacation for two. Visitors still pull off to stand at his enormous feet and crane their necks toward the hard hat far above. He is part advertisement, part monument, part in-joke, and entirely Tulsa. More than seventy years after Mid-Continent first raised a driller for a trade show, the statue remains a towering reminder that this city was built on oil.

## Flight Context

The Golden Driller stands at 36.1336°N, 95.9312°W in front of the Tulsa Expo Center at Expo Square, the Tulsa County fairgrounds, in midtown Tulsa. From the air, look for the sprawling fairgrounds complex and the huge roof of the Expo Center exhibition hall; the 76-foot statue at its entrance can be picked out from low altitude on a clear day. Downtown Tulsa's skyline lies about 4 miles to the west-northwest and the Arkansas River curves past to the west. Tulsa International (KTUL) is only about 5 miles to the north-northeast and Richard Lloyd Jones Jr. Airport (KRVS) about 7 miles to the south-southwest, and the site sits within Tulsa's Class C airspace, so expect to work with Tulsa Approach. Around 1,500–2,000 feet AGL gives the best view. Spring storms are common; fall offers the clearest skies.
