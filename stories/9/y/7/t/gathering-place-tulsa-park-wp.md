# Gathering Place (Tulsa park)

Built on former Muscogee allotment land beside the Arkansas River, Tulsa's free, $465 million Gathering Place expected a million visitors in its first year and drew roughly three million.

**Location:** 36.1244, -95.9845

**Categories:** Parks in Oklahoma, Tourist attractions in Tulsa, Oklahoma, Buildings and structures on the Arkansas River, Landscape architecture, 2018 establishments in Oklahoma

## Introduction

The planners guessed a million. They were off by a factor of three. When Gathering Place opened on the Arkansas River in Tulsa on September 8, 2018, officials expected the park to attract about a million visitors a year, according to the Tulsa World. More than 55,000 people came through during the two-day grand opening alone, and first-year attendance reached roughly three million. The appeal was not hard to read: 66.5 acres at opening (the park now spans nearly 100) of playgrounds, gardens, lawns and trails, all open free of charge. Created by the George Kaiser Family Foundation and designed by landscape architect Michael Van Valkenburgh, the park cost $465 million, billed as the largest private gift to a community park in U.S. history.

## Allotment Ground

Long before anyone drew a playground here, this was land of the Muscogee Nation. In the early 1900s the Muscogee Reservation was divided into allotments for individual tribal members, and the ground that would become Gathering Place went to two of them. One was Tuckabache, a Muscogee medicine man. The other was Joe Hamilton Tiger, a Muscogee Freedman. After Oklahoma became a state, the land was sold to developers. More than a century after the allotments, the tract along the river would open again to everyone, as Gathering Place.

## A Retreat Inside the City

The George Kaiser Family Foundation envisioned a vibrant and inclusive space where diverse communities could come together to explore, learn and play. After years of planning, donations and community input, construction began in 2014, with Crossland Construction Company managing the multi-year build. Michael Van Valkenburgh Associates shaped the grounds as a nature-inspired retreat within a city environment. The architects Mack Scogin Merrill Elam designed the park's three major buildings, Williams Lodge, the ONEOK Boathouse and a restroom cabana, each meant to embrace the surrounding landscape and to draw on Oklahoma's natural materials.

## Five Acres of Adventure

The Chapman Adventure Playground covers 5 acres on its own. Around it spread the QuikTrip Great Lawn, the Energy Transfer Sports Courts, a BMX pump track and skate park, Peggy's Pond, and a web of gardens, pathways and trails. The recognition came fast. In 2019, Time magazine named Gathering Place to its list of the World's 100 Greatest Places, National Geographic included it among 12 Mind-Bending Playgrounds Around the World, and the American Planning Association listed it among six great public spaces in America. A second wave followed. Construction on the 57,000-square-foot Discovery Lab began in February 2020, a hands-on museum designed to spark young children's interest in science and technology, with classrooms, a café, a grand plaza and a 300-seat amphitheater. It opened on January 24, 2022, replacing a much smaller lab that had operated in the Owen Park Recreation Center since 2013.

## When the River Rose

Less than a year after opening, the park met the river at its worst. In 2019, unusually heavy rains along the Arkansas River forced major releases from Keystone Dam upstream, pushing the river above flood stage in many places downstream. Gathering Place closed for a week until the water began to subside. On the east bank, damage was relatively light: water covered two of the five sports courts south of 31st Street, and once crews hosed off the mud and debris, inspectors found no significant damage to the playing surfaces. The trail fared worse. Washouts cut along the East Bank Trail, and at 58th Street and Riverside Drive the current carried off a light stanchion. Matt Meyer, executive director of the Tulsa River Parks Authority, said the washout showed a need for cable-concrete bank reinforcement, noting that a similar city project two years earlier had cost about $1,000 per linear foot.

## A New Way Across

City officials had promised a new pedestrian bridge over the Arkansas soon after the park's first phase opened. Michael Van Valkenburgh Associates won the design contest for the project, then called "The Gateway," which the Tulsa River Parks Authority estimated would take three years to build and cost the city $24.4 million. It replaced a bridge with a long history: the Midland Valley Railroad's span from the early 1900s, which the city acquired in 1974 and converted to foot traffic. The new Williams Crossing opened over Labor Day weekend in 2024, and in related work the city spent about $50 million overhauling the adjacent Zink Dam on the river.

## Flight Context

Gathering Place lies at approximately 36.12°N, 95.98°W on the east bank of the Arkansas River, about two miles south of the downtown Tulsa skyline. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL, look for the broad green park strip along the river, the Williams Crossing pedestrian bridge and the Zink Dam just beside it. Nearest airports: Tulsa Riverside (KRVS), about 5 nm south, and Tulsa International (KTUL), about 6.5 nm northeast; the park sits beneath busy Tulsa terminal airspace, so coordinate with Tulsa approach. Heavy rain upstream can raise the river quickly, as it did in 2019.
