# Battle of Round Mountain

On a November evening in 1861, Confederate troops caught up with Opothleyahola's Unionist Creeks and Seminoles at the foot of the Round Mountains, opening the campaign later called the Trail of Blood on Ice, and historians still cannot agree where it happened.

**Location:** 36.1300, -96.2869

**Categories:** Trail of Blood on Ice, Battles of the American Civil War in Indian Territory, Battles of the Trans-Mississippi theater of the American Civil War, 1861 in Indian Territory, Payne County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

Two places claim the Battle of Round Mountain, and one of them may now be underwater. Some historians put the fight near the present-day town of Yale, Oklahoma. Others place it near Keystone, a town that now lies beneath the waters of Keystone Lake. Whichever is right, something happened at the foot of the Round Mountains in the failing light of November 19, 1861, that opened a harrowing winter in Indian Territory. It was the first battle of the campaign later known as the Trail of Blood on Ice, in which Confederate forces tried to stop Union supporters of the Creek Nation, led by Opothleyahola, from escaping to the protection of Union forces in Kansas.

## A Nation Divided Against Itself

Colonel Douglas H. Cooper, the Confederate commander of the Indian Department, could not reconcile his differences with Opothleyahola, who led a band of Creeks and Seminoles loyal to the Union. Opothleyahola's group was estimated at about seventeen hundred people, and it included Union supporters from the Comanches, Delawares, Kickapoos, Wichitas and Shawnees. On November 15, 1861, Cooper set out with between 1,300 and 1,400 men, depending on the account, either to compel Opothleyahola's submission or, in the Confederate phrasing, to "drive him and his party from the country." The makeup of Cooper's force tells the deeper story. Alongside a detachment of the 9th Texas Cavalry rode companies of the 1st Choctaw-Chickasaw Mounted Rifles, detachments of the 1st and 2nd Creek Mounted Rifles under Daniel N. McIntosh and Chilly McIntosh, and Seminoles under Major John Jumper. The Civil War had split the nations of Indian Territory, and at Round Mountain, Creek and Seminole soldiers rode against other Creeks and Seminoles.

## Fire in the Prairie Grass

Cooper's column rode up the Deep Fork of the Canadian River only to find Opothleyahola's camp deserted. On November 19, captured prisoners told him that part of the band was building a fort at the Red Fork of the Arkansas River. His men reached it around four in the afternoon. Charging cavalry found that camp freshly abandoned too, so the Confederates followed the stragglers, and a Texas unit blundered into Opothleyahola's warriors along the tree line at the foot of the Round Mountains. The Unionist fighters drove the Confederate cavalry back onto Cooper's main force. By then night was falling, and the darkness kept Cooper from counterattacking until the opposing force was within 60 yards. The fight that followed was short. As it ended, Opothleyahola's men set the prairie grass alight and retreated.

## A Victory Claimed by Absence

When Cooper advanced on the new Unionist camp the next morning, he found it empty. The Confederates declared victory on the strength of Opothleyahola's departure, and they carried off what the band had left behind: Opothleyahola's carriage, a dozen wagons, food, cattle and ponies. For people on the move with winter coming, that was no small loss. Confederate casualties were one captain and five men killed, three severely wounded and one slightly, and one missing. Cooper estimated Opothleyahola's losses at about 110 killed and wounded, though the actual toll is unknown. Round Mountain was only the beginning. It was the first of three encounters between Opothleyahola's Unionist bands and Confederate troops, and after the Battle of Chustenahlah at the end of the year, the Unionists were forced to flee to Kansas.

## The Argument Over the Ground

The battle is sometimes called the Battle of Red Fork, for the river where Cooper's men were told to look, and even its location has been contested for many years. Two candidates lead the field: a site near Yale and a site close to the old town of Keystone, which disappeared beneath Keystone Lake when the reservoir filled in the 1960s. The noted Oklahoma historian Angie Debo weighed the case for and against each in "The Site of the Battle of Round Mountain, 1861," published in the Chronicles of Oklahoma in 1949. She concluded that the evidence pointed more strongly to Yale. If the other camp is right, the ground where the grass burned may now lie beneath the lake.

## Flight Context

The marked position, approximately 36.13°N, 96.29°W, sits near Keystone Lake west of Tulsa, close to the Keystone candidate site; the lake's broad arms, where the Cimarron meets the Arkansas River, are the obvious visual reference. Yale, the site Angie Debo favored, lies about 20 nm to the west in Payne County, so a leg between the two covers both claims. A viewing altitude of 3,000-4,500 ft MSL shows the lake shoreline and surrounding country well.
