# Cain's Ballroom

A 1924 Tulsa garage became a ten-cent dance hall, the radio home of Bob Wills, a near-empty relic, a Sex Pistols tour stop and, after a century, one of the busiest club venues in the world.

**Location:** 36.1607, -95.9931

**Categories:** Music venues, Western swing, Tulsa, Oklahoma, National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Music history

## Introduction

At the Cain's, a dance lesson once cost a dime. In the 1930s and 1940s, this Tulsa building ran as a "dime-a-dance" hall, teaching steps for ten cents apiece, and it was on this floor that Bob Wills broadcast to the region almost every day. A century after it was built in 1924, Cain's Ballroom still stands in downtown Tulsa and still books shows, and a banner above its main stage still makes its oldest boast: "Home of Bob Wills."

## A Garage Learns to Dance

The building did not start out as a music hall. W. Tate Brady built it in 1924 as his garage. In 1930, Madison W. "Daddy" Cain bought the place and turned it into "Cain's Dance Academy," the name that has stuck to the building ever since. Through the 1930s and 1940s, it operated as a dime-a-dance, offering lessons for ten cents. Long before it drew touring bands, the Cain's drew dancers, and the lessons were cheap enough that a single coin bought a turn on the floor. The room that would later ring with western swing, red dirt and punk rock began as a place where people came simply to learn how to move.

## Home of Bob Wills

Bob Wills arrived in Tulsa in the 1930s under a cloud. He had parted ways with his manager, who retaliated by barring him from performing on Texas and Oklahoma City radio stations. Tulsa's KVOO-AM gave him an opening. After Wills impressed the station's reluctant manager, he began performing almost daily on broadcasts from the Cain's, sending his music out across the airwaves from the old garage. His first concert at the Cain's came on New Year's Night in 1935. The partnership turned a dance academy into a landmark, and today the banner over the main stage reads simply "Home of Bob Wills," a reminder of who put this room on the map.

## Empty Years and Surprise Guests

Fame did not last. In the late 1940s and 1950s the Cain's declined in popularity as public perception of the venue soured, and poor management left it "virtually empty" through the 1960s. It became known as a spot for "baby bands," emerging musicians, and rumors spread that the ballroom had shut for good. Even so, the room could still surprise. Eric Clapton made an unannounced appearance at a show in 1975, the earliest of the pop-up performances the venue has hosted over the years. In 1976, concert promoter Larry Shaeffer bought the ballroom for $60,000. On January 11, 1978, the Sex Pistols played here, one of only seven venues on their only North American tour. After the show, a frustrated Sid Vicious reportedly punched a hole in the drywall of the green room.

## A Century on the Floor

In 1999 the ballroom passed to brothers Danny and Mark Finnerty, and in 2002 neurosurgeon James Rodgers and his wife, Alice, bought it. The Rodgers family has owned it since. The building was in disrepair and needed several renovations to bring it up to modern standards, and when the work was done, Dwight Yoakam was the first artist to perform in the refreshed room. The Cain's has since become a beacon for red dirt musicians and a bucket-list stop for fans, and in the 2020s it has been a hotspot for Oklahoma artists such as JD McPherson and Wyatt Flores. In 2021, Pollstar ranked it number 13 in the world for ticket sales among club venues. The Cain's marked its 100th anniversary in 2024 with a year of concerts, and plans were announced for a documentary, Raisin' Cain, directed by Tate Wittenberg.
