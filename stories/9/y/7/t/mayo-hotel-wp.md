# Mayo Hotel

Once Oklahoma's tallest building and the place where presidents, movie stars and oilmen stayed in Tulsa, the Mayo Hotel stood gutted and empty for twenty years before a $250,000 purchase began its long return.

**Location:** 36.1509, -95.9920

**Categories:** Hotel buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Skyscraper hotels in Oklahoma, Chicago school architecture in Oklahoma, Hotel buildings completed in 1925, Tulsa, Oklahoma

## Introduction

The selling point was ice water. In the Tulsa summers of the 1920s, the Mayo Hotel offered something no one in the city had enjoyed before: running ice water, straight from the tap, along with a ceiling fan in every one of its 600 rooms. When it opened in 1925 at 115 West 5th Street, the Mayo was the tallest building in Oklahoma, and for the next half century it was where the famous slept when they came to town. Then it went dark for twenty years, stripped to its bones and seemingly bound for demolition. Its story runs from grandeur to ruin and, against the odds, back again.

## Doric Columns and a Stone Crown

The hotel was financed by John D. and Cass A. Mayo and designed by the architect George Winkler, and it follows the Chicago school's formula for a tall building: a distinct base, a long shaft and a crown. At street level, a base of two-story Doric columns carries the weight. Above it rise fourteen floors marked by false terracotta balconies, decorative touches that give the long shaft of the building its rhythm. At the top sits a two-story crown of stone finished with a dentiled cornice, a row of small, tooth-like blocks along the roofline. In 1925, no building in the state stood taller.

## The Guest Register

For decades the Mayo was Tulsa's front parlor. President John F. Kennedy stayed there, as did Bob Hope, Charles Lindbergh, Babe Ruth, Jack Dempsey, Will Rogers, Charlie Chaplin and Mae West. Some of the era's notable oilmen made it home, J. Paul Getty among them. The hotel even worked its way into American drama. In William Inge's play Picnic, which won the Pulitzer Prize in 1953, the Mayo is where the lead character, Hal, intends to find work as a bellhop. By then the hotel's name alone could stand for Tulsa.

## A Call From the White House

The glamour had a hard edge. The Mayo was a segregated hotel, and it did not admit a Black guest until 1951. Even then, it took a president. Harry Truman personally telephoned the hotel from the White House to ask that his daughter Margaret be allowed to bring her maid, a Black woman, into the building. The hotel agreed, but the concession went only so far: she was still required to use the service elevator. The episode captures the terms of the time with uncomfortable precision. A woman traveling with the president's daughter could cross the threshold only by presidential request, and could not ride the same elevator as the guests upstairs.

## Twenty Dark Years

Change came gradually, then all at once. In 1955 the original two-story lobby was cut down to one floor, and a new function room, the Pompeian Court, was created in the former lobby space on the mezzanine. The hotel changed hands in 1977 and returned to its original name, and in 1980 it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. On January 9, 1981, Daon Corp, a Newport Beach subsidiary of a Vancouver-based firm, bought the Mayo for $4.6 million and announced a 12- to 14-month renovation that would restore the two-story lobby and enlarge the rooms. The company sold the fittings, laid off the staff and began gutting the interior, tearing out floors and walls. By November 1981, the work had stopped. The shuttered, half-stripped tower went up for sale that December, and restoration concepts came and went for years. When Tulsa voters rejected a measure to finance a new convention center in November 2000, one more hope for a reopened hotel went with it.

## Rebuilt From the Basement Up

In June 2001, after two decades of vacancy, John Snyder bought the Mayo for $250,000. The renovation began at the bottom. The partially flooded basement was drained and turned into a public parking garage, which opened in 2002, and the garage's revenue paid to restore the historic ground-floor public rooms. They reopened in 2003 and quickly filled with galas, proms and receptions. In 2008, an $11.2 million project began converting seven upper floors into 76 loft apartments, backed by $4.9 million from Tulsa County's voter-approved Vision 2025 package and $6.3 million from the owners. The full renovation was reported to cost between $40 million and $42 million, and it took special care with the Crystal Ballroom, named for its crystal chandeliers. Tenants moved in during August 2009. The 102-room hotel opened privately that September for Britney Spears, who booked 80 rooms for her Tulsa tour stop, and a formal grand reopening followed in December. The Mayo has since turned up on screen as home to Sylvester Stallone's Dwight "The General" Manfredi in the Paramount+ series Tulsa King.

## Flight Context

The Mayo Hotel stands at approximately 36.15°N, 95.99°W in the heart of downtown Tulsa, at 5th Street. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL, look for the dense cluster of downtown towers with the Arkansas River curving past to the west and southwest; the Mayo is one of the older high-rises in the cluster. Nearest airports: Tulsa International (KTUL), about 6 nm east-northeast, and Tulsa Riverside (KRVS), about 7 nm south. Downtown lies close under Tulsa's terminal airspace, so coordinate with Tulsa approach.
