# Woody Guthrie Center

In Tulsa's Arts District, a museum and archive holds the world's largest collection of material on Woody Guthrie's life, along with the papers of Phil Ochs and a virtual-reality porch where visitors watch a Dust Bowl storm roll in.

**Location:** 36.1586, -95.9922

**Categories:** Museums in Tulsa, Oklahoma, Biographical museums in Oklahoma, Music museums in Oklahoma, Woody Guthrie, Phil Ochs, Museums established in 2013, Music archives in the United States

## Introduction

In downtown Tulsa, a pane of glass separates the public from Woody Guthrie's own handwriting. Standing in the galleries of the Woody Guthrie Center, visitors can look through windows into a climate-controlled archive that holds the world's largest collection of material relating to Guthrie's life: his manuscripts and lyrics, his letters and scrapbooks, his recordings and photographs. The man who wrote "This Land Is Your Land" was born in Okemah, Oklahoma, on July 14, 1912. Since 2013, the paper trail he left behind has lived in his native state.

## Handwriting Behind Glass

The center, at 102 East Reconciliation Way in the Tulsa Arts District, is built to let visitors get close. Its interactive museum displays musical instruments Guthrie played, samples of his original artwork, and notebooks and lyrics written in his own hand, alongside photographs and memorabilia that trace his life, his music, and his political activities. A short biographical film sets the scene, and listening stations play his songs next to the work of artists he influenced and inspired. For those who want to dig deeper, the archive behind the glass is open to researchers by appointment. Guthrie was never shy about what he believed music could do. He hand-painted a slogan on his guitar that still startles: "This machine kills fascists."

## How the Archive Came to Tulsa

The archives once belonged to the Woody Guthrie Foundation, headed by Guthrie's daughter, Nora Guthrie. Then the Tulsa-based George Kaiser Family Foundation acquired the collection, and the Woody Guthrie Center officially opened on April 27, 2013, turning the archive into a public destination as well as a research library. The Kaiser foundation's commitment to songwriters did not stop there. It also operates the Bob Dylan Center, just nearby in the same Tulsa Arts District, so two songwriters who shaped American music now keep company a short walk apart. The center also sponsors folk music events, keeping the building full of the live sound its namesake spent his life making.

## Dust on the Porch

In April 2018 the museum opened a virtual-reality Dust Bowl experience. Wearing a headset, visitors sit on a replica of a front porch in the Oklahoma Panhandle on Black Sunday, April 14, 1935, and watch a dust cloud roll across the prairie until it swallows them. The choice of subject is no accident. The Dust Bowl inspired a number of Guthrie's songs, including the material on his album Dust Bowl Ballads, and it led him to take up the causes of migrant workers and others displaced by an ecological disaster. Standing inside that simulated storm, it becomes easier to understand why a man from Okemah gave so much of his songwriting to people who had lost their footing.

## Fellow Travelers

Guthrie's influence runs through the building in other voices, too. In September 2014, Meegan Lee Ochs announced she was donating the archives of her father, the singer-songwriter Phil Ochs, to the center. Ochs had been heavily influenced by Guthrie and was a troubadour and social activist in his own right. His notebooks, photographs, videotapes, and memorabilia became the first collection at the center from an artist other than Guthrie himself. The center also looks forward. Each year it awards the Woody Guthrie Prize to an artist who, in the center's words, "best exemplifies Woody Guthrie's spirit and work" by speaking for the less fortunate through their art. Pete Seeger received the first one in 2014. Later honorees include Bruce Springsteen, Joan Baez, Mavis Staples, Tom Morello, U2, and Pussy Riot.
