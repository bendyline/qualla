# Creek Council Oak Tree

In 1836 the Lochapoka Creeks, survivors of forced removal from Alabama, lit a new ceremonial fire beneath this oak above the Arkansas River, founding the settlement that became Tulsa; the tree still stands and is being returned to the Muscogee (Creek) Nation, with a transfer agreement signed in 2025.

**Location:** 36.1362, -95.9901

**Categories:** History of Indian Territory, History of Tulsa, Oklahoma, Muscogee Nation, Protected areas of Tulsa County, Oklahoma, Individual oak trees, Individual trees in Oklahoma, National Register of Historic Places in Tulsa, Oklahoma

## Introduction

The Lochapoka people carried fire with them into exile. When this tribal town of the Creek Nation was forced from its homeland in Alabama, its members brought coals and ashes from their last fires along the trail west. Of the 630 who began the journey, 161 died before it ended. In 1836 the survivors stopped on a hill overlooking the Arkansas River, chose an oak at its crest, and lit a new ceremonial fire in its shade. The village they built nearby would one day grow into the city of Tulsa. The oak is still alive, standing in a small park a short distance from downtown.

## Fire Carried From Alabama

The trek from Alabama to Indian Territory was slow and painful, and more than a quarter of the Lochapokas who set out never finished it. When the survivors finally stopped in 1836, at a point just before the Arkansas River made its great bend to the west, they marked the end of the journey with a solemn ceremony, depositing ashes brought over the trail from their last fires in Alabama. Beneath the oak on the hilltop, they lit a new ceremonial fire using the coals they had carried with them and established a busk ground, where all council business would be conducted. It was an act of continuity in the face of catastrophe. A people who had lost their homeland, and so many of their own, carried the heart of their civic and spiritual life with them and rekindled it in a new place.

## From Talasi to Tulsa

Near the Council Oak, the surviving Creeks built their village and named it talasi, or "Old Town." Lochapoka was a daughter town of Tallasi, and the name Tulsa is believed to derive from it. That makes this hilltop the symbolic birthplace of modern Tulsa. The tree itself was probably already mature when the Creeks arrived, though its exact age is unknown. Even its species is a matter of mild dispute. One source identifies it as a post oak, while the Tulsa Preservation Commission called it a bur oak. Both are white oaks native to Oklahoma. Whatever its botanical name, the oak has watched a city rise around the council ground it once shaded, from a village of exiles to a metropolis of skyscrapers and freeways.

## Saved From the Pavement

The twentieth century nearly swallowed the site. Private citizens acquired the land in the early 1900s, and at one point oilman Harry Ford Sinclair lived in a large house right beside the tree. A later owner, the Oral Roberts Evangelistic Association, razed the house but kept the oak. By 1960, it looked as though the tree and its surroundings would be cleared for a parking lot. Instead, the Creek Nation and several individuals bought the site and donated it to the City of Tulsa, which turned the 1.86-acre plot into Creek Nation Council Oak Park. On September 29, 1976, the tree was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. It now stands between Seventeenth and Eighteenth Streets and Cheyenne and Denver Avenues, a living landmark surrounded by the city that grew from the Lochapokas' village.

## Morning Prayer and a Homecoming

In 2008 the Oklahoma Centennial Commission sponsored a monument in the park honoring the suffering of the Creeks on their forced march from Alabama to Indian Territory. Created by Creek artist Dan Brook, the sculpture depicts a flame rising from a hearth, an image that recalls the fire the Lochapokas carried and rekindled here. It is named Morning Prayer. The latest chapter began in 2024. On November 9, at the annual Council Oak Tree Day ceremony, Tulsa Mayor G.T. Bynum announced that he was initiating the transfer of the tree and its park to the Muscogee (Creek) Nation, an effort supported by the grassroots work of the Riverview Neighborhood Association and the foundation the group created to preserve, protect and promote the site. On November 1, 2025, the Nation and the City of Tulsa signed a memorandum of understanding conveying Council Oak Park and neighboring Stickball Park to the Nation, on the condition that they remain public parks. Nearly two centuries after the Lochapokas lit their fire beneath its branches, the Council Oak is returning to the nation whose council fire it sheltered.

## Flight Context

The Creek Council Oak stands at 36.1362°N, 95.9901°W in Creek Nation Council Oak Park, between 17th and 18th Streets and Cheyenne and Denver Avenues, just south of downtown Tulsa and a short distance east of the Arkansas River. The tree itself is hard to pick out from the air, so use the downtown skyline to the north and the broad Arkansas River to the west as references. Tulsa International (KTUL) is about 7 miles to the northeast and Richard Lloyd Jones Jr. Airport (KRVS) about 7 miles to the south-southwest; the park lies within the lateral limits of Tulsa International's Class C airspace, so expect to work with Tulsa Approach and plan to view it from around 2,000 feet AGL. Spring thunderstorms are common; clear fall days offer the best visibility along the river.
