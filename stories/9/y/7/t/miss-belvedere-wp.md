# Miss Belvedere

In 1957 Tulsa buried a brand-new Plymouth in a vault built to withstand nuclear attack, and in 2007 the city lifted it out of four feet of water.

**Location:** 36.1492, -95.9953

**Categories:** History of Tulsa, Oklahoma, Time capsules, Plymouth vehicles, Individual cars, Oklahoma centennial

## Introduction

Fourteen bobby pins. A tube of lipstick, a package of gum, a plastic rain hat, a bottle of tranquilizers, an unpaid parking ticket and under three dollars in bills and coins. In 1957, Tulsa's festival organizers packed what were considered the typical contents of a woman's purse into a brand-new Plymouth Belvedere, added a case of Schlitz beer, and lowered the whole thing into a concrete vault on the courthouse lawn. The gold and white sport coupe, with four miles on its odometer, was meant to be a 50-year time capsule. Half a century later, Tulsa found out just how well its vault had kept a secret.

## Built for the Bomb

The burial on June 15, 1957, was part of Tulsarama, the city's Golden Jubilee Week celebrating Oklahoma's 50th year of statehood. Event chairman Lewis Roberts Sr. explained the choice of car: it was "an advanced product of American industrial ingenuity with the kind of lasting appeal that will still be in style 50 years from now." Plymouth Motors and a group of Tulsa-area Plymouth dealers donated it. Its tomb was a 12-by-20-foot poured-in-place concrete vault, its interior walls sprayed with gunite, its top three feet below the lawn, and it was advertised as capable of withstanding nuclear attack. The car sat on a steel skid with its tires off the ground and was lowered into the vault several times so photographers could get their shots. Concrete beams formed the lid, gunite sealed them, and after the dirt and sod were replaced, a bronze marker donated by a local cemetery marked the spot.

## A Bet on 2007

The organizers believed the buried items would help acquaint future generations with life in 1957, and they thought practically as well. The trunk held a supply of gasoline and a case of motor oil, meant to start the car in 2007 in case gasoline was no longer the fuel of choice. The Plymouth was also a prize. Entrants could guess what Tulsa's population would be in 2007, and the car would go to whoever came closest, or to his or her descendant. There were 812 entries. Nobody would learn who had won for fifty years.

## Four Feet of Water

As the opening approached, some people speculated the car would emerge in near pristine condition, while others worried that moisture had found its way in. No one knew whether a 1973 construction accident, which damaged a water main and flooded the area, had reached the vault. After eighteen months of preparation by volunteers, the vault was opened on June 14, 2007, during Oklahoma's centennial, drawing news organizations and visitors from abroad. A crane service volunteered for the lift, and test lifts with an identical Belvedere confirmed the center of gravity. Then the lid came off. The car sat in nearly 2,000 gallons of standing water, 4 feet deep. Once the water was pumped out, the Plymouth was lifted, taken to the Tulsa Convention Center and unveiled on June 15, 2007, fifty years to the day after its burial. Custom car builder Boyd Coddington and his team were on hand to start it, but the car's condition made that impossible.

## What Survived

The corroded keys were still in the ignition, and the tires would not hold air. Yet the signatures of the people present at the 1957 burial were still legible. Items that had been sealed in a steel container emerged unscathed, while everything left unprotected inside the car was heavily damaged or had disintegrated entirely; the Schlitz came out as rusty cans. The glass containers of gasoline and motor oil went to the University of Oklahoma for testing. The contest had its own bittersweet ending. Raymond Humbertson had guessed 384,743, closest to the actual 382,457, but he had died in 1979, his wife in 1988, and the couple had no children. The car, along with a savings account that had grown from $100 to $666.85, went to his surviving sisters and nephew. His relatives noted that he had never lived in the Tulsa area and were unsure why he had entered.

## A Long Road to a Museum

In November 2007, Humbertson's family shipped the car to Ultra One, a New Jersey restoration firm whose de-rusting solution is designed to remove only rust while leaving metal, paint and decals intact. The Plymouth, by then nicknamed Miss Belvedere by a member of the 2007 organizing committee, spent the next ten years in storage while a permanent home was sought. In 2015 the Historic Auto Attractions museum in Roscoe, Illinois (since renamed Historic Attractions), accepted it. The car shipped there in 2017, and the exhibition opened on June 9, 2020. After half a century underground and a decade in a warehouse, Miss Belvedere is back where a time capsule belongs: in front of people curious about 1957.

## Flight Context

The burial vault site is on the grounds of the courthouse in downtown Tulsa, Oklahoma, at approximately 36.15°N, 96.00°W; the car itself is now displayed in Roscoe, Illinois. From 2,000-3,500 feet AGL, look for the cluster of downtown office towers, with the Arkansas River curving along the west and south. Tulsa International Airport (KTUL) is about 7 miles northeast and Richard Lloyd Jones Jr. Airport (KRVS) about 7 miles south. Watch for Tulsa Class C airspace, and expect spring thunderstorms in northeastern Oklahoma.
