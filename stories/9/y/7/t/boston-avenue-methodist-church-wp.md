# Boston Avenue Methodist Church

Tulsa's Boston Avenue Methodist Church, a 1929 Art Deco masterpiece crowned with praying hands, is still the subject of a friendly dispute between an art teacher and the star pupil who became a famous architect.

**Location:** 36.1439, -95.9844

**Categories:** Art Deco architecture in Oklahoma, Churches in Tulsa, Oklahoma, Bruce Goff buildings, National Historic Landmarks in Oklahoma, Downtown Tulsa

## Introduction

"Its detail is daringly new, its ornamental idioms fresh and vital," the influential critic Sheldon Cheney wrote of a new church in Tulsa. Then he added a question: "But why for the Methodists?" Completed in 1929 for a congregation of the Methodist Episcopal Church, South, Boston Avenue Methodist Church is considered one of the finest examples of ecclesiastical Art Deco architecture in the United States. Its 225-foot tower rises over downtown Tulsa, crowned with a stylized sculpture of two hands raised upward in prayer.

## The Teacher and the Pupil

Who designed it remains an open question. Credit for the $1.25 million building goes to two people. Adah Robinson taught art at Tulsa's Central High School and eventually chaired the art department at the University of Tulsa, and she sketched the original ideas for the church. Bruce Goff had once been her high school student, and between 1924 and 1926 he had designed her home and studio. He took her sketches and developed them into the design. Officially, the architecture firm of record was Rush, Endacott and Rush, where Goff had apprenticed since the age of 12 and where he became a partner in 1930. The debate has never been settled. The church credits Robinson with the design, while Goff experts maintain it is clearly his. Even the definitive book on the subject, Tulsa Art Deco, sidesteps the fight, declaring that "it is not the purpose of this book to offer a resolution to that controversy."

## Hands Raised in Prayer

The original building had three parts: a semicircular auditorium, the soaring tower and a wing of classrooms. The tower's straight vertical lines link it, physically and philosophically, to the Gothic cathedrals of earlier ages, while letting its designers indulge Art Deco's celebration of the vertical. At its summit and on many of the building's other high points, the praying-hands sculpture appears much as crockets and finials did on medieval churches, and the motif, traceable to Robinson's early drawings, is echoed throughout. The broad semicircular auditorium had a precursor in another Methodist church, Louis Sullivan's St. Paul's Methodist Church, designed in 1910 and built, somewhat modified, in Cedar Rapids, Iowa, in 1914. Like many Art Deco buildings, Boston Avenue revels in its materials: metal, glass, terra cotta, Indiana limestone and Minnesota granite all appear in its walls and details.

## Circuit Riders in Terra Cotta

The exterior carries numerous terra cotta sculptures by the Denver sculptor Robert Garrison, himself a former student of Adah Robinson in Oklahoma City. Groups of figures at prayer represent Spiritual Life, Religious Education and Worship, their hands once again pressed together. Above the south entrance ride the Circuit Riders, early Methodists on horseback spreading their faith. Two portray real men, Bishop Francis Asbury, the first American Methodist bishop, and Bishop William McKendree. The central rider symbolizes all the preachers who carried the message from the saddle, and Garrison modeled his face on the Rev. T. L. Darnell, the father-in-law of the church's minister, who had in fact been a circuit rider for half a century. Over the north entrance stand idealized statues of John Wesley, his brother Charles and their mother, Susanna. Elsewhere, organic plant designs and the stained glass windows draw on Oklahoma flora, most notably tritomas and coreopsis flowers.

## A Landmark That Keeps Growing

The building made an impression on the architectural world quickly; Cheney gave it five pictures in his book. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1978 and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1999. The congregation has kept adding to it in the same spirit. For its 100th anniversary in 1993, Chicago artist Angelo Gherardi designed murals that carry the Art Deco style consistently through the interior. A columbarium designed by Tulsa architect Roger Coffey was completed in 2000, with a cut glass window measuring 6 by 25 feet by Richard Bohm of the Tulsa Stained Glass company. The church's pipe organ, a Möller dating to the early 1960s, was rebuilt by Foley-Baker in 2019 and now has 76 ranks. The building's reach even extends to Chicago, where a stone from Boston Avenue is embedded in the walls of the Chicago Tribune building, one of more than 100 gathered from historic buildings and famous sites around the world.

## Flight Context

The church stands at approximately 36.14°N, 95.98°W, on the southern edge of downtown Tulsa, Oklahoma. From 2,000-3,500 feet AGL, look for its slender 225-foot tower just south of the downtown office towers, with the Arkansas River curving to the west. Tulsa International Airport (KTUL) lies about 6 miles northeast, and Richard Lloyd Jones Jr. Airport (KRVS) about 7 miles south. Watch for Tulsa Class C airspace around KTUL, and expect spring thunderstorms in northeastern Oklahoma.
