# Jackson Barnett No. 11 Oil Well

Struck in 1916 south of Drumright, the Jackson Barnett No. 11 became the most productive well in the Cushing Oil Field and set an Oklahoma record, while the Muscogee (Creek) landowner whose ground it tapped saw courts and others control his fortune.

**Location:** 35.9739, -96.5844

## Introduction

The well produced thousands of barrels a day. The man who owned the ground under it received, at first, a few hundred dollars a year. On February 17, 1916, the Gypsy Oil Company struck oil on land south of Drumright belonging to Jackson Barnett, a Muscogee (Creek) landowner, and the Cushing Oil Field gained its most productive well. Jackson Barnett No. 11 would set the Oklahoma record for production from a single well, and Barnett himself would become known as the "world's richest Indian." The title concealed a harder truth. Much of what flowed from his land was never really his to control.

## Into the Tucker Sand

The Gypsy Oil Company drilled the well in 1916 in the Shamrock Dome section of the Cushing field, where Barnett's land had already been yielding oil for several years. The bit went down into a formation called the Tucker sand, somewhere between 2,800 and 3,000 feet below the prairie. When it hit, the result was immediate. On its first day the well produced 4,000 barrels of oil. Production then climbed to 10,000 barrels a day, and at its peak the well was delivering 18,000 barrels every twenty-four hours. Numbers like that made a single hole in the ground the pride of the Cushing field.

## A Million Barrels and a Record

Jackson Barnett No. 11 became the first well in the Cushing field to produce one million barrels of oil, and it established the Oklahoma record for production from a single well. It kept working for nearly half a century before it was finally capped in the mid-1960s. Even then the ground around it was not exhausted. In 1980, eight neighboring wells were still pumping oil from the same area. What remains at the site today is modest: concrete foundations, the capped well casing, and a sign. It is an unassuming marker for a well that once led an entire field, and a reminder that the landmarks of an oil boom were often nothing more than steel pipe and concrete sunk into the prairie.

## Rich on Paper

Barnett owned 160 acres of land held in trust in Creek County, and that land began producing oil in 1912, four years before No. 11 came in. His share was a 12.5 percent royalty on the oil taken from it. Over his lifetime, that royalty earned him between $3 million and $4 million. Yet in the early years he received only a few hundred dollars annually. His fortune was managed as a trust by the Creek County courts and the U.S. Department of the Interior, because Barnett was illiterate and had been found legally incompetent after a head injury. The arrangement placed a Muscogee (Creek) man's wealth in the hands of officials who decided, on his behalf, how much of it he was allowed to have.

## Fortune Hunters and Hearings

Money on that scale drew attention. In 1920, Barnett was pursued by Anna Laura Lowe, and he married her after meeting her only once. When a court later handed part of the trust's money to Anna to administer, the couple moved to Los Angeles and bought a mansion there, far from the Oklahoma land that had paid for it. Barnett's fortune became the subject of extensive litigation, and the dispute eventually reached Congressional hearings. Through it all, the man at the center of the case was treated less as a person with his own wishes than as an estate to be managed. Barnett died in 1934. His name survives on a capped well in the Oklahoma prairie, which was placed on the National Register of Historic Places on July 27, 1982.

## Flight Context

The Jackson Barnett No. 11 site lies at 35.9739°N, 96.5844°W, just south of Drumright in Creek County, Oklahoma. The well itself is only a capped casing and concrete foundations, so from the air the goal is to take in the old Cushing field around it; 2,000–3,000 feet AGL works well. The most striking landmark nearby is the town of Cushing about 10 miles to the west, whose vast crude-oil tank farms are hard to miss from the air. Cushing Municipal Airport (KCUH) is roughly 10 miles to the west, Stillwater Regional (KSWO) about 30 miles to the northwest, and Tulsa's Richard Lloyd Jones Jr. Airport (KRVS) and Tulsa International (KTUL) are about 35–45 miles to the northeast.
