# Bill Tilghman

Buffalo hunter, Dodge City marshal, captor of Bill Doolin and silent-film star of his own legend, Bill Tilghman worked the frontier for half a century and died wearing a badge at seventy.

**Location:** 35.7003, -96.9044

**Categories:** Old West lawmen, People from Dodge City, Kansas, Law enforcement officials from Oklahoma, People murdered in 1924, Chandler, Oklahoma

## Introduction

Within a month of becoming a deputy under Sheriff Bat Masterson on January 1, 1878, Bill Tilghman was charged as an accessory to an attempted train robbery. The charge was dropped for lack of evidence on February 12. That April, Masterson himself arrested his new deputy on a charge of horse theft, and that case was dismissed as well. It was an unpromising start for a man who would become one of the best-known lawmen of the Old West: Dodge City marshal, captor of the outlaw Bill Doolin, one of Oklahoma's "Three Guardsmen," and eventually a filmmaker who starred as himself in the story of his own career.

## Buffalo Meat and Saloon Doors

William Matthew Tilghman Jr. was born on July 4, 1854, in Fort Dodge, Iowa, the third of six children. In 1857 his family moved to the newly created Kansas Territory and settled on a farm near Atchison. At seventeen he won a contract to supply buffalo meat to the men building the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe Railroad, and between September 1871 and April 1872 he is reputed to have killed 3,300 buffalo, a total he later claimed as the "all time record." By 1877 he was in Dodge City, where he and Henry Garris opened the Crystal Palace Saloon. A local paper noted that summer that the place was getting a new front and awning, which would draw attention to "the never ceasing fountains of refreshment flowing within." Barely a year later, the partners sold it.

## A Solid Gold Badge

Tilghman's first important post came on April 10, 1884, when he was appointed city marshal of Dodge City. On May 2, the town's citizens presented him with a solid gold badge. He resigned in March 1886 to tend his ranch, only to see the great blizzard of 1886 wipe out the livestock on many ranches in the area, his among them. He kept a commission as a Ford County deputy sheriff, and on his thirty-fourth birthday, July 4, 1888, duty took him to Farmer City, Kansas. There a man named Ed Prather, who had been making threats against him, reached for his revolver; Tilghman drew first, ordered him three times to take his hand off the gun, and fired when Prather tried to get into a better position. A coroner's jury ruled the killing justifiable. The next January he joined other Dodge City gunmen in the Gray County War, a county seat fight between Ingalls and Cimarron that left one man dead and five wounded. Tilghman escaped with a sprained ankle.

## Hunting the Wild Bunch

On April 22, 1889, Tilghman joined the first Oklahoma land rush. In Guthrie, a city that had not existed the day before, he built a commercial building on his Oklahoma Avenue lot and used the rent to re-establish himself as a rancher. For the remaining thirty-five years of his life he was an Oklahoman. In May 1892 he was appointed a Deputy U.S. Marshal, joining Heck Thomas, Chris Madsen, Frank Canton and Bud Ledbetter against the outlaws plaguing the territory, above all Bill Doolin's Wild Bunch. One by one the gang fell. On September 6, 1895, Tilghman brought down William "Little Bill" Raidler with a shotgun blast; Raidler survived and was sentenced to ten years. Then, on January 15, 1896, Tilghman trailed Doolin to a bathhouse in Eureka Springs, Arkansas. Doolin, seated in the lobby, failed to recognize him. Tilghman grabbed him, and after a brief struggle, subdued him without a shot fired. He, Thomas and Madsen became known as the "Three Guardsmen" of Oklahoma.

## Derby Horses and a Movie Camera

Prosperity followed. In 1899 Tilghman established the Oakland Stock Farm to breed thoroughbreds, bringing Chant, winner of the 1894 Kentucky Derby, from Kentucky as one of his studs. He was elected sheriff of Lincoln County in 1900 and re-elected two years later. In 1904, while traveling with Oklahoma Democrats, he looked up his old friend Bat Masterson in New York, and Masterson introduced him to President Theodore Roosevelt, who later invited him to be his special guest at William Howard Taft's 1909 inauguration. Tilghman won a seat in the Oklahoma Senate in 1910 and became Oklahoma City's chief of police in 1911. Then he turned to film. With Evett Dumas Nix and Chris Madsen he formed the Eagle Film Company in 1915, and The Passing of the Oklahoma Outlaws premiered in Chandler that May, with Tilghman starring as himself. Only thirteen minutes survive. The academic Frank Richard Prassel later called it "a major source of popular disinformation" for presenting staged scenes as real.

## Last Watch in Cromwell

In 1924, at the age of seventy, Tilghman came out of retirement to serve as marshal of Cromwell, a booming oil town. On the night of October 31, 1924, he was shot on the town's streets by Wiley Lynn, a corrupt federal prohibition agent, and he died early the next morning. Lynn was acquitted at trial, and he was himself shot dead in a gunfight in 1932. Governor Martin E. Trapp ordered Tilghman's body to lie in state in the rotunda of the Oklahoma capitol under an honor guard, making him the third person ever, and the first police officer, to receive that honor. The governor served as one of his pallbearers. Tilghman was buried in Chandler, where a park bears his name, and his homestead two miles northwest of town is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. In 1960 he was inducted into the Hall of Great Westerners of the National Cowboy & Western Heritage Museum.
