# Battle of Chustenahlah

The day after Christmas 1861, Confederate troops stormed Opothleyahola's winter camp on Bird Creek, and the pro-Union Creeks and Seminoles who survived were driven north through snow toward Kansas on the flight remembered as the Trail of Blood on Ice.

**Location:** 36.3878, -96.0752

**Categories:** Trail of Blood on Ice, Battles of the American Civil War in Indian Territory, Osage County, Oklahoma, Forced migrations in the United States, 1861 in Indian Territory

## Introduction

On Christmas Day, 1861, Confederate colonel James M. McIntosh learned that the column he had been counting on would not reach him for a while. He decided to attack anyway. The next day, December 26, his men moved on a camp tucked into a well-protected cove on Bird Creek, in what is now Osage County, Oklahoma, where Chief Opothleyahola's pro-Union Creeks and Seminoles had taken shelter after two earlier fights. By four that afternoon the defenders had been driven out. What followed was worse than the battle: a band of some 9,000 Native people forced to flee toward Kansas through bitter cold and snow, on a journey remembered as the Trail of Blood on Ice.

## A Sandbar in the Creek

The name Chustenahlah is a corruption of the Cherokee word "U-s-ta-la-na," meaning a shoal or sandbar in a stream or creek, a gentle name for a hard place. The Civil War had reached Indian Territory, which would not become the state of Oklahoma until 1907, and Confederate troops had undertaken a campaign to subdue the Native Americans there who sided with the Union and to tighten Southern control. They had already struck Opothleyahola's band of Creeks and the Seminoles led by Chief Halek Tustenuggee at Round Mountain and at Chusto-Talasah. Chustenahlah was meant to finish the job. McIntosh and Colonel Douglas H. Cooper, commander of the Confederate Indian Department, planned a combined attack, with their columns converging on the camp from different directions. McIntosh left Fort Gibson, in the eastern part of the territory, on December 22 with 1,380 men.

## Straight Up the Bluff

Without Cooper's column, McIntosh went ahead outnumbered and in severe cold. He attacked at noon. About 1,700 pro-Union defenders were hidden in the underbrush along the slope of a rugged hill, and McIntosh's plan was to converge on its crest from three sides. The South Kansas-Texas Cavalry, also called the 3rd Texas, was ordered to charge directly up the steep bluff on foot. To its left, the 11th Texas advanced through a narrow defile that kept it out of sight, while the 6th Texas circled around to the right. Under the attack the defenders gave ground slowly, taking cover, holding for a while, then falling back again. When the Confederates reached the camp itself, the retreat became a rout. The Unionists tried once more to hold their position, but by 4 p.m. they had been forced away.

## What the Victors Counted

Confederate casualties were 9 killed and 40 wounded. In his official report, McIntosh estimated the Unionist Native American losses at 250. The Confederate tally of what they captured reads like an inventory of a community's life: 30 wagons, 70 yoke of oxen, about 500 horses, several hundred head of cattle, 100 sheep and large quantities of supplies. The same list records 160 women and children and 20 Black people, counted among the spoils alongside the livestock. Behind those entries were people, not property. The Unionist commanders that day were Opothleyahola of the Creeks and, for the Seminoles, Halek Tustenuggee and Sonuk Mikko, also known as Billy Bowlegs.

## The Road to Fort Row

The survivors fled. Colonel Stand Watie, leading 300 Cherokee fighting for the Confederacy, intercepted some of the fleeing Creeks and Seminoles and killed 15 of them. After that, Opothleyahola's band mounted no further resistance, and the Confederates followed no further. Many of those who escaped went all the way to Kansas, where they found loyal Unionists. The remaining Union fighters and their families trekked to Fort Row, Kansas, stripped of much of what they needed because they had been forced to flee in haste. Winter did the rest. By the most commonly cited estimate, nearly 2,000 people died on the way to Fort Row or shortly after arriving there, mostly from exposure and disease, though other accounts give lower counts. The battle had lasted an afternoon. By that estimate, the flight north cost many times more lives than the fighting on the hillside at Bird Creek.

## Flight Context

The marked site is at approximately 36.39°N, 96.08°W in Osage County, northeast Oklahoma, near Skiatook. There is no large monument to spot from the air; the course of Bird Creek, where the camp stood, is the thread to follow. A viewing altitude of 2,500-4,000 ft MSL works well. Nearest airports: Skiatook Municipal (FAA identifier 2F6, no ICAO code), about 4 nm southeast; Tulsa International (KTUL), about 15 nm southeast; and Bartlesville Municipal (KBVO), about 23 nm north. Round Mountain, the campaign's first battle, was fought to the southwest, near either Keystone or Yale; historians still disagree which.
