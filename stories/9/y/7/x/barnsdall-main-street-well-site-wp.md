# Barnsdall Main Street Well Site

In the middle of Main Street in Barnsdall, Oklahoma, a fenced pumping unit marks a 1914 oil well believed to be the only one of its kind in the world, and a company history that once spanned the whole Osage lease.

**Location:** 36.5616, -96.1657

**Categories:** Oil wells, Oil and gas history, Osage County, Oklahoma, National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Roadside curiosities

## Introduction

Drivers on Main Street in Barnsdall, Oklahoma, share the road with an oil well. It sits right in the middle of the street, 28 feet west of 8th Street, a pumping unit wrapped in a four-foot chain-link fence in the middle of the roadway. Officially it is Indian Territory Illuminating Oil Company Well #20, Osage County, and it is believed to be the only oil well anywhere in the world standing in the middle of a town's main street. According to signage at the site, it was completed on March 18, 1914, at a depth of 1,771 feet.

## Well Number Twenty

The well was never a gusher. At its maximum, according to its National Register application, it could have produced eight to ten barrels of oil a day, and it stayed active until the 1960s. The pumping unit standing there now is not the original "flathead" model, which was replaced sometime after 1962. Its replacement is a skid-mounted unit with an electric motor, gears, wellhead connections and rods that still appear to be in good condition, though the unit no longer runs. What makes the site precious is its accessibility. The application calls it "the only publicly accessible historic well site in the Barnsdall oil field." It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 1, 1997, with a period of significance running from 1914 to 1930, as part of a group of energy-related resources in northeastern Oklahoma.

## Bigheart Becomes Barnsdall

The town's very name is an oil story. It was founded in 1905 as Bigheart, named for the Osage Chief James Bigheart. On January 1, 1922, it was renamed Barnsdall in honor of Theodore N. Barnsdall, after the Barnsdall Oil Company had bought the local refinery the year before. Barnsdall's name was already woven into the story of the well in the street. In 1903 he had bought 51 percent of the stock in the Indian Territory Illuminating Oil Company, known as ITIO, the firm whose name Well #20 carries. The company did well from that point on, and in 1906 it won renewal of its Osage lease, though now limited to 680,000 acres on the east side of the reservation. By the 2020 census, the town that took his name had 1,034 residents.

## Carving Up the Osage Lease

ITIO had formed in the early 1900s, when two companies operating the lease lands combined. Edwin B. Foster had signed a lease covering the entire Osage reservation, some 1.5 million acres. His successor, Henry V. Foster, divided the lease into blocks three miles long from east to west and half a mile wide from north to south, then subleased them to other companies on a bonus and royalty basis. In the end he leased to dozens of separate companies while keeping a number of blocks for ITIO to develop itself. Barnsdall's controlling stake was eventually sold to a subsidiary of Cities Service Company. The Osage lease was lost in 1916, but ITIO kept drilling elsewhere in Oklahoma, around Seminole among other places. On December 4, 1928, it brought in the discovery well of the Oklahoma City Oil Field and became a major player there. By 1940 Cities Service had acquired Foster's stock and all the rest, and it merged ITIO's operations into other companies. The humble well on Main Street kept pumping into the 1960s, outlasting the company whose name it bears.

## Flight Context

The Main Street well is at 36.5616°N, 96.1657°W in Barnsdall, Osage County, Oklahoma. The pumping unit itself is far too small to pick out from altitude, but a low pass at 1,500–2,000 feet AGL (where permitted) shows Barnsdall's compact street grid; the well sits in the middle of Main Street just west of 8th Street in the northwest part of town. Nearby airports: Pawhuska Municipal (FAA identifier H76), about 13 nm WNW; Bartlesville Municipal (KBVO), about 14 nm NNE; and Tulsa International (KTUL), about 26 nm SSE. Northeast Oklahoma sees frequent spring and summer thunderstorms.
