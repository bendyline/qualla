# Cushing, Oklahoma

A Payne County town born in the Land Run of 1891 became an oil boomtown, lost its refineries and its railroad, and then became the "Pipeline Crossroads of the World" and the official delivery point for NYMEX light sweet crude futures.

**Location:** 35.9825, -96.7642

**Categories:** Cities in Oklahoma, Oil and gas, Energy infrastructure, Payne County, Oklahoma, Oklahoma history

## Introduction

In 1982, the last trains stopped running to Cushing, Oklahoma. A year later, the New York Mercantile Exchange chose this small Payne County city as the official delivery point for its light sweet crude futures contract. The railroad was gone, but pipelines still converged here from every direction. Today Cushing is a major trading hub for crude oil and a price settlement point for West Texas Intermediate on NYMEX. It is known as the "Pipeline Crossroads of the World," a grand title for a city of about 8,300 people.

## A Trader's Claim

The land that became Cushing was part of the Sac and Fox Nation until the Land Run of 1891 opened it to settlement. William Rea "Billy" Little, a former government trader for the tribe, built a house, established his claim and laid out town lots. A post office followed on November 10, 1891, and the town took its name from Marshall Cushing, private secretary to U.S. Postmaster General John Wanamaker. Rails came next. The Eastern Oklahoma Railway built its line to Cushing in 1902, and the Missouri, Kansas and Texas Railway added service on its own line in 1903. For two decades, that was Cushing: a land-run claim, a post office and two railroads.

## Slick's Gusher

Everything changed in March 1912, when the wildcatter Thomas B. Slick brought in a gusher east of town. The discovery well, the Wheeler No. 1, came in near present-day Drumright, other wells followed quickly, and the field became known as the Cushing-Drumright Oil Field. Production centered on the new town of Drumright, while Cushing became a refining center after the Consumers Oil Company opened a refinery in 1913. The field was enormous. Daily production peaked in May 1915, when 3,090 wells produced 310,000 barrels of oil, and as late as 1919 the field still produced 17 percent of all the oil produced in the nation. Eventually 23 oil companies and five oil-field supply houses located in Cushing. The town's population eventually peaked at 9,301 in the 1930 census. Among its buildings on the National Register of Historic Places is the Gillespie Drilling Company Building, a name that speaks to those oil years.

## Where the Pipelines Meet

Refining eventually faded. It continued through the 1970s and 1980s until the last two refineries, Kerr-McGee and Hudson, closed, and rail service ended in 1982. What remained was the web of pipelines. Crude oil flows into Cushing from all directions and out again through dozens of pipelines, and the tank farms around the city hold about 77 million barrels of working crude storage capacity, roughly 14 percent of the national total by the U.S. Energy Information Administration's 2021 count. In May 2023, Cushing was chosen as the site of a planned $5.56 billion refinery designed to process 250,000 barrels per day of light, sweet crude into low-carbon transportation fuels, with a goal of zero-carbon-footprint operation. At its announcement, the project was expected to create more than 420 direct jobs and to be operational in 2027.

## A Town Beyond the Tanks

Cushing had 8,327 residents at the 2020 census, and life here extends well past the tank farms. Cushing Municipal Park grew from Cushing Memorial Park, built by the WPA in 1935, and today the city lists a duck pond, a skate park, a nine-hole disc golf course, an amphitheatre and pavilions among its amenities. The city's seasonal aquatic center opened for the summer of 2026 on May 25, with day swim, family swim and water aerobics. Buffalo Rock Golf and Venue, which began as the Cushing Country Club in 1921, is open to the public, and Cushing Lake, six miles west, offers a boat ramp and dock. The town counts champions among its notable people, too: Frank Lewis and Robert Pearce won Olympic gold in freestyle wrestling at the 1936 and 1932 Summer Games.

## Flight Context

Cushing sits at 35.9825°N, 96.7642°W in Payne County, Oklahoma, where State Highway 33 meets State Highway 18. From 2,500–4,000 feet AGL, the tank farms surrounding the city are the unmistakable landmark: fields of large round crude oil storage tanks spread across the countryside. Cushing Municipal Airport (KCUH) is about 2 nm SSW with a paved 5,201 x 100 ft runway; Stillwater Regional (KSWO) is about 19 nm NW, and Tulsa International (KTUL) about 45 nm ENE. Visibility over north-central Oklahoma is usually good, but spring and summer thunderstorms can build quickly.
