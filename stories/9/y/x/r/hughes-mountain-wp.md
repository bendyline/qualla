# Hughes Mountain

Atop a modest Ozark summit in Washington County, Missouri, ancient volcanic rock has cracked into the many-sided columns of the Devil's Honeycomb, a small cousin of Devils Tower and the Giant's Causeway.

**Location:** 37.8107, -90.7154

**Categories:** St. Francois Mountains, Mountains of Missouri, Rock formations of Missouri, Conservation Areas of Missouri, Proterozoic volcanism, Landforms of Washington County, Missouri

## Introduction

At the top of Hughes Mountain, the ground turns geometric. Across an open glade, the rock breaks into columns with four, five and six sides, and seen from above their tops fit together like the cells of a honeycomb. The formation is known as the Devil's Honeycomb. It is Precambrian rhyolite, and it crowns a summit in the St. Francois Mountains of Missouri's Ozarks that reaches just over 1,200 feet, rising about 380 feet above the Big River floodplain. The mountain is modest in height, but its summit ranks among Missouri's catalogued geologic curiosities.

## Stone That Cracked Into Columns

Hughes Mountain is made of extrusive rhyolite, volcanic rock that erupted onto the surface in the Precambrian. As it cooled, it fractured along vertical joints, splitting into polygonal columns. The columns here have from four to six sides and stand up to three feet tall, their flat tops forming the honeycomb pattern that gives the glade its name. Geologists call the process columnar jointing, and it is not limited to rhyolite. The Devil's Honeycomb is a smaller example of the same kind of structure found at Devils Tower National Monument in Wyoming, Devils Postpile National Monument in California and the Giant's Causeway near Bushmills, Northern Ireland. Missouri's version earned its own place in the literature: it is listed in Thomas Robinson Beveridge's Geologic Wonders and Curiosities of Missouri, published by the Missouri Division of Geology and Land Survey in 1978.

## A Family Name on the Map

The mountain's name comes from the family that settled around it. John Hughes, his wife Susannah and their children arrived in the early 1800s, and the high ground near their home became Hughes Mountain. The summit itself stayed public land for another half century, until John's son Mahlon Hughes bought it in 1861. After that it remained in Hughes hands for more than a century, until the Missouri Conservation Commission purchased it in 1982 and designated it a State Natural Area. Today the Missouri Department of Conservation's Hughes Mountain Natural Area covers 462 acres of the mountain, including the rhyolite glade at its crown.

## Cactus on the Glade

About two-thirds of the natural area is forest, divided among three forest types. Post oaks and white oaks grow here with stunted blackjack oaks, eastern red cedar and black hickory, along with farkleberry, winged elm, lowbush blueberry, serviceberry, red elm, sassafras and both winged and aromatic sumac. The remaining third is glade, the open, rocky ground where the columns surface, and its plant life is a different world: prickly pear cactus, flame flowers, little bluestem, broomsedge and poverty grass, yellow star grass, spiderwort, wild hyacinth, lance-leaf coreopsis, pineweed, rushfoil and rough buttonweed, with a variety of lichens spreading across the stone. Lichen grasshoppers live here, and so do two lizards, the fence lizard and the collared lizard. Prairie warblers are among the birds recorded on the mountain.

## Up From Highway M

The mountain sits in southern Washington County, just south of the Big River and Highway M on Cedar Creek Road. Visitors reach the summit by a trail of under a mile that begins at a small parking area on Highway M, about three miles southeast of Irondale. It is a short walk for a strange reward: the glade at the top, where the honeycomb spreads underfoot and the Big River floodplain lies about 380 feet below. The rock beneath your boots dates to the Precambrian, the deepest chapter of Earth's history, and it took on these geometric shapes as it cooled, long before anyone was here to name them for the devil.

## Flight Context

Hughes Mountain is at approximately 37.81°N, 90.72°W in southern Washington County, Missouri, just south of the Big River and Highway M, about 3 miles southeast of Irondale. The summit reaches just over 1,200 feet MSL, so a viewing altitude of 2,500–3,500 feet MSL keeps a safe margin over the surrounding St. Francois Mountains; look for the bare rocky glade on the wooded summit. Nearby airports: Farmington Regional (KFAM), about 14 nm east-southeast; Washington County Airport at Potosi (FAA identifier 8WC, no ICAO code), about 7 nm north; Bismarck Memorial (FAA identifier H57, no ICAO code), about 6 nm east-southeast. Expect possible mechanical turbulence over the ridges on windy days and valley fog on calm mornings.
