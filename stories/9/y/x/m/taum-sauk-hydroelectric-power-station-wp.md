# Taum Sauk Hydroelectric Power Station

A mountaintop reservoir in the Missouri Ozarks that stores electricity as water, burst in 2005 and flooded a state park, and was rebuilt as North America's largest roller-compacted concrete dam.

**Location:** 37.5206, -90.8344

**Categories:** Pumped-storage hydroelectric power stations in the United States, Dam failures in the United States, Disasters in Missouri, Dams in Missouri, St. Francois Mountains, Energy infrastructure

## Introduction

The Taum Sauk plant uses more electricity than it makes, and it still earns its keep. Every night, power from the grid pumps water up through a tunnel in Proffit Mountain into a walled reservoir on a ridge just below the summit. By day, when demand peaks, the water falls back through turbines to a lower reservoir on the East Fork of the Black River, generating electricity when the grid needs it most. Its operator, Ameren Missouri, has called it "the biggest battery that we have." On the morning of December 14, 2005, that battery split open.

## A Battery Made of Water

The physics are unforgiving: pumping water uphill always takes more energy than the water returns on the way down, and at Taum Sauk no stream feeds the upper reservoir, so every gallon has to be lifted. The economics work anyway because the pumping happens at night, when the grid runs on cheap baseline power. The scale is enormous. The upper reservoir holds about 1.5 billion gallons behind a wall nearly 100 feet tall, built on a flat ridgetop so the dam has to run all the way around it. That water sits 760 feet above the 440-megawatt plant, a greater head than Hoover Dam's, and reaches it through a 7,000-foot tunnel bored through the mountain. Despite its name, the reservoir is on Proffit Mountain, not Taum Sauk Mountain, which rises roughly seven miles to the east-northeast.

## A Mountain Swap

Union Electric began looking for a pumped-storage site in 1953, and by 1958 it wanted at least 300 feet of vertical drop. Consultants Sverdrup-Parcel & Associates studied the St. Francois Mountains, close to St. Louis and rich in steep relief, and intended to recommend Taum Sauk, Missouri's highest point. Strong political opposition to developing that scenic peak, later part of a state park, ruled it out. Nearby Proffit Mountain, at 1,720 feet the state's sixth-highest point, sat close to the Black River and only 27 miles from the transmission grid. Built from 1960 to 1962 and running by 1963, the plant was by far the largest pumped-storage station in North America by several measures. At the time its pump-turbines were the largest ever produced and its head the highest in the country, and it could be run by operators 90 to 120 miles away or entirely automatically. In September 2005 it was named an IEEE Milestone.

## Twenty-Five Minutes in December

A few months later, in the pre-dawn hours near the end of a pumping cycle, the upper reservoir overfilled. Water-level probes meant to prevent overtopping had been set higher than the lowest point of the parapet wall, and the backup systems failed too. Water spilled over, and a section of the embankment 656 feet wide gave way. In about 25 minutes, according to the Association of State Dam Safety Officials, roughly 4,300 acre-feet of water poured down the mountainside, sending a flash flood 20 feet deep down the Black River. It tore through Johnson's Shut-Ins State Park, destroying 281 acres of it, and ripped the park superintendent's home from its foundation with the superintendent, his wife and their three young children inside. All five were swept away in the wreckage, and all five survived. By chance, no one was killed, and only four people were injured.

## Beyond Imprudent

The reckoning was severe. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission settled with Ameren for $15 million, a $10 million civil penalty plus $5 million for project enhancements, then the largest fine in the agency's history and 30 times the previous record. The Missouri Public Service Commission concluded that the loss was due to imprudence by Ameren's subsidiary, which had kept operating after learning its water-level monitoring was unreliable, a decision the commission called "frankly beyond imprudent – it is reckless." A Missouri Highway Patrol criminal investigation named no suspect, and the attorney general announced no charges would be filed. Ameren settled with the superintendent's family in 2006 without disclosing the terms, and it was reported to have paid close to $200 million in settlements related to the breach.

## Rebuilt From the Ground Up

The new upper reservoir dam was built from scratch, and it is the largest roller-compacted concrete dam in North America. Water was pumped into it for the first time on February 27, 2010, and engineers raised and lowered the level again and again while they monitored how the structure responded. Federal regulators approved a return to normal operations on April 1, and on April 21, 2010, the plant generated electricity again. That year it was rededicated as an IEEE Milestone. Before the failure, visitors could drive up Proffit Mountain to an observation deck above the reservoir, and geology students came to see a striking Precambrian-Cambrian unconformity exposed during construction, a place where rocks separated by a vast gap in geologic time sit one atop the other.

## Flight Context

The upper reservoir sits at approximately 37.52°N, 90.83°W on the ridge just below the summit of Proffit Mountain in Reynolds County, about 85 miles south-southwest of St. Louis near Lesterville. From 3,500–5,000 feet MSL the ring-shaped concrete reservoir on the ridgetop is one of the most distinctive landmarks in the St. Francois Mountains, and the lower reservoir on the East Fork of the Black River lies below it. Taum Sauk Mountain, the state's highest point, is roughly seven miles to the east-northeast, and Johnson's Shut-Ins State Park lies in the valley that took the brunt of the 2005 flood. Farmington Regional Airport (KFAM) is an airport with services, roughly 28 miles to the northeast. Terrain rises to about 1,700–1,800 feet MSL nearby; expect valley fog on calm mornings.
