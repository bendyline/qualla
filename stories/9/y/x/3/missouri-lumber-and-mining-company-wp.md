# Missouri Lumber and Mining Company

Pennsylvania lumbermen built the Ozark company town of Grandin around what became, by some measures, the largest lumber mill in the country, then left when the pine ran out.

**Location:** 36.8294, -90.8267

**Categories:** Timber industry in the United States, Company towns in Missouri, History of Missouri, Ozarks, Buildings and structures on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri

## Introduction

Payday in Grandin, Missouri, came on the tenth of every month, at a teller booth inside the company headquarters, a two-story pine building with a vault. Nearly everything around it belonged to the same employer. The Missouri Lumber and Mining Company owned the houses its workers rented, ran the store, staffed the hospital, oversaw the school and paid the ministers. At its peak, around 1905, it employed 1,500 people in Grandin, and one of every six residents of Carter County worked for it. Its sawmill beside a flooded sinkhole was ranked, in 1900 and 1901, among the largest in the world by capacity and was billed as the largest in the country. Within a decade the timber was gone, and so was the company's mill.

## Cheap Pine and Distant Owners

The company was the idea of Pennsylvania men. In the 1860s O.H.P. Williams, a Pittsburgh lumberman, and his son-in-law Elijah Bishop Grandin, who had prospered in oil at Tidioute, heard about the timber of Missouri's Courtois Hills and began buying land, later paying an average of about $1 an acre in Carter County. With John Livingston Grandin and Jahu Hunter they incorporated the Missouri Lumber and Mining Company in 1880. They expected cheap land, a poor population eager for work, winters mild enough for year-round cutting, and a ready market on the growing, thinly wooded Great Plains. All four partners stayed in Pennsylvania and sent Tidioute mill operator John Barber White to run things; in 1879 and 1880 he bought land at sheriff's sales for as little as five cents an acre. Their first mill, on the Black River near Williamsville, failed because the Iron Mountain Railroad refused to give it direct rail access. It closed in 1884, and its 125 workers lost their jobs.

## Ox Teams to Toliver Pond

In February 1887 they struck a deal with the Kansas City, Fort Scott and Memphis Railway, which agreed to build a spur from Willow Springs to the company's land in the Current River valley in exchange for guaranteed lumber shipments. The new mill site was Toliver Pond, a flooded 3.5-acre sinkhole near the upper Little Black River that could hold logs waiting to be sawed. The old mill was dismantled and moved there, and a locomotive was taken apart and hauled by ox teams over the final stretch, along with machinery and enough iron rail for six miles of logging railroad. The effort was called unprecedented, and it produced a $250,000 complex of mills and kilns. When the Current River Railroad arrived in June 1888, six million board feet of lumber were already stacked in the yard waiting for it.

## Beaver Dam Soft Pine

Production reached 32 million board feet in 1892 and averaged 60 million a year after 1895. The mill could handle up to 75 million a year, though the most it ever cut was just under 66 million in 1901. Six locomotives and 300 log cars ran on standard-gauge tram lines through the forest, and above Chicopee mules dragged logs to the Current River, where they floated downstream in flotillas up to 15 miles long. Logs soaked in Toliver Pond for days so grit would not dull the steam-driven saws. The company sold its shortleaf pine as "Beaver Dam Soft Pine," with a beaver for a trademark, from Ohio to the plains; in 1901 half its output went to Kansas City dealers. White moved his office to Kansas City and in 1897 took the lead of the Missouri Land and Lumber Exchange, a cartel that set production and prices. The company grew powerful enough to raise lumber prices ten times in 1899.

## Inside the Company Town

Grandin was planned by a company architect with a landscaped main street and residential streets laid out for up to 1,000 houses. The company built more than 475 of them and rented them to families for $1 per room per month. Single women lived in a company boarding house, where the firm could maintain "its vision of morality"; most single men rented rough shacks for a couple of dollars a month. From around 1890 the company ran a hospital funded by monthly payroll fees, offering unlimited care and even a clinic on a railroad flatcar that traveled to the forest work camps, an early example of company-sponsored health care. It was needed: serious injuries and deaths were not uncommon, especially among railroad crews. The company aided widows of men killed on the job, usually for up to two years. Its generosity had limits. In 1906 it built a sidewalk on Main Street to help business at its stores but refused to add sidewalks on the residential side streets.

## When the Pine Ran Out

The industry did not manage the forest for a second harvest. Missouri taxed cut and uncut land at the same rate, so replanting made no financial sense, and cleared land was sold off for farming. By 1900 few trees stood near the mill, and operations pushed north into Shannon County. A 1905 fire destroyed a secondary mill, and after lumber prices fell in the recession of 1907 the long haul back to Grandin no longer paid. The last local logs were cut in 1909. In 1910 the mill closed and was moved, with many houses, to West Eminence, and the company sold Grandin's remaining homes for $50 to $100 each. Its Missouri operations ended in 1919. In 1980 Toliver Pond and 29 buildings, including the old company office, later a Masonic lodge and a bank, and the 22-room company hotel, were listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Flight Context

Grandin lies at approximately 36.83°N, 90.83°W in Carter County, in the Courtois Hills of the southeastern Missouri Ozarks. From 2,000–3,500 feet AGL, look for the small grid of Grandin's streets and the spring-fed Toliver (Mill) Pond on the east side of town, where the mill complex once stood. Poplar Bluff Regional Business Airport (KPOF) is the nearest airport with services, roughly 28 miles to the east. Expect valley fog on calm mornings and summer haze and thunderstorms.
