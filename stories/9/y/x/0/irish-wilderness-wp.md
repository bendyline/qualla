# Irish Wilderness

In the Missouri Ozarks, a priest's short-lived colony for Irish immigrant families was scattered by the Civil War, and the land it left behind has grown back into a 16,277-acre federal wilderness above the Eleven Point River.

**Location:** 36.7308, -91.1750

**Categories:** Protected areas of Oregon County, Missouri, Wilderness areas of Missouri, Mark Twain National Forest, Protected areas established in 1984

## Introduction

The name sounds like a contradiction. Wilderness, by definition, is land without people, yet this one is named for the people who tried to make a home in it. In the years just before the Civil War, some forty Irish immigrant families left the crowded city of St. Louis for these Ozark hills, led by a young priest who believed they deserved land of their own. Within a few years the war had scattered them. The forest took the land back, and today the Irish Wilderness is a 16,277-acre federal wilderness area in southern Missouri, still carrying the name of the settlers who vanished from it.

## A Priest's Promise

In the 1840s, St. Louis received a large influx of Irish immigrants. They faced discrimination in the city, and most found work on the railroads and in the mines. Father John Joseph Hogan knew their world firsthand. Born in County Limerick in 1829, he had come to the United States in 1848 and was ordained a priest in 1852, and he would later become a bishop. Hogan saw another possibility for his countrymen: land of their own, where they could farm and settle as families. In 1858 he founded a settlement in Oregon and Ripley counties in the Missouri Ozarks, and a Reverend James Fox bought and donated some of the land.

## Forty Families

By 1859 around forty families had begun settling in the hills, and more were on their way. The colony was large on the map. Its western border was the Eleven Point River in Oregon County, and from there it stretched east past the Current River into Ripley County, reaching as far as Doniphan. For laborers who had faced discrimination in the city, this was a different kind of promise: ground they could own, homes of their own, and a future to build as families. In the Ozark hills they set about making homes and farms.

## Caught Between the Armies

The settlement lasted only from about 1859 through 1863. When the Civil War came, the Irish Wilderness found itself in a no man's land, raided by Union and Confederate troops alike and by bushwhackers who roamed southeast Missouri. The settlers fled, abandoning the farms they had worked so hard to start. Hogan grieved for them. "Who will now lead back those poor scattered settlers to their humble but ruined home?" he lamented. No one did. The settlement was wiped out, and the land returned to forest.

## The Forest Returns

In 1984 Congress formally designated the Irish Wilderness, one of eight wilderness areas in Missouri. It lies within the Eleven Point Ranger District of the Mark Twain National Forest, about ten miles northeast of Alton. The 18.6-mile White's Creek Trail winds through it for hikers and horseback riders, passing Fiddler and Bliss Springs, overlooks of the Eleven Point River and faint traces of old tramway railroads. White's Creek Cave is closed to the public and gated to protect endangered bats. Karst topography shapes the scenery, and the woods now shelter black bears, whitetail deer, timber rattlesnakes, wild turkeys and bald eagles, with the occasional wandering mountain lion passing through. Walking here, it is hard to picture the homes and farms of Hogan's colony. That is the quiet ending to the story: the settlers' dream faded, and their name became the name of the wild.

## Flight Context

The Irish Wilderness is centered near 36.7308°N, 91.1750°W in Oregon County, southern Missouri, about 10 miles northeast of Alton, with the Eleven Point River along its western side. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, follow the winding, tree-lined Eleven Point River; the wilderness appears as an unbroken stretch of forested Ozark ridges and hollows with no roads or buildings. As a designated wilderness in the Mark Twain National Forest, it is a noise-sensitive area where pilots are asked to stay at least 2,000 feet above the surface. West Plains Regional Airport (KUNO) lies about 36 nm to the west-northwest, and Poplar Bluff Municipal (KPOF) about 41 nm to the east. Expect morning fog in the river valleys.
