# Black Madonna Shrine and Grottos

In the wooded hills south of Pacific, Missouri, a Polish Franciscan brother spent twenty-three years building cave-like grottos of rock, concrete, seashells and costume jewelry in honor of the Black Madonna of Częstochowa, working on them until the day he died.

## Introduction

The lambs and rabbits resting at the feet of St. Francis began life as cake pans. Brother Bronislaus Luszcz used the pans as molds for concrete animals, one of many small improvisations in the grottos he built by hand in the Missouri woods south of Pacific. Seashells, costume jewelry, ceramic figurines and colorful scraps brought by pilgrims all found their way into his work. The Black Madonna Shrine and Grottos is what a single immigrant brother made from rock, cement and devotion over twenty-three years.

## Brothers From Poland

The story begins with a request. In 1927, John J. Glennon, Archbishop of St. Louis, asked a group of Franciscans to come from Poland and set up a nursing home in the countryside. A number of Franciscan Missionary Brothers of the Sacred Heart of Jesus made the journey to the St. Louis area, and among them was Brother Bronislaus, then thirty-four years old. The brothers were given an abandoned convent in the foothills of the Ozarks, where they built an infirmary and worked a small farm that fed both the patients and themselves. The infirmary operated for decades before closing in 2008. The shrine that grew up beside it has outlasted it.

## A Cedar Chapel for the Madonna

In 1937, Brother Bronislaus began clearing the thickly wooded land next to the infirmary. He built a chapel of cedar wood and hung above its altar a portrait of the Black Madonna of Częstochowa, the revered Polish image of Mary, known as the Black Madonna for its dark brown skin tones. He dedicated his shrine to Mary as "Queen of Peace and Mercy." The chapel soon became a center of devotion, drawing pilgrimages, prayer services and masses. Then, in 1958, an arsonist set fire to the altar, and the chapel burned. The loss did not end the shrine. In the 1960s an open-air chapel was built to replace the one that had been destroyed.

## Caves Made by Hand

After finishing the chapel, Brother Bronislaus turned to the grottos. He built them from concrete and native Missouri tiff rock brought from the nearby mining town of Potosi, shaping the stone to resemble natural cave formations. Within them stand statues of Saint Francis, Saint Joseph, Our Lady of Perpetual Help, Jesus and the disciples at Gethsemane, Our Lady of Sorrows, Mary's Assumption and the Nativity. Each grotto is an intricate mosaic, glittering with seashells, costume jewelry, ceramic figurines and the colorful bits that pilgrims brought to him from time to time. The altar stone at the Our Lady of Sorrows Grotto came from the original monastery chapel. The crucifix of the Gethsemane Grotto serves local hikers as a landmark as they make their way through the surrounding woods.

## The Unfinished Grotto

Brother Bronislaus spent twenty-three years building the shrine, and he never stopped. In 1960, at the age of sixty-six, he died of heat stroke while working on a grotto of Our Lady of Fatima. His grottos remain, still decorated with the gifts of the people who came to pray among them. The shrine has had to weather more than one intrusion; in 2013 a thief broke into the gift shop and made off with sterling silver medals, silver-plated necklaces and a donation jar full of change. Yet the place endures, a folk-art sanctuary in the Missouri hills, complete with a picnic pavilion for the visitors who still arrive.

## Flight Context

The Black Madonna Shrine and Grottos lies at approximately 38.4129° N, 90.6829° W in the wooded hills south of Pacific, Missouri, at the northern edge of the Ozark foothills. The grottos are small and hidden under tree cover, so they are hard to spot from the air; use Pacific and Interstate 44 to the north and the winding Meramec River valley as references. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet above ground level, the transition from the open river corridor to the forested hills to the south is easy to see. Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) in Chesterfield lies to the north, and Washington Regional Airport (KFYG) is to the northwest; be mindful of the St. Louis Class B airspace to the northeast. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common, and autumn color on the wooded hills offers the best scenery.
