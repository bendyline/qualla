# Daniel Boone Home

In the hills above Defiance, Missouri, stands the stone house Nathan Boone built for his family, where his aging father Daniel spent much of his final years mending traps and guns and where the famous frontiersman died at sunrise on September 26, 1820.

## Introduction

"He died on the morning of September 26, 1820, about sunrise—the fourteenth day after his arrival here." The words belong to Nathan Boone, youngest son of Daniel Boone, and the "here" was his own house in the Femme Osage country of Missouri, in what is now Defiance. One of America's most celebrated frontiersmen did not die in a lean-to in the wilderness. He died in his son's home, a house that still stands, and he had been exceedingly anxious, while recovering from a fever, to be brought back to it.

## From Log to Stone

The Boones came to Missouri from Kentucky in late 1799, when the land was still part of Louisiana. Nathan wasted little time putting down roots. "In the summer of 1800, I erected a good substantial log house," he later recalled, "and several years after that I replaced it with a commodious stone building." That stone house is the one visitors see today. Its architecture follows the Georgian style, orderly and symmetrical, a statement of permanence from a family that intended to stay. Nathan and his family lived there until 1837, when they moved farther south, but by then the house had already earned its place in history as the home where Daniel Boone spent much of the last chapter of his life.

## The Old Man at the Workbench

Retirement, for Daniel Boone, still meant work. Nathan remembered that his father "built himself a shop and had a set of tools, and when at home he would make and repair traps and guns. In fact he did all the needed smith work for the family and sometimes for neighbors to oblige them." It is a quietly revealing picture. The man whose name had become shorthand for the frontier spent his days at a workbench, keeping the household's tools in order and doing favors for the people who lived nearby. The arrangement did not last forever. "After a few years," Nathan added, "he disposed of his tools." Even so, the image of the old hunter mending traps in his son's household captures something that the legends often miss: a working man, useful for as long as he could be.

## A Colonel Before the Land Board

Daniel's move to Nathan's place survives in an official record. In 1806, a statement to the Federal Land Commission concerned his own original land grant, which remained unsettled. The record states that "Colonel D. Boone" had, on arriving in Louisiana, taken up residence "with his lady" at the home of his son Daniel M. Boone in the district of Femme Osage, and that about two years earlier he had moved to the home of "a younger son, Nathan Boone, where he now lives." The board noted that the claimant was about seventy years old and his wife about sixty-eight. Daniel and his wife Rebecca lived primarily with Nathan from at least 1804 to 1813. Rebecca died in March 1813 at the home of their daughter Jemima Callaway near present-day Marthasville, and Daniel returned to Nathan's house for much of the time from late 1816 onward.

## The Last Ride Home

Daniel still visited Jemima's family, the Callaways, near Marthasville, and he spent the summer of 1820 with them. Nathan's account of what followed is plain and tender. "He had an attack of fever, not severe, and while recovering was exceedingly anxious to be taken to my house," he wrote. "Finally I took him back in a carriage." Two weeks later, at sunrise on September 26, Daniel Boone died under his son's roof. He was first buried in Missouri, near Rebecca. In 1845 their remains were reportedly disinterred and reburied in Frankfort, Kentucky, though there is some dispute over whether the bones moved were truly his. The house where he died, by contrast, has never been in doubt.

## A Village Around the House

The house was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1973. Around it has grown Boonesfield Village, a collection of 19th-century buildings in various architectural styles that surround the Georgian stone house and help visitors picture the settlement the Boones knew. For years the site belonged to Lindenwood University, which donated it to St. Charles County in May 2016. It now operates as the Historic Daniel Boone Home at Lindenwood Park under the county's Parks and Recreation Department, with guided tours offered for a fee during park hours for much of the year and reduced, weekend-only tour hours from mid-December to March 1.

## Flight Context

The Historic Daniel Boone Home lies at approximately 38.6517° N, 90.8539° W in the wooded hills of western St. Charles County, near the small town of Defiance, Missouri. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet above ground level, use the Missouri River as your main reference: it curves through the valley to the south, with the Katy Trail following the river bottoms near Defiance. Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) in Chesterfield sits to the east across the Missouri River, St. Charles County Smartt Airport (KSET) lies to the northeast, and Washington Regional Airport (KFYG) is to the southwest. Remain clear of the St. Louis Class B airspace to the east. Autumn foliage makes the hills especially striking, while summer haze and thunderstorms can reduce visibility.
