# Washington, Missouri

A Missouri River town that grew from a Spanish-era settlement into a German, Union-loyal river port, and the home of the world's oldest and largest maker of corncob pipes.

**Location:** 38.5519, -91.0133

**Categories:** Cities in Franklin County, Missouri, Missouri populated places on the Missouri River, Missouri Rhineland, German-American culture in Missouri, Industrial history

## Introduction

In 1869, a woodworker named Henry Tibbe began making pipes out of corn cobs and selling them in his shop in Washington, Missouri. He likened them to meerschaum pipes, and the comparison eventually gave his business its name. Missouri Meerschaum still sits on the city's riverfront, described as the world's oldest and largest manufacturer of corncob pipes, and it anchors Washington's claim to be the "corncob pipe capital of the world". The city itself sits on the south bank of the Missouri River, about 50 miles west of St. Louis. With 14,500 people counted in the 2020 census, it is the largest city in Franklin County.

## A Fort, a Ferry and Free Land

Washington's story begins under Spanish rule. The first settlement here was called St. John Meyer's Settlement, and it was the site of a Spanish log fort, San Juan del Misuri, which stood from 1796 to 1803. Starting in 1799, the family and followers of Daniel Boone settled the land across the river. In 1814 a ferry was licensed to cross the Missouri to the north, and the settlement became known as Washington Landing, named for George Washington after it came under American control. A town was laid out in 1827, and lots went on sale in 1829. To attract residents, the town waived the cost of land for any buyer who could build a substantial house within two years. The offer drew many new settlers.

## Newcomers Against Slavery

In 1833, substantial numbers of German families opposed to slavery began moving to Washington, and they soon outnumbered the town's existing population of slaveholders. Many of those slaveholders' homes still stand throughout the town, reminders that slavery was part of Washington's early life. During the Civil War, Washington was a strong supporter of the Union. Confederate General Sterling Price's soldiers ransacked the town, but they could not hold the area. The German connection endures. The Missouri Rhineland, the German American wine region along the river, lies all around, and in 1990 Marbach am Neckar in Germany became its sister city, with occasional student exchanges and visits between the two.

## Cobs, Rails and Steamboats

After the war, Washington became a transportation center for both railroads and steamboats. Of all the things made in town, the corncob pipe was the most distinctive. Tibbe, the woodworker who started it all, patented his method of fireproofing the pipes in 1878 by applying a plaster-like substance to the outside of the cob. In 1883, he and his son Anton filed for trademark protection, and in 1907 the business became the Missouri Meerschaum Company. Other local names made their mark too. James L. Miller Sr. founded the Washington Missourian newspaper and was inducted into the Missouri Press Association's Hall of Fame, and Dr. Mary Hancock McLean became the first woman admitted to the St. Louis Medical Society. Washington's manufacturing is still strong, although it is not as vibrant as it was decades ago.

## Old Buildings and a New Bridge

What remains from all those eras is a remarkable stock of old buildings. Washington has hundreds of buildings listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and their historic charm has helped build a growing heritage tourism industry, with visitors also drawn to the nearby Missouri Rhineland. The river still shapes the town. For decades, the Washington Bridge, which opened in 1936, was the only bridge spanning the Missouri River in Franklin County. A new, wider bridge opened to traffic on December 3, 2018, and the original was demolished on April 11, 2019. The town holds a modern footnote as well: in 1988, the nation's first Walmart Supercenter opened here. Visitors who come to walk the old streets find a climate of humid springs and summers, when most of the year's precipitation falls, and generally drier autumns and winters, where snow is not rare but is less frequent than in the upper Midwest.

## Flight Context

Washington lies at approximately 38.5519° N, 91.0133° W, on the south bank of the Missouri River about 50 miles west of St. Louis. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, the town is easy to identify by its riverfront grid facing the Missouri and the highway bridge crossing to the north bank. Washington Regional Airport (KFYG) lies just across the river, about 4 km to the north-northeast, Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) is about 34 km to the east-northeast, and Hermann Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 63M, no ICAO code) is about 45 km to the west-northwest. Watch for river fog on calm mornings and for St. Louis terminal airspace to the east.
