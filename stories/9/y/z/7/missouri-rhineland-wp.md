# Missouri Rhineland

After a German lawyer praised the Missouri valley as a better Rhineland, German immigrants planted vineyards on the steep slopes of the Missouri River valley, built a wine industry that Prohibition wiped out, and saw it reborn with the nation's first American Viticultural Area.

**Location:** 38.6000, -91.0000

**Categories:** Missouri Rhineland, Missouri wine, German-American culture in Missouri, Regions of Missouri, Immigration history

## Introduction

In 1829, a German attorney named Gottfried Duden published a book that would reshape a stretch of the American Midwest. Titled Bericht über eine Reise nach den westlichen Staaten Nordamerikas, or Journal of a Trip to the Western States of North America, it described the Missouri River valley as a better Rhineland. German settlers soon followed. Within a few decades, the valley from west of St. Louis to just east of Jefferson City had become the Missouri Rhineland, a German American cultural region of river towns, farms and hillside vineyards on both banks of the Missouri.

## A Promise on Lake Creek

Duden had seen the place for himself. He first visited land along Lake Creek, on the north side of the Missouri River, in 1824, and bought property there while studying whether his countrymen could settle the area. In 1827 he returned to the Rhineland, which he felt was overpopulated, and there wrote his book extolling the Missouri valley. Rhinelanders who settled here noticed the same resemblance in the soil and the lay of the land, which reminded them of the wine-growing country along the Rhine, and they gave the region its name. It overlaps with Little Dixie, a cultural region of central Missouri originally populated by "Old Stock" settlers from Kentucky, Virginia and Tennessee.

## Societies Crossing the Ocean

Germans often came in organized groups. In 1832, members of the small Berlin Society communally bought the land that became Dutzow, one of the first German settlements in Missouri, founded by the "Baron" Johann Wilhelm von Bock. In 1834, the Giessen Emigration Society arrived under Friedrich Muench and Paul Follenius. Two years later, the German Settlement Society began searching for a place to build a German community insulated from the increasing mix of nationalities in many American settlements. It chose Hermann, and the first settlers arrived in 1837 by way of Philadelphia. George Bayer, an early leader, followed in 1838. The hillsides around Hermann were poorly suited to most crops but ideal for grapes, so the town's trustees sold tracts on the condition that they be planted as vineyards.

## Limestone, Chert and Wine

The ground explains the vines. Soils in the Missouri River valley are mainly rocky residual soils, left behind as limestone and other carbonate bedrock weathered away into clay and chert fragments. The steep slopes could support other crops, but they were better used for viticulture, and German settlers established the first wineries in the mid-19th century. Friedrich Muench became known for his expertise in grape growing and winemaking. Writing often under the name "Far West", he published his vineyard manual Amerikanische Weinbauschule, on American grape culture, in 1859, the same year his brother George moved to Augusta, where the family's Mount Pleasant vineyards and winery later grew. Italian immigrants later planted vineyards of their own, especially near Rolla in Phelps County. By the mid-1880s, Missouri made more wine by volume than any other state; it later slipped to second, and by 1920 it had more than 100 wineries.

## Silenced Presses, Empty Vineyards

Then came two blows in quick succession. Anti-German sentiment rose with World War I, and the Trading with the Enemy Act of 1917 led to the closure of several Missouri German-language newspapers, among them the Osage County Volksblatt and the Sedalia Journal. Missouri communities caught up in wartime feeling tried to outlaw German and campaigned to replace "offensive-sounding" German street names with American ones. Prohibition followed and ruined the wine industry. Vineyards were pulled up or left untended, and winery buildings were converted to other uses or left to decay. In Missouri, just one winery was allowed to keep operating: Saint Stanislaus Seminary in Florissant, which made sacramental wine.

## The First Appellation

Local winemakers began to rebuild in the 1960s, part of a movement across the country. In 1980, the federal government designated an area around Augusta, Missouri, as the first American Viticultural Area, and an area around Hermann became an AVA in 1983. The Hermann AVA now lies within the larger Ozark Mountain AVA, which covers southern Missouri and is adjacent to the Augusta AVA. The German heritage has not disappeared either. Hermann German is a form of Rhenish German, and on some German settlements and German American farms in the region, German is still spoken today.

## Flight Context

The Missouri Rhineland is a region rather than a point; the reference coordinates of 38.6° N, 91.0° W fall near the town of Washington, and the region runs along the Missouri River valley from west of St. Louis to just east of Jefferson City. From 2,500–4,000 feet AGL, follow the river westward and look for the bluffs and wooded hillsides with vineyard rows, and for the river towns of Augusta, Washington and Hermann on or near its banks. Washington Regional Airport (KFYG) sits in the heart of the region just north of Washington, Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) is about 31 km to the east, and Hermann Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 63M, no ICAO code) lies about 44 km to the west. River valley fog is common on calm mornings, especially in autumn.
