# Koster Site

Beneath a Greene County cornfield, thirty feet of Illinois River valley soil held 25 separate human occupations spanning more than 8,000 years, along with one of eastern North America's oldest cemeteries and the oldest known dog burials in the Americas.

**Location:** 39.2092, -90.5492

**Categories:** Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois, Geography of Greene County, Illinois, Archaic period in North America, National Register of Historic Places in Greene County, Illinois

## Introduction

About 10,000 years ago, people in the Illinois River valley buried their dogs. Three of those graves lay at the Koster Site, south of Eldred, Illinois, and together with a fourth found roughly 30 kilometers away at the Stilwell II site in Pike County, they are the oldest dog remains discovered anywhere in the Americas. They are only one layer of the story. Beneath a farm field here, the earth held evidence of people coming back to the same ground again and again, across more than eight thousand years.

## A Tip From a Farmer

The discovery began with a conversation. In 1967 Stuart Struever, an anthropologist at Northwestern University, followed a tip from a local farmer and found his way to the fields of Theodore and Mary Koster. Beneath their cornfields lay a rich trove of historically significant artifacts, and the site was named after the couple. Struever had recently founded the Center for American Archeology in nearby Kampsville, and the Koster farm would become the organization's defining work. Excavation began in 1969 and continued until 1978, and under Struever's leadership Koster grew into one of the largest excavations of its era. It was also a public spectacle in the best sense, drawing more than 10,000 visitors a year to watch the past come up out of the ground.

## Thirty Feet of Time

What made Koster extraordinary was its depth. The site covers more than 3 acres and extends 30 feet down into the alluvial deposits of the Illinois River valley. Over thousands of years, the river valley kept laying down fresh soil, and each time people returned to live here, their traces were sealed beneath the next layer. Excavators identified 25 distinct occupations, each separated from the next by layers of soil, like pages in a book that had been pressed shut. The oldest reached back to the early Archaic period, roughly 9,000 to 10,000 years ago, and the youngest to the Mississippian period, around AD 1000. Because the layers were kept apart, the site was exceptionally well preserved, letting archaeologists read changes in daily life across an immense span of time, one horizon at a time.

## Villages, Not Wanderers

Koster changed how archaeologists pictured the valley's early inhabitants. Excavators found remains of permanent residences, along with belongings that could not easily be carried from place to place, evidence that this was a large, permanent village. A wide variety of stone tools, made for many different tasks, pointed to the same conclusion of long-term living here. The site also yielded early evidence of North Americans using stones to grind food and keeping domesticated dogs. Together, these findings helped shift the picture of the region's early people from nomads constantly on the move to a settled community living in year-round structures. They were neighbors to one another, generation after generation, in a place they chose to stay.

## The Oldest Graves

The people of Koster cared for their dead, too. The site includes one of the oldest known cemeteries in eastern North America, and it has given researchers evidence that Early Archaic communities followed specific burial practices, carefully positioning their dead in prepared oval pits, and later Middle Archaic cemeteries show differences in treatment by age and sex. These were people, not specimens, and the care they took with their dead is written into the soil. Recognition followed the excavation's early seasons: on June 19, 1972, the Koster Site was added to the National Register of Historic Places. The Center for American Archeology considers it the most important discovery in its history, and the three dogs in their graves remain one of its most touching finds, evidence that the bond between people and dogs in this valley reaches back nearly ten thousand years.

## Flight Context

The Koster Site lies at about 39.209°N, 90.549°W in Greene County, Illinois, on the floor of the lower Illinois River valley just south of the village of Eldred and a few miles downstream of Kampsville. The site itself is hard to pick out from the air, so orient from 2,000-3,000 feet AGL on the broad, flat river bottoms and the wooded bluffs that frame the valley, with the Illinois River itself as the main line feature. The nearest larger airports are St. Louis Regional (KALN) at Alton, about 30 nm to the southeast, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL), about 29 nm to the south-southeast. Watch for river fog in the valley on calm mornings.
