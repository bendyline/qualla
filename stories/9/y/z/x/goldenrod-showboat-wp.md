# Goldenrod (showboat)

The last showboat to work the Mississippi outlived every rival, a devastating fire and a string of owners, only to end her century as a burned hull on the Illinois River and a landmark struck from the national list.

**Location:** 39.3408, -90.6192

**Categories:** Showboats, 1909 ships, Former National Historic Landmarks of the United States, Landmarks of St. Louis, National Register of Historic Places in Calhoun County, Illinois

## Introduction

For 75 cents a head, St. Louis playgoers around 1950 could board a floating theater and "sass the actors" on stage. The boat was Goldenrod, and by then she was already the last of her kind: a showboat, a floating entertainment palace that had outlasted every rival on the Mississippi. She would go on to survive fire, sinkings, low water and a succession of owners who found her costly to keep. In October 2017, after a long decline on the rivers north of St. Louis, she burned to her hull and was sold for scrap.

## Twenty-Two Boats, Then One

In 1910, twenty-two showboats plied the Mississippi, visiting 15 midwestern states. Most were non-powered barges with entertainment palaces built on top. By 1928 their number had dwindled to twelve, by 1938 only four remained in operation, and by 1943 only Goldenrod was left. Built in the "Steamboat Gothic" style, she was one of the largest and most elaborately decorated of the showboats, offering vaudeville and serious drama. Under the ownership of Capt. Bill Menke she was moored at the St. Louis riverfront in 1937. In 1947 her original wooden hull was set into a steel barge at the St. Louis Shipbuilding & Steel Company, and by 1950 she had been partially sunk and salvaged twice. The shows went on. Red Skelton, Pearl Bailey, Cab Calloway and Bob Hope all performed aboard, and Goldenrod is said to have been an inspiration for Edna Ferber's novel Show Boat, although Ferber's own research was done aboard the James Adams Floating Theatre in North Carolina in 1925.

## Beneath the Crystal Chandelier

On June 1, 1962, a fire caused by an electrical short all but destroyed the superstructure of the auditorium and severely damaged the rest of the boat. A group of St. Louis businessmen headed by Frank C. Pierson and Don Franz bought her and restored her to her original glory and beyond. Plush carpet went down in the auditorium, with cabaret seating under a huge crystal chandelier. Many antique appointments were salvaged from old St. Louis mansions being torn down, and the brass fixtures, rails, tin ceiling and elaborate woodwork were restored or replaced. A cocktail lounge gained a small bandstand, and the upstairs staterooms became a buffet dining room. After the $300,000 renovation, Goldenrod celebrated her Grand Re-Opening in May 1965. She was designated a National Historic Landmark on December 24, 1967, and a St. Louis City Landmark in 1972. Pierson also owned the Becky Thatcher, a former packet boat moored beside her, with a restaurant, lounges and a gift shop.

## Ragtime on the Levee

For many years, the St. Louis Ragtimers played traditional ragtime from that little bandstand, and the music became part of the boat's identity. From the early 1960s to about 1985, the National Ragtime Festival was held aboard Goldenrod every June, featuring vintage jazz and ragtime bands including Turk Murphy and The Salty Dogs. From 1975 to 1984 she also operated as a sister theater to the Heritage Square Opera House in Golden, Colorado, presenting melodrama plus a vaudeville olio in a high-energy format created by G. William Oakley of Denver. During those same years, the festivals Oakley produced became an annual phenomenon on the St. Louis riverfront.

## A Gift Nobody Could Keep

In 1989 the city of St. Charles bought Goldenrod for $300,000 and moved her to the historic Missouri River town, where she ran as a popular dinner theater. Restoration and renovation cost the city about $3.5 million over the next 12 years. Then, in 2001, low water on the Missouri left her aground, and she was closed because of Coast Guard structural repair requirements. That same year she was placed on the "Threatened Historical Landmarks" list. The repair estimates came in far higher than expected, and in 2002 the council decided to sell. When no one offered to buy her, the city gave her away. Four groups submitted proposals, and the winner moved her to storage near the Poplar Street Bridge in downtown St. Louis and later to a mooring on the Illinois River near Kampsville. A planned protective basin was never built. A dispute over unpaid mooring fees ended in a sheriff's auction in October 2007, where the showboat sold for $5,000 with no other bids.

## The Final Curtain

The rivers were not kind to her in the end. In 2015, river levels dropped suddenly while she was being moved to the bank, and her hull buckled on uneven ground. Lacking the funds to save the whole boat, the Historic Riverboat Preservation Association decided to rescue the pilothouse and as much of the interior furnishings as possible for museum displays. On April 1, 2016, the vessel was given to the owners of the dock where she was moored. In May 2017 she stuck on a sandbar and took on about 7 feet of water as the river rose, and on October 21, 2017, she burned. In August 2023 the National Park System Advisory Board recommended withdrawing her landmark status because she had lost her historical integrity, and the designation was withdrawn that December.

## Flight Context

The coordinates recorded for Goldenrod, approximately 39.34 N, 90.62 W, place her on the Illinois River in Calhoun County, Illinois, a few miles north of Kampsville, where she spent her last years moored. Nothing of the showboat remains to be seen, but the river valley itself is a fine sight from 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL: the Illinois River runs south toward its confluence with the Mississippi at Grafton, and Kampsville sits on the river just downstream. The nearest public-use fields are Pittsfield Penstone Municipal Airport (KPPQ), about 19 nm north-northwest, Jacksonville Municipal Airport (KIJX), about 31 nm northeast, and St. Charles County Smartt Airport (KSET), about 26 nm south-southeast. The area is rural with few lights at night; expect river fog on calm mornings.
