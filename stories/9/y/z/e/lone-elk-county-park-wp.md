# Lone Elk County Park

West of St. Louis, the elk and bison herds were destroyed in 1958 and a single bull elk survived; today Lone Elk Park lets visitors drive among elk and bison on land that once served a wartime powder plant.

**Location:** 38.5325, -90.5414

**Categories:** County parks in the United States, Protected areas established in 1964, Protected areas of St. Louis County, Missouri, 1964 establishments in Missouri, Tourist attractions in St. Louis, Parks in St. Louis County, Missouri

## Introduction

In 1958 the elk and bison herds in these wooded hills were destroyed, for safety reasons, on land the federal government had taken back during the Korean War. One bull elk survived. Eight years later, the ground where he had outlasted his herd carried a new name: Lone Elk Park. Today this 546-acre county park west of Valley Park, Missouri, is a place where you can roll along a park road and find elk and bison close enough to study through the windshield.

## From Powder Plant to Pasture

These quiet slopes once served a war. During World War II the land was part of the Tyson Valley Powder Plant, and it still sits beside Interstate 44 within a cluster of protected places: the World Bird Sanctuary, Castlewood State Park and the Tyson Research Center all lie next door. When the war ended, the area became a county park. In 1948 herds of elk and bison were established here, large grazing animals settling onto ground that had so recently belonged to a wartime powder plant. For a few years, the conversion from military land to wildlife park looked complete.

## The Survivor

It was not. During the Korean War the federal government reacquired the land, and in 1958 the wildlife herds were destroyed for safety reasons. Only one animal is recorded as surviving, a lone bull elk. St. Louis County returned for the land in 1964, acquiring 405 acres for what it called Tyson County Park. In 1966 the park became Lone Elk Park, a name that still echoes that solitary survivor. The official opening came on October 17, 1971. Two summers later, in July 1973, six bison arrived from the St. Louis Zoo, and the big shaggy animals became part of the landscape once again.

## Close Encounters from the Car

Most visitors meet the park's residents from its driving routes, which wind past elk and bison, often at close proximity. The large animals share the hills with many deer, wild turkeys and waterfowl, so a slow drive can turn up a bird on the wing as easily as a bull elk in the trees. One rule here is absolute: domestic animals are not permitted at all, not even if they stay shut inside a vehicle. The park's lake has had troubles of its own. In 2016 research was carried out to determine whether a sinkhole lay beneath it, the boat dock has since been removed, and fishing was closed in 2017 in an effort to rebuild the fish population. The herds that browse these hills now are newer arrivals, but the name on the map still remembers the one that made it through.

## Flight Context

Lone Elk County Park lies at 38.533N, 90.541W in west St. Louis County, Missouri, just west of Valley Park and alongside the Interstate 44 corridor. From 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for a block of wooded hills and open grassy clearings bordering the highway, with the neighboring Castlewood State Park and the Tyson Research Center forming a larger green belt around it. Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) is about 11 miles to the northwest, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 18 miles to the northeast; plan around the Lambert Class B airspace when approaching from the east. Morning and evening light brings out the contrast between forest and clearing best.
