# Times Beach, Missouri

Born as a newspaper subscription giveaway on the Meramec River, Times Beach became a town of two thousand people and then a ghost town, bought out and bulldozed after its dirt roads were found to be laced with dioxin.

**Location:** 38.5086, -90.6025

**Categories:** Environmental disaster ghost towns, Ghost towns in Missouri, Superfund sites in Missouri, Ghost towns on U.S. Route 66, Disasters in Missouri, Environment of Missouri

## Introduction

In 1925, a six-month subscription to the St. Louis Star-Times cost $67.50, and it came with a piece of land: a lot 20 by 100 feet on former farmland along the Meramec River, about 17 miles southwest of St. Louis. That promotion created Times Beach. Six decades later, the town that more than two thousand people called home was gone, its residents bought out, its buildings demolished, and its name attached to what has been called the largest civilian exposure to the toxic compound TCDD, a type of dioxin, in the history of the United States. Today the land is a state park, and the old street grid is only faintly visible from above.

## Summer Cottages, Dirt Roads

Times Beach began as a summer resort on the Meramec floodplain. The Great Depression and gasoline rationing during World War II made summer homes impractical, and over the following decades the town became a place of mostly low-income housing, prone to chronic flooding. Still, it grew. By 1970 Times Beach had more than 1,200 residents, and by 1980 it had 2,041. What it lacked was pavement. The town's 23 miles of roads were dirt, and the way the town chose to treat them would decide its fate.

## Still Bottoms From Verona

The oil came with a hidden history. In the late 1960s, the Northeastern Pharmaceutical and Chemical Company, or NEPACCO, began operating from a facility near Verona, in southwestern Missouri, owned by Hoffman-Taff, a company that produced the Agent Orange herbicide for the Vietnam War. NEPACCO's chemical process yielded trace amounts of TCDD, an extremely toxic compound, and left behind heavily concentrated dioxin residues called still bottoms. At first these were sent to Louisiana for incineration. Then, between February and October 1971, waste oil hauler Russell Bliss collected six truckloads, nearly 18,500 gallons, of the heavily contaminated waste. On May 26, 1971, Judy Piatt and Frank Hampel, owners of Shenandoah Stable, paid Bliss $150 to spray the floor of their indoor arena. Within days, birds began dropping dead from the rafters, and horses developed sores and lost their hair. Bliss denied responsibility, saying the material was nothing more than old motor oil.

## Oil on Twenty-Three Miles

In 1972, Times Beach hired Bliss to oil its roads. For $2,400, he sprayed approximately 160,000 gallons of waste oil across the town over a period of four years. The EPA did not become heavily involved in Missouri's dioxin contamination until 1979, when a former NEPACCO employee reported toxic waste buried on a farm about seven miles from Verona; investigators unearthed 90 corroded, leaking drums, eleven holding still bottoms with dioxin concentrations as high as 2,000 parts per million. In Times Beach, EPA soil sampling completed on December 3, 1982, found dioxin as high as 0.3 parts per million along the town's entire network of roads. The next day, the Meramec River rose more than 14 feet above flood stage. On December 23, the Centers for Disease Control publicly recommended that Times Beach not be re-inhabited.

## A Town Unmade

On February 22, 1983, the EPA announced that the federal government would use $33.1 million in Superfund money to buy out Times Beach's 800 residential properties and 30 businesses. For the families who lived there, it meant leaving homes, neighbors and a whole community behind. The town was evacuated by 1985, and on April 2 of that year Missouri Governor John Ashcroft disincorporated it by executive order. By 1992 it had been completely demolished. Because no one lived there any longer, Times Beach became the site of a new incinerator, built starting in June 1995, that burned more than 265,000 tons of contaminated material from across the state. The statewide cleanup was completed in 1997 at a reported cost of well over $100 million. More than 14,000 citizens' suits were filed against NEPACCO and its officers, Syntex Agribusiness, IPC and Bliss, who maintained that he had not known the waste contained dioxin.

## Route 66 Revisited

The health risks themselves became a subject of debate. Several months after the evacuation, the American Medical Association publicly criticized the news media for spreading unscientific information about dioxin, stating there was no evidence of adverse consequences from low-level exposure. In 1991 Vernon Houk, a senior CDC official, said the agency had overreacted by evacuating the population of Times Beach, one voice in a long argument over how dangerous the exposure really was. The land itself has found a new purpose. In 1999, a state park of roughly 400 acres opened on the site of the vanished town, and when the EPA retested its soil in June 2012, it reported that November that samples showed no significant health risks for park visitors or workers. Of the town itself, little remains beyond the faint outline of its old streets.

## Flight Context

The site of Times Beach, now Route 66 State Park, lies at approximately 38.5086° N, 90.6025° W, on the Meramec River floodplain about 2 miles east of Eureka and 17 miles southwest of St. Louis, beside Interstate 44. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, follow the winding Meramec and look for open parkland beside the river, where the grid of the old town's roads remains faintly visible. Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) is about 18 km to the north-northwest, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) about 34 km to the northeast. Mind the St. Louis terminal airspace to the northeast, and expect morning fog along the river.
