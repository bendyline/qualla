# Big Chief Restaurant

Opened in the late 1920s as the dining room of a 62-unit Mission Revival tourist camp on brand-new Route 66, the Big Chief in Wildwood, Missouri, outlived a highway reroute, wartime boarders from a munitions plant and decades of closure to serve meals again as the Big Chief Roadhouse.

## Introduction

"There is no driving back and forth in the night." So a 1928 newspaper in Pacific, Missouri, described the rules at the new Big Chief tourist camp on U.S. Route 66. A locked gate kept out everyone but paying guests, and, apparently, kept the guests in. Each of the camp's 62 units came with its own garage, and at the center of it all stood a two-story restaurant with a bell tower. Nearly a century later that restaurant still stands at 17352 Manchester Road in Wildwood, a survivor of the earliest days of the Mother Road.

## A Chain Planned for the Whole Road

Route 66 made it possible to drive from Chicago to Santa Monica, California, on a single highway, and as Americans set out to explore the country on the new thoroughfare, restaurants and lodgings sprang up to meet them. Once it was known that the road would pass through the community of Pond, now part of Wildwood, plans were drawn up for the Big Chief Highway Hotel complex. The ambitions ran much larger than one camp. A 1929 article in the Washington Citizen credited Walter H. Petring of the Highway Hotel Corporation in St. Louis with planning a series of tourist camps along the route, "one day's drive apart," stretching the length of Route 66. Later accounts gave the credit instead to William Clay Pierce of the Texas-based Pierce-Pennant Oil Company. Whoever dreamed it up, the National Park Service found no evidence that the chain was ever built.

## Mission Revival Comfort

What did get built was impressive enough. The 62-unit camp opened in 1928 or 1929, depending on the source, in the Mission Revival style, at an estimated cost of $150,000 to $200,000. Its restaurant was no roadside lunch counter. The two-story building with its bell tower was a full-service establishment, more formal than the small diner-style eateries usually found at tourist camps, serving lunch and dinner and offering space for bridge parties and banquets. Beyond the standard amenities of the day, every unit had a garage for the traveler's car. A full-service gas station operated on the premises, and there were recreational facilities for both children and adults, including a concrete area set aside as an open-air dance floor.

## Bypassed and Pressed Into Service

The Big Chief's fortunes were tied to the highway, and when Route 66 was rerouted in 1932 to bypass Pond, business began to sag. The complex adapted to the times. During World War II it housed employees of the Weldon Spring Ordnance Works, which manufactured explosives for the military, and it later provided housing for workers at the Weldon Spring uranium processing plant. Instead of tourists pulling in for a night on their way west, the cabins held workers from the Weldon Spring plants. The boarders kept the property in use, but the restaurant itself had closed by 1949.

## The Long Road Back

After the closure, Marco and Rose Aceto owned the property. They removed the bell tower and used the restaurant building for various business ventures. They had intended to keep it running as a restaurant, but that plan never materialized, and the cabins were eventually demolished. Only the restaurant building remained when G. Todd DeVille bought the property in the 1990s, and it reopened as a restaurant that same decade. Over the years it has carried several names, from Big Chief Highway Hotel to Big Chief Dakota Grill to Big Chief Roadhouse. Now listed on the National Register of Historic Places, it remains one of the tangible links to the early days of Route 66 in the St. Louis area.

## Flight Context

The Big Chief Restaurant stands at approximately 38.5811° N, 90.6605° W on Manchester Road in Wildwood, Missouri, in the former community of Pond in far western St. Louis County. From 2,000 to 2,500 feet above ground level, follow Manchester Road, the old Route 66 alignment, through the wooded, rolling suburbs of Wildwood; Interstate 44 lies to the south, and the Missouri River valley lies to the north. Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) in Chesterfield is only a few miles to the north, making the area easy to reach, and Washington Regional Airport (KFYG) is to the west. The St. Louis Class B airspace begins to the east, so plan altitudes carefully. Castlewood State Park and the Meramec River valley lie several miles to the east-southeast. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
