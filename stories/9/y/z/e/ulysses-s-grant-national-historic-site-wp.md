# Ulysses S. Grant National Historic Site

At White Haven, the Dent family farm where he courted Julia, the future Union general spent five pre-war years managing a plantation worked by enslaved people, and the National Park Service now tells that story plainly.

**Location:** 38.5511, -90.3519

## Introduction

In the spring of 1844, a young Army lieutenant proposed marriage on the front porch of a farmhouse in St. Louis County. The farm was White Haven, it belonged to Colonel Frederick Dent, and the lieutenant was Ulysses S. Grant. The house still stands on 9.65 acres in Grantwood Village, southwest of downtown St. Louis, where the National Park Service preserves five historic structures from the old estate, among them the childhood home of Julia Dent Grant. The site honors Grant's life, military career and presidency. It is also frank about something less comfortable: before he became a Union general, Grant spent five years running a plantation worked by enslaved people.

## A Roommate's Invitation

Grant came to White Haven through a friendship. Frederick T. Dent, Julia's brother, had been his roommate at West Point, and he invited Grant to visit the family home. Grant was then stationed at Jefferson Barracks, a military post five miles south of the farm. Julia, born in St. Louis on January 26, 1826, had grown up on a place where many of her childhood playmates were enslaved children. She and the lieutenant took walks and rode horses together, and the proposal on the porch followed. The wedding had to wait. War with Mexico intervened, and the couple did not marry until August 22, 1848.

## Five Years Behind the Plow

In 1854, after two and a half years away from his family, Captain Grant resigned his commission and came home to St. Louis. From 1854 to 1859 he farmed White Haven for his father-in-law. The property was then an 850-acre plantation, and Grant worked alongside, and from 1857 directed, the forced labor of the people Colonel Dent enslaved. Beginning in 1855 he also built a log house he called Hardscrabble on 80 acres north of White Haven, felling oak and hickory there and hauling cords of firewood into the city to sell. Two more children arrived during these years: Ellen, born on July 4, 1855, and Jesse, in February 1858. Then the financial panic of 1857 and bad weather that ruined many farmers' crops sent Grant looking for other income, and for a short time he worked in St. Louis in real estate and as a customs house clerk.

## Slavery at the Edge of the City

Slavery in Missouri took many shapes. Most slaveholders in the state enslaved only a few people, and owning ten was a mark of wealth. Large single-crop plantations were concentrated in the southeastern Bootheel and along the Missouri River valley known as Little Dixie. In St. Louis itself the system was less entrenched: the city's African American population fell from 25 percent in 1830 to 2 percent in 1860. Enslaved people in the city were often hired out by their enslavers for an agreed wage, a portion of which sometimes reached the worker. In rare cases, that money let them buy their freedom. At White Haven, individual lives surface in the record. Grant acquired ownership of an enslaved man named William Jones from his father-in-law, and in 1859 he freed Jones at the St. Louis courthouse. Jule, a woman enslaved by Colonel Dent, helped care for the Grant children on their travels until early 1864, when she ran away to freedom.

## From Galena Back to White Haven

In the spring of 1860, Ulysses, Julia and their four children left for Galena, Illinois, where he went to work with his brothers selling leather goods made in his father's tannery. Within a few years he would be a Union general and, later, President of the United States. The years at White Haven may have shaped how he approached both roles. After the Civil War, Grant bought the property himself. White Haven was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979 and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1986. In 1989 it became part of the National Park Service, one of more than 400 sites the agency manages.

## Flight Context

White Haven sits at 38.551N, 90.352W in Grantwood Village, south St. Louis County, Missouri. The site is small, just under 10 acres, so fly at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL and look for a pocket of lawn and trees among the surrounding suburbs; Jefferson Barracks lies about six miles to the southeast, and the Mississippi River runs to the east. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 14 miles north, and its Class B airspace covers much of the metro area. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) in Cahokia, Illinois, is about 11 miles east across the river, and Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) is about 18 miles west-northwest. Summer haze and thunderstorms can cut visibility over the river valley.
