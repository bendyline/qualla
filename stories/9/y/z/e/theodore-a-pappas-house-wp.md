# Theodore A. Pappas House

Theodore and Bette Pappas spent four years building their Frank Lloyd Wright house by hand, block by concrete block, creating Missouri's only Usonian Automatic and a landmark listed on the National Register when it was just fifteen years old.

**Location:** 38.6369, -90.4842

**Categories:** Frank Lloyd Wright buildings, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Houses completed in 1964, Houses in St. Louis, National Register of Historic Places in St. Louis County, Missouri

## Introduction

Theodore and Bette Pappas did not simply commission a Frank Lloyd Wright house. They built one. Between 1960 and 1964, working alongside day laborers, the couple raised a Wright design in St. Louis County out of plain concrete blocks, setting them in place without mortar and knitting them together with steel. Wright never saw the result. He had drawn the plans at their request between 1955 and 1959, and he died in April 1959, before construction began. What the Pappases finished is the only Usonian Automatic in Missouri, and it was striking enough to be listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979, when the house was just fifteen years old.

## A House in a Kit

The Usonian Automatic rested on a radical premise. The entire body of the Pappas House, roof included, is made of plain concrete blocks standardized to a module and placed on top of and beside one another with no mortar. The hidden side of each block has hollows, and steel rods were threaded through them both vertically and horizontally, creating a "knit" effect through the walls. Once the rods were in place, grout filled the hollows. In theory, every element of such a house could be manufactured and sent to its owners as a kind of do-it-yourself kit, and that was the "automatic" part of the name. The owners, not a construction company, would become the builders.

## Built by Hand

In practice, the kit needed improvising. Metal molds for the blocks were not available, so the blocks had to be cast in molds created by a local craftsman. From there the work fell to Theodore and Bette themselves, with day laborers helping them over four years of construction. The house that emerged is a rambling four-bedroom home, and among the Usonian Automatics that were actually built, only the Gerald B. and Beverley Tonkens House is larger. It is also one of just two houses in St. Louis designed by Wright. A system designed around simplicity produced, in this case, a house of real size and presence.

## Color In, Not On

Wright held that color should be in a material, not painted on its surface, so the concrete here was pretinted before it was cast. The result is a monochromatic interior in a warm, natural, earthen tone that runs through every wall. Against it, the trim and built-in furniture are made from uniform, unstained Philippine mahogany, so wood and block carry the whole palette between them. The limited scheme gives the rooms a calm consistency, as if the house had been carved from a single piece of earth rather than assembled from modular parts.

## No Passing Fancy

The house stayed in the Pappas family for more than half a century. In 1985 Bette Pappas wrote a book about the house, titled No Passing Fancy, and she remained there for the rest of her life, dying at the home in February 2018 at the age of 91. Two years later, in 2020, the Pappas children sold the house to the Frank Lloyd Wright Revival Initiative, a non-profit created by filmmaker Michael Miner, with the expectation that it would be refurbished and turned into a museum and event center. In May 2021 the house opened for public tours by appointment, a chance to step inside the rooms that a husband and wife once built with their own hands. Tour availability changes, so check with the Frank Lloyd Wright Revival Initiative before planning a visit.

## Flight Context

The Pappas House sits at 38.637N, 90.484W in west St. Louis County, Missouri, in a wooded residential area where the low, earth-toned house is hard to pick out from the air. Use Saint Louis Abbey as a reference: its circular white church with stacked concrete arches lies only about half a mile to the north-northeast and is far easier to spot from 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 10 miles to the northeast, and the area lies beneath the Lambert Class B airspace. Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) is roughly 9 miles to the west. Leaf-off months in late autumn and winter give the best chance of glimpsing the house through the trees.
