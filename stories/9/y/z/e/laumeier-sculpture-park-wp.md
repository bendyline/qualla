# Laumeier Sculpture Park

A widow's wish that her country estate never become ballfields grew into a roughly 100-acre open-air museum near St. Louis, where more than 70 large sculptures stand among lawns and woods.

**Location:** 38.5510, -90.4120

**Categories:** Sculpture gardens, trails and parks in Missouri, Art museums and galleries in Missouri, Culture of St. Louis, Parks in St. Louis County, Missouri, Public art

## Introduction

Eighteen salvaged steel oil tanks, painted cadmium red, rise 65 feet above a lawn in Sunset Hills, Missouri. Alexander Liberman completed The Way in 1980, and at 102 feet wide, 100 feet deep and 55 short tons, it is often cited as the signature work of Laumeier Sculpture Park. It is far from alone. This open-air museum of roughly 100 acres near St. Louis holds more than 70 large-scale outdoor sculptures along a 1.4-mile walking trail, and it exists because one woman, asked what should become of her country estate, said she did not want playing fields.

## The Stove Maker's Grandson

The first piece of the land was a tract of 47.67 acres on Rott Road, owned by Joseph Griesedieck of Falstaff Brewing. In September 1916 he sold it to Roland L. Kahle, whose grandfather, George August Kahle, was apparently one of the founders of the American Stove Company, the firm now known as Magic Chef. In April 1917, Kahle obtained a permit for a stone house, adding a stone garage in 1931 and a gatehouse in 1936. He listed himself as owner, builder and architect, though one account credits the design to Ernst C. Janssen, whose practice of more than sixty years centered on the German-American families of St. Louis' south side. Brewery buildings were the mainstay of Janssen's work, and his residential style has been nicknamed "Brewer's Baronial". Among his earlier commissions was the 12,000-square-foot Magic Chef Mansion, built in 1908 for American Stove Company co-founder Charles Stockstrom.

## Matilda's Condition

Kahle died in 1938, and in 1940 his widow, the former Ada B. Riegel, sold the property to Henry Laumeier. He married Matilda Cramer in 1941, and the couple restored and modified the house, glazing its large south porch and expanding the estate. After Henry's death in 1959, Wayne C. Kennedy, director of the St. Louis County parks department, was searching the shrinking rural land around the county for acreage to add to the park system. Laumeier's nieces urged him to talk with Matilda, and in 1963 he did. She liked the idea of a park, but not one with playing fields. She wanted uses that kept the character of the landscape, perhaps a formal garden or a conservatory, and plantings that suited the specimen trees she and Henry had placed across the lawns and meadows. When she died, she left the grounds and the seven-room house to the county in Henry's memory, on the condition that they be used for passive purposes.

## A Swimming Pool Turned Sculpture

The estate soon became a popular attraction, and the Friends of Laumeier added 20 more acres, mostly woods, reserved for site-specific sculptures. One of them began as an abandoned Depression-era concrete pool left from the Orchard Valley estate that once occupied the land. Artist Mary Miss transformed it into Pool Complex: Orchard Valley, built between 1983 and 1985. The park's founding executive director, Dr. Beej Nierengarten-Smith, led the nonprofit for 22 years, from 1979 to 2001. Under her direction it won six operating grants and two conservation grants from the Institute of Museum and Library Services and 22 grants from the National Endowment for the Arts, built an endowment worth $2 million by 2000, and grew its operating budget from an initial $30,000 from the county to more than a million dollars. Half of the featured artists were women. Attendance rose from several hundred people to a reported 500,000, and the park twice earned accreditation from the American Alliance of Museums.

## Minotaurs, Cats and Surveillance

Walking the grounds means meeting a roster of notable artists. The collection includes works by Mark di Suvero, Beverly Pepper, Donald Judd, Sol LeWitt, Jenny Holzer, Anthony Caro, Vito Acconci, Dan Graham and Jackie Ferrara. The titles alone suggest the range. Tony Rosenthal's House of the Minotaur dates from 1980, Niki de Saint Phalle's Ricardo Cat from 1999, and Donald Lipski contributed Ball? Ball! Wall? Wall! in 1994. Tea Mäkipää's Not Without My Dog arrived in 2011, and Ahmet Ögüt's 2009 piece carries the deadpan title This area is under 23 hour video and audio surveillance. The park has also featured national and international sculptors such as Terry Allen, Manuel Neri, Andy Goldsworthy, Judith Shea and Joyce J. Scott.

## An Old House, a New Purpose

In 2015, Laumeier closed its first major capital campaign, Sculpting the Future. The St. Louis firm Trivers designed the project in 2010, and it was completed in 2015. The 1917 estate house became the Kranzberg Education Lab, and a new Aronson Fine Arts Center opened for exhibitions, programs and events. Inside the Aronson are the Emerson Visitor Center, a gift shop stocked mostly with work by local artists, and the 3,280-square-foot Whitaker Foundation Gallery for temporary exhibitions. The Siteman Carriage House next to the old estate house holds curatorial offices and a collection library. Every Mother's Day weekend since 1987, the park has hosted the Laumeier Annual Art Fair. Today Laumeier is a nonprofit arts organization accredited by the American Alliance of Museums and run in partnership with St. Louis County Parks and Recreation, on land Matilda Laumeier gave on the condition that it serve passive purposes rather than sports fields.

## Flight Context

Laumeier Sculpture Park lies at approximately 38.5510° N, 90.4120° W, in Sunset Hills in southwestern St. Louis County. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, look for open lawns and woodland amid the suburban grid; Alexander Liberman's 65-foot, cadmium-red The Way is the sculpture most likely to stand out from above. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) lies about 22 km to the east across the Mississippi, St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) about 22 km to the north, and Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) about 24 km to the northwest. The park lies within the busy St. Louis terminal area, so coordinate with ATC.
