# Red Cedar Inn

Two brothers whose bootlegging business died with Prohibition built a log restaurant on Route 66 just after it reached Pacific, Missouri, and served legal cocktails to travelers and ballplayers for generations before the town bought it and turned it into a museum.

**Location:** 38.4817, -90.7177

**Categories:** U.S. Route 66, Restaurants in Missouri

## Introduction

The end of Prohibition put James and Bill Smith out of business. The brothers had made their living bootlegging liquor from the family farm at Villa Ridge, and when repeal came in 1933, that trade folded. Their answer was to go legitimate. On U.S. Route 66, which had only just reached Pacific, Missouri, they built a log restaurant that served cocktails openly, and the Red Cedar Inn went on to feed travelers on the Mother Road for decades.

## From Moonshine to the Main Road

Repeal did not end the Smiths' interest in the liquor business, only their need to hide it. After the bootlegging operation collapsed, James opened a tavern in Eureka and Bill opened one in Fenton. Around the same time, they built the Red Cedar Inn on Route 66, which had reached Pacific in 1932. The timing mattered. Before the highway arrived, Pacific's main commerce had been the mining of silica, used to make fine glassware and construction materials such as bricks. The new road brought a stream of travelers and gave the town an economic boost, and the Red Cedar Inn became a significant part of it. A full-service restaurant that could legally pour drinks, it proved so successful that the Smiths added a bar in 1935.

## Logs From the Family Farm

The brothers wanted their inn to look like Missouri's pioneer days, reasoning that a rustic building would draw tourists off the highway. The logs came from the brothers' own family farm. Inside, knotty pine lined the walls. Outside, lines of white chinking ran between the logs, giving the exterior a striped, hand-built look. It was a deliberate piece of theater: a roadside business built to look like a frontier cabin, aimed squarely at tourists. The approach worked, and the inn became popular with travelers on Route 66.

## A Family Business With a Baseball Following

Once the building was finished, the brothers handed management to James II. He later married one of the restaurant's waitresses, Katherine Brinkman, and in 1944 the couple bought the business from James I. They ran it as a family operation alongside their children, James III and Virginia, known as Ginger, until James II retired in 1972. Over those years some famous faces came through the door. Baseball players Bob Klinger, Dizzy Dean and Ted Williams all visited, a reminder that the road carried celebrities as well as vacationing families through this corner of Missouri.

## Closed Doors and a Second Life

After the family retired from the business, the inn was leased for a time and then stood shuttered until 1987. It carried on long enough to be listed on the National Register of Historic Places in April 2003, but the restaurant closed for good in 2005, with its owners citing rising insurance costs. For years the log building sat quiet. In 2017 the City of Pacific bought it for $290,000, intending to turn it into a genealogy and history center and museum, though the final use of the property was opened to public comment. In the end the museum idea prevailed. In August 2023, the Red Cedar Inn Museum and Visitor Center held its grand opening, welcoming visitors into the same knotty-pine rooms where travelers once stopped for supper and a drink.

## Flight Context

The Red Cedar Inn sits at approximately 38.4817° N, 90.7177° W in Pacific, Missouri, on the historic Route 66 alignment on the southwestern edge of the St. Louis metropolitan area. From 2,000 to 2,500 feet above ground level, look for Interstate 44, which runs past Pacific on its way southwest from St. Louis, and the Meramec River, which winds along the town. The building itself is small and best appreciated on the ground, but the town and the highway corridor are easy to pick out. Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) in Chesterfield lies to the northeast, Washington Regional Airport (KFYG) lies to the west near the Missouri River, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is farther northeast; be mindful of the St. Louis Class B airspace when routing from the east. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common in eastern Missouri.
