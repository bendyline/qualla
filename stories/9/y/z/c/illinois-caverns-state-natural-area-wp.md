# Illinois Caverns State Natural Area

Beneath the sinkhole-pocked farmland of Monroe County lies Illinois Caverns, the state's second-largest cave, a failed turn-of-the-century show cave with roughly six miles of mapped, wet, wild passages and an endangered amphipod found almost nowhere else on Earth.

**Location:** 38.2356, -90.1369

**Categories:** Caves of Illinois, Protected areas of Monroe County, Illinois, State parks of Illinois, Protected areas established in 1985, Landforms of Monroe County, Illinois, 1985 establishments in Illinois, State Natural Areas of Illinois

## Introduction

At the bottom of the concrete stairs, the temperature settles at 58 degrees Fahrenheit and stays there, summer and winter alike. Beyond the reach of daylight, a stream runs through passages that can stand 20 feet high and just as wide, and the only light is the light you carry in. This is Illinois Caverns, also known as the Mammoth Cave of Illinois, the second-largest cave in the state, with roughly 9.6 kilometers, or about six miles, of mapped passages winding beneath the countryside of Monroe County.

## The Show Cave That Never Paid

The cave has gone by many names, among them Burksville Cave, Egyptian Cave and Eckert Cave. Its first paying customers arrived in April 1901, when a Mr. White of East Saint Louis opened it to the public. It remains the only cave in Illinois ever operated commercially, and it drew many visitors from St. Louis during the 1904 World's Fair. Even so, the venture was never considered a success, and it closed in 1907; a later attempt by a new owner also failed. Those failures turned out to be a gift, because the cave remained almost completely unimproved, never fitted out with the walkways and lighting of a polished show cave. The concrete entrance stairs and a single ladder are nearly all that human hands added, which is why the cave today still feels wild.

## Water at Work

Illinois Caverns was carved by water, and the carving is not finished. The cave is still forming in Mississippian-age limestone and dolomite, the soluble rocks of the karst country common to this part of Illinois, as water slowly dissolves the stone. Along the way it has decorated the passages with stalactites, stalagmites, rimstone dams, flowstone and delicate soda straws. The layout is what geologists call a branchwork cave: a central cavern and stream, with smaller passages and tributaries branching away from it like limbs from a trunk. The same forces show up on the surface. Around the entrance, numerous circular, crater-like depressions pock the land, sinkholes typical of karst topography and easily visible from the air.

## Small Lives in the Dark

The cave supports a surprising range of animals, including bats, salamanders, frogs, crickets, amphipods and isopods. One resident gives the site special importance. Gammarus acherondytes, the Illinois cave amphipod, is a federally endangered species found nowhere else in the world except a few neighboring caves in the karst of southwestern Illinois. The cave's bats were the reason for its most recent long closure. In early 2010, Illinois Caverns was closed to visitors to stop the spread of white-nose syndrome, a disease that affects bats. The closure lasted more than a decade, and the cave did not reopen until June 16, 2021.

## Going Underground

The State of Illinois bought the cave and its surroundings in 1985, and care of the site was entrusted to Armin Krueger, who looked after it until his death. Visiting today is a genuine caving experience rather than a stroll, and the cave is open only seasonally, roughly April through October. Anyone who wants to enter needs a permit, and parties must include at least four people. Each person must wear a hardhat, boots and sturdy clothing and carry three separate sources of light. Some portions of the cave may be off-limits at times, and those areas should be clearly marked. Parts of the cave also flood during wet weather, a reminder that the stream which shaped these passages is still very much in charge down here.

## Flight Context

Illinois Caverns State Natural Area lies at approximately 38.2356° N, 90.1369° W in the New Design Precinct of Monroe County, Illinois, off Illinois Route 3 south of Waterloo, near the unincorporated community of Burksville. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet above ground level, the most striking feature is the surface karst: numerous round, crater-like sinkholes scattered across the farmland around the cave entrance, easily visible from the air. Illinois Route 3 provides a clear north-south reference, and the Mississippi River floodplain lies to the west beyond the bluffs. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) lies to the north, and Sparta Community Airport (KSAR) is to the east-southeast; stay clear of the St. Louis Class B airspace to the north. Low sun angles in morning and late afternoon make the sinkholes stand out best, while summer haze can soften the view.
