# Valmeyer, Illinois

After the Great Flood of 1993 swamped their Mississippi River floodplain village twice in one summer, the people of Valmeyer voted to move their whole town up onto the bluffs, creating one of the country's most studied community relocations.

**Location:** 38.3000, -90.3083

**Categories:** Metro East, Villages in Monroe County, Illinois, Ghost towns in Illinois, Villages in Illinois

## Introduction

"It was like flying over the ocean," Dennis Knobloch recalled of the day in 1993 when he first surveyed his town from a helicopter. As mayor of Valmeyer, Illinois, he looked down on a village swallowed by the Mississippi River. "All you would see were just the tip of the houses standing out like small islands. Everything else was covered." What Valmeyer did next made it something rare in American history: a town that packed up, climbed the bluffs, and started over on higher ground.

## Life on the American Bottom

The original Valmeyer sat in the American Bottom, the broad floodplain of the Mississippi River in western Monroe County, about three miles from the river's main channel. Illinois Route 156 ran through the center of the village, leading east toward Waterloo, the county seat, and west toward Harrisonville and the Mississippi. It was a tight-knit farming community of around 900 people, and it knew high water well. Valmeyer had endured flooding since its incorporation in 1909, and every time the river receded, residents cleaned up and carried on. The summer of 1993 would break that pattern for good.

## The One-Two Punch

In the Great Flood of 1993, the swollen Mississippi overtopped Valmeyer's levee system. Up to sixteen feet of floodwater engulfed the village and lingered for months, damaging 90 to 95 percent of its buildings. When the first flood began to recede about two weeks after it arrived, some residents returned to their gutted homes to start cleaning, fully intending to stay. Then, as Knobloch remembered it, a second surge came through in September and pushed the water back up. "That one-two punch was a major psychological blow," Knobloch said later. "After that most people were like: 'This is enough. We don't want to ever go through something like this again.'"

## A Crazy Idea

Representatives of the Federal Emergency Management Agency brought an unusual proposal as part of a then-new hazard mitigation program: the government would buy out damaged properties, and residents could use the proceeds to rebuild on higher ground. Knobloch and other officials first thought it was "a crazy idea." Only a handful of towns had ever made such a move work, and none had attempted it on Valmeyer's scale. "There was no blueprint or playbook whatsoever for us to look at," he said. After a series of meetings, the town voted in September, and nearly 70 percent welcomed the move. Officials set their sights on a 500-acre parcel of cornfields and woods on the neighboring bluffs, land more than 300 feet higher than the floodplain. Residents dipped into personal savings for down payments on lots, which let the village buy the land, and construction began in mid-December, just three months after the vote.

## Building a Town from a Cornfield

The move needed about $22 million to build the new town and $23 million to buy out residents' homes, and state and federal governments eventually pledged 90 percent of the funding. Progress was never smooth. Archaeological sites turned up during excavation and required remediation, a cluster of sinkholes forced changes to the plans, and construction stopped for four months when trees on the site proved to be breeding habitat for an endangered bat. Knobloch quit his job as an insurance broker to work full-time on permits and planning. Some families lost patience and moved elsewhere, but in the end well over half of the 900 people from the old town relocated to the new one. The whole process took only a few years, far less time than federal officials had forecast.

## Two Valmeyers

Today there are, in a sense, two Valmeyers. Down in the floodplain, the old townsite has been described as a ghost town, though Route 156 still makes it an important traffic artery. Its roads have faded to gravel, a dozen or so families still live there, and corn and soybeans grow where buildings once stood. Up on the bluff, the new village has a post office, a school, a gas station, banks, churches, and a restaurant. Not everything worked out: many businesses moved to neighboring towns after the flood and never came back. But the population, 608 in 2000, passed 1,200 by 2010 and stood at 1,233 in 2020. Communities from across the country and as far away as Japan and Australia have come to study how Valmeyer did it, and geologist Nicholas Pinter has called it "a textbook example of how a town can recover, start over and thrive after devastation."

## Flight Context

Valmeyer lies at 38.3°N, 90.3083°W in western Monroe County, Illinois, where the Mississippi River floodplain known as the American Bottom meets the bluffs to the east. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, the contrast tells the story: the flat, farmed floodplain with the scattered remains of the old townsite along Illinois Route 156, and the orderly new village on the higher ground above it. The Mississippi River lies a few miles to the west and makes an unmistakable reference. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 18 nm north-northeast, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) about 27 nm north, so stay aware of the busy St. Louis airspace to the north. Clear days show the floodplain and bluff line best.
