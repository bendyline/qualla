# Meramec Caverns

Near Stanton, Missouri, a 4.6-mile limestone cavern system that sheltered Native Americans and drew a French miner in 1722 became, thanks to showman Lester Dill, a Route 66 legend complete with a Jesse James hideout story, an underground ballroom and a formation billed as the world's rarest.

## Introduction

The sheriff waited at the mouth of the cave, the story goes, certain that Jesse James and his gang were trapped inside. He waited and waited. The outlaws, meanwhile, had found another way out. It is a wonderful tale, and there is scant historical evidence that any of it happened. But at Meramec Caverns, near Stanton, Missouri, a good story has always mattered almost as much as the stone itself, and this 4.6-mile cavern system in the Ozarks has plenty of both.

## Before the Legends

The caverns were formed as water eroded large deposits of limestone over millions of years, hollowing out rooms and passages beneath the hills. People found them long before tourists did. In centuries past, Native Americans used the cavern system for shelter, and pre-Columbian artifacts have been found inside. The first European visitor on record was a French miner, who came in 1722 (some accounts say 1720) and found saltpeter, an ingredient of gunpowder, making Meramec one of the first caves west of the Mississippi River explored by Europeans. By 1890, the caverns had become part of community life: one chamber, now called the Ballroom, was used for square dances and other gatherings, a cool underground hall for the people of the surrounding hills.

## The Outlaw's Hideout

According to local legend, Jesse James and his brother and partner in crime, Frank, used the caves as a hideout in the 1870s. The most famous version of the tale is the one with the patient sheriff, stationed at the entrance while the gang slipped out through a second exit. Historians have found little to support it. Still, the legend fits the place perfectly: a cave of long, winding passages in the Missouri countryside, just the sort of refuge an outlaw might dream of. Whether or not the brothers ever sheltered here, their story has become part of the caverns' identity, and visitors have been happy to believe in it.

## Lester Dill's Big Idea

The modern caverns began with a discovery. In 1933 the extended cave system was discovered, revealing the full 4.6 miles known today. Two years later, in 1935, Lester B. Dill opened the caverns to the public as a tourist attraction, and he proved to be a promoter of rare talent. Dill is credited with inventing the bumper sticker as a way to advertise the caverns, turning visitors' cars into rolling billboards. Conventional billboards did their part too, and many still line Interstate 44. In 1960 the caverns even rented out billboard space inside the cave, which the owners claimed was the only underground billboard in the world. The strategy worked. Meramec Caverns became one of the primary attractions along former U.S. Highway 66, and it is now one of the most visited caves in Missouri, with attendance reported at about 150,000 a year.

## The Wine Table and the Stage Curtain

Deep inside, on the cavern's fifth level, lies the Wine Room, home to a formation the cave bills as the world's rarest: the Wine Table. This onyx table stands six feet high on three natural legs. It is an aragonite formation, built almost entirely underwater, and both the table and the room are covered in grape-like clusters called botryoids. Those grapes gave the room its name, although the cave now jokes that it is called the Wine Room because of the "whining" visitors do on learning it can only be reached by climbing 58 steps. Elsewhere, a massive sheet wall of formations, grown over the past several thousand years, serves as the cave's "stage curtain" and the backdrop for a light and sound show called the Greatest Show Under the Earth.

## Tricks of Light and Water

Some of the cave's wonders are illusions. In the Mirror Room, a stream only about a foot and a half deep lies perfectly still. When the lights come on, the undisturbed water reflects the cavern's roof so faithfully that many visitors perceive it as 50 feet deep. Hollywood took notice of the setting, too. An episode of Lassie was filmed in front of the stage curtain, and in midsummer 1972 the caverns provided the cave scenes for the musical film Tom Sawyer, released the following year. The Ballroom, meanwhile, still gathers people together. It is one of the first stops on the tour, and it hosts a few annual concerts by the Lester Family.

## Flight Context

Meramec Caverns lies at 38.2413°N, 91.0924°W in Franklin County, Missouri, near Stanton and just south of Interstate 44, along the wooded valley of the Meramec River. The caverns are underground, so navigate by the river's winding course and its bluffs a few miles south of the interstate. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL the Meramec valley is easy to follow in good weather. Sullivan Regional Airport (KUUV) is only about 3–4 nm to the west, and Washington Regional Airport (KFYG) roughly 21 nm to the north. Expect fog in the river valley on cool mornings and summer thunderstorms in the afternoons.
