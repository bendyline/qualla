# Green Tree Tavern (Janis-Ziegler House)

Built around 1790 on a plot once called LaBreche's Cow Pasture, Ste. Genevieve's Green Tree Tavern is the oldest house in Missouri verified by tree-ring dating, and in 1807 it hosted the first meeting of the first Masonic lodge west of the Mississippi.

## Introduction

Before it was a tavern, a Masonic meeting hall or a museum, the ground beneath this house in Ste. Genevieve, Missouri, went by a humble name: "LaBreche's Cow Pasture." Nicolas Janis bought the plot in 1789, and the house he began raising there the following year still stands. Tree-ring dating has since put a firm date on it: the Green Tree Tavern, also known as the Janis-Ziegler House, is the oldest house in Missouri dated by tree rings, built around 1790 to 1791.

## A Family From Across the River

Nicolas Janis had lived in Kaskaskia, Illinois, before he and his family moved to Ste. Genevieve in 1789. That year he purchased the pasture land from Jean Baptiste LaBreche, and in 1790 he started building. Six years later, in 1796, he transferred the property to his son, Francois Janis. It was Francois who would give the house its public life. Around 1803 he opened a tavern in his home, turning the family residence into a public house. That same year he built a stone retaining wall on the property, a practical piece of masonry that would still be standing, and still worth seeing, more than two centuries later.

## Posts, Mud and Hair

The house is an excellent example of poteaux-sur-sol construction, a French colonial technique in which upright posts stand on a sill. The spaces between the timbers are packed with bousillage, a mix of mud and debris typically bound with straw, grass and hair. Inside, the Janis-Ziegler House preserves one of the least altered interiors of any French Creole house in the area. Yet it is not purely French. Look up at the roof framing and the house reveals a second influence: pegged Anglo-American rafters take the place of the Norman truss system traditionally used in French houses around Ste. Genevieve. Federal-style details add to the blend, making this an Anglo-French transitional house that records, in wood and mud, a frontier community in the middle of changing cultures.

## The Lodge at the Tavern

On November 14, 1807, a group of Freemasons met at Francois Janis's tavern. It was the first meeting of Louisiana Lodge 109, the first Masonic lodge in Missouri. Approved on July 17, 1807, it was also the first Masonic lodge west of the Mississippi River, so the first gathering of that pioneering lodge took place inside this modest French colonial house. The Janis family ran the tavern until 1833, when they sold the house to Mathias and Barbara Ziegler, who added a tobacco shop. Only in the 1850s did the Zieglers close both and convert the building back into a private house. The Ziegler family, whose name the house also carries, lived there into the 1920s.

## Surveyed, Flooded and Saved

The 20th century brought both recognition and disaster. In 1938 the Historic American Buildings Survey documented the house under the direction of Alexander Piaget and Charles van Ravenswaay. In 1966 it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places as part of the Ste. Genevieve Historic District, a National Historic Landmark. Local preservationists Norbert and Frankye Donze bought the house in 1976 and ran it as a house museum until 1992. Then came the Great Flood of 1993, which damaged the house and covered Francois Janis's 1803 retaining wall. In 1996 Hilliard J. Goldman bought the property and began an extensive restoration. Today the wall is uncovered again for visitors, and the house belongs to Ste. Genevieve National Historical Park, authorized in 2018 and established in 2020.

## Flight Context

The Green Tree Tavern stands at approximately 37.9765° N, 90.0422° W in the historic town of Ste. Genevieve, Missouri, on the west side of the Mississippi River. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet above ground level, the town's compact grid sits a short distance back from the river, with the wide Mississippi floodplain spreading to the east; individual colonial houses are small and best seen on foot, but the historic district is easy to locate near the town center. Roughly ten miles to the northwest, across the river on the Illinois side, lies Fort de Chartres. Perryville Regional Airport (KPCD) is to the southeast, and Sparta Community Airport (KSAR) lies to the northeast in Illinois. River fog is common on cool mornings, and summer brings haze and thunderstorms.
