# Ste. Genevieve, Missouri

Missouri's oldest permanent European settlement still keeps rare French colonial houses whose walls are timbers stood on end, some sunk straight into the earth.

**Location:** 37.9769, -90.0486

**Categories:** French colonial settlements of Illinois Country, Missouri populated places on the Mississippi River, County seats in Missouri, French-Canadian culture in Missouri, Pre-statehood history of Missouri

## Introduction

Anglo-American frontier builders stacked their logs sideways. The French colonists of Ste. Genevieve stood theirs on end, sometimes sinking the timbers straight into the dirt. That choice left the town with something almost nowhere else has: three of the five known surviving poteaux en terre, or "posts-in-the-ground," houses in North America. They stand in a Mississippi River town of about 5,000 people that was the first organized European settlement west of the river in present-day Missouri, and that since 2020 has been home to Ste. Genevieve National Historical Park, the 422nd unit of the National Park Service.

## A Birth Date Still in Dispute

Even the founding is a matter of careful argument. Traditional accounts put it at 1735, but historian Carl Ekberg documented a more likely date of about 1750. Either way, the settlers came from the older French villages of the Illinois Country just across the Mississippi, where the soils had grown exhausted and families needed new land. Many had lived in the region for two or three generations. Improved relations with the Osage, whose hunting and war parties ranged into the area, made the move possible. The newcomers named their village for Saint Genevieve, the fifth-century patron saint of Paris. It completed a French triad in the mid-Mississippi Valley: Fort de Chartres, the official capital, stood about ten miles northwest across the river, and Kaskaskia, later Illinois's first state capital, lay about eight miles southeast. Jacques-Nicolas Bellin's map of 1755 was the first to mark Ste. Genevieve at all.

## French Hearts Under a Spanish Flag

In 1762, after France's defeat in the French and Indian War, the Treaty of Fontainebleau secretly handed the west bank of the Mississippi to Spain. The next year, the Treaty of Paris gave Great Britain the land to the east, and French-speaking families who wanted no part of British rule crossed the river, many of them to Ste. Genevieve. The Spanish moved the capital of Upper Louisiana to St. Louis and governed lightly, often through French-speaking officials, so for more than 40 years the village stayed French in language, custom and character. Frontier life was rarely calm. In the 1770s, bands of Little Osage and Missouri raided for horses, and in the 1790s the Big Osage pressed harder, killing some settlers. Yet the fur trade and marriages between French-Canadian men and Native American women also bound the communities together. When the Spanish governor granted Pierre and Auguste Chouteau of St. Louis exclusive trade with the Big Osage in 1794, commercial diplomacy eased some of the tension.

## Houses That Stand on End

The oldest surviving buildings, described as French Creole colonial, all date from the late 18th century, when Spain ruled. Their walls are rows of upright wooden posts, either dug into the ground or set on a raised stone or brick sill, a style called poteaux sur solle, or "posts-on-a-sill." In the rarer posts-in-the-ground houses, the walls don't even carry the floor, which rests on separate stone pillars, and the buried timbers left them exposed to flood, termites and rot. That fragility makes the survivors precious. The Louis Bolduc House, a National Historic Landmark, carries its own flood story. Bolduc first built a smaller home in 1770 at the village's original riverfront site; after flooding badly damaged it, parts were dismantled and moved north as the town developed a new site in 1785. He folded the salvaged materials into a larger house in the early 1790s, its three big ground-floor rooms a statement of his wealth. The Beauvais-Amoureux House, the Bequette-Ribault House and the 1818 Felix Vallé House keep it company.

## The Big Field and the Far Shore

For decades this was a farming village. Townspeople lived on lots in town and worked a common field, the Grand Champ, divided in the French manner into long, narrow strips running back from the river toward the hills so that each settler had some waterfront. Oxen pulled wheeled plows like those of 18th-century France. The habitants grew wheat, corn and tobacco, out-producing St. Louis in wheat, and their grain went south to sustain French New Orleans, whose climate was wrong for such crops. That prosperity rested partly on enslaved African Americans, who worked the small farms and, in some cases, the lead mines. On the night of September 4, 1852, eight enslaved men, five of them from Ste. Genevieve and three from the Vallé lead mines in neighboring Jefferson County, tried to escape across the Mississippi toward Sparta, Illinois, a town widely reputed as a haven for freedom seekers. Six slaveholders, among them members of the Vallé family, claimed the men. A week later, on September 11, a newspaper advertisement offered a $1,600 reward for their return. All eight were recaptured in Illinois within about two weeks.

## A Ferry Called the French Connection

After the Louisiana Purchase, Anglo-Americans and German immigrants arrived and the town turned toward trade, yet it held on to its French ways. The Sisters of St. Joseph, a French teaching order, opened a convent and taught in a Catholic school, and in 1876 the parish built its third church, modeled on the Gothic churches of France. By the late 19th century, lime kilns and quarries drew African American migrant workers from Tennessee, Mississippi and Arkansas. From very different backgrounds, they mixed little with the town's long-established Black Catholic families, some of whom had spoken French in colonial times. The heritage is still celebrated with La Guiannée at the turn of the year, French Fest, Jour de Fête and the King's Ball. And on its posted schedule, unless the river is flooding, the Ste. Genevieve-Modoc Ferry carries cars, cyclists and walkers across to Illinois and the old French sites of Fort de Chartres and Fort Kaskaskia. Its nickname: the French Connection.

## Flight Context

Ste. Genevieve sits at 37.9769°N, 90.0486°W on the west bank of the Mississippi River, about 46 miles south of St. Louis, served by Interstate 55, U.S. Route 61 and Missouri Route 32. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, follow the Mississippi and look for the compact historic town just back from the west bank, with the broad Illinois floodplain opposite; the Modoc ferry crossing links the two shores. Nearby airports: Perryville Regional Airport (KPCD, FAA PCD, formerly K02), about 11 nm southeast; Sparta Community Airport-Hunter Field (KSAR), about 20 nm east-northeast in Illinois; Farmington Regional Airport (KFAM), about 22 nm southwest; and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL), about 49 nm north. The climate is humid subtropical, so expect summer haze and afternoon convection over the river valley.
