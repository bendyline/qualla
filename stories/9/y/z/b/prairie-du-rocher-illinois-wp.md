# Prairie du Rocher, Illinois

Founded by French colonists in 1722 beneath the bluffs of the American Bottom, this tiny Illinois village has outlasted three flags, kept its medieval New Year's caroling, and dynamited its own levee to survive the Great Flood of 1993.

**Location:** 38.0831, -90.0958

**Categories:** French colonial settlements of Illinois Country, French-American culture in Illinois, Villages in Randolph County, Illinois, New France, Mississippi River floods

## Introduction

Every New Year's Eve, Prairie du Rocher sings La Guiannée. The caroling custom dates back to medieval France, and it is traditionally dated to the settlement of this Illinois village in 1722. That is a long run for a town of 502 people, counted in the 2020 census. Prairie du Rocher, "the Rock Prairie" in French, sits near the bluffs that flank the east side of the Mississippi River, on the floodplain often called the American Bottom. It is one of the oldest communities in the United States that was founded as a French settlement, and it still carries that heritage in its houses, its customs and its name.

## The Fort and the Rock Prairie

About four miles west, closer to the river, stands Fort de Chartres. Pierre Dugué de Boisbriand built the first fort there, and it became a French military fortification and colonial headquarters when France claimed a vast territory stretching from present-day Louisiana through the Illinois Country to Canada. In 1722, St. Thérèse Langlois, Boisbriand's nephew, founded Prairie du Rocher on a tract donated by the Royal Indian Company. Its first settlers were French colonists, mostly migrants from Canada. The fertile bottomland rewarded them. Surpluses from the habitants' fields helped supply critical wheat and corn to New Orleans and other communities in lower Louisiana. D'Artaguette, an inspector in the early 18th century, called the country "one of the most beautiful in all Louisiana", where every kind of grain and vegetable grew in the greatest abundance and the settlers lacked "none of the necessaries or conveniences of life."

## Long Lots and a Shared Common

In 1743 the territorial government granted the village the Prairie du Rocher Common, land used by all the villagers, and it remained in use until 1852. Farm plots followed the typical French pattern of long, narrow lots, and the villagers kept them open within the common, fencing the whole area to keep out livestock. A school existed as early as the 1760s, with students boarding with local families. Abundance did not last forever. Because the habitants did not fertilize, the soil became exhausted, and a growing population left too little land to go around. Some villagers crossed to the west side of the Mississippi and, around 1750, founded Ste. Genevieve in present-day Missouri, quickly building an agricultural community much like the one they had left.

## Three Flags Over the Bottom

Empires then took turns with the village. After the French and Indian War, the 1763 Treaty of Paris gave Britain the French lands east of the Mississippi, excluding New Orleans, though British forces did not arrive in strength until 1765. To avoid British rule, many residents moved across the river to towns such as Ste. Genevieve and St. Louis, which by the secret Treaty of Fontainebleau now lay in Spanish Louisiana. King George III's Royal Proclamation of 1763 also designated the land between the Appalachians and the Mississippi as an Indian Reserve and tried to keep colonial settlers out. During the American Revolution, George Rogers Clark captured Prairie du Rocher for the colonies in the same Illinois campaign that took Vincennes. Reportedly, the campaign drove more French settlers west, and many of the newcomers who replaced them had served with Clark or were related to someone who had, drawn by tales of the American Bottom's fertile soil.

## Dynamite at 3:30 A.M.

In the Great Flood of 1993, Prairie du Rocher escaped flooding, and it did so by dynamiting a levee of its own. Levees broke to the north near Columbia and Valmeyer, and the water engulfed Fort de Chartres. Only the Prairie du Rocher Creek levee protected the town, and residents discovered that the floodwater pressing against it stood higher than the Mississippi's main channel. The Army Corps of Engineers used a barge-mounted shovel to cut the main levee near the fort, but the water barely dropped, and the Corps decided against further action. At 3:30 a.m., with water at the top of the sandbags on the creek levee, all three commissioners of the Fort de Chartres – Ivy Landing Drainage and Levee District voted to dynamite the levee in two more places. The village called for help and added a foot and a half of sandbags. The two new openings let enough water escape to the channel, and the town was saved.

## Houses That Remember

At least twelve surviving houses in the village were built in the 18th century, among them the Melliere House of 1735. The Creole House, built in 1800 in the French Colonial style, was the work of an unknown English-speaking immigrant from the eastern United States, a sign of how thoroughly French building traditions had rooted here. Fort de Chartres, its buildings partly reconstructed after falling into ruin, is now an Illinois state historic site. Each summer and winter it hosts Rendezvous, when people from a broad area camp out for the weekend dressed as 18th-century French soldiers and Native people and re-enact the wars that decided who owned the fort.

## Flight Context

Prairie du Rocher lies at approximately 38.0831° N, 90.0958° W, at the foot of the bluffs on the Illinois side of the Mississippi River's American Bottom floodplain. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, look for the small village where the bluffs meet the flat farmland; Fort de Chartres lies about 5 km (just over three miles) to the west toward the river, and Illinois Route 155 links the two, continuing northeast to Ruma. Ste. Genevieve lies across the Mississippi about 12 km to the south-southeast. Perryville Regional Airport (KPCD) is about 31 km to the southeast, Sparta Community Airport (KSAR) about 36 km to the east, and St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) about 54 km to the north. River valley fog is common on calm mornings.
