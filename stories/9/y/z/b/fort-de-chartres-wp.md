# Fort de Chartres

Built three times on the Mississippi floodplain as the seat of French power in the Illinois Country, Fort de Chartres passed to the British, crumbled into the river and the neighbors' walls, and survives through a stone powder magazine believed to be the oldest building in Illinois.

**Location:** 38.0844, -90.1578

## Introduction

The oldest surviving building in Illinois is believed to be a storehouse for gunpowder. Built of stone masonry, the magazine at Fort de Chartres outlasted the fort it was meant to supply, outlasted French rule and British rule, and outlasted a river that kept trying to take everything around it. Four miles west of Prairie du Rocher, on the Mississippi floodplain known as the American Bottom, the fort that once governed the Illinois Country has been partly rebuilt around that stubborn stone building.

## A Fortune in the Wilderness

The fort began as a business venture. In 1717, the French government chartered John Law's Company of the West with a monopoly on trade with Louisiana, and the company, which hoped to make a fortune mining precious metals, built a fort to protect its interests. A French contingent from the Louisiana colony, led by Pierre Dugué de Boisbriant, raised the original wooden fort between 1718 and 1720. When administration of the Illinois Country moved from Quebec City to Louisiana, governance passed to the Company of the Indies, and the fort became the seat of government for the province, then part of New France. It also served as an instrument of colonial control over the Native nations of the region, particularly the Meskwaki. The fort took its name from Louis, duc de Chartres, son of the Regent of France.

## Three Forts Against One River

The Mississippi made the fort's builders pay for their choice of site. The first fort was a palisade of logs with two bastions at opposite corners, and within five years flooding had left it in bad condition. In 1725 work began on a second fort farther from the river, though still on the floodplain, again built of logs, this time with a bastion at each of its four corners. Flooding was the main reason the fort was rebuilt twice. The final version, raised in limestone beginning in 1753 and mostly finished by 1754, was meant to last. Its walls were three feet thick and enclosed four acres, an imposing stone statement of French authority in the heart of the continent, finished only about a decade before France would lose it.

## Redcoats on the Mississippi

The Seven Years' War ended that authority. Under the Treaty of Paris, control of the Illinois Country passed to Great Britain, and on October 10, 1765, a small detachment of the 42nd Regiment of Foot under Captain Thomas Stirling took control of the fort and the surrounding area. The 34th Regiment of Foot later renamed the post Fort Cavendish after its colonel, but the new name did not stick. From 1768, when the 18th Regiment of Foot under Lieutenant Colonel Wilkins replaced the 34th, it was known again as Fort Chartres. British tenure was brief. In May 1772 most of the 18th was ordered back to Philadelphia and the post was abandoned, though a small party under Captain Hugh Lord remained at nearby Kaskaskia until May 1776.

## Walls Into the River

Without a garrison, the fort was left to the river. In 1772 the south wall and one bastion collapsed into the Mississippi. The remaining walls slowly deteriorated, and by the 1820s visitors noted trees growing out of them. Over the years, local residents carted stones away for their own construction. By 1900 the walls were gone entirely. Only the stone powder magazine remained of the original fort, having survived both the scavenging and the floods.

## Rebuilding a French Outpost

The State of Illinois acquired the ruins in 1913 and restored the magazine in 1917. In the 1920s the foundations of the fort's walls and buildings were exposed, and by the end of the 1930s the gateway and two stone buildings had been rebuilt, much of the work carried out by the WPA. A museum and office building went up in 1928 on an original foundation, and the large stone Guards House, reconstructed in 1936, holds a Catholic chapel furnished in 1750s style along with a priest's room, a gunner's room, an officer-of-the-day room and a guard's room. The site was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1960 and placed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966, and partial reconstruction of the walls followed in 1989. A working bake oven, a shed built in poteaux-sur-sol (post-on-sill) style and a kitchen garden round out the grounds. Each June, the site hosts a Rendezvous, an 18th-century reenactment described as one of the largest of its kind in the Midwest. The river still intrudes: in 1993, a levee break about 100 feet away left the fort submerged in up to fifteen feet of water for two weeks.

## Flight Context

Fort de Chartres lies at approximately 38.0844° N, 90.1578° W, four miles west of Prairie du Rocher in Randolph County, Illinois, on the flat floodplain of the Mississippi's east bank known as the American Bottom. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet above ground level, look for a square of pale stone walls with corner bastions set among farm fields between the river and the line of bluffs to the east; the reconstructed fort stands out clearly against the flat bottomland. Ste. Genevieve, Missouri, lies about 15 km to the southeast across the river, and St. Louis is well to the north. Sparta Community Airport (KSAR) lies to the east in Illinois, and Perryville Regional Airport (KPCD) is to the southeast on the Missouri side. River fog can blanket the bottomland on cool mornings, and spring flooding can transform the landscape around the levees; summer haze is common.
