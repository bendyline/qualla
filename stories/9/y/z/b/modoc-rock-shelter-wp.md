# Modoc Rock Shelter

Found in 1951 when road grading at the foot of the Mississippi bluffs exposed buried artifacts, the Modoc Rock Shelter holds layer upon layer of Archaic-period camps spanning some five thousand years, the first deeply stratified Archaic site discovered in Illinois.

**Location:** 38.0628, -90.0636

**Categories:** Archaic period in North America, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois, National Historic Landmarks in Illinois, National Register of Historic Places in Randolph County, Illinois, Geography of Randolph County, Illinois, Rock shelters in the United States

## Introduction

In 1951, road work along the foot of the sandstone bluffs southeast of Prairie du Rocher, Illinois, scraped away a layer of sterile fill. Irvin Peithmann, an amateur archaeologist, noticed what the grading had exposed: artifacts lying on the surface beneath and near the overhang. Had he not looked, this deeply layered record of ancient life might still be unknown. Instead, the Modoc Rock Shelter became one of the key sites for understanding the Archaic period in eastern North America, and in 1961 it was declared a National Historic Landmark.

## A Chance Find at the Bluff

Peithmann was an unconventional figure, reportedly known for teaching himself about Native American customs by living among Native people. When he spotted the artifacts, he told Dr. Melvin Fowler, then at the Illinois State Museum, and the museum went on to conduct major excavations at the site in the 1950s and again in the 1980s. What they found was remarkable for its depth. Modoc was the first site in Illinois, and one of the first in eastern North America, where deeply stratified Archaic deposits had been discovered, meaning that successive occupations lay stacked one atop another like pages in a book. Fowler believed that many more buried Archaic sites like Modoc lay hidden at the base of the bluffs along the Mississippi floodplain.

## Five Thousand Years in the Layers

The shelter sits beneath the sandstone bluffs that form the eastern edge of the Mississippi River floodplain, and Native American peoples returned to it for thousands of years. Excavations between 1952 and 1956 documented the antiquity and cultural sequence of the Archaic period in the midcontinent. Some researchers later questioned whether the layers were as orderly as they seemed, because radiocarbon dates and artifacts did not always line up. New digs in 1980, 1984 and 1987 settled the matter, producing more than 40 additional radiocarbon samples from controlled contexts. They revealed two separate sequences, one in the Main Shelter and one in the West Shelter, each holding occupations from the Early, Middle and Late Archaic periods, between roughly 9,000 and 4,000 years ago.

## Life Under the Overhang

The people who camped here left behind the tools of daily life: projectile points, scrapers, choppers, hammerstones and bone awls. The remains of their meals tell the rest of the story. They ate deer, raccoon and opossum, along with birds and fish, a menu that drew on both the land and the water. The overhang offered shelter from rain and wind, and the setting placed its occupants at the meeting point of two landscapes. Over millennia, the same rock ledge sheltered generation after generation of people, long before any written record of the region existed.

## Credit and Recognition

The discovery left its finder unhappy. Over the years Peithmann grew bitter about what he saw as a lack of recognition from professional archaeologists, even though Fowler clearly acknowledged him and credited him with the discovery in his 1950s publication on the site. The shelter itself fared better: it was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1961. It also stands among the contributing properties of the French Colonial Historic District in the region, where it represents the deep past amid landmarks of the eighteenth century, from Fort de Chartres to the Kolmer Site. Visitors reach it on County Road 7, the Bluff Road, where the shelter lies on the northeastern side of the road beneath the cliffs.

## Flight Context

The Modoc Rock Shelter lies at approximately 38.0628° N, 90.0636° W, on the northeastern side of County Road 7 (Bluff Road) southeast of Prairie du Rocher in Randolph County, Illinois. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet above ground level, follow the sharp line where the flat, farmed American Bottom meets the wooded bluffs that bound it to the east; the shelter sits at the base of those bluffs, and while the overhang itself is hard to pick out from the air, the bluff line and the road running along it are clear. Fort de Chartres, with its reconstructed stone walls, lies several miles to the west-northwest and makes a useful reference, and the Mississippi River lies beyond the fields. Sparta Community Airport (KSAR) is to the east, and Perryville Regional Airport (KPCD) is to the southeast on the Missouri side. Morning fog often fills the bottomland, and summer brings haze and thunderstorms.
