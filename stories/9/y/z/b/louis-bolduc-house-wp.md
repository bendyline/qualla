# Louis Bolduc House

Built in the early 1790s by a prosperous lead-mining merchant after Ste. Genevieve fled the Mississippi's floods for higher ground, the Louis Bolduc House preserves its mud-and-hair walls, Norman roof trusses and great keeping room as the first historic building in town to be authentically restored.

## Introduction

There is hair in the walls of the Louis Bolduc House. Between its heavy oak timbers, the builders packed bousillage, a mixture of mud, straw and horsehair that hardened to a cement-like texture, and the recipe sometimes called for other animal hair or even human hair. Those walls enclose the rooms where a prosperous merchant raised his family at 123 South Main Street in Ste. Genevieve, Missouri, and the house stands today as a National Historic Landmark and a prime example of traditional French Colonial architecture.

## A Town That Moved Uphill

Ste. Genevieve, the first permanent European settlement in what is now Missouri, was founded in the mid-eighteenth century by French-Canadian settlers. Most of them had come from villages on the east bank of the Mississippi, such as Prairie du Rocher in Illinois. Their first town sat too close to the river. After repeated floods, and an especially bad one in 1785, the settlers decided to move to higher ground farther from the water. Louis Bolduc was among those who built at the new site, about three miles north of the original village. He was a successful merchant and trader who also owned lead mines to the west, and in the early 1790s he built a house that showed it.

## Life in the Keeping Room

The first room Bolduc built was the heart of the household: a large keeping room, about 26 by 27 feet, where the family conducted most of its daily life. A big fireplace warmed its north end. Underfoot lay a wide-plank puncheon floor, made from logs cut flat on only one side and laid with the curved side down. Up in the attic, above the family's heads, Bolduc stored lead, corn and other goods. Around 1793-94 he added a wide hallway and a sleeping chamber of the same generous size. Historians believe that chamber held two "sleeping cells," areas partially walled off for privacy, one for Bolduc and his wife and one shared by their three children. Both large rooms received tall windows fitted with glass, another quiet signal of the owner's prosperity.

## Oak, Mud and a Norman Roof

The house is built in the poteaux-sur-solle, or posts-on-sill, manner. Heavy oak timbers stand about six inches apart, and diagonal timbers in each supporting wall add stability. Above them rises a steep hip roof of cedar shakes, carried on heavy, hand-hewn Norman trusses held together with mortise-and-tenon joints. The roof sweeps out over porches on all four sides of the house, shading the walls and helping keep the rooms cool through Missouri summers. Around the property runs a reconstructed stockade fence of the kind common in the period, built to keep out the livestock that roamed the village. Inside the fence, reconstructed gardens and a rebuilt detached kitchen complete the picture of a well-to-do colonial household.

## Peeling Back the Years

Bolduc's descendants owned the house until the late 1940s, when it was sold to The National Society of The Colonial Dames of America in the State of Missouri, which restored it under the direction of architectural historian Dr. Ernest Allen Connally. The restorers found that the original colonial elements were still intact beneath later changes, and they returned the house to its original colonial style, making it the first historic structure in Ste. Genevieve to be authentically restored. In 2020 the Dames transferred the house to French Colonial America, the nonprofit they created to run their museum campus, and today it operates as a house museum, furnished with pieces typical of the period, some of them original to the Bolduc family. The house was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1970, and it is also a contributing property in the Ste. Genevieve Historic District, itself a National Historic Landmark.

## Flight Context

The Louis Bolduc House stands at approximately 37.9786° N, 90.0431° W on South Main Street in the historic core of Ste. Genevieve, Missouri, on the west side of the Mississippi River. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet above ground level, the town's compact street grid sits back from the river with broad floodplain fields to the east; the house itself is small, but the historic district near the town center is easy to find, and the Green Tree Tavern stands a short distance away. Fort de Chartres lies across the river to the northwest on the Illinois side. Perryville Regional Airport (KPCD) is to the southeast, and Sparta Community Airport (KSAR) lies to the northeast in Illinois. Expect river fog on cool mornings and haze with thunderstorms in summer.
