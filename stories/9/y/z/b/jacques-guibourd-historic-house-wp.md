# Jacques Guibourd Historic House

A French Creole house of upright posts and clay-and-grass infill in Ste. Genevieve, built around 1806 for a merchant whose road there ran through an uprising of enslaved people, the Reign of Terror and a reported shipwreck.

**Location:** 37.9795, -90.0479

**Categories:** Creole architecture in the United States, French colonial architecture in the United States, Historic house museums in Missouri, French-American culture in Missouri, Poteaux-sur-sol framing, Slavery in the United States

## Introduction

The man who built this house escaped a revolution hidden in a cargo barrel. Jacques Jean-René Guibourd had been working as secretary to a wealthy plantation owner in Saint-Domingue (now Haiti) when the enslaved people of the colony rose in rebellion. According to his story, he was smuggled out inside that barrel by Moros, a man Guibourd himself held in slavery. Years later and a continent away, Guibourd settled in Ste. Genevieve, Missouri, and around 1806 raised La Maison de Guibourd, a French Creole house of upright timber posts that still stands on the block the Spanish government granted him.

## From Candé by Way of Chaos

Guibourd was born on July 29, 1755, in Candé, France. After Saint-Domingue, he and Moros made their way back to France, only to find the country torn apart by the Reign of Terror of 1793–94. They decided to leave again. Someone bound for Philadelphia told Guibourd of French-speaking settlers in America, possibly royalists loyal to King Louis XVI, and the two set sail. There is some evidence that they were shipwrecked on the voyage and lost all their belongings. Whatever happened at sea, Guibourd arrived in Ste. Genevieve sometime in the late 1790s penniless. Jean-Baptiste Barbeau took him in, and presumably Moros as well, until he could get settled. The record preserves Guibourd's story in detail but says almost nothing more of Moros, the man who had smuggled him out of Saint-Domingue.

## A Merchant and a Magistrate

Staying with the Barbeaus changed Guibourd's fortunes. He met his future wife, Ursula Barbeau, and came to know Jean-Baptiste Vallé, the commandant of Ste. Genevieve. In 1799 he obtained a Spanish land grant for an entire block, two arpents by two arpents, an arpent measuring 192 feet. While his house was going up, he ran a mercantile from his residence just across the street, and copies of his ledger still show what he sold, to whom, at what price and how customers paid. He married Ursula in June 1800 and quickly became a figure in town affairs, serving as a judge on the territorial district court and as Commissioner of Rates and Levies for the Ste. Genevieve District. He was one of the original trustees of the Ste. Genevieve Louisiana Academy of 1808, described as the first public institution of higher education in the Louisiana Territory. He also owned a tannery on the River aux Vases and a lead mine in what is now Washington County.

## Posts, Clay and a Kitchen Under the Porch

The house is built in the poteaux-sur-solle style, with vertical posts set on a sill and the gaps sealed with bouzillage, usually a mixture of clay and grass. The same basic design appears across the French-inhabited regions of the Illinois Country, eastern Canada and the Louisiana territory. Wide galleries originally surrounded the house, keeping the interior cool in summer and the snow off in winter. A few years after the house was built, a brick kitchen, which also served as slave quarters, was added in a semi-attached position under one corner of the porch, so meals could be served hot, dry and on time. Those meals were served by the family and by the enslaved people who lived in the quarters, and their rooms survive with the house. It is one of the few original kitchen structures and slave quarters of this period remaining in Ste. Genevieve.

## Sons, Heirs and a Doctor

Jacques and Ursula's children were all born in Ste. Genevieve: Eugene in 1801, Omer in 1807 and Jules in 1811. Two others died young, an infant who died in November 1803 and a son, Jean Edward, who died in January 1805. Jacques died on May 29, 1812. After Ursula's death on October 20, 1843, the house and the south half of the property passed to Jules and Omer, and the north half to Eugene, who had returned to Ste. Genevieve in 1839. Eugene and his wife, Marie Therese St. Gemme Beauvais, had 12 children, most born in Old Mines in Washington County. One of them, Felix, traveled to Angers, France, at age 11 with his uncles Jules and Omer. He earned a degree in science and art, attended medical school in Paris, and returned in 1865 to practice medicine in Ste. Genevieve until his death in 1885.

## A House Left to the Town

According to the Historic American Buildings Survey, Jules and Omer sold their share of the lot to Eugene in 1859, and it later became Felix's property. In 1907 the property was sold to Clovis G. Boyer, who in turn sold the house to Jules Felix and Anne Marie Vallé. In January 1973, Anne Marie's will left it to the Foundation for Restoration of Ste. Genevieve, which operates it today as a historic house museum. Its design has changed only slightly over two centuries to suit its various residents, and it keeps much of its early 19th-century French Creole character. The house is a contributing property in the Ste. Genevieve Historic District, a National Historic Landmark.

## Flight Context

The Guibourd House stands at approximately 37.9795° N, 90.0479° W, in the historic core of Ste. Genevieve, Missouri, near the west bank of the Mississippi River about 73 km south of St. Louis. The house itself is too small to single out from the air; from 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, use the compact grid of the old town beside the river as your reference. Perryville Regional Airport (KPCD) lies about 20 km to the southeast, Sparta Community Airport (KSAR) across the river in Illinois about 36 km to the northeast, and Farmington Regional Airport (KFAM) about 41 km to the southwest. Mississippi River valley fog is common on calm mornings.
