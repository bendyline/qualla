# Kolmer Site

A Michigamea village on the Mississippi's Illinois bank lasted just 32 years before a sudden attack in 1752 drove out its people, and the belongings they left behind, now buried under farmland a mile from the river, make it a tightly dated record of Native life alongside French colonists.

## Introduction

The village at the Kolmer Site lasted barely a third of a century, and that brevity is exactly what makes it valuable. Established in 1720 by the Michigamea, a subgroup of the Illini, it stood on the bank of the Mississippi River in what is now western Randolph County, Illinois, near Kaskaskia and Prairie du Rocher. In 1752 it was destroyed in a sudden attack, and its people never came back to rebuild. What they left behind still lies in the ground, a record sealed by catastrophe.

## Neighbors of New France

The Michigamea settled here in a region that France had claimed as its own. French explorers under René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, traveled the Mississippi and Ohio rivers and claimed them for New France. Catholic missionaries led by Jacques Gravier followed and won converts among the Illini, some of whom founded riverside villages at Cahokia, Kaskaskia and Peoria. These communities were small, but so were the European settlements around them. In letters written in 1750, one missionary counted fewer than eight hundred people in the three Illini villages of the American Bottom, while the five French villages in the same region held eleven hundred French colonists and three hundred Black people, most of them enslaved. The Michigamea village at Kolmer was part of this intertwined world, closely tied to the most prominent French settlements in the upper Mississippi valley.

## The Attack of 1752

The village was fortified with a stockade, but that was not enough. In 1752 a war party from the north came against it suddenly; accounts name the attackers variously as Fox (Meskwaki), Sioux, Cherokee or Sauk fighters. The inhabitants could not hold off the attack. Many of the Michigamea were killed, many others were taken captive, and the survivors fled to Kaskaskia. The village burned. When the Michigamea rebuilt their lives, they did not return to the ruins. Instead they established a new village nearby, at a place archaeologists now call the Waterman Site. For the families who lived through it, the attack meant the loss of homes, relatives and most of what they owned. For archaeologists today, it also means that much of the village's material life was left where it lay.

## What the Soil Still Holds

Because the attack came so suddenly, the Michigamea are believed to have abandoned most of their possessions, and because they never reoccupied the site, most of the artifacts that survived the burning are likely still in place. The river has since moved on. When the Michigamea lived here, the village sat on the Mississippi's bank, but later shifts in the river's course left the site a full mile from the shoreline. Today the ground is farmland, its soil a mix of sandy humus and gumbo. Its archaeological promise comes from its tight dates. Anything original to the village must fall within the same third of a century, so comparing Kolmer's artifacts with those from other French-influenced sites could help archaeologists date places that have no clear timeline. The artifacts may also reveal how deeply life in the village changed through close contact with the French.

## One Piece of a French Colonial Landscape

In the spring of 1974 the Kolmer Site joined the National Register of Historic Places twice over. First, it was named one of the most significant contributing properties of the French Colonial Historic District, which preserves 22 square miles of land connected to early French settlement. About a month later, in early May, it was listed individually for its archaeological significance. Its companions in the district range from ancient places such as the Modoc Rock Shelter to French and French-influenced landmarks including the Creole House, the Pierre Menard House, Fort de Chartres and the site of Fort Kaskaskia, as well as the Waterman Site where the Michigamea moved. Together they make up a region often described as without parallel in the United States for preserving the traces of colonial France, all the more so because the larger village of old Kaskaskia now lies beneath the Mississippi.

## Flight Context

The Kolmer Site lies at approximately 38.0917° N, 90.1808° W on the American Bottom floodplain in western Randolph County, Illinois, about a mile from the present Mississippi River shoreline and a short distance west of Fort de Chartres. Nothing of the village is visible from the air; the site is farmland, so the reconstructed stone walls and bastions of Fort de Chartres make the best visual anchor. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet above ground level, look for the flat, cultivated bottomland between the Mississippi to the west and the line of bluffs near Prairie du Rocher to the east. Sparta Community Airport (KSAR) lies to the east, and Perryville Regional Airport (KPCD) is to the southeast on the Missouri side. Fog often settles in the river bottoms on cool mornings, and summer haze is common.
