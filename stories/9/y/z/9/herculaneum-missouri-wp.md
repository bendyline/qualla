# Herculaneum, Missouri

Laid out by Moses Austin in 1808 to ship lead down the Mississippi, Herculaneum cast shot for the War of 1812, ran one of the nation's largest lead smelters, and then reckoned with the lead left in its streets.

**Location:** 38.2592, -90.3878

**Categories:** Cities in Jefferson County, Missouri, Missouri populated places on the Mississippi River, Lead mining, Environmental history, War of 1812

## Introduction

At the top of the bluff, molten lead poured through copper sieves and fell through open air. By the time the droplets hit the water basin waiting below, they had become round shot. Carried to a neighboring building, the balls were tumbled through cylinders until they were smooth enough to fire. By 1813 three of these shot towers stood on the limestone bluffs of Herculaneum, Missouri, above the Mississippi River, and lead from this town went to war. For the next two centuries, lead would make Herculaneum, sustain it and, in the end, poison it.

## An Amphitheater Above the River

The town was Moses Austin's idea. A settler from Connecticut and the father of Stephen F. Austin, who would later lead American settlers into Mexican Texas, Moses obtained a Spanish land grant in 1798 after learning of the area's rich mineral deposits. He brought equipment and workers from Virginia and began mining and smelting lead near Potosi, despite frequent conflict with the neighboring Osage. His lead needed a river port closer than Ste. Genevieve, so in 1808 Austin and Samuel Hammond laid out a town at the mouth of Joachim Creek. Austin is said to have named it after the Roman city buried by Mount Vesuvius in 79 A.D., because the eroded limestone ledges above the river reminded him of the seats of its amphitheater. Two centuries later, the town nodded back to its namesake with a volcano-themed playground called Kade's Playground and an adjoining Bay of Naples Splash Zone.

## Shot for New Orleans

The shot towers made Herculaneum's early name. Lead cannonballs from the town were used in the War of 1812, and the Maclot Shot Tower, the first shot tower west of Pittsburgh, supplied lead shot for the Battle of New Orleans. A bronze plaque on a boulder in Shot Tower Memorial Park, dedicated in 2003, marks it today. When Jefferson County was carved from parts of St. Louis and Ste. Genevieve counties in December 1818, Herculaneum became its first county seat, from January 1, 1819. It was a modest capital of thirty to forty homes, three stores, a jail, a courthouse, a school and the county's first post office, which remained its only post office for nearly thirty years. Then the town slipped. The county seat moved to Hillsboro in 1839, and in the early 1850s the St. Louis, Iron Mountain and Southern Railway passed Herculaneum by.

## The Smelter Years

Lead brought the town back. In 1887 the St. Joseph Lead Company chose Herculaneum as a smelting site, a railroad ran track into town, and in 1892 the smelter fired up its first calcine furnaces, two blast furnaces, a refinery and a powerhouse. It grew into one of the largest lead smelting operations in the United States, producing roughly 225,000 tons of refined lead a year, and the Doe Run Company later became its owner and operator. In a curious legal footnote, the town itself was revived in the 1970s. Attorneys showed that Herculaneum had been incorporated on July 27, 1819, two years before Missouri became a state, and on January 7, 1972, a Jefferson County court reactivated that incorporation. Residents elected a board of trustees that April and opened city offices in the old Douglass School building that September.

## Dust on the Streets

The smelter's wealth came at a cost that took decades to be counted. Herculaneum's soil and groundwater were contaminated with lead, and the problem drew national attention. According to 60 Minutes, Doe Run trucks hauled hazardous lead ore and slag in open beds, leaving lead dust on the town's roads and streets. In August 2001, local officials grew concerned about the residue, and state testing of road dust found lead at 300,000 parts per million, 750 times the hazardous threshold. In 2010 Doe Run agreed in a settlement with the EPA and the state to stop smelting at Herculaneum by the end of 2013, and the company said the cost of meeting tighter regulations left it no alternative. At the end of 2013 the furnaces went cold, closing the last primary lead smelter in the United States and ending an era in a region that had long supplied much of the nation's lead. Demolition of the smelter took place throughout 2018.

## Governors on the Bluff

Herculaneum, home to 4,672 people at the 2020 census, has deep roots in Missouri's political story too. Thomas C. Fletcher, the state's 18th governor, was born here. Daniel Dunklin, its 5th governor, who served from 1832 to 1836, died in Herculaneum in 1844 and lies with members of his family in a small grave site overlooking the Mississippi. The state took over Gov. Daniel Dunklin's Grave State Historic Site in 1965, and at about an acre it is one of the smallest sites in Missouri's state park system. Nearby, Dunklin-Fletcher Memorial Park honors both men with a sweeping view of the river the town was built to serve.

## Flight Context

Herculaneum is at approximately 38.26°N, 90.39°W on the west bank of the Mississippi River in Jefferson County, Missouri, south of St. Louis. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, follow the Mississippi south from the St. Louis area and look for the town on the bluffs where Joachim Creek enters the river. Nearby airports: St. Louis Downtown (KCPS), about 22 nm north-northeast; St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL), about 29 nm north, whose busy terminal airspace should be respected when approaching from the north; Bonne Terre Municipal (FAA identifier 1BT, no ICAO code), about 22 nm south-southwest. River haze and morning fog are common along the Mississippi.
