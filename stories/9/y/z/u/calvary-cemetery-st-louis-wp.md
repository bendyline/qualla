# Calvary Cemetery (St. Louis)

Born of a cholera epidemic on land that had belonged to Henry Clay, St. Louis's great Catholic cemetery now holds more than 300,000 graves, from the city's co-founder and Dred Scott to William Tecumseh Sherman and Tennessee Williams.

**Location:** 38.6974, -90.2336

**Categories:** Cemeteries in St. Louis, Roman Catholic cemeteries in Missouri, Archdiocese of St. Louis, 1854 establishments in Missouri, History

## Introduction

Cholera came to St. Louis in 1849 and killed more than 4,000 people. In the aftermath, city officials banned the creation of new cemeteries within city limits, believing the measure might keep more people from falling sick, even though the existing burial grounds were nearly full and had no room to expand. The answer lay several miles northwest of town, on a farm that had belonged to Senator Henry Clay. Today Calvary Cemetery covers 470 acres and holds more than 300,000 graves, among them a Union general, a playwright, a ragtime musician and a man whose fight for his freedom reached the U.S. Supreme Court.

## An Archbishop in Clay's House

Archbishop Peter Richard Kenrick, the first archbishop west of the Mississippi River, recognized the need for a new rural cemetery. In 1853 he purchased the "Old Orchard Farm" that had belonged to Clay. He set aside the eastern half of the 323-acre property for burials and kept the western half for himself, living for many years in the former Clay Mansion. Calvary opened in 1854 with Kenrick as its first president, and it is now the second oldest cemetery in the Archdiocese of St. Louis, which still operates it. As the graves multiplied, the cemetery kept acquiring land until it reached its present size. Along with its casketed graves it holds two public mausoleums and columbaria, plus private family mausoleums and sarcophagi.

## Older Than Its Own Gates

The ground was not empty when Kenrick arrived. Parts of the Clay farm had already served as a burial place for Native Americans and for soldiers from nearby Fort Bellefontaine. After 1854, their remains were reinterred together in a mass grave beneath a large crucifix at one of the highest points in the cemetery. Other Catholic burial grounds gave up their dead as well: graves at Old Cathedral, Rock Springs, Holy Trinity, Old St. Patrick's, New Bremen and others were dug up and moved to Calvary. That helps explain why its rolls include people who died decades before it opened. René Auguste Chouteau, the fur trader who co-founded St. Louis, died in 1829. Alexander McNair, the first governor of the State of Missouri, died in 1826. Two men on the list were killed in duels on Bloody Island in the Mississippi: Charles Lucas, who faced Thomas Hart Benton, and Thomas Biddle, a War of 1812 hero who faced Missouri Congressman Spencer Pettis.

## Four Travelers From Nez Perce Country

In 1831, four Nez Perce men traveled to St. Louis from their home in present-day Idaho. When they arrived they could find no one who spoke their language, and they had to rely on hand gestures to communicate. Two of them, Black Eagle and Speaking Eagle, fell ill and died in the city, a long way from home. Both are buried at Calvary. In 2003 a Lewis and Clark Bicentennial Grant funded a monument here to honor all four men, a belated acknowledgment of a journey that cost at least half of those who made it their lives. The cemetery is also the resting place of Pierre-Jean De Smet, the Belgian Jesuit priest who served as a missionary to Native Americans.

## A City Written in Stone

Walk the grounds and St. Louis history reads like a directory. William Tecumseh Sherman, the Union major general known for his "March to the Sea" through Georgia, lies here, and so does his wife, Ellen Ewing Sherman. So is Dred Scott, an enslaved man whose suit for freedom produced the landmark Supreme Court decision Dred Scott v. Sandford. Writers and artists are well represented, from the author Kate Chopin and the ragtime musician Louis Chauvin to the designers Charles and Ray Eames. Tennessee Williams, the Pulitzer Prize-winning playwright, died in New York City, but his brother chose to bring his body back to Calvary. Some names carry a smile: Martin Wilkes Heron, the bartender who created the liqueur known as Southern Comfort, and Mickey Carroll, one of the Munchkins in the 1939 film The Wizard of Oz.

## Room for Three Centuries

Calvary is still a working cemetery, and for many it is a homecoming. Former St. Louisans often choose to be returned here for burial, among them a great-great-great-grandson of the city's founder who had lived most of his life in Los Angeles. According to archdiocesan sources, space for full-casket traditional burials will last for the next 300 years. The cemetery also keeps a place for those whose names were lost. The Garden of Innocents is a section set aside for unidentified decedents, including a child known only as St. Louis Jane Doe, who was found murdered in 1983 in an abandoned building and has never been identified. She rests in the same ground as mayors, archbishops and generals.

## Flight Context

Calvary Cemetery lies at approximately 38.70 N, 90.23 W in north St. Louis, a broad expanse of green lawn and trees that stands out sharply against the surrounding city grid. The Mississippi River runs a couple of miles to the east, and the Gateway Arch downtown is about 5 nm to the south-southeast. The site sits beneath the St. Louis Class B airspace, so plan any overflight with St. Louis Approach; 2,500 to 3,500 feet MSL gives a good view of the grounds where permitted. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 7 nm west-northwest, St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) at Cahokia, Illinois, is about 8 nm south-southeast, and St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) at Alton is about 14 nm northeast. Summer haze and humidity can reduce visibility over the metro area.
