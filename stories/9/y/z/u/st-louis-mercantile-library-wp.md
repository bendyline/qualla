# St. Louis Mercantile Library

Founded in 1846 to keep young men out of trouble, the oldest extant library west of the Mississippi hosted the 1861 convention session that voted to keep Missouri in the Union and now guards Audubon's Birds of America, Chouteau's founding journal and a railroad library of more than 45,000 volumes.

## Introduction

The founders were worried about the young men. In December 1845 a group of St. Louis civic leaders and philanthropists set out to create a place "where young men could pass their evenings agreeably and profitably, and thus be protected from the temptations to folly that ever beset unguarded youth in large towns." Their answer was a membership library, and it opened on April 19, 1846. Nearly two centuries later the St. Louis Mercantile Library is the oldest extant library west of the Mississippi River, and its shelves hold everything from a signed first edition of Audubon's Birds of America to a plaster death mask of Napoleon.

## Evenings Well Spent

The State of Missouri chartered the library in its opening year, and James E. Yeatman served as its first president. It began modestly. By 1847 it held 1,600 volumes and counted 283 subscribing members, a small circle of readers who paid for the privilege. In 1851 it merged with the St. Louis Lyceum. Three years later the library moved into a new building at 510 Locust Street, on the corner of Broadway and Locust, and that building turned a reading room into a civic stage. Its 2,000-seat Grand Hall was the largest auditorium in the city at the time. The St. Louis Symphony played its first concerts in the library, and the lecture series drew speakers including Mark Twain, Carl Schurz, Ralph Waldo Emerson and Oscar Wilde, who came through on his lecture tour in 1882.

## A Vote for the Union

The library's most consequential evenings had nothing to do with books. In 1861, as the Civil War began, the first session of the Missouri Constitutional Convention, which had opened in Jefferson City, reassembled in the library on March 4 and voted against secession. In a state where human beings were still held as property, the stakes of that session could hardly have been higher. Four years later, another constitutional convention abolished slavery, and for the enslaved people of Missouri that vote meant legal freedom. The Mercantile Library had been founded to steer young men away from idle evenings. Within fifteen years of its opening, it had become the room where Missouri's course in a civil war was put to a vote.

## Six Stories and a Model T

In 1884 Robert S. Brookings began a campaign for a new fireproof building. The old one came down in 1887, the botanist Henry Shaw laid the new cornerstone, and in 1889 a six-story structure was dedicated on the same site. The library kept pace with modern life: it offered a telephone service, and in 1915 it launched a book delivery service using a Ford Model T. Then the city changed around it. When the free St. Louis Public Library opened in 1893, the Mercantile's mission shifted from being the city's primary modern library to preserving its major historic collections of books, papers and art. It leaned into that role. A dedicated railroad collection was established in 1983, built around the large personal library of railroad executive John W. Barriger III, and in 1985 a waterways collection followed, named for river industry executive Herman T. Pott.

## Treasures on Open Display

In 1998, after a process lasting several years, the library moved to the Thomas Jefferson Library on the campus of the University of Missouri-St. Louis, where it operates as a special collections library. Its holdings, which the city has named a City Landmark, fall into four groups: the general collection, the Barriger railroad library of more than 45,000 volumes, the Pott waterways library and an art museum. Much of the collection is kept in open storage, so a large share is always on display. The collections include the authenticated journal of Auguste Chouteau describing the founding of St. Louis, Harriet Hosmer's 1857 marble Beatrice Cenci, commissioned for the library by an anonymous donor, and four Charles Deas portraits of Winnebago (Ho-Chunk) chiefs. The St. Louis Globe-Democrat's photo morgue and clippings, more than 10 million documents, are here too, along with the photo and film archives of Trans World Airlines.

## Flight Context

The Mercantile Library is housed in the Thomas Jefferson Library on the University of Missouri-St. Louis campus at about 38.710°N, 90.311°W in Normandy, north St. Louis County. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL the campus is easy to spot as a cluster of large buildings and parking lots, but it lies only about 3 nm southeast of St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL), beneath the approach to Lambert's northwest-facing runways and inside its Class B airspace, so coordinate with St. Louis approach. Downtown St. Louis and the Gateway Arch are about 8 nm to the southeast. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) in Cahokia Heights, Illinois, lies about 11 nm southeast.
