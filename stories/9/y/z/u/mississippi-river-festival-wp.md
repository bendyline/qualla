# Mississippi River Festival

For twelve summers a hillside on the SIU Edwardsville campus became a sea of blankets beneath a circus-like tent, where The Who, the Grateful Dead and a rising Jackson Browne played to crowds that sometimes topped 30,000.

**Location:** 38.8100, -90.0000

**Categories:** Rock festivals in the United States, Music festivals in Illinois, Pop music festivals in the United States, Southern Illinois University Edwardsville, Edwardsville, Illinois, Music festivals established in 1969

## Introduction

Jackson Browne worked his way up this hill. In 1972 he played the Mississippi River Festival as the opening act for Yes, and in 1973 he opened again, this time for America. By 1977 he was the headliner. Somewhere along the way he recorded two of the songs for his live album Running on Empty in a room at the Holiday Inn in Edwardsville, near the intersection of I-270 and Illinois Route 157, just down the road from the festival grounds. From 1969 through 1980, this outdoor concert series on the campus of Southern Illinois University Edwardsville drew some of the biggest names in music to a grassy slope east of St. Louis, and by one estimate more than a million people came to hear them.

## A Tent on the Slope

The festival began in 1969 as a joint effort between the university and the St. Louis Symphony Society, and its home was built to suit an orchestra as much as a rock band. Designers George Anselevicius and George Dickie laid out the venue on a hill that formed a natural amphitheater. A large, circus-like tent covered the prime seating, an acoustic shell stood at the bottom of the slope, and a single entrance welcomed crowds at the top. Beneath the canvas sat roughly 1,900 director-style chairs arranged on white gravel. Permanent structures were few, but the entrance, concession stands and restrooms were dressed with large canvas sails designed by Gyo Obata. That first summer brought a surprise: in July 1969, Bob Dylan turned up for a short, unannounced set with The Band.

## A Sea of Blankets

Most of the audience never sat in a chair at all. They spread blankets across the lawn, and two pathways running from the entrance down to the stage gave people a way through the patchwork to find a spot. Students got in at a discount. For many, the day began long before the music, with a short road trip to Edwardsville and a gathering of friends in the parking areas around the venue, a 1970s youth version of tailgating. The lineups ranged across rock, folk, bluegrass and classical music. When the biggest acts came through, among them The Who, Yes, Chicago, the Eagles and the Grateful Dead, the hill filled, and some shows drew crowds of more than 30,000. Those who went remember all-day outdoor parties with friends and the chance to see top concert talent.

## Behind the Mixing Board

Big crowds on an open hillside need serious sound, and for seven years that job belonged to Bob Heil. The president and founder of Heil Sound was also a production adviser to national touring groups, the Grateful Dead and The Who among them, two bands that also played the festival. His colleague Ed Drone of Heil Sound mixed the house sound six nights a week for seven years, a punishing schedule. The festival's reputation rested on more than its setting. It became known for its central midwestern location, the natural ambience of its outdoor venue and the consistently high quality of the performers who came through.

## The Lease That Ended the Music

The end came through a contract. In 1978 the Nederlander Organization was hired to manage the facility and book its acts. Two years later, SIUE officials asked for more eclectic programming, including classical symphonies and operas, not just popular bands. It was a striking request for a festival first built with a symphony society. Nederlander refused. After negotiations broke down, the university decided to close the venue, and because Nederlander held a ten-year lease, the grounds could not be used for anything else. On May 22, 1981, SIU officials announced that there would be no Mississippi River Festival that summer. The 1980 season had been the last.

## Shadows at Dusk

The festival lived on in memory and in print. A book, The Mississippi River Festival, gathered its history along with an extensive collection of black-and-white photographs, and Dr. Stephen Kerber assembled a virtual exhibit of history and video for the SIU Edwardsville website. On June 13, 2009, the university marked the 40th anniversary of the first season with a picnic and the dedication of a plaque at the old festival site. The SIUE Alumni Association commissioned artist Steve Hartman to paint the scene, and his Mississippi River Festival: Shadows at Dusk became a gift to the former Director of Alumni Services. Attempts to revive the festival ran into funding and the other hard realities of staging such an event. Then, in 2024, after a 44-year hiatus, the Mississippi River Festival returned, this time at the Liberty Bank Alton Amphitheater in Alton, Illinois, and it came back for a second year in 2025.

## Flight Context

The original festival grounds lie at about 38.81°N, 90.00°W on the Southern Illinois University Edwardsville campus, east of the Mississippi River and northeast of St. Louis. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for the broad SIUE campus amid the farmland and suburbs of Madison County; I-270 crosses Illinois Route 157 nearby, and the Mississippi runs several miles to the west. St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) at Alton is about 5 nm north-northwest and makes a handy reference; St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) lies about 17 nm to the west-southwest, so stay clear of its Class B airspace. Summer haze is common over the river bottoms.
