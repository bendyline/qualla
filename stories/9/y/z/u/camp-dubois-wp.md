# Camp Dubois

Lewis and Clark spent the winter of 1803-1804 drilling their men at a frontier fort on the Illinois side of the Mississippi, deliberately placed on American soil while the lands they meant to explore still flew a foreign flag.

**Location:** 38.8027, -90.1012

**Categories:** Pre-statehood history of Illinois, Lewis and Clark Expedition, Mississippi River

## Introduction

When William Clark's men began building a winter camp on December 12, 1803, the choice of riverbank was not casual. They set up on the east side of the Mississippi, where the Rivière du Bois, the Wood River, flows in, because that side was still United States territory. The vast Louisiana Purchase lands across the water had been bought, but not yet handed over. For the next five months, Camp Dubois, near present-day Wood River, Illinois, would be the training ground and launch point for the Lewis and Clark Expedition.

## Waiting for a Flag to Change

The paperwork of empire moved slowly. Spain did not formally transfer the Louisiana Purchase lands to France until March 9, 1804, and France passed it to the United States only a day later, on March 10. Until then, the country the expedition was meant to explore was not yet American ground, so the camp stayed on the Illinois side. The land itself came through a local connection. In 1803, at Cahokia, Lewis and Clark had met Nicholas Jarrot, a well-known French citizen who owned 400 acres on the river du Bois, and he agreed to let them establish their camp there. The English name of the place, Camp Wood, is simply a translation of the river's.

## Clark Builds, Lewis Gathers

The two captains split the work. Clark established the camp with men he had recruited from Kaskaskia and Fort Massac, and together they built a frontier fort on the riverbank. Captain Meriwether Lewis arrived several weeks later. He had spent that time collecting information about Upper Louisiana and the West from Cahokia, Kaskaskia, St. Louis and other settlements, and he used the same visits to smooth relations with the Spanish authorities in St. Louis so that the coming transfer would go more easily. One captain was shaping a crew, the other was mapping the political and geographic ground ahead of it.

## Drill, Guard and Hunt

Camp Dubois was no loose gathering of adventurers. It was a fully operating military camp, and the men lived by the rules of the United States Army. They trained, kept themselves clean, policed the grounds and carried out the other duties the military spelled out. They stood inspections, marched and stood guard, and they went out hunting to stretch their army rations. When both captains were away at the same time, Sergeant John Ordway took charge. Over that winter on the river the Corps of Discovery prepared for what lay ahead, turning recruits into a unit ready for the journey up the Missouri.

## Upstream and Home Again

On May 14, 1804, the expedition left Camp River Dubois under Clark's command and sailed up the Missouri River. More than two years later, on September 23, 1806, the travelers came back to the camp on their return. The original site no longer exists in any form a visitor can reach. The rivers have shifted their courses since 1804, making it inaccessible. Instead, the Lewis and Clark State Historic Site in Hartford, Illinois, stands south of the true winter camp and, like the original, faces the mouth of the Missouri across the Mississippi. It holds a reconstructed replica of Camp Dubois and an interpretive center with an exhibition gallery and a theater. Perhaps its most interesting feature is a full-scale replica of the keelboat the Corps of Discovery used to ascend the Missouri.

## Flight Context

Camp Dubois is commemorated near 38.8027°N, 90.1013°W on the Illinois side of the Mississippi River, opposite the mouth of the Missouri River near Hartford and Wood River, Illinois. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, the confluence of the two great rivers is the unmistakable landmark, with the Missouri joining the Mississippi from the west. The state historic site and its reconstructed fort lie just east of the river. St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) near Alton is about 7 miles to the north-northeast and is a natural base for a sightseeing flight. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) lies roughly 15 miles to the west-southwest, so stay clear of or coordinate with the St. Louis Class B airspace. The old Chain of Rocks Bridge crosses the Mississippi about 5 miles downstream to the southwest. River fog can form on cool mornings, especially in spring and fall.
