# Kinloch, Missouri

Missouri's oldest incorporated African-American community fought for its schools, split from its white neighbors at the ballot box and then lost most of its people to the airport next door.

**Location:** 38.7403, -90.3231

**Categories:** Cities in St. Louis County, Missouri, Populated places in Missouri established by African Americans, African-American history, Aviation history of the United States, Cities in Missouri

## Introduction

History remembers her only as Mrs. "B." She and her husband are thought to have been the first Black family to buy a home in Kinloch Park, a commuter suburb outside St. Louis first developed in the 1890s. As soon as the neighbors discovered that the new owners were Black, they sold their properties, and new sales to permanent white residents of south Kinloch Park ceased. Within a few years, more than 30 Black families had bought into a six-block area that became known as South Kinloch Park. From that small foothold grew Kinloch, the oldest African-American community to be incorporated in Missouri.

## Paying Twice for the Same Ground

Kinloch developed through a land-purchase model similar to one used in Brooklyn, Illinois. The Olive Street Terrace Realty Corporation sold parcels to white buyers for an average of $150, and those owners then resold the plots to Black families for an average of $350. The arrangement let the company use the white buyers' loans as collateral for further bank notes, and to attract white investors it circulated testimonials from people who had paid $50 toward a parcel and received returns of $500 to $1,000. Black families paid the markup, and the community took root anyway. For much of the 20th century, Kinloch was home to a vibrant and flourishing Black community. The town hosted the 198th chapter of the Universal Negro Improvement Association, chaired by Elijah Woodson, which fostered Black-owned businesses in the area.

## A President in the Air

Kinloch's name also belongs to the early age of flight. The Kinloch Airfield is credited with a remarkable string of firsts: the first control tower, the first meal served on a flight, the first airmail shipped, the first parachute jump, the first aerial photo and the first animal airlifted. At an international air meet there in October 1910, former president Theodore Roosevelt became the first U.S. president, sitting or former, to fly in an airplane. Decades later, aviation would shape the town in a far less celebratory way. Kinloch sits along the eastern edge of St. Louis Lambert International Airport, and a noise-abatement buyout tied to the airport would eventually empty most of its streets.

## Schoolhouses and Color Lines

One of Kinloch's longest struggles was over its classrooms. Before 1902, Black students were using the 1885 Vernon schoolhouse, which had been built for the children of servants who worked for white families in Kinloch. In 1902 the white residents of Kinloch Park withdrew from the neighboring Ferguson school district and built the Nuroad school for themselves, handing their old two-room school to Black students. In 1913 Dunbar Elementary, named after Paul Laurence Dunbar, opened for the Black children of South Kinloch; it was still segregated when it closed in 1975. In 1924 Kinloch became the first community in Missouri to elect a Black man to its school board, the Reverend Walter Johnson, who later became a vocal critic of Kinloch's refusal to build a high school for its Black students. Another Vernon School opened in 1927, and although the U.S. Supreme Court had ordered desegregation in 1954, it stayed segregated until it closed in 1967.

## The Vote That Drew a Border

By the late 1930s, the district counted 543 Black students and 349 white ones, and South Kinloch was poised to elect a second Black board member. The white north answered by putting a split of the school district on the ballot, its third attempt to divide the district. It failed. Voters in the Black south cast 415 votes against and voters in the white north cast 215 in favor. The result led to the founding of a new municipality, Berkeley, which took in all of North Kinloch Park plus land to the northwest and formed its own school district. South Kinloch Park became Kinloch.

## Jet Noise and a Slow Return

Kinloch began to decline in the 1980s, when St. Louis started buying up property under an FAA noise-abatement program for Lambert International. Between 1990 and 2000, the city lost more than 80 percent of its population, and the 2020 census counted 263 residents on its 0.73 square miles. Rebuilding efforts have followed, with the community service organization Faith Beyond Walls mobilizing volunteers. Around 2005, the City of St. Louis, St. Louis County, Kinloch, Berkeley and Ferguson agreed on a plan to redevelop 600 acres at the northeast corner of I-70 and I-170, land that was once part of the airport buyout, with office, retail and industrial space that county officials projected would create about 12,000 jobs. The developers, NorthPark Partners, said they would make improvements in Kinloch and donate a new civic center to the town. Among the notable people associated with the town are Silicon Valley pioneer Roy Clay, actress Jenifer Lewis, singer Ann Peebles and California congresswoman Maxine Waters.

## Flight Context

Kinloch lies at approximately 38.74 N, 90.32 W, immediately east of St. Louis Lambert International Airport (KSTL), whose airport reference point is about 1.8 nm to the west-northwest. The town sits inside the core of the St. Louis Class B airspace and close to Lambert's approach paths, so any overflight requires coordination with St. Louis Approach and is best appreciated from an airliner window on arrival or departure. Look for the interchange of I-70 and I-170 at the airport's eastern edge; the redevelopment area lies at its northeast corner, with the small grid of Kinloch nearby. Other nearby fields include St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) at Cahokia Heights, Illinois, about 13 nm southeast, and St. Charles County Regional Airport/Smartt Field (KSET), about 12 nm north-northwest. Summer haze is common over the metro area.
