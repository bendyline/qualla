# TWA Flight 427

On a November night in 1994, a TWA jet bound for Denver struck a small charter plane that had taxied onto the wrong runway at St. Louis, killing the two people aboard the Cessna and prompting a lasting change in how controllers confirm runway assignments.

## Introduction

The airspeed callout had just come: 80 knots. It was 10:02 on the night of November 22, 1994, and TWA Flight 427, a McDonnell Douglas MD-82 bound for Denver with 140 people aboard, was accelerating down Runway 30R at St. Louis. Two or three seconds later, Randy Richardson, an additional crew member riding in the cockpit jumpseat, yelled, "There's an airplane!" A small Cessna was sitting on the runway ahead. Within seconds the two aircraft collided, and the two people aboard the Cessna were killed.

## Two Airplanes, One Runway

Flight 427, registration N954U, carried 132 passengers and eight crew on its regularly scheduled run to Stapleton International Airport. Captain Rick Carr was in command, with First Officer Randy Speed at the controls for takeoff. The jet had pushed back about 15 minutes late, but otherwise ground operations were routine. Elsewhere on the field, a Cessna 441 Conquest II operated by the charter company Superior Aviation had flown in from Iron Mountain, Michigan, dropped off its passenger around 9:40 p.m., and was preparing to fly home to Iron Mountain. At 9:58 its pilot asked to taxi for departure. The ground controller told him to "back-taxi into position" and hold on Runway 31, a phrase with no formal aviation definition. The pilot acknowledged without repeating the runway number. Then he taxied onto Runway 30R, the runway Flight 427 was about to use.

## Two Seconds, Then Contact

At 10:01:23 Flight 427 was cleared for takeoff, and at 10:02:27 it began its roll. Two seconds later, the Cessna's pilot radioed that he was "ready to go on the right side." When Richardson shouted his warning, Speed and Carr saw the Cessna at the same moment and hit the brakes, and Carr applied hard left rudder to steer the big jet around it. About two or three seconds after they first saw it, the crew felt the impact on the right side. The MD-82's right wing tore the horizontal and vertical stabilizers off the Cessna and sheared away the top of its fuselage and cockpit. Its two occupants, a commercial-rated pilot and a pilot-rated passenger who was married to a Superior Aviation employee, were killed. The crew aborted the takeoff and brought the jet to a stop on the left side of Runway 30R.

## "All That Later"

Inside the MD-82, the cockpit and cabin were undamaged, and no one aboard had been killed. Carr shut down the engines and immediately called for emergency vehicles. When he asked the tower whether there was a fire, the controller said he saw no flames, then began explaining that the Cessna was supposed to be on Runway 31. Carr cut him off: "All that later, I just want to make sure everything's safe here." It was not entirely safe. A large amount of jet fuel had spilled, creating a fire hazard, and the passengers had to be evacuated. Eight of them suffered minor injuries getting out. Remarkably, the airport authority never officially closed the airport. While passengers were evacuating, other aircraft continued landing on Runway 30L and taxiing nearby.

## A Preconceived Runway

The National Transportation Safety Board could not determine for certain why the Cessna pilot turned onto the wrong runway. Investigators weighed fatigue from the late hour and anxiety to beat deteriorating weather at his destination, but concluded that he had most likely formed a preconceived notion that he would depart from 30R, the runway he had landed on. The system around him did not help. The ATIS broadcast listed only Runways 30L and 30R for arrivals and departures, even though Runway 31 was occasionally used as a departure runway by general-aviation aircraft. The board also found that the ground controller's initial taxi clearance used non-standard phrasing, though it did not fault him for failing to ask the pilot to read back his runway, which was not then required. It cleared the TWA crew, too, finding their actions typical of normal airline operations. An NTSB spokesman reportedly said their swerve to the left before impact "avoided what would have been a much worse accident."

## Read It Back

The collision changed the rules. The NTSB recommended that pilots be required to read back runway assignments and that controllers verify those readbacks. In response, the FAA developed a change to its air traffic controller handbook to require controllers to obtain confirmation of the runway assignment from pilots after issuing taxi instructions. The board endorsed the change, saying that had it been in place that night, the accident might not have happened. It also urged a formal definition of "back-taxi," working ground-surveillance radar at St. Louis, where the newly installed system was out of service that night, and immediate airport closures after any incident until the situation is assessed. The jet itself flew on. N954U was repaired, passed to American Airlines after TWA ceased operations in 2001, and was still flying for American in 2017. The two people who died in the Cessna had no second chance. The readback rule that followed their deaths was meant to keep the next pilot from making the same wrong turn.

## Flight Context

The collision occurred at about 38.741°N, 90.346°W on the airfield of St. Louis Lambert International Airport (KSTL) in Bridgeton, Missouri, on Runway 30R. The site is on the airfield of a busy Class B airport, so it is best seen while arriving or departing KSTL with a clearance, using the parallel runways and terminal complex as the reference. The Missouri River lies a few miles to the north. Nearby general-aviation alternatives include Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) in Chesterfield to the west-southwest and St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) in Cahokia Heights, Illinois, to the southeast. Expect heavy airline traffic.
