# Chemetco

For three decades a Hartford, Illinois, smelter turned scrap into copper, until a hidden pipe, worker exposures and dioxins traced as far as the Arctic left behind a Superfund site.

**Location:** 38.8350, -90.0950

**Categories:** Corporate crime, Copper industry, Superfund sites in Illinois, Defunct companies based in Illinois, Dioxins

## Introduction

The pipe did not appear on any site diagram, and its exposed sections were covered with straw. On September 18, 1996, acting on an anonymous tip from a security guard, an Illinois EPA inspector found it anyway: a concealed 10-inch pipe in a ditch near the truck parking lot, water bubbling up from its mouth. Employees reportedly called it "the mystery pipe to nowhere." Investigators concluded it had been carrying metal-laden water from the Chemetco copper smelter in Hartford, Illinois, into nearby Long Lake since the mid-1980s. Its discovery set in motion the fall of the last large secondary copper smelter in the United States.

## Copper From Scrap

Construction began in 1969 on a plant first called Chemico, set within Madison County's American Bottom floodplain about a mile east of the Mississippi River. Renamed Chemetco in 1972, it occupied roughly 41 acres of a 230-acre tract and was built around three 70-ton gas-fired converters, with a fourth added later, each paired with a melting furnace. The business was recycling. Chemetco fed its furnaces scrap copper and residues, about 250,000 tonnes in an average year, and at its peak turned out about 125,000 tonnes of refined copper annually, a major share of the country's secondary refining capacity. Lead and tin ingots came out too, and the tankhouse sludges held gold and silver. By 1999 the company reported revenues of roughly $500 million. But what went into the furnaces mattered: the company admitted using low-grade materials, and its inventories show insulated wire and computer scrap going into the furnaces in quantity.

## What the Workers Breathed

Inside the plant, the danger came from the metal itself. Copper-bearing alloys often carried arsenic as an impurity, and at smelting temperatures it formed arsenic trioxide, which concentrated in flue dust and spread through the facility. A federal Health Hazard Evaluation by NIOSH documented overexposures to arsenic, lead and sulfuric acid, with arsenic overexposure recorded across several job categories, and found contamination even in eating areas, evidence that the plant lacked effective controls and basic hygiene measures. Beryllium posed another threat. Investigations documented chronic beryllium disease, and NIOSH found that 17 percent of the company's own air samples from 1981 exceeded NIOSH exposure standards. One affected worker had been charging small quantities of scrap into a sample furnace without respiratory protection, because his job classification never required it. These were the people who made the copper, and the hazard was facility-wide rather than confined to a few work areas.

## Smoke That Reached the Arctic

The pollution did not stay in Hartford. Illinois entered consent orders with Chemetco over air quality in 1988 and again in 1993, and state regulators later documented violations of the Illinois lead standard in 1997 and from 1998 through 2001. Records show the smelter charged its furnaces with large quantities of incinerator ash from burning PVC-covered electrical wire, a practice known to generate very high levels of dioxins and furans. In 2000, a Commission for Environmental Cooperation study led by Barry Commoner and others modeled more than 44,000 North American dioxin sources and traced where their emissions landed. Chemetco ranked among the highest single contributors at two Arctic receptor sites, Coral Harbour and Sanikiluaq in Nunavut, accounting for about three to four percent of the dioxin deposited there. Among the thousands of sources in the model, that put a single Illinois smelter within the top group.

## The Reckoning

The pipe drew in the Illinois EPA, the federal EPA's criminal investigators, the Illinois State Police, the FBI and the U.S. Army Criminal Investigation Command. Samples showed cadmium, copper, lead and zinc at highly elevated levels, and prosecutors concluded the pipe had been used to dispose of the plant's piles of so-called "zinc oxide," which EPA testing had classified as hazardous waste. Chemetco, its president and several employees were indicted in 1999. The company pleaded guilty to conspiring to violate and knowingly violating the Clean Water Act, and on appeal the Seventh Circuit ruled that a judge, not a jury, could decide how many days of violation to fine it for. Chemetco shut down on October 31, 2001, and filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy on November 13. Its closure marked the end of large-scale secondary copper smelting in the United States.

## A Mountain of Slag

No viable owner remained to clean up. The bankruptcy estate, working with federal and state agencies, demolished several structures and fenced the 41-acre industrial area, and on March 2, 2010, the site was added to the Superfund National Priorities List. EPA estimated about 452,000 cubic yards of iron-silicate slag and 62,000 cubic yards of scrubber sludge on the property, laced with lead, cadmium, copper and zinc, and material discharged into Long Lake and the surrounding wetlands had built up a sludge meters deep. A 2013 consent decree had Paradigm Minerals, the buyer from the bankruptcy estate, carry out a roughly $20 million removal action that includes processing slag on site under EPA oversight. The site remains a fenced Superfund site, with a remedial investigation and feasibility study under way and, as of 2024, an EPA target of choosing a final remedy in 2026. The cleanup bill has become its own saga: litigation named more than 300 firms that had supplied copper-bearing scrap or waste between 1969 and 2001, from small regional scrapyards to Fortune 500 corporations.

## Flight Context

The former Chemetco site lies at about 38.835°N, 90.095°W near Hartford, Illinois, on the flat American Bottom floodplain roughly a mile east of the Mississippi River, north of St. Louis. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for the industrial tract with its large slag pile and the wetlands and Long Lake drainage around it, with the Mississippi to the west. Nearby airports: St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN), just 4 nm northeast, is the obvious reference; St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) lies about 14 nm southwest and St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) about 16 nm south. Expect busy St. Louis terminal traffic, and summer haze over the river bottoms.
