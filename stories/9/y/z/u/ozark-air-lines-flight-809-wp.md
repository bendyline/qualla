# Ozark Air Lines Flight 809

On July 23, 1973, an Ozark Air Lines turboprop finishing a five-leg run from Nashville flew into a thunderstorm on final approach to St. Louis and was driven into a wooded ravine in Normandy, Missouri, killing 38 of the 44 people aboard.

## Introduction

At 5:42 in the evening on July 23, 1973, the tower controller at St. Louis cleared Ozark Air Lines Flight 809 to land on Runway 30L and told the crew the wind was gusting. A severe thunderstorm was sweeping across the approach. "Roger, we see it," the first officer replied. It was the flight's last transmission. Within about a minute the Fairchild-Hiller FH-227 came down in a wooded ravine beside a residential neighborhood in Normandy, Missouri, 2.3 miles short of the runway, and 38 of the 44 people aboard were killed. Investigators would conclude that a severe downdraft, born of the storm the flight was trying to beat, had pushed the airliner into the ground.

## Five Stops to St. Louis

Flight 809 was the kind of trip that stitched small cities into the national network. The regularly scheduled service ran from Nashville, Tennessee, to St. Louis with four intermediate stops: Clarksville, Tennessee; Paducah, Kentucky; Cape Girardeau, Missouri; and Marion, Illinois. That day Ozark flew it with one of its Fairchild-Hiller FH-227 turboprops, registration N4215. The legs into Clarksville, Paducah, Cape Girardeau and Marion all went normally. At 5:05 p.m. the flight left Marion on its final segment, and by 5:26 it had reached the vicinity of St. Louis. The approach controller vectored it toward Runway 30L at what was then Lambert-St. Louis International Airport. The crew knew there were thunderstorms around the airport. What they did not have, as investigators later saw it, was a timely warning of how severe those storms were.

## Beating the Storm

The warning signs were there. A Trans World Airlines flight landing just ahead of Flight 809 reported being caught in a strong updraft and was forced to abandon its landing and execute a missed approach. Yet the captain, as investigators reconstructed it, apparently believed he could land ahead of the thunderstorm. On the ground, one witness watched the turboprop suddenly climb about 400 to 500 feet and then rapidly descend to 200 feet above the ground, after which lightning struck its left wing. Other witnesses reported heavy rain and very hard wind. The aircraft appeared to perform several "evasive maneuvers" before it went into the trees. From the cockpit, the captain later recalled hearing something like hail hitting the airplane, pushing the throttles forward and pulling back on the control column. When the wreck was examined, it showed the aircraft had struck the trees in a high nose-up attitude.

## The Ravine in Normandy

The FH-227 hit in a wooded ravine next to homes in Normandy, near the University of Missouri-St. Louis, directly in the approach path to the airport. The impact broke the aircraft into several pieces. The cockpit ended up clear of the main wreckage, and four passengers were thrown clear; all four survived. The rest of the fuselage was broken open, and everyone in that section was killed on impact. The captain and first officer both lived. The first officer could remember nothing of the crash, while the captain remembered fire after the impact. In all, 38 people died and six survived. Those who died had set out along a route through four states that was meant to end with a routine landing in St. Louis. Reports circulated of a tornado near Ladue around the same time, but the Weather Service never confirmed one.

## What the Board Found

Investigators focused on two questions: why the captain had continued an approach into a severe thunderstorm when other options were open to him, and why the flight had received no timely severe-weather warning. Apart from two minor malfunctions the crew had reported earlier, an inoperative fuel boost pump and main inverter, they found no failure that affected the aircraft's performance. Instead, the investigators came to believe that a severe downdraft forced the plane below the glide slope. The National Transportation Safety Board gave the probable cause as the aircraft's encounter with a downdraft following the captain's decision to initiate and continue an instrument approach into a thunderstorm. That decision, the board said, probably was influenced by the National Weather Service's failure to issue a timely severe weather warning and by the improper assessment of conditions by the flightcrew and the flight dispatcher. Today Flight 809 is often catalogued among the airliner accidents attributed to microbursts, a term the 1974 report did not use.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies at about 38.719°N, 90.308°W in a wooded ravine in Normandy, Missouri, near the University of Missouri-St. Louis campus, roughly 2.3 miles short of the southeast end of Runway 30L at what is now St. Louis Lambert International Airport (KSTL). The area sits beneath Lambert's final approach corridor and inside its Class B airspace, so any low sightseeing requires coordination with St. Louis approach; the airport itself is the dominant visual reference, just to the northwest. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) in Cahokia Heights, Illinois, is about 11 nm to the southeast. Summer afternoons here still bring fast-building thunderstorms, the same hazard that brought down this flight.
