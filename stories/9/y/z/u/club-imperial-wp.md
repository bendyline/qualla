# Club Imperial

In a 1928 ballroom on West Florissant Avenue, George Edick booked Ike Turner's Kings of Rhythm, gave St. Louis the 'Imperial Style' of swing dancing and hosted part of the recording of the Turners' first live album, and in 2018 the building narrowly escaped demolition, though it was slated for deconstruction in 2026.

**Location:** 38.7133, -90.2556

**Categories:** Music venues in St. Louis, Music venues in Missouri, Buildings and structures in St. Louis County, Missouri, Ike Turner, Music of St. Louis

## Introduction

Jimi Hendrix lost a job here, and the reason tells you what mattered at the Club Imperial. Hendrix once played guitar in the Ike & Tina Turner Revue, and Greg Edick, who grew up inside the club his father owned, remembered him being fired for a solo so long that it "brought the dancers to a halt." At the Imperial, the dancers came first. Through its heyday in the 1950s and 1960s, this ballroom at 6306-28 West Florissant Avenue in north St. Louis drew Chuck Berry, Bob Kuban and the In-Men and, above all, Ike and Tina Turner.

## A Ballroom Built for Swing

The building is older than the club. Erected in 1928, the complex on West Florissant held a dance hall, a bowling alley and a restaurant, in a neighborhood that was then entirely white. That same year a Chicago-born man named George S. Edick moved to St. Louis. Decades later he bought the place, and on March 22, 1952, he opened the Club Imperial with the music of Al Tucker & his Orchestra. Edick knew what a ballroom was for. He booked swing bands such as Stan Kenton's orchestra, and he brought in Louis Prima. But the sound of the city was shifting under his feet. By the mid-1950s rhythm and blues was taking over St. Louis, and word kept drifting across the Mississippi about the exciting bands playing in East St. Louis, Illinois.

## The Buzz From Across the River

The buzz had a name. In 1954 bandleader Ike Turner had moved his Kings of Rhythm from Clarksdale, Mississippi, to East St. Louis, where he built the Club Manhattan nightclub. Edick heard the talk about Turner and booked his band to revitalize the Imperial. It worked. The Kings of Rhythm became the hottest attraction in the St. Louis music scene, drawing Black and white audiences to a ballroom in an all-white neighborhood. The DJ known as Gabriel, Mitchell Hearns, later summed up Turner's impact in one line: "Ike Turner just took over this area." Turner, he said, "created a ripple effect with his energy and ambition," sending word back to Mississippi, and Albert King and Little Milton followed him north.

## Live on West Florissant

Success eventually carried Ike and Tina Turner away. After their single "A Fool in Love" took off, the duo moved to California, yet they kept returning to play the Imperial from time to time. In 1964 they recorded their first live album, Ike & Tina Turner Revue Live, partly at the club and partly at another St. Louis venue, the Harlem Club. Remarkable musicians crossed paths in that room. Hendrix met the jazz musician Miles Davis at the club, and Davis remarked that Hendrix's guitar sounded like a "machine gun." Then, in 1966, the Turners were performing at the Imperial when the Rolling Stones paid a visit. The British band invited them to be the opening act on its 1966 British tour, and an evening on West Florissant Avenue ended with an invitation across the Atlantic.

## Dancing the Imperial Style

The dancers were never an afterthought here. In the 1950s a type of swing dance known as the "Imperial Style" originated on this floor, and Tommy Clements ran his studio, the Tommy Clements School of Dance, inside the club. Television came calling too: in 1959 Edick hosted a show called TV Party from the Imperial, broadcast on KTVI-2. The nightclub era ended in the early 1970s, when Edick closed the club and ran the building as a banquet and reception hall. His son Greg, who had grown up there, later took over ownership. Even so, the dance outlived the nightclub. After the club closed, dancers formed the non-profit St. Louis Imperial Dance Club, carrying the name forward. George Edick died of congestive heart failure on June 11, 2002, at the age of 86.

## A Reprieve in 2018

In the decades that followed, the building passed through different owners. In August 2017 Robert Vroman bought it at auction, intending to find a buyer who would renovate it. No one offered. By 2018 Vroman had concluded that the old ballroom was too dilapidated to save, and a beauty products company wanted to buy it, demolish it and put up a new structure. Preservationists fought for the site of the historic music venue. In January 2018 the St. Louis Preservation Board unanimously backed the denial of a demolition permit. The reprieve did not last. The city later took ownership of the building, its roof and both floors collapsed, and in February 2026 the Land Reutilization Authority moved to deconstruct and demolish it, citing stabilization costs of $18 million or more. A local preservation blog reported that it was demolished in early August 2026. The room where Ike Turner drew Black and white crowds together, and where the Rolling Stones found an opening act, now lives mostly in the recordings, photographs and stories it left behind.

## Flight Context

The former Club Imperial site lies at about 38.713°N, 90.256°W on West Florissant Avenue in north St. Louis, near the city's northern edge, and may now be a cleared lot. Even when it stood, a single ballroom building was hard to pick out from the air, so orient from 2,000-3,000 feet AGL: the Mississippi River runs a few miles to the east, and the Gateway Arch and downtown skyline lie roughly 6 nm to the south-southeast. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 6 nm west-northwest, so the site sits beneath Lambert's Class B airspace; St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) in Cahokia, Illinois, is about 10 nm south-southeast. Expect busy terminal traffic and frequent summer haze over the river.
