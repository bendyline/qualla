# Lewis and Clark Expedition

The Corps of Discovery wintered on the Illinois bank across from the Missouri's mouth, then spent more than two years crossing a continent that dozens of Native nations already knew well.

**Location:** 38.8027, -90.1012

**Categories:** Lewis and Clark Expedition, Exploration of North America, Missouri River, 1804 in the United States, Madison County, Illinois

## Introduction

The journey into the American West began with a winter of waiting in Illinois. Through the cold months of 1803 and 1804, the men of the Corps of Discovery camped at Camp Dubois, on the east bank of the Mississippi across from the mouth of the Missouri, waiting for the Louisiana Purchase to be formally transferred to the United States. The rivers have since shifted their courses and the original campsite is lost, but a reconstruction now stands at the Lewis and Clark State Historic Site in Hartford, Illinois. From that starting line, Meriwether Lewis and William Clark would push all the way to the Pacific and back.

## Portable Soup and an Air Rifle

President Thomas Jefferson wanted "the most direct and practicable water communication across this continent, for the purposes of commerce," along with a claim of American sovereignty over the new territory before European powers could stake their own. He asked Congress for money in a secret message, and it appropriated $2,500, of which Lewis spent $2,324 on supplies. In Philadelphia, Lewis studied medicine under Benjamin Rush, navigation under the astronomer Andrew Ellicott, and specimen preservation under Benjamin Smith Barton. The supply list ran to 193 pounds of portable soup, 130 rolls of pigtail tobacco, mosquito netting and oilskin bags. The men carried Indian Peace Medals stamped with Jefferson's portrait and a .46 caliber Girandoni air rifle with a 20-round magazine, meant partly to display American firepower. In Pittsburgh, Lewis bought a Newfoundland dog named Seaman, who helped with hunting, guarded against bears, and became the only animal to complete the entire trip.

## Upstream Into Contested Country

At 4 p.m. on May 14, 1804, Clark led the Corps away from Camp Dubois in a keelboat and two pirogues. Lewis joined them at St. Charles, and on May 21 the expedition turned up the Missouri with as many as 45 members, including York, an African American man enslaved by Clark. On August 20, Sergeant Charles Floyd died, apparently of acute appendicitis; he was the only member of the Corps to die on the journey, and he was buried on a bluff in what is now Sioux City, Iowa. On September 25, near present-day Fort Pierre, South Dakota, Lakota chiefs including Black Buffalo and the Partisan demanded tribute for passage. Warriors took hold of the boat and one of its oars, Clark brandished his sword, and the standoff nearly turned violent before Black Buffalo ordered his men back. That winter, at Fort Mandan in present-day North Dakota, the captains met a French-Canadian trapper, Toussaint Charbonneau, and his young Shoshone wife, Sacagawea, who gave birth to a son there in February 1805.

## Ocean in View

In the spring they followed the Missouri to its headwaters, crossed the Continental Divide at Lemhi Pass and the Bitterroots at Lolo Pass, then paddled down the Clearwater, Snake and Columbia rivers past Celilo Falls. On November 7, 1805, they sighted the Pacific. Storms pinned them on the north bank of the Columbia at a place Clark called Dismal Nitch, and on November 24 the party voted on where to spend the winter. Sacagawea and York both cast votes, and the group moved to the south side, where they built Fort Clatsop near modern Astoria, Oregon. In March 1806, as the Corps prepared to leave, a Russian expedition under Nikolai Rezanov reached the river's mouth, but its ship could not cross the Columbia Bar, and neither party knew the other was there. The Corps started home on March 23, 1806, and reached St. Louis on September 23. Spain, meanwhile, had sent armed parties from Santa Fe to arrest them, but the Spanish reached the Platte River many days too late.

## The People Who Kept Them Alive

The expedition's success rested on others. Lewis and Clark established relations with two dozen Native nations, without whose help they would have risked starving in the winters or becoming hopelessly lost in the Rockies. Sacagawea was not the expedition's guide, as legend later claimed, but she mattered as an interpreter. The sight of a woman and her infant would have reassured some nations, and she helped ease tensions with chiefs and give the impression of a peaceful mission. The captains' own journals called her and other Indigenous people by the slurs of their era. York hunted, hauled boats upstream and proved popular with Native people who had never seen a Black man. When the Corps returned, the other men received double pay and hundreds of acres of land. York received nothing. Clark refused to free him and allowed him only a brief visit to his wife in Kentucky before forcing him back to Missouri.

## A Legacy That Surfaced Slowly

Lewis and Clark drew about 140 maps, the first accurate maps of the region, and described more than 200 new plant and animal species. The Native nations they met, including more than 70 tribes, already knew those plants well, and the medals, flags and proclamations the captains carried were steps toward claiming Indigenous lands under the doctrine of discovery. Much of the science stayed hidden. Nicholas Biddle's 1814 edition of the journals omitted much of the natural history, and for 90 years it was the only printed account, so many discoveries were later rediscovered and renamed. Only with Reuben Gold Thwaites's publication of the original journals in 1904 and 1905 did the public grasp the full extent of what the expedition had found. Through much of the 19th century the expedition was largely forgotten; its fame grew in the 20th century and surged again around the 2004 bicentennial.

## Flight Context

The coordinates (38.803 N, 90.101 W) mark the Lewis and Clark State Historic Site at Hartford, Illinois, on the east bank of the Mississippi just across from where the Missouri River now enters it. From 2,000-3,000 feet MSL the confluence is the key landmark: the Missouri joins the Mississippi from the west, with the city of Alton and its river bridges a few miles north and the St. Louis skyline to the south. St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) near Alton is about 6 nm north-northeast, St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) about 13 nm west-southwest, and St. Charles County Smartt Airport (KSET) about 17 nm west-northwest. The area lies under the outer shelves of St. Louis Class B airspace. River valleys here can hold morning fog, and summer brings haze and thunderstorms.
