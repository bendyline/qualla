# Fort Belle Fontaine

The first U.S. military post west of the Mississippi rose on a Missouri River bluff in 1805, greeted Lewis and Clark with a 17-round salute on their last night out, and left behind a hilltop cemetery of soldiers and a Depression-era stone staircase.

**Location:** 38.8274, -90.2155

**Categories:** 1805 establishments in the Louisiana Territory, 1826 disestablishments in Missouri, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Former installations of the United States Army, Forts on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Lewis and Clark Expedition, Military installations established in 1805, Military installations closed in 1826, Missouri Territory in the War of 1812, National Register of Historic Places in St. Louis County, Missouri

## Introduction

Rain had kept them in St. Charles all morning. Toward evening on September 22, 1806, the Lewis and Clark Expedition, homeward bound down the Missouri, found something that had not been there when they left: a fort. John Ordway, one of the party's journal keepers, wrote that they "arived at Bell fountain a Fort or cantonement on South Side which was built since we ascended the Missouri & a handsome place." The artillery company stationed there fired 17 rounds from its field pieces in greeting, and most of the explorers spent the night quartered in the cantonment. It was Fort Belle Fontaine, the first U.S. military installation west of the Mississippi.

## Trade Goods and Treaties

The site on the south bank of the Missouri River is said to have begun as a Spanish military post. The Louisiana Purchase changed its flag, and a treaty changed its purpose. On November 3, 1804, William H. Harrison, acting for the U.S. government, signed a treaty with representatives of the Sauk and Meskwaki nations, and in 1805 the post became a government fur trading house. Rodolphe Tillier served as its factor, the agent in charge of trade, while Colonel Thomas Hunt commanded the troops. The store was not a small operation. William Clark, stopping there on that rainy homecoming, noted that the U.S. kept "60000$ worth of indian Goods" in its public store. The trading post was discontinued after 1808, but the soldiers stayed.

## Headquarters of a Frontier War

From 1809 to 1826 Belle Fontaine served as a military fort. In 1809 Lieutenant Colonel Daniel Bissell moved the original encampment to a new location, and the rebuilt fort, completed in 1811, included 30 buildings, several blockhouses, a rectangular palisade and an arsenal. From about 1809 to 1815 it was the headquarters of the Department of Louisiana and, during the War of 1812, the Army's regional headquarters. Its sister posts stretched along the frontier: Fort Osage on the Missouri near modern Kansas City controlled trade with the western nations, and Fort Madison, in what is now Iowa, controlled the trade of the Upper Mississippi. The war went hard upriver. Fort Osage was abandoned in 1813, the same year Fort Madison fell, and Fort Shelby was taken in 1814. Through these years Belle Fontaine also served as a starting point for many expeditions into the American West.

## The Soldiers on the Bluff

Not everyone who came to Belle Fontaine left it. The fort's cemetery was established in 1809, about 100 yards southwest of the cantonment buildings, and between 1805 and 1826 it received at least 30 to 40 military officers and about 100 enlisted soldiers. In 1904, newspaper stories, most notably in The St. Louis Republic, recorded the recovery of 33 burials with headstones and their removal to the new Jefferson Barracks National Cemetery. When W.T. Norton visited the bluff in 1911, he found the old burial ground strewn with rocks and mortar, the debris of tombs built of masonry about two feet above the ground. All were more or less in ruins, and most of the inscriptions could no longer be read. A considerable number of unmarked burials still lie at the now-decertified cemetery. Despite the similar name, it has no connection to Bellefontaine Cemetery, founded in 1849 on the road leading to the fort.

## Stone Steps to the River

In 1826 the fort was abandoned, replaced by Jefferson Barracks. The bluff did not stay forgotten. In the 1930s the Works Progress Administration built a "grand staircase" of stone steps from the riverbank up to the top of the bluff, along with trails and facilities. Today part of the old fort's site is preserved as Fort Belle Fontaine County Park, a 305-acre unit of the St. Louis County park system, and in 2016 an archaeological site associated with the fort was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The place also closes a loop in the Lewis and Clark story. In May 1804, at the very start of the journey, the expedition had camped on an island opposite nearby Coldwater Creek. More than two years later, they spent their last night out just a short way from where they had begun, and at about noon the next day they arrived at St. Louis.

## Flight Context

Fort Belle Fontaine County Park lies at about 38.827°N, 90.216°W on a wooded bluff along the south bank of the Missouri River in north St. Louis County, a few miles upstream of the Missouri's confluence with the Mississippi. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, follow the Missouri's final bend before it meets the Mississippi; Alton, Illinois, sits across the rivers to the north-northeast. The meeting of the two rivers is the best visual anchor. Nearby airports: St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) at Alton is about 9 nm northeast, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 9 nm southwest, so mind Lambert's Class B airspace. River haze and low morning fog are common in the bottomlands.
