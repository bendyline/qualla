# Coldwater Creek (Missouri river tributary)

A 19-mile suburban stream in north St. Louis County carries the legacy of St. Louis's wartime uranium work: radioactive waste traced to its bed, to a nearby park and to an elementary school.

**Location:** 38.8317, -90.2189

**Categories:** Rivers of St. Louis County, Missouri, Rivers of Missouri, Superfund sites in Missouri, Environmental history, Nuclear history

## Introduction

"It runs past schools, golf courses, and soccer fields." That is how St. Louis Magazine described Coldwater Creek, and on the surface the description fits: a 19-mile stream that rises in a small spring-fed lake in Overland and winds north through the suburbs of north St. Louis County to meet the Missouri River. What the description leaves out is what lies in the creek bed. Several miles upstream of its northern mouth, Coldwater Creek is contaminated with radioactive waste, a legacy of the nation's nuclear weapons work that reached homes, a park and an elementary school.

## A Stream Beneath the Runways

The creek's course reads like a roll call of suburban towns. From its source in Overland it flows north through Breckenridge Hills, St. Ann and Bridgeton, then disappears into a culvert underneath Lambert International Airport. North of the airport it bends northeast, passing through Hazelwood, Berkeley, Florissant, Old Jamestown, Black Jack and Spanish Lake before it reaches the Missouri. Its name is spelled two ways, Coldwater and Cold Water, and for most of its length it slips past the playing fields and fairways that St. Louis Magazine noted. The trouble in its bed began elsewhere, in a chemical factory during the Second World War.

## Drums Beside the Airfield

In 1942, Mallinckrodt Chemical Works reached an exclusive agreement with the U.S. government's Manhattan Engineering District to process uranium at its factory north of downtown St. Louis. That work produced radioactive waste. From 1947, the company and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers used a 21.7-acre property near Lambert Field to bury steel drums of waste from the downtown plant and other locations, and the tract became the St. Louis Airport Storage Site. Other material was stored on Latty Avenue, while Cotter Corp. worked with B&K Construction to dry material and transport it to Cañon City, Colorado. In 1973, when extraction was determined to be unprofitable, the radioactive waste was hauled in uncovered dump trucks to a landfill site on Latty Avenue and to the West Lake Landfill in Bridgeton. Material from the airport site and from Latty Avenue eventually made its way into the creek bed and onto many neighboring homes and properties.

## "In and Along"

The public reckoning came in December 1989, when the U.S. Department of Energy reported that radioactive material was present "in and along" the creek. The news halted a flood-control project that the Army Corps and local suburban communities had spent the previous eleven years planning. The material was traced to two nearby dump sites with a common source, Mallinckrodt Chemical Works, and that same year the creek was finalized on the National Priorities List of the Environmental Protection Agency's Superfund program. The findings kept coming. In August 2015 the Army Corps confirmed low levels of thorium-230 at St. Cin Park, which sits near the creek and close to Jana Elementary School, and flooding prompted further testing of soil and water along its banks.

## Counting the Human Cost

The toll on people has been harder to measure, and official findings have not always agreed. In 2004, Mallinckrodt employees who had worked with uranium used in nuclear weapons manufacturing became eligible for compensation under a federal occupational illness program, though they had to prove how much radiation they had been exposed to at the plant. In 2013 the Missouri Department of Health and Senior Services reported that from 1996 to 2011, rates of radiation-related cancers such as thyroid cancer and leukemia were not higher near Coldwater Creek than in the rest of Missouri, but a later state report, dated September 2014, found increased rates of certain cancers in the area around the creek. In June 2018, the federal Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry, part of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, released a public health assessment finding that people exposed to the creek's contamination, especially children and adults who lived near it from the 1960s through the 1990s, may face a higher risk of bone cancer, lung cancer or leukemia, although it said it could not tie any individual's illness to that exposure. In 2025, federal legislation expanded the Radiation Exposure Compensation Act to cover 21 Missouri ZIP codes.

## A School Goes Quiet

In 2022, the Boston Chemical Data Corp., in a report prepared in connection with lawsuits, said it had found high levels of radioactive lead and polonium inside Jana Elementary and in the playground soil. That October, the Hazelwood School District moved the school to virtual instruction, and the Army Corps announced it would expand its sampling to cover the entire property, with soil samples and surveys inside and outside the building. Follow-up testing by the Corps and a consultant hired by the district found that levels at Jana did not pose an immediate risk and did not exceed natural background levels, and the Corps repeated that conclusion in an April 2023 report. The two sets of results conflict, and in March 2023 the district said Jana would not reopen. The creek's cleanup is run by the Army Corps under its Formerly Utilized Sites Remedial Action Program. Crews were still at work along the creek in 2026, when the Corps planned to demolish a clubhouse at a former Hazelwood apartment complex to reach buried contaminated material, after Senator Josh Hawley announced in December 2025 that the Corps had committed another $40.5 million to the cleanup. Meanwhile, the creek keeps flowing north toward the Missouri.

## Flight Context

The story's reference point, at approximately 38.83 N, 90.22 W, lies on the lower creek in north St. Louis County, a short distance from where Coldwater Creek meets the Missouri River. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet MSL, follow the Missouri River's broad channel along the north edge of the county and look for the narrow creek corridor threading south through the suburbs of Spanish Lake, Black Jack and Florissant toward Lambert International Airport, where the creek passes beneath the airfield in a culvert. The area lies under the St. Louis Class B airspace, so coordinate with St. Louis Approach. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 8.6 nm southwest, St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) at Alton is about 8.8 nm east-northeast across the rivers, and St. Charles County Regional Airport/Smartt Field (KSET) is about 11.5 nm northwest. Expect summer haze over the metro area.
