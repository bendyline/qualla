# Cementland

On the grounds of an old cement factory north of St. Louis, City Museum creator Bob Cassilly began building a vast sculpture park out of concrete, machinery and other people's dirt, and died there before it was finished.

**Location:** 38.7342, -90.2155

**Categories:** Museums in St. Louis, Art museums and galleries in Missouri, Sculpture gardens, trails and parks in Missouri, Proposed museums in the United States

## Introduction

Most artists pay for their materials. Bob Cassilly found a way to get paid for his. Local construction companies used a 54-acre former cement factory site just north of St. Louis as a place to dump dirt, and after Cassilly bought the land, they paid him for the privilege. The sculptor, who had also created St. Louis's City Museum, shaped that dirt into the landscape of his next great project. He called it Cementland. It was never finished.

## A Park Made of Leftovers

Cementland was conceived as a public art exhibit, and an ambitious one. Giant concrete sculptures share the ground with obsolete machinery, and Cassilly planned navigable waterways among many other features. Much of the landscaping sits on the dirt the construction companies delivered. The arrangement was practical as much as whimsical. Cassilly was funding the construction himself, and the free material, along with the income from the dumping, helped underwrite what he said would "otherwise be an unaffordable project." Where another artist might have seen a dumping ground, he saw raw material, and where a developer might have seen an old industrial site, he saw the bones of a park. The ground itself was, quite literally, built from other people's leftover dirt.

## A Death on the Hill

On September 26, 2011, Bob Cassilly was killed at Cementland. The first reports said the bulldozer he was driving had flipped down a hill. Five years later, that account was challenged. In October 2016, Dr. Arthur Combs, a medical expert hired by Cassilly's family, concluded that "almost every rib on both sides of Cassilly's body were broken," damage that he said "could not have come from a bulldozer accident." The St. Louis medical examiner, Michael Graham, did not change course. "We will stick with our original findings," he said. "We feel this was an accident." The official finding and the outside expert's conclusion stand in direct conflict, and the sudden death of the man who conceived Cementland left his open-air work without its builder.

## Unfinished, and Sold

The Cassilly family said they hoped to continue construction, but the estate sold 16 riverfront acres in 2020, and in May 2022, nearly eleven years after his death, it sold the remaining 40 acres at auction for $785,400 including the buyer's premium. The buyer was not named, and the auctioneer expected industrial use. That same year, St. Louis photographer Richard Sprengeler photographed Cementland extensively as part of a series, preserving a record of what Cassilly had managed to build. What Cassilly left behind was an odd and haunting place: part factory site, part sculpture garden, part unfinished plan, and a monument of sorts to the imagination that began it.

## Flight Context

Cementland sits near 38.7342°N, 90.2155°W on the northern edge of St. Louis, roughly 8 miles north of the Gateway Arch and about 8 miles east of St. Louis Lambert International Airport (KSTL). The old Chain of Rocks Bridge over the Mississippi lies about 3 miles to the northeast and makes a useful reference. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, the sculpted earthworks and large concrete forms are easier to spot than any individual artwork. The site is inside Lambert's Class B airspace, so coordinate with St. Louis approach control. St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) near Alton, about 14 miles to the northeast, is a convenient general aviation base.
