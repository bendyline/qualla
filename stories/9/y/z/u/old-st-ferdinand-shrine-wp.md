# Old St. Ferdinand Shrine

In Florissant, Missouri, a convent, church, rectory and schoolhouse still stand where Rose Philippine Duchesne taught and prayed and where Pierre-Jean De Smet was ordained — a complex whose parish roots reach back to a 1789 log church.

**Location:** 38.7964, -90.3339

**Categories:** Properties of religious function on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Religious buildings and structures in Missouri, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, National Register of Historic Places in St. Louis County, Missouri

## Introduction

At midnight on December 24, 1819, Father de la Croix offered the first Mass in a brand-new convent chapel in the village of St. Ferdinand. The sisters of the Religious of the Sacred Heart had arrived just in time for Christmas, and among them was Rose Philippine Duchesne, a woman the Catholic Church would one day declare a saint. The building they moved into still stands at no. 1 rue St. Francois in Florissant, Missouri, alongside the 1821 church, the 1840 rectory and the 1888 schoolhouse. All four occupy their original locations, and together they form Old St. Ferdinand Shrine.

## A Place Called Fleurissant

The village predates the convent by half a century. Around 1767, just a few years after Pierre Laclede and Auguste Chouteau founded the fur-trading village of St. Louis, French plantation owners settled an area they called "Fleurissant." In 1782 Chouteau named François Dunegant "Commandant of Fleurissant," charged with protecting the settlement from attack. By then the Louisiana Territory belonged to Spain, to which France had ceded it in 1762, and a 1788 census recorded the settlement as "St. Ferdinand," with 40 people and seven plantations. Both names stuck. The area that became the city of Florissant was also known as the city of St. Ferdinand, and the parish at its center carried the name St. Ferdinand. In 1789 Father Bernard de Limpach, a Capuchin friar, began serving as its first pastor, in a log church that served as the village's spiritual and civic heart.

## The Woman Who Prays Always

The convent's story begins with Bishop Louis DuBourg, who arrived in St. Louis in 1818 to establish his see and went to great lengths to attract missionaries and teachers to the territory's settlements. One of those he recruited was Duchesne. Her convent was built in the Federal style under the supervision of Father Duran, a Trappist monk then serving as pastor, and it housed the sisters, their boarding students and classrooms where French, English and Native American pupils were taught. It is the site of one of the first Catholic schools between the Mississippi River and the Rocky Mountains, opened the year after Duchesne's pioneering free school in St. Charles. Duchesne lived here from 1819 to 1827 and again from 1834 to 1840. Late in life, after she went to minister to the Potawatomi in Kansas in 1841, members of the Potawatomi nation called her "Quah-kah-ka-num-ad," "Woman-who-prays-always." The Religious of the Sacred Heart left St. Ferdinand in 1846, and the Sisters of Loretto took their place the following year.

## Billed as the Oldest Church West of the River

In 1821 a new church replaced the old log one, and it is still standing, billed as the oldest remaining church building between the Mississippi River and the Rocky Mountains. The parish dedicated it to the Sacred Heart under the invocation of St. Ferdinand III and St. Francis Regis, and in 1832 Bishop Joseph Rosati, the first bishop of St. Louis, consecrated it. In 1880 the church was lengthened. It served the parish until 1955, and today artifacts from virtually every era of Old St. Ferdinand's history are on display in the museum and throughout the buildings. For a church that began in a small French colonial plantation settlement, it carries a surprising weight: this was the home of St. Rose Philippine Duchesne, canonized in 1988.

## Novices From Belgium

DuBourg also wanted teachers for boys and missionaries for Native peoples, so he invited the Jesuits, and Duchesne helped them settle in Florissant. In 1823 Charles Van Quickenborne arrived with a large group of Belgian novices and was promptly put in charge of the parish. Four years later, Pierre-Jean De Smet was ordained a priest in St. Ferdinand church. De Smet went on to travel 180,000 miles, visiting almost every Native American tribe in the West and crossing the Atlantic nineteen times. He established missions at the request of tribal chiefs and bishops, arranged peace between warring tribes, negotiated a treaty for the U.S. government and eventually befriended Sitting Bull. The Jesuits put down roots of their own nearby: in 1840 they built a large limestone building with walnut beams, the centerpiece of their St. Stanislaus Seminary until they moved away in 1971.

## Keeping the Doors Open

The complex grew slowly. The 1840 rectory housed Jesuit priests above first-floor parish meeting rooms, and a parish school went up in 1888, gaining a second floor in 1889. Then, in 1955, Old St. Ferdinand ceased to be a parish when its boundaries were redrawn. Supporters formed the Friends of Old St. Ferdinand, Inc. in 1958 to preserve it. Cardinal John Carberry, Archbishop of St. Louis, transferred all the buildings and property to the group in 1978, and in 1979 all four buildings were added to the National Register of Historic Places. When Pope John Paul II canonized Duchesne in 1988, the Archdiocese of St. Louis designated the complex a shrine in her honor. Record rainfall on July 26, 2022, flooded the site, putting about three feet of water into the convent, church and rectory and about two feet into the schoolhouse. The non-profit owns and maintains the complex, still restoring it, and offers tours of the convent, church and rectory, along with religious pilgrimages, by booking through its website, while the schoolhouse can host public or private gatherings.

## Flight Context

Old St. Ferdinand Shrine sits at about 38.796°N, 90.334°W in the historic old-town district of Florissant in north St. Louis County, Missouri. The complex is small, so orient from 1,500-2,500 feet AGL using St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL), whose runways lie only about 3 nm to the south-southwest; the site is well inside Lambert's Class B airspace, so coordinate with approach. The Missouri River curves a few miles to the north. Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) in Chesterfield is about 17 nm west-southwest for general aviation. Watch for summer haze and busy airline traffic.
