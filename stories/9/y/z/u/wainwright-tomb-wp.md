# Wainwright Tomb

In St. Louis's Bellefontaine Cemetery, Louis Sullivan's domed limestone cube, carved with never-repeating floral patterns, was built for a brewer's young wife who died at 34 and is counted among the architect's masterpieces.

**Location:** 38.6889, -90.2244

## Introduction

Charlotte Augusta Dickson Wainwright was only 34 when peritonitis took her on April 15, 1891. She was said to be the most beautiful woman in St. Louis, and her husband, the wealthy brewery owner Ellis Wainwright, set out to give her a resting place worthy of that reputation. He turned to Louis Sullivan, the Chicago architect who also designed the downtown office building that carries the Wainwright name. The result, finished in 1892, is a small stone building in Bellefontaine Cemetery that has been called one of Sullivan's masterpieces.

## A Cube Crowned with a Dome

From a distance the tomb looks almost severe: a gray limestone cube topped by a dome. Its walls are concrete, sheathed on the outside in limestone, and it faces northeast, where the entrance is guarded by a double-leafed bronze grille over a pair of doors. Each side has a window, also screened in bronze. Up close, the severity dissolves. A continuous band of carved tulips and leaves runs along the top of the walls, down the sides, and over the door, and the floral patterns cut into the limestone never repeat. In a building made to hold grief, the stone seems to bloom.

## Mosaic and Silence

Inside, the space is intimate and still. Two burial slabs rest beneath a mosaic ceiling, above a mosaic floor, so that the chamber is wrapped in mosaic between its stone walls. Wainwright commissioned the tomb for his wife and for himself, and what Sullivan achieved in so small a space has drawn high praise. One assessment called it "the most sensitive and the most graceful of Sullivan's tombs," and another described it simply as "one of Sullivan's masterpieces." Built for the same client as a downtown office building, it shows Sullivan's gifts working at the scale of a single room.

## A Husband's Long Road Home

Ellis Wainwright's own story did not end quietly. In 1902, eleven years after his wife's death, he was indicted for conspiracy to bribe state legislators and became a fugitive in Paris. He did eventually return, and when he died in St. Louis in 1924, he was entombed beside Charlotte in the mausoleum he had built for them both. After his death, an endowment was set up to pay for the tomb's reconstruction or renovation should it ever be damaged by an earthquake or vandalism, a provision meant to ensure that it would endure.

## Recognized in Stone

The tomb was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on June 15, 1970, and it became a St. Louis City Landmark the following year. Its significance rests on its designer. Sullivan, a leading figure of the Chicago school, left his mark on St. Louis in two very different forms for the same client: an office building downtown, now itself a National Historic Landmark, and this small, graceful tomb on the city's north side, where a young woman who died too soon still lies surrounded by carved flowers.

## Flight Context

The Wainwright Tomb stands at 38.6889°N, 90.2244°W in Bellefontaine Cemetery, 4947 West Florissant Avenue, in north St. Louis, Missouri. The tomb itself is too small to pick out easily, but the cemetery grounds make a good target from 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, with the Mississippi River a short distance to the east. This area lies close to busy airspace: St. Louis Lambert International Airport (KSTL) is about 7 nm west-northwest and St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) about 8 nm south-southeast, so coordinate with St. Louis approach. The Wainwright Building downtown and the Gateway Arch to the south make good companion sights on the same flight.
