# Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse

On December 10, 2021, a tornado tore through an Amazon delivery station in Edwardsville, Illinois, killing six workers who had taken cover in a bathroom other than the designated shelter, amid confusion over where to go, and raising hard questions about warehouse safety.

**Location:** 38.7661, -90.0361

**Categories:** 2021 disasters in the United States, Disasters in Illinois, Building and structure collapses in 2021, F3, EF3 and IF3 tornadoes, Workplace safety

## Introduction

The warehouse had two bathrooms that mattered that night. The northern one was the designated storm shelter at Amazon's DLI4 delivery station in Edwardsville, Illinois, and when a tornado warning arrived at 8:06 p.m. on December 10, 2021, the manager and an assistant set out across the building on foot, telling workers to gather there. But amid confusion over where the shelter actually was, ten people took cover in a bathroom on the southern side. Some twenty minutes later a tornado struck the warehouse, and its west wall collapsed onto that bathroom. Six people died there, and one more was critically injured.

## Holiday Rush at DLI4

DLI4 stood in the Lakeview Commerce Center off Illinois Route 111, near the I-270/I-255 interchange, about 13 miles northeast of downtown St. Louis. Completed in September 2020, the 594,000-square-foot building handled delivery logistics for the Greater St. Louis region. Ahead of Christmas 2021 it had around 190 workers across all shifts, most of them contracted delivery drivers, and the company had added more contractors to handle holiday demand. On the evening the tornado came, 46 people were in the building, and only seven full-time employees were on site. As conditions worsened, drivers got mixed instructions: one contractor said some were told to park in a designated area or go home, while others were told to return to DLI4. One delivery worker later recalled talking with dispatcher Kevin Dickey, who asked the worker to check on conditions in surrounding areas and then said to "get home safe."

## A Storm That Was Forecast

The danger had been forecast. The day before, the Storm Prediction Center had placed St. Louis and surrounding areas under an enhanced risk for damaging wind, large hail and tornadoes, and National Weather Service forecasters in St. Louis warned that storms could produce fast-moving tornadoes. At 5:20 p.m. on December 10, a tornado watch covering Madison County took effect until 11 p.m. That evening a single supercell thunderstorm produced an EF3 tornado that touched down at 7:35 p.m. and damaged Defiance, Missouri, killing one person. The same parent storm spawned the tornado that touched down west of the interstate interchange at 8:27 p.m., hit DLI4's western face at peak intensity, and brought down about 150 yards of the building. Rated EF3, with winds estimated at 150 miles per hour, it stayed on the ground for about six minutes. Elsewhere in Edwardsville the damage was relatively minor.

## The Night of the Search

Radar confirmed what had happened: debris lofted from the warehouse formed a tornado debris signature, and at 8:33 p.m. a new warning described "a confirmed large and extremely dangerous tornado" over Edwardsville. Surveyors later found DLI4 debris tens of miles away along the storm's path. First reports estimated 85 people inside, and Edwardsville Fire Chief James Whiteford called in mutual aid from Glen Carbon, Pontoon Beach, Troy, Granite City and other nearby communities, along with specialized rescue teams from Madison and St. Clair counties and St. Louis. A local television station estimated 30 to 40 emergency vehicles at the scene. In the northern bathroom, one employee said they sheltered for "at least two-and-a-half hours" before leaving. All of the dead were found in the southern bathroom.

## Six Lives

Deandre S. Morrow, 28, of St. Louis, had taken the day off, his mother said, but was called in to work. Kevin D. Dickey, 62, of Carlyle, Illinois, was the dispatcher who had told at least one driver to get home safe. Clayton Lynn Cope, 29, was from Alton, Illinois. Etheria S. Hebb, 34, of St. Louis, was the mother of a one-year-old; she spent her last minutes in the shelter talking with her cousin, a coworker, and when the tornado struck both were thrown to the floor. Larry E. Virden, 46, of Collinsville, had come back to restock his delivery vehicle, and according to a text exchange six minutes before the tornado, he had not been allowed to leave "because of the weather condition." Afterward, a family member tried without success to locate him through his iPhone. Austin J. McEwen, 26, lived in Edwardsville itself, the town where the warehouse stood.

## Rebuilt Without a Shelter

Scrutiny followed quickly. Wrongful death lawsuits questioned the facility's emergency plans and construction, the U.S. House Oversight Committee opened an investigation into Amazon's worker safety policies during natural disasters, and OSHA recommended safety drills, location-specific emergency plans and readily accessible warning devices such as megaphones. The city required the rebuilt warehouse to meet the 2015 International Building Code rather than the 2006 edition used originally, and its new walls were specified for 114-mile-per-hour gusts instead of 90. DLI4 reopened on September 4, 2024, about 1,000 days after the tornado. Yet St. Louis Public Radio noted that neither the rebuilt building nor any other in the commerce center had a storm shelter. In 2023 Illinois lawmakers formed a Warehouse Safety Standards Task Force, which recommended site-specific emergency plans and better tornado preparation.

## Flight Context

The DLI4 site lies at about 38.7661°N, 90.0361°W in the Lakeview Commerce Center off Illinois Route 111, near the I-270/I-255 interchange about 13 miles northeast of downtown St. Louis. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, the interstate interchange and the cluster of very large warehouse roofs around it make the area easy to identify on the edge of the American Bottom. Nearby airports: St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN), about 7.5 nm north; St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS), about 13 nm south-southwest; and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL), about 16 nm west. The December 2021 storms came with anomalously warm, humid air drawn in by a low-pressure system, a reminder to watch convective outlooks closely even in winter.
