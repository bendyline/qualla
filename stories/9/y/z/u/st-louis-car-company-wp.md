# St. Louis Car Company

For 87 years a north St. Louis factory built streetcars, subway cars and interurbans for cities from Chicago to Paris, and along the way turned out automobiles, flying boats and the capsules built to carry visitors to the top of the Gateway Arch.

## Introduction

A company that built streetcars also built flying boats. For most of its 87 years, the St. Louis Car Company did one thing on a grand scale: it made the vehicles that carried city dwellers to work on rails and under wires, from Chicago's elevated lines to the New York City Subway. Yet the same enterprise also produced automobiles, and its aviation offshoot turned out gliders, trainers, tracked amphibious "alligators" and gondolas for dirigibles. One of its most unusual jobs was right at home. In the mid-1960s, the company built the passenger capsules designed to ferry visitors to the top of the Gateway Arch.

## Rolling Stock for the World

The company was formed in April 1887 to build and sell streetcars, trolleys and other rolling stock to the street railway industry. It supplied transit companies in St. Louis, New York City and Chicago, and even the Paris Metro. Lisbon imported a batch of 75 cars of its São Luís type in 1901; they served until as late as 1973, and while most were scrapped, three remain operational in the Portuguese capital, and five spent a second career in heritage service in Detroit from 1978 to 2003. The catalog grew long: interurban cars, trolleybuses, gas-electric railcars and Peter Witt streetcars. One of its biggest hits was the Birney Safety Car, built from 1915 until 1930. From San Diego's Class 1 streetcars of 1910 to the Pacific Electric "Hollywood" cars of the 1920s, St. Louis-built equipment rolled through cities across the continent.

## Automobiles and Alligators

The factory never limited itself to rails. Between 1906 and 1911 it built automobiles, including the American Mors, the Skelton and the Standard Six. In 1917 the company joined with Huttig Sash and Door to launch the St. Louis Aircraft Corporation, which operated for about a year and then went dormant. Revived in 1938, the aircraft venture built gliders, trainers, amphibious tracked vehicles known as alligators, flying boats and dirigible gondolas until 1945. Meanwhile the main company kept building for the railroads. In 1939 it made Fairbanks-Morse OP800 railcars for the Southern Railway, along with experimental Fairbanks-Morse center-cab diesel switchers, and in 1941 it delivered the Electroliner for the Chicago North Shore and Milwaukee Railroad. Its New York work had already included two R8A revenue-collection cars for the subway in 1939 and, in 1938, the 50 World's Fair cars built for the IRT's Flushing Line ahead of the 1939 World's Fair.

## The Streamlined Years

Among the company's most successful products was the PCC streetcar, built from the mid-1930s until 1952. The design spread in several directions. In Chicago, St. Louis Car built PCC-based elevated-subway cars in several batches from the late 1940s, including the 6000-series delivered between 1950 and 1959, whose last cars were not retired until December 1992. The Illinois Terminal Railroad ordered streamlined interurbans between 1948 and 1950, and Cleveland's Red Line received its "Bluebirds" in the 1950s. The company's leadership passed from father to son when president Edwin B. Meissner Sr. died on September 12, 1956, at age 71, and Edwin B. Meissner Jr. took over. In 1960 General Steel Industries acquired the firm. New York remained a steady customer through the decade: in 1964 the company completed 430 World's Fair picture-window cars for the subway, 390 of them R36 cars and 40 of them R33S cars, while building 162 PA-1 cars, 110 single units and 52 trailers, for the Port Authority's PATH line to New Jersey.

## Last Cars Out of Hall Street

The Gateway Arch capsules, designed by Planet Corporation and built in the mid-1960s, tied the company to its hometown's most famous monument. Its final products went east. St. Louis Car built R44 subway cars for the New York City Subway and for Staten Island Rapid Transit, and the Staten Island R44s of 1973 were the last cars to leave the plant. In 1972 it also produced an R44-based State-of-the-Art Car, a rapid transit demonstrator set for the U.S. Department of Transportation, which now survives at the Seashore Trolley Museum. The company closed in 1974. Its assembly plant and general office at 8000 Hall Street have taken on a new life as the St. Louis Business Center, a mixed-use industrial and commercial complex redeveloped beginning in 2005.

## Flight Context

The former St. Louis Car Company plant, now the St. Louis Business Center, sits at about 38.711°N, 90.226°W at 8000 Hall Street in north St. Louis, in the industrial corridor near the Mississippi riverfront. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for the large industrial complex near the river; the Gateway Arch and downtown skyline lie about 5.5 nm to the south-southeast and make an easy reference. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 7 nm west-northwest, so the site is inside Lambert's Class B airspace; St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) in Cahokia Heights, Illinois, is about 9 nm south-southeast. Expect summer haze and busy terminal traffic.
