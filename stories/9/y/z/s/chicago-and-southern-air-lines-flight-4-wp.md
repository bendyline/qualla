# Chicago and Southern Air Lines Flight 4

Minutes after leaving St. Louis on an August night in 1936, a Lockheed Electra named City of Memphis flew into thick ground fog near the Missouri River and came down in a cornfield, killing all eight people aboard.

**Location:** 38.8076, -90.3709

**Categories:** Airliner accidents and incidents caused by weather, Airliner accidents and incidents in Missouri, 1936 in Missouri, Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 1936

## Introduction

Ben Knobbe had stepped into his barn for a minute when he heard aircraft motors "roaring wide open." He came out into the yard and saw a plane coming straight toward the barn, so low that it grazed a tall tree. "I was afraid it was going to strike the barn," the farmer told reporters afterward. A few minutes later the motors stopped. He looked toward the north and could see nothing at all. It was the night of August 5, 1936, and Chicago and Southern Air Lines Flight 4 had just disappeared into the fog along the Missouri River.

## The City of Memphis

Flight 4 was a regularly scheduled run from New Orleans to Chicago, with stops at Jackson, Mississippi; Memphis, Tennessee; and St. Louis. The aircraft was a Lockheed Model 10 Electra, registered NC16022 and named "City of Memphis." On that August evening it left New Orleans at 5:30 p.m. and worked its way north without trouble, calling at Jackson, Memphis and St. Louis in turn. Six passengers and two crew members were aboard when it departed Lambert-St. Louis at 9:52 p.m., scheduled to reach Chicago at 12:55 in the morning. One of those passengers was Vernon Omlie, a flight instructor and aviation entrepreneur from Memphis who, with his wife Phoebe, had run an aero service and flying school there. Years earlier he had been the pilot who carried Phoebe aloft for a record parachute jump. That night he was simply a passenger on his way to Chicago.

## Five Minutes of Silence

The Electra climbed out of St. Louis on a northerly track toward the Missouri River. Five minutes after departure, all radio contact with the aircraft was lost. Chicago and Southern's company radio controller called again and again without an answer, then notified the Chicago station that the flight was missing. On the ground north of the airport, people were hearing what the radio could not. Knobbe's account describes an airplane flying far too low, its motors roaring wide open, passing over his home and heading north. Then the sound ended. The flight had covered only a few miles from the field it had just left.

## A Swath Through the Corn

Newspaper accounts described the pilot apparently trying to nose up as the ten-passenger transport came down. It cut a triangular swath across a cornfield before turning over. Wreckage was scattered across a radius of about 50 feet. Part of a motor dropped out, and the left wing was thrown to one side. All but one of the occupants were hurled from the fuselage, their seats torn loose with them. None of the eight survived. For Phoebe Omlie, who had made her name competing in aerial events, the loss was personal: her husband and longtime flying partner, a pilot himself, had died as a passenger. By one historical account, Chicago and Southern had never before suffered a fatality.

## Fog on the River

A. S. Koch, a federal aeronautical inspector, conducted the preliminary investigation, and Major R. W. Schroder, chief of the Department of Commerce's airway inspection service, arrived to take charge. Koch pointed to the weather. "So far we have not found any evidence of mechanical failure," he said. "The indications are the pilot ran into trouble as a result of a thick ground fog soon after leaving the airport and crashed while attempting the circle back to the field." The investigation concluded that the Electra was turning at low altitude, close to the ground, when a wingtip struck the earth. On a trip that had gone normally all the way from New Orleans, it was the last five minutes that proved fatal.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies near 38.8076°N, 90.3709°W, near the Missouri River in north St. Louis County, roughly four miles due north of St. Louis Lambert International Airport (KSTL). Today the area sits close to Lambert, inside its Class B airspace, so any sightseeing flight requires coordination with St. Louis approach control; a view from 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL along the Missouri River works well. The Missouri River north of the airport is the key landmark, and it was toward the river that Flight 4 was heading when it went down. St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) near Alton lies to the northeast across the Mississippi, and Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) is to the southwest. Late-summer nights and early mornings here can still bring dense ground fog near the river, the very hazard the preliminary investigation blamed for the loss of the City of Memphis.
