# Ozark Air Lines Flight 965

On a clear March evening in 1968, an Ozark DC-9 and a training Cessna converged on the same runway at St. Louis's Lambert Field; the jet landed safely, but the Cessna's instructor and student were killed, and the investigation exposed how little stood between busy airliners and small planes in visual traffic.

**Location:** 38.7727, -90.3599

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 1968, Mid-air collisions in the United States, Airliner accidents and incidents in Missouri, Ozark Air Lines, Aviation safety

## Introduction

In March 1968, the control tower at St. Louis's Lambert Field had a radar scope, but in bright daylight it was useless. The display had been designed for nighttime lighting and could not be read in daylight, so controllers sequencing a crowd of arriving aircraft were watching them through binoculars. On March 27, in clear weather and strong winds, that gap helped set up a collision between an Ozark Air Lines DC-9 carrying 49 people and a small Cessna on a training flight, both lining up for the same runway. Everyone on the jet walked away. The two men in the Cessna did not.

## Two Aircraft, One Runway

Ozark Air Lines Flight 965 was a scheduled service from Chicago to St. Louis with a stop in Peoria, Illinois. The aircraft, a Douglas DC-9-15 registered N970Z, was less than two years old and carried 44 passengers and five crew. Three pilots were in the cockpit: Captain R. J. Fitch, with more than 24,000 flight hours; First Officer W. C. Oltman, who was flying the approach from the right seat; and a second captain, R. W. Traub, riding as an observer. The other aircraft was a single-engine Cessna 150F, N8669G, owned by the flight school Interstate Airmotive. Aboard were instructor B. L. Allen, 31, a certified flight instructor with about 381 hours, and his trainee, John Brooks, 34, who held a commercial pilot certificate and had about 174 hours of flying experience.

## Crowded Skies on Final

At 5:49 p.m., Flight 965 was handed to St. Louis Approach Control. Skies were clear apart from high, thin, broken clouds, but surface winds were strong from 170 degrees. The crew began an instrument approach to runway 12R, then reported the airport in sight, and at 5:55 they were cleared for a contact approach to runway 17 and passed to the tower. A minute later they reported a right base leg, and the controller warned of Cessna N8669G ahead and to the right. The pilots never answered, though the cockpit voice recorder caught one saying, "I don't see it (out there) at all," and another replying, "Naw." The Cessna, on a visual training flight, had been told to report on a right downwind leg. Spotting it near the jet, the controller told it to cross the final approach course and follow the Ozark DC-9, which he said might be "to your left and above you." The Cessna replied, "Six nine golf roger."

## A Thump Over the Approach

Seconds later, at 5:57 p.m., the tower called Ozark 965 again: "that Cessna off to your right looks like he's eastbound." All three pilots looked right and saw the small plane directly beside their cockpit. The captain tried to dodge it, but they heard and felt the thump of impact. The collision and the uncontrolled descent that followed tore off the Cessna's right wing, and its main wreckage and left wing came down in an empty parking lot about 6,500 feet from the approach end of runway 17. Investigators judged the crash nonsurvivable. B. L. Allen and John Brooks, an instructor and the student he was training, were killed. The DC-9 stayed under control and landed on runway 17, its right wing and a flap lightly damaged and smeared with paint from the Cessna's propeller and wing. None of its 49 occupants was hurt.

## Seeing and Not Seeing

The National Transportation Safety Board issued its final report on June 30, 1969. It found that Lambert had no published procedures for visual approaches: pilots simply called the tower within about 5 miles, and controllers sequenced them as traffic allowed, relying on everyone to see and avoid one another. Traffic and workload were heavy that evening, with visual flights landing on runway 17 from both directions, including at least two other Cessnas. Reconstructed flight paths showed the Cessna had not flown the pattern it was given and had turned directly into the final approach path, while the busy controller, without usable radar and with a poor vantage point, did not realize it. A visibility study concluded the Cessna should have been visible to the DC-9's first officer for 58 seconds, to the captain for 22 and to the observer for 12. From the high-wing Cessna, the jet approaching from above and behind would have been very hard to see, and the controller's call had led its crew to expect the jet above and to their left, not overtaking them from behind.

## Radar for Daylight

The board's probable cause statement named a chain of factors: inadequate visual separation standards in controlled airspace, the DC-9 crew not sighting the Cessna in time, the absence of traffic pattern procedures, the controller not ensuring his instructions to the Cessna were received and understood amid heavy traffic without radar, and the Cessna crew's deviation from their instructions without reporting their progress. Its recommendations followed. The NTSB urged the Federal Aviation Administration to install daylight radar displays at Lambert Field, to make better use of radar for approaching traffic and to establish visual traffic corridors at Lambert and similar airports. The FAA installed the daylight displays, worked toward a radar sequencing program and took the corridor idea under advisement.

## Flight Context

The collision occurred on approach to St. Louis Lambert International Airport (KSTL), about 1.5 miles north of the field on the approach to runway 17; the map pin (approximately 38.773 N, 90.360 W) marks the approximate crash site, placed from the NTSB's chart, where the Cessna came down in a parking lot about 6,500 feet from the approach end of that runway. KSTL is a busy airport inside Class B airspace, so any flight in the area requires ATC clearance and careful traffic scanning, the very lesson of this accident. The Missouri River lies a few miles to the north. Nearby alternatives include Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS), about 17 miles to the west-southwest, and St. Charles County Regional Airport/Smartt Field (KSET), about 12 miles to the north-northwest. Follow published procedures and ATC altitude assignments rather than attempting low sightseeing over the airport. Afternoon convection and haze are common in summer.
