# St. Louis Lambert International Airport

From a balloon field where Theodore Roosevelt became the first president to fly, to Lindbergh's airmail runs and TWA's giant hub, Lambert has lived through many of American aviation's major chapters.

**Location:** 38.7472, -90.3614

**Categories:** Airports in Greater St. Louis, Airports established in 1928, Aviation history, Concrete shell structures, Trans World Airlines, St. Louis County, Missouri

## Introduction

Theodore Roosevelt had ruled out a flight. Yet on October 11, 1910, at the first International Air Meet on a field in suburban Kinloch Park, the former president climbed aboard with pilot Arch Hoxsey and took off, becoming the first U.S. president to fly. The Wright brothers had brought six airplanes to the meet. In March 1912 the same field launched what is generally regarded as the first parachute jump from an airplane. That patch of ground, first set up in 1909 as a balloon base called the "Permanent Aviation Field and Dirigible Harbor," was the seed of St. Louis Lambert International Airport.

## The Golfer Who Loved Airplanes

The field closed in 1912 when the Aero Club's lease expired, and its grandstand was torn down. Aviation in St. Louis found a new champion in Albert Bond Lambert, an Olympic silver medalist in golf at the 1904 Games, president of the company that made Listerine, and the first person in St. Louis to receive a pilot's license. He promoted the city's flying field so vigorously that in 1923 it was renamed Lambert-St. Louis Flying Field. A young Charles Lindbergh got his first piloting job there, flying airmail for Robertson Aircraft Corporation, and he stopped at the field about a week before his 1927 flight to Paris. In 1928 Lambert sold the airport to the city, making it one of the first municipally owned airports in the country. The next year, according to the airport, St. Louis hired Archie League as the nation's first air traffic controller; he started out directing planes with a pair of signal flags.

## Domes Over the Ticket Counters

In 1956 a new terminal opened, designed by Minoru Yamasaki, with the arched, domed roof that still defines the airport. Lambert's own history calls it the forerunner of many modern airline terminals. Ozark Air Lines established its only hub here in the late 1950s, and when jets arrived in 1966 with the Douglas DC-9, the airline began its fastest period of growth. The McDonnell Aircraft Corporation worked right beside the field, and that closeness figured in one of the airport's most haunting accidents. On February 28, 1966, astronauts Elliot See and Charles Bassett, the original crew of Gemini 9, were killed when their T-38 trainer crashed while trying to land in bad weather. It struck the same McDonnell building where their spacecraft was being assembled.

## TWA's Crossroads

Airline deregulation in 1978 pushed carriers toward hub-and-spoke networks, and Trans World Airlines needed a primary hub. Kansas City's terminals were unsuitable and Chicago was already losing TWA $25 million a year, so St. Louis became its only viable option. TWA swapped three Chicago gates for five of American's St. Louis gates and outgrew the terminal so fast that it had to use temporary buildings, mobile lounges and airstairs. In 1986 it bought Ozark, and the merged airline controlled over 80 percent of Lambert's traffic. TWA announced in 1993 that it would move its headquarters to St. Louis. By September 1999 the airport had 515 daily flights to 103 destinations, and in 2000 it handled its all-time peak of 30.6 million passengers. To relieve the congestion, the city built a new 9,000-foot runway, 11/29. At $1.1 billion it was the costliest public works program in St. Louis history, and it required destroying about 2,000 homes, six churches and four schools in Bridgeton.

## The Hub That Emptied

The runway outlived the airline it was built for. American Airlines bought TWA in April 2001, and on December 1, 2001, TWA flew its last day of operations, including a ceremonial final flight to its original hometown of Kansas City before returning to St. Louis one last time. The September 11 attacks had already erased much of American's need for a hub that bypassed Chicago and Dallas/Fort Worth. By November 2003 service had fallen to 207 flights. When the new runway finally opened on April 13, 2006, the traffic it was meant to absorb was shrinking, and by 2010 Concourse B and the rest of Concourse D had closed. Other chapters ended too. On June 13, 2009, the last two F-15C Eagles of the Missouri Air National Guard made a low approach over the field and flew away, closing an 86-year chapter of the airport's history.

## Still Looking Across the Atlantic

Southwest Airlines stepped into the gap and still dominates the airport. When the city floated a new name in 2016 that would have pushed "Lambert" to the end, the aviator's descendants objected, and the commission settled on St. Louis Lambert International Airport. Transatlantic service returned with Lufthansa to Frankfurt in 2022, and British Airways began seasonal nonstop flights to London-Heathrow on April 19, 2026. In 2024 the airport served nearly 16 million passengers. A plan announced in 2022 would replace the old concourses with a single new concourse of up to 62 gates while keeping the domed terminal building, though in July 2026 the airport director said the gate count will probably be scaled back, and history still hangs on the walls and from the ceilings: a red Monocoupe 110 Special, built in St. Louis in 1931, floats over Terminal 2's ticketing hall, and Terminal 1 holds the 51-foot mural "Black Americans in Flight."

## Flight Context

Lambert (KSTL/STL) sits at 38.747 N, 90.361 W, field elevation 618 feet, about 14 miles northwest of downtown St. Louis between Berkeley and Bridgeton. It has four concrete runways: parallels 12R/30L (11,020 ft), 12L/30R (9,013 ft) and 11/29 (9,000 ft, opened 2006), plus crosswind 6/24 (about 7,600 ft). The airport lies inside St. Louis Class B airspace; VFR sightseers should contact approach or stay outside the shelf. Good visual references are I-70 along the south side of the field, I-270 just west and I-170 just east, the Missouri River a few miles north, and the Yamasaki domes of Terminal 1. Nearby alternatives include Creve Coeur Airport (1H0), St. Charles County Regional/Smartt Field (KSET), Spirit of St. Louis (KSUS) and St. Louis Downtown (KCPS). Watch for spring and summer thunderstorms; the 2011 tornado that struck Terminal 1 is a reminder of the region's severe weather.
