# West Lake Landfill

A former limestone quarry in Bridgeton, Missouri, West Lake Landfill became a Superfund site after Manhattan Project-era radioactive waste was illegally dumped there in 1973, and a smoldering underground fire nearby turned a slow cleanup debate into a community alarm.

**Location:** 38.7660, -90.4440

**Categories:** Superfund sites in Missouri, Radioactive contamination, Landfills in the United States, Environmental controversies, Environment of Missouri

## Introduction

There are no flames and, unless it reaches the surface, no smoke. Deep inside the landfill in Bridgeton, Missouri, a subsurface smoldering event is a form of chemical combustion that burns slowly through buried waste, often for years. In March 2013, high temperatures were measured more than 150 feet down across an area of about 20 acres, only 1,000 feet from ground that holds radioactive waste from America's first atomic weapons program. That combination has drawn heavy scrutiny to the closed, unlined West Lake Landfill, a Superfund site featured in three documentaries, among them HBO's Atomic Homefront in 2017.

## A Quarry Filled with Trash

The site began in 1939 as a limestone quarry operated by the Westlake Quarry Company. In the early 1950s, landfilling began, and the pit gradually filled with mixed waste. Nothing lined the bottom to separate that waste from the ground beneath it. Over the following decades the property changed hands, and responsibility for overseeing it, several times. It is now closed and divided into multiple sectors, including two operable units, OU-1 and OU-2, that have been shown to contain radioactive material. By one estimate, OU-1 holds about 940 cubic yards of such material at the surface and 24,000 below, while OU-2 holds about 8,700 cubic yards at the surface and 109,000 below.

## Leftovers of the Atomic Age

The radioactive material traces back to Mallinckrodt Chemical Works in St. Louis, whose uranium work for the Manhattan Project and later nuclear weapons production left behind leached barium sulfate. In 1973, B&K Construction Co., working under contract for the Cotter Corporation, took part of the radioactive material that had been kept at a nearby storage facility and dumped it at the landfill. About 8,700 short tons of the leached barium sulfate, the material with the lowest relative radioactivity, were mixed with some 39,000 short tons of topsoil to dilute it. The dumping was illegal. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission discovered it, investigated, and published a report in 1977. More than a decade later, on October 28, 1989, the site was proposed for the Superfund list, and it was formally listed on August 30, 1990.

## Cap or Dig?

The EPA named four potentially responsible parties: the U.S. Department of Energy, the Cotter Corporation, and two Republic Services subsidiaries, Bridgeton Landfill and Rock Road Industries. A remedial investigation report on OU-1 followed in April 2000 and a feasibility study in May 2006. In May 2008 the agency issued its record of decision, choosing to contain the waste under a multilayer cover spread over 40 acres of OU-1 and to monitor groundwater over the long term. After further comments from environmental groups and the public, the EPA asked the responsible parties to study alternative cleanup options. Their supplemental feasibility study was released to the public in December 2011, and in 2015 crews began gamma cone penetration testing and sonic core sampling to map the extent of the radiologically impacted material.

## Fire Without Flame

The smoldering event in the OU-2 area sharpened every worry. Excavating an area as large as the 2013 hot zone to put out the reaction would be difficult, if not impossible, and would likely increase toxic fumes and the risk of the reaction breaking through to the surface. In September 2013, Republic Services ordered an isolation barrier built to keep smoldering waste from reaching the radioactive material in OU-1. In December 2015, the EPA, working with the Missouri Department of Natural Resources, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and others, decided to install a physical isolation barrier along with other controls such as cooling loops. The landfill's owners also planned a cap over the North Quarry, with trenches to capture liquid and gas beneath it. Monitoring offered some reassurance: radon levels were well below those that cause lung cancer, nearby groundwater moves away from surrounding communities and is not used for drinking, and soil samples showed no evidence of radioactive contamination.

## Excavation Plus

Pressure for a fuller cleanup kept building. In mid-2016 a movement pushed to shift the site to the Formerly Utilized Sites Remedial Action Program, established in 1974 to clean up radioactive waste from early U.S. nuclear work and administered by the Army Corps of Engineers, though the corps' director of civil works, Steven Stockton, said the change would not speed remediation. Then, in February 2018, EPA head Scott Pruitt announced a proposed plan known as "Excavation Plus." It called for removing radioactively impacted material more concentrated than 52.9 picocuries per gram, down to a maximum depth of 16 feet, taking out about 67 percent of the landfill's radioactivity over five years at an estimated cost of $236 million, with the responsible parties liable for the costs. A new cover system would then protect the Bridgeton community for the long term. EPA adopted the excavation remedy later in 2018 in an amended record of decision. Excavation had not yet begun when, on March 24, 2026, EPA announced that the responsible parties had finished pre-excavation confirmation sampling; the agency projects that digging could start in 2027.

## Flight Context

West Lake Landfill lies at approximately 38.766 N, 90.444 W in Bridgeton, Missouri, about 4 miles west of the runways of St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL). From the air, look for the large closed landfill a couple of miles east of the Missouri River. Because it sits so close to KSTL, the area lies within busy Class B airspace; overflight will normally be at altitudes assigned by ATC, and any low sightseeing is inappropriate. Nearby general aviation alternatives include St. Charles County Regional Airport/Smartt Field (KSET), about 11 miles to the north, and Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS), about 13 miles to the southwest. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are the main visibility concerns.
