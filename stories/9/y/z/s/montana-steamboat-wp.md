# Montana (steamboat)

Built in 1879 as one of three giant stern-wheelers at the tail end of the Missouri's steamboat era, the Montana sank near Bridgeton five years later, and its ribs still rise from the Missouri River whenever the water drops low enough.

**Location:** 38.7947, -90.4669

**Categories:** Paddle steamers, 1879 ships, Steamboats of the Missouri River

## Introduction

"The average lifespan of a steamboat on the Missouri River was five years," Lindenwood University anthropology professor Steve Dasovich told reporters in 2012. The Montana hit that average almost exactly. Built in 1879 as one of three "mega-steamboats" on the Missouri, it went down near Bridgeton, Missouri, in June 1884. Yet the Montana has refused to stay entirely out of sight. Every so often the river drops, the sand shifts, and the skeleton of a 250-foot stern-wheeler rises back into the open air, more than a century after its last voyage.

## A Leviathan for the Plains

The Montana came off the ways in 1879 alongside two sister boats, the Wyoming and the Dakota. Their timing was late. The steamboat era on the Missouri was near its end, and the nation's expanding railroad network was about to take its trade away. Its advantage was size. The Montana measured 250 feet long, not counting its paddle wheel, and 48.8 feet wide, with a registered tonnage of 959 tons. News accounts would later describe it as an elaborate steamer as long as a football field, with lavish touches aimed at pleasing a mostly wealthy clientele. For five years that scale let the Montana haul freight on the difficult Missouri River trade. But the rails kept closing the gap, and the big boat's working life turned out to be short.

## Bridge or Snag?

On June 22, 1884, the Montana met its end near Bridgeton. What exactly sank it depends on whom you ask. Some accounts say it collided with the Wabash Bridge; others blame a snag, a submerged tree lurking beneath the surface, and some suggest both. In 2012 Dasovich was quoted attributing the wreck to the boat's striking a submerged tree rather than the bridge. Whatever the cause, the stricken Montana was beached on the Bridgeton side of the river so its cargo could be unloaded. There the hull stayed. In the years that followed, its rotting frame was repeatedly buried and uncovered as the riverbanks shifted, a slow-motion burial that the Missouri never quite finished.

## The Skeleton in the Sand

In the early 2000s the wreck surfaced again, and at Dasovich's invitation members of East Carolina University's Maritime History Program excavated and investigated it. Television followed. In September 2003 the History Channel's Deep Sea Detectives devoted an episode to it, titled "Skeleton in the Sand: The Montana," and in December 2004 the boat appeared in the Wild West Tech episode "Biggest Machines in the West." Annalies Corbin later published a book-length study, The Steamboat Montana and the Opening of the West, published in 2008. Then, in the drought of 2012, low water exposed the wreck once more near St. Charles, and it made the news all over again. The Montana is not alone down there. Dasovich estimated that the remains of 500 to 700 steamboats sit at the bottom of the Missouri River.

## Flight Context

The Montana's wreck site lies near 38.7947°N, 90.4669°W on the Missouri River between Bridgeton, on the St. Louis County side, and St. Charles on the opposite bank. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, follow the river's course and watch the sandbars and banks along the Bridgeton side; the wreck itself shows only during unusually low water, as in the drought of 2012. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) lies about 6 miles to the east-southeast, so the site is within Class B airspace and requires coordination with approach control. Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) in Chesterfield is roughly 13 miles to the southwest. Morning fog is common along the river in late summer and fall.
