# Far West (steamship)

The shallow-draft sternwheeler Far West served as the Army's floating headquarters before the Battle of the Little Bighorn, then raced 710 river miles in 54 hours carrying the wounded and the first news of Custer's defeat, before sinking near St. Charles, Missouri, in 1883.

**Location:** 38.7848, -90.4764

**Categories:** Steamboats of the Missouri River, Shipwrecks of the Missouri River, Paddle steamers of the United States, Great Sioux War of 1876, Military history

## Introduction

In the last days of June 1876, a Crow scout named Curley stepped out of the willows along the riverbank and climbed aboard the steamboat Far West, tied up at the mouth of the Little Bighorn River. Her crew had no idea what had happened upriver. With drawings and signs, Curley made them understand: Custer had been "wiped out." Within days, the little sternwheeler would carry the wounded and the news of the Battle of the Little Bighorn down the Yellowstone and Missouri Rivers in record time. Seven years later she sank far downstream, near St. Charles, Missouri.

## Built to Float on a Heavy Dew

The Far West was built in Pittsburgh in 1870 for the Coulson Packet Company, and she was designed for the shallow waters of the upper Missouri and Yellowstone. At 190 feet long with a 33-foot beam, three decks, a cupola-like pilot house and two tall smokestacks, she drew only 20 inches of water empty and just 30 inches carrying 200 tons of freight. She was light, strong and speedy. That first year she ran to and from Fort Benton, Montana, the upper terminus of river travel on the Missouri, and in 1872, with Mark Coulson as master, she made the quickest trip on record from Sioux City, Iowa, to Fort Benton: seventeen days and twenty hours.

## Headquarters on the Yellowstone

In 1876, during the Great Sioux War, the Army contracted the Far West to support the expedition under General Alfred Terry, Colonel John Gibbon and Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer, which was hunting a large Sioux and Cheyenne encampment moving through the river valleys to the south. Captained by Grant Marsh, the boat hauled supplies from the railhead at Bismarck, Dakota Territory, up the Missouri and Yellowstone, meeting the column at the mouth of the Powder River near present-day Terry, Montana. Terry made her his headquarters and used her to ferry troops. On June 21, moored at the mouth of Rosebud Creek, the Far West hosted the fateful officers' meeting that mapped out the campaign's next steps. The next day, Custer and the 7th Cavalry rode south up the Rosebud, while the boat was ordered up the Yellowstone and Bighorn to the mouth of the Little Bighorn to keep supplies close.

## A Deck Turned into a Hospital

On June 25, Custer found the Sioux and Cheyenne village on the Little Bighorn and suffered a disastrous defeat. Five companies of the 7th Cavalry were destroyed along with Custer, and the rest suffered many dead and wounded. Unaware, Marsh had the Far West on station at the mouth of the Little Bighorn by late June, which is where Curley found her. The steamboat became a refuge for the wounded soldiers. Cordwood and grain sacks were stacked along the gunwales to shield the wounded from possible attack, and sheets of boiler iron were placed around the pilot house. For days the boat waited as the injured men were brought aboard, a floating field hospital deep in Montana Territory.

## Fifty-Four Hours to Bismarck

At 5 p.m. on July 3, Terry ordered the Far West to carry the wounded to Bismarck, 710 river miles away. Marsh took her down the Yellowstone and then the Missouri under a full head of steam, traveling night and day with only brief stops. At 11 p.m. on July 5 she reached Bismarck, completing the run in a record 54 hours. She also carried the first news of the defeat, which came to be popularly known as the "Custer Massacre." The report went straight onto the telegraph and reached the East on the afternoon of July 6, as the nation was still celebrating the centennial of its independence. Just nine days later, Marsh and the Far West were steaming back upriver with horses and supplies for Terry's column.

## The Snag at St. Charles

After 1876 the Coulson line sold the boat to the Northwest Transportation Company, known as the Peck Line, and she later passed to Captain Henry M. Dodds and Victor Bonnet. The Far West kept carrying freight and passengers on the upper Missouri and Yellowstone, and she kept setting records. In 1881 the Missouri ran so high that upriver arrivals were delayed, yet the Far West was still the first boat to reach Fort Benton that year, on May 19. Her end came in October 1883, when she struck a snag in the Missouri near St. Charles and was lost. By then railroads were taking over transport on the northern plains, and the Far West had become an iconic symbol of the shallow-draft steamboats that served the frontier before them.

## Flight Context

The recorded location of the Far West's loss is near 38.7848 N, 90.4764 W on the Missouri River at St. Charles, Missouri, though accounts of the exact spot differ (a Lindenwood University search team put it about five miles upstream of St. Charles, while a river-steamer register lists Mullanphy Bend about seven miles below the city); nothing of the wreck is visible from the air. Use the broad Missouri River and the St. Charles riverfront as references. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is only about 6 miles to the east-southeast, so this area lies close beneath its busy Class B airspace and any low sightseeing requires coordination with ATC. St. Charles County Regional Airport/Smartt Field (KSET) is about 10 miles to the north, and Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) about 13 miles to the southwest. A viewing altitude of 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, as ATC permits, shows the river's curves well. River fog is common on autumn mornings.
