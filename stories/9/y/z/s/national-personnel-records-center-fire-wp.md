# National Personnel Records Center fire

In July 1973 a fire in suburban St. Louis destroyed some 16 to 18 million U.S. military service records that had never been copied, and the paper that survived was dried in a chamber built to test space capsules.

## Introduction

Of the Army personnel files for soldiers discharged between November 1912 and January 1960, roughly 80 percent were lost. They did not fade or go missing one by one. They burned together, in a single week in July 1973, on the sixth floor of the Military Personnel Records Center in Overland, a suburb of St. Louis. No duplicate copies had ever been kept, and no microfilm had been made. For millions of American veterans and their families, the record of their service became, overnight, something that would have to be pieced back together from other sources.

## Shortly After Midnight

The fire was reported shortly after midnight on July 12, 1973. Firefighters managed to reach the burning sixth floor, but the heat and smoke forced them to withdraw, and for 22 hours the blaze burned out of control. Crews from 42 fire districts eventually joined the fight, struggling against insufficient water pressure as they poured millions of gallons of water into the building. Even then the fire refused to die quickly. It was not until July 16, nearly four and a half days after the first reports, that the local fire department declared it officially out. By then the sixth floor was almost entirely destroyed, and many records the flames had spared were soaked by the water used to stop them.

## Sixteen Million Service Records

The building was the custodian of U.S. military service records, part of the National Personnel Records Center. The building had opened in 1956 as the Defense Department's Military Personnel Records Center. In 1966 that center was merged with the St. Louis Federal Records Center to form the NPRC, which handled the records of people in federal civil service or American military service. The fire destroyed some 16 to 18 million Official Military Personnel Files. The losses fell unevenly. Around 80 percent of the files for Army personnel discharged between November 1, 1912, and January 1, 1960, were lost. For the Air Force, the damage struck about 75 percent of the files for those discharged between September 25, 1947, and January 1, 1964, whose names fell alphabetically after Hubbard, James E. That precise cutoff means two airmen who served side by side could have entirely different fates on paper.

## Drying Paper in a Space Chamber

Saving what remained became its own improbable project. Burned and water-damaged records were sent to McDonnell Douglas, where technicians used a chamber that had previously simulated the temperature and pressure conditions for the Mercury and Gemini space missions. Vacuum-drying drew the water out of the soaked paper. Equipment built for the space race was now rescuing the service records of soldiers and airmen. The recovery work saved approximately 6.5 million burned and water-damaged records, a significant salvage from a fire that had consumed so much more. Every file pulled back from the brink mattered, because for these records there was no backup copy anywhere to fall back on.

## The Burned File

Investigators ruled out deliberate arson almost immediately. Some personnel who had been in the building just 20 minutes before the first alarm reported nothing out of the ordinary. In 1974, General Services Administration investigators concluded that an electrical short was most likely the cause, but the near-total destruction of the sixth floor made a specific investigation of the electrical systems impossible. The National Archives says investigators were never able to determine the source of the fire. The fire's longer legacy lives in the archive's daily work. Damaged records are known as the "Burned File" or "B" registry file, and when a requested file was lost or damaged, staff reconstruct the service information from other sources, such as Veterans Administration claims files and individual state records. Half a century on, the work of answering veterans and their families continues, one reconstructed file at a time.

## Flight Context

The fire site is near 38.6851°N, 90.3707°W in Overland, a St. Louis suburb. It lies roughly 4.4 miles due south of St. Louis Lambert International Airport (KSTL), well inside Lambert's Class B airspace, so any overflight requires coordination with St. Louis approach control. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, Lambert's runways make the clearest reference point; fly south from the airport field and the dense suburban grid of Overland spreads below. The Gateway Arch on the Mississippi riverfront lies about 11 miles to the east-southeast for broader orientation. Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) in Chesterfield is a general aviation field about 15 miles to the west. Summer thunderstorms and haze are common in July, the month of the 1973 fire.
