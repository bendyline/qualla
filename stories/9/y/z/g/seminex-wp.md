# Seminex

In 1974 most of Concordia Seminary's students and faculty walked out in a dispute over how to read the Bible, founding a 'seminary in exile' in St. Louis that split the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod.

**Location:** 38.6392, -90.2317

**Categories:** Lutheran Church – Missouri Synod, 20th-century Lutheranism, Lutheran seminaries, History of St. Louis, History of Christianity in the United States

## Introduction

On the morning of February 19, 1974, a large majority of the students at Concordia Seminary in St. Louis voted to keep studying under the professors their church had targeted, even if that meant leaving campus. Then, with most of the faculty beside them and the local press invited to watch, they sang "The Church's One Foundation" and processed out of the seminary grounds, past white crosses they had planted bearing their own names. The next day, classes began at Concordia Seminary in Exile, known ever after as Seminex, and the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod was headed for schism.

## A Synod Built on Freedom and the Word

The story begins with Saxon Germans who immigrated in the 1830s, settling in St. Louis and in Perry County, Missouri. They were fleeing a forced union of German churches imposed by royal fiat, and here, led eventually by C. F. W. Walther, they built what became the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod. Walther stressed the inspiration and authority of the Bible and strict adherence to the Lutheran Confessions. He also designed a decentralized, congregational church: no congregation could be compelled to accept a convention resolution or presidential decree contrary to Scripture and the Confessions, and the synod president could act against an official only if a convention empowered him. In 1932, synod president Franz Pieper's Brief Statement attacked the new critical theologies, and well into the 20th century most LCMS pastors described themselves as Pieperians. That governing structure would be sorely tested.

## Two Ways to Read a Book

By the 1950s and 1960s, professors at Concordia Seminary, the synod's flagship school, had begun using the historical-critical method to analyze the Bible rather than the traditional historical-grammatical approach, which treated Scripture as the inerrant Word of God. Many clergy and lay members grew alarmed. Between 1959 and 1973, the laity pushed through seventeen resolutions affirming full biblical inerrancy or condemning "antiscriptural teaching," and some conservatives asked whether the seminary was serving the denomination or the denomination was serving the seminary. The campus was changing in other ways, too. In February 1969, about 250 students petitioned for a three-day break from classes to air grievances, including how "full-time" enrollment was defined, which determined eligibility for draft deferment during the Vietnam War. After the Kent State killings in May 1970, students held a vigil, raising four crosses in the seminary quadrangle as the bells tolled.

## An Election and an Upset

In May 1969, John Tietjen, a former New Jersey pastor well known in the ecumenical movement, was chosen as Concordia's president, unusually, before his predecessor had even retired. The timing mattered. Two months later, Jacob Preus, president of the synod's other seminary in Springfield, Illinois, was elected synod president in an upset over the incumbent, Oliver Harms. Within a year, Preus formed a Fact Finding Committee to examine what the faculty taught, and its report reached the seminary in 1971. The seminary's board still backed its professors, commending each as faithful by a 6-5 vote in February 1973. But that summer the synod's convention in New Orleans condemned the faculty for abolishing sola Scriptura, the principle that Scripture is the sole norm of doctrine, and elected a new, more conservative board. It suspended Tietjen in August 1973, then vacated the suspension while reconciliation was attempted. On January 20, 1974, it suspended him again.

## School Across Town

Seminex opened in borrowed space at Eden Seminary and Saint Louis University. Because it lacked accreditation at first, its earliest graduates received diplomas through the Lutheran School of Theology at Chicago, and the first commencement took place in the neo-Gothic quadrangle of Washington University. Within a year and a half the school had its own building at 607 North Grand Boulevard, then moved to 539 North Grand after water damage. Tietjen, formally removed from Concordia in October 1974, became Seminex's president the following February. Faculty and students stopped using their old school's name, calling it simply "801," after its address on DeMun Avenue, and after legal action from Concordia, Seminex renamed itself Christ Seminary-Seminex in 1977. The opposing side told its own version of the walkout: the seminary's board accused the students of posturing, noting that they had returned to the cafeteria for lunch right after leaving and stayed in student housing through the term.

## Fewer Than Expected, Longer Than Imagined

Tietjen expected at least 1,200 congregations to follow. In the end, more than 200 left the synod and in December 1976 formed the Association of Evangelical Lutheran Churches, with about 100,000 members, less than 4 percent of the LCMS's 2.7 million. Seminex graduates could not find enough pulpits, and enrollment fell sharply. The synod made clear that ordination required credits from its own seminaries, and by the end of the 1970s Tietjen was laying off faculty who had walked out. Seminex held its last graduation in 1983 and was formally merged into the Lutheran School of Theology at Chicago in 1987, its people scattered to seminaries including Wartburg and Pacific Lutheran. Concordia Seminary rebuilt quickly and regained its place among the country's largest Lutheran seminaries, while the synod emerged more conservative. The exiles' influence lasted, though: the AELC helped form the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America, and in 2009 the ELCA's presiding bishop counted five bishops and two seminary presidents among Seminex graduates.
