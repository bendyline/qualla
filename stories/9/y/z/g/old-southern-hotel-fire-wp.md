# Old Southern Hotel fire

On April 11, 1877, fire gutted St. Louis's grand Southern Hotel, killing at least 16 and by the most common count 21 people, as two firefighters were credited with saving some 20 lives and a half-dressed Joseph Pulitzer escaped from the third floor.

**Location:** 38.6242, -90.1899

**Categories:** 19th century in St. Louis, April 1877, Hotel fires in the United States, 1877 disasters in the United States, 1877 in Missouri, Demolished buildings and structures in St. Louis, 1870s fires in the United States

## Introduction

Joseph Pulitzer left the Southern Hotel in a hurry. He had been staying on the third floor when fire swept through the building in the early hours of April 11, 1877, and he escaped, as one account put it, "sans shirt, stockings, or anything else." Between 16 and 21 other people did not escape at all, with 21 the most commonly cited count. By the time the fire was done, the grand hotel on the block between Fourth, Fifth, Walnut and Elm streets in downtown St. Louis had been utterly destroyed, reduced to what was described as "jagged, smoking ruins."

## Defenses That Failed

On paper, the Southern Hotel should have been ready for fire. Built in 1865 at a reported cost of US$1,000,000, it was a grand hotel with some 400 guest rooms, thick brick walls inside and out, water pipes and fire hose on every floor, and an "annunciator" fire alarm. None of it was enough. The fire started at about 1 a.m., and the building may have been burning for half an hour before the alarm was sounded. It was surmised afterward that the "immense draft of the baggage elevator" had pulled the flames upward through the building like a chimney. Worse, the thick smoke apparently put out the hotel's gas lighting, so the people inside, in the dark hours after midnight, could not see where they were going.

## Two Firefighters and a Twisted Sheet

Out of the chaos came two heroes. Phelim O'Toole of Hook and Ladder No. 3 and Michael J. Hester, both reported to be Irish immigrants, were credited with saving some 20 lives between them; accounts credit O'Toole with about a dozen and Hester with ten. O'Toole, born in County Wicklow, had worked as a sailor before coming to America in the 1860s. At the Southern he swung himself off a ladder on a twisted sheet, dangling fifty feet above the ground, and used his rope to get trapped guests out through the windows. His courage was remembered in a song titled "Phelim O'Toole." He did not live long to enjoy the fame. In July 1880, responding to a small fire in a vacant house on Locust Street, he was killed when a fire extinguisher blew up in his hands.

## The Dead

The dead, put at 21 in the 1914 St. Louis Firemen's Fund history though other tallies are lower, came from many walks of life. Among them were a vicar from Stockcross, in Berkshire, England, an American reverend, a Masonic secretary, two women who worked as servants and an executive of the Missouri Pacific Railway. Travelers and workers, visitors and residents, they shared a building on the night it burned. Some of the survivors had stories of their own. Pulitzer was one. Another was the actress Kate Claxton, who had already lived through the Brooklyn Theatre fire of 1876 and now survived a second deadly blaze. The Southern did not stay gone for long. Beginning in 1880, a new Southern Hotel was built on the same location, rising over the site where the old one had stood.

## Flight Context

The Southern Hotel stood at approximately 38.6242 N, 90.1899 W in downtown St. Louis, on the block bounded by Fourth, Fifth, Walnut and Elm streets. The site lies only about 0.1 mile south of the domed Old Courthouse and a few hundred yards west of the Gateway Arch grounds, with Busch Stadium just to the southwest, so the Arch and stadium make easy reference points. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL the downtown grid and riverfront are clearly visible. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) lies about 4 miles to the south-southeast across the Mississippi, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 13 miles to the northwest. Downtown sits beneath busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. Summer haze along the river can reduce visibility.
