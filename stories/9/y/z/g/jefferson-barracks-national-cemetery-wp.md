# Jefferson Barracks National Cemetery

Begun as an Army post cemetery in 1826 and made a national cemetery in 1866, this 331-acre field of white headstones above the Mississippi holds some 237,000 people - Revolutionary War veterans, Black Union soldiers lost to cholera, the victims of a wartime massacre, and a Vietnam serviceman who once lay in the Tomb of the Unknowns.

## Introduction

The first known grave at Jefferson Barracks belongs not to a soldier but to a baby. Elizabeth Ann Lash was the infant child of an officer stationed at the Army post on the banks of the Mississippi, and hers is the earliest known burial in what was then the Jefferson Barracks Military Post Cemetery. Today the ground she was laid in has grown into a national cemetery of 331 acres, with approximately 237,000 people interred beneath long, straight rows of white headstones - a vast roll call of American military service.

## From Post Cemetery to National Ground

The post cemetery started in 1826. After the Civil War, the federal government set out to build a formal network of military cemeteries, and in 1866 this one became a United States National Cemetery. It is now administered by the Department of Veterans Affairs on the former site of Jefferson Barracks and is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The Old Post Section reaches back to the nation's founding. Veterans of the American Revolution rest there. Private Richard Gentry was present at the surrender of Cornwallis at Yorktown, and his son, Colonel Richard Gentry, a War of 1812 veteran, lies nearby. Major Russell Bissell served in the Revolution and the Indian Wars. Colonel Thomas Hunt stood as a Minuteman at Concord in April 1775, was wounded at Stony Point and Yorktown, and later commanded the 1st Infantry Regiment.

## The Soldiers of the 56th

One monument here tells a story of service cut short by disease. In the summer of 1866, officers and soldiers of the 56th United States Colored Infantry died of cholera while being transported along the Mississippi River and at St. Louis, many of them at a quarantine hospital in the city. They had worn the Union uniform. A stone obelisk at Koch Quarantine Hospital marked their resting place with only a brief inscription. The War Department claimed that the names of the unknown soldiers would be added once they were discovered. The remains of 175 of the regiment's officers and soldiers were later reinterred together in a mass grave at Jefferson Barracks, and on May 19, 1939, a monument was erected over them. A bronze marker added in 2014 finally states their story plainly: "Dedicated to the soldiers of the 56th Regiment U.S. Colored Troops who died of cholera while in transport along the Mississippi River and at St. Louis, Missouri in Summer of 1866."

## Graves Shared by Many

Several mass graves here hold people who died together. Sixty-one merchant mariners and sailors who died in the fire aboard the SS J. Pinckney Henderson after a collision at sea on August 19, 1943, lie in one. Another holds 123 of the 139 victims of the Palawan Massacre. On December 14, 1944, Japanese guards at a prison camp near Puerto Princesa in the Philippines doused the shelters holding American prisoners of war with aviation fuel and set them alight. A group burial here is for five crewmen of a B-36 bomber lost in British Columbia during a training mission on February 13, 1950. Michael Blassie also rests here. He lay in the Tomb of the Unknowns as the Vietnam unknown soldier from 1984 until DNA testing positively identified his remains in 1998, and he was reinterred here that year.

## Heroes, Singers and a Ballpark Voice

Walk the rows and the names become a cross-section of American life. Medal of Honor recipients include Major Ralph Cheli, honored for heroism leading a World War II bombing mission, Army Air Forces pilot Donald D. Pucket and Navy pilot Bruce Avery Van Voorhis, recognized for action in the Pacific. Music runs deep here. Dennis Edwards, former lead singer of The Temptations, rests at Jefferson Barracks, as do rock pioneer Johnnie Johnson, opera singer Robert McFerrin Sr. and musician Henry Townsend. So does Jack Buck, the former St. Louis Cardinals baseball announcer. Cherokee artist Franklin Gritts, art director of The Sporting News, is here, and so is college football player Walter Mayberry, who died in Japanese captivity. All of them came to rest on the same quiet ground beside the river.

## Flight Context

Jefferson Barracks National Cemetery lies at approximately 38.4991 N, 90.2882 W in south St. Louis County, on the Missouri bank of the Mississippi River. From the air, the cemetery is striking: hundreds of acres of precise white headstone rows on rolling green ground above the river, on the former grounds of the Jefferson Barracks Army post. The Interstate 255 Jefferson Barracks Bridge crosses the Mississippi just to the south, a useful reference. Downtown St. Louis and the Gateway Arch are about 10 miles to the north-northeast. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is roughly 9 miles to the northeast across the river, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 18 miles to the north; check the St. Louis Class B boundaries before descending. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,500 feet AGL shows the headstone patterns clearly. Please fly respectfully over this active cemetery, especially during services. Watch for river fog on cool mornings and summer haze.
