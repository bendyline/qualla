# Sumner High School (St. Louis)

Won through the lobbying of Black St. Louisans in 1875, Sumner was the first high school for African American students west of the Mississippi, and its halls produced Arthur Ashe, Tina Turner, Dick Gregory and a century of leaders.

**Location:** 38.6597, -90.2392

**Categories:** Historically segregated African-American schools in Missouri, High schools in St. Louis, William B. Ittner buildings, African American history, Education

## Introduction

Charlton Tandy looked at the site chosen for the city's first high school for Black students and protested. It sat in a heavily polluted area, close to a lead works, lumber and tobacco warehouses, the train station and brothels. Black students, he argued, deserved clean and quiet schools just as white students did. The location did not change. Sumner High School opened there anyway in 1875, the first high school for African American students west of the Mississippi River, and over the next century and a half its classrooms sent out opera singers, jazz musicians, a tennis star, a four-star general and a rock and roll legend.

## A School Won by Petition

Sumner did not arrive as a gift. In 1865 Missouri Republicans passed a new, "radical" state constitution that abolished slavery and required school boards to support Black education, and it still took extensive lobbying by African American residents of St. Louis to get a high school established. It opened in 1875 on Eleventh Street between Poplar and Spruce, named for the abolitionist Senator Charles Sumner. The surroundings remained a problem. In the 1880s the school moved, because parents complained that their children had to walk past the city gallows and the morgue on the way to class. For decades Sumner stood alone: it was the only Black public high school in the City of St. Louis until Vashon High School opened in 1927, and the two remained the city's only public high schools for African American students under segregation.

## Training the Teachers

Sumner soon took on a second mission. In 1890 a normal school opened at the high school to train more teachers, known in its early years as the Cottage Avenue School, at Cottage Avenue and Pendleton. Between 1908 and 1910 Sumner moved into its current building on Cottage Avenue, designed by architect William B. Ittner. Its faculty included noted instructors Herman Dreer, Edward Bouchet and Charles H. Turner, and sociology professor Florence Beatty-Brown taught at the teachers college. The normal school became Stowe Teachers College in 1929 and, in the wake of Brown v. Board of Education, the U.S. Supreme Court decision that ruled school segregation unconstitutional, was merged with Harris Teachers College in 1954. Beyond the city limits, other Black high schools followed in St. Louis County, including Douglass High School in 1925 and Kinloch High School in 1936.

## A Roll Call of Bulldogs

Sumner's list of graduates is long and luminous. Tennis star Arthur Ashe studied here, as did Tina Turner, the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame singer, and comedian Dick Gregory. Opera singers Grace Bumbry and Robert McFerrin, father of Bobby McFerrin, walked its halls, along with actor Robert Guillaume, famous as Benson DuBois on Soap and Benson. The jazz world claims trumpeter Lester Bowie, saxophonist and arranger Ernie Wilkins and composer Oliver Nelson. Roscoe Robinson Jr. became the first Black four-star general in the U.S. Army, and Margaret Bush Wilson the first Black woman to head the board of the NAACP. Wendell O. Pruitt, a pioneering Tuskegee Airman, is also an alumnus; the Pruitt in the Pruitt-Igoe housing project, named after his death, honors him. Poet Naomi Long Madgett, chemist Moddie Taylor and suffragist Victoria Clay Haley complete a remarkable roster.

## Champions and Hard Days

Sumner's mascot is the Bulldog, and in 1969 its basketball team won the Missouri Class L state championship with a lineup that included future NBA and ABA players Harry Rogers and Marshall Rogers and David Brent, a sixth-round draft pick of the Los Angeles Lakers. Today students can play football, volleyball, basketball, baseball, track and field, tennis and soccer. The school has known grief as well. Three young people were shot and killed in violence at the school: Stephen Goods, a 16-year-old bystander, in 1975, Tony Hall in 1993 and Lamon Jones, 17, in 1996. In 2009 the district superintendent proposed steps to help the school, including having Sumner alumni mentor current students and setting achievable goals for each year.

## After the Storm

On May 16, 2025, a tornado damaged twelve St. Louis schools, and Sumner was among them. The storm severely damaged its roof and some of its tennis courts and brought down trees, and later rainstorms did heavy water damage inside. St. Louis Public Schools first announced that Sumner students would move to Clyde C. Miller College Prep Academy in Grand Center that fall, but by August they were placed at Stevens Middle School instead. Alumni worried the move could become an excuse to close the school for good; the district said it would not, and in November 2025 it said the building should be ready by December 2026. As of May 2026, tarps still covered the building and the district had given no new reopening date. The school's enrollment had been climbing, from 221 students to 361 in four years. For a school that began with a fight over where it could stand, the question of its building's future carries a familiar weight.

## Flight Context

Sumner High School stands at approximately 38.6597 N, 90.2392 W on Cottage Avenue in north St. Louis. From the air, look for the large early-20th-century school building and its athletic fields and tennis courts set within the residential street grid. Downtown St. Louis and the Gateway Arch lie about 4 miles to the southeast. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 9 miles to the northwest, and the school lies beneath busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 7.5 miles to the southeast across the Mississippi. A viewing altitude of 2,000-2,500 feet AGL suits the dense urban setting. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are the usual visibility concerns.
