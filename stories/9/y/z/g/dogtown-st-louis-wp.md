# Dogtown, St. Louis

Most likely named for the miners' shelters that clustered around its clay and coal pits in the 1800s, Dogtown is the traditionally Irish corner of St. Louis, a neighborhood the city map doesn't officially recognize but that fills Tamm Avenue with green every March 17.

**Location:** 38.6258, -90.2933

**Categories:** Irish-American culture in Missouri, Irish-American neighborhoods, Neighborhoods in St. Louis

## Introduction

On August 14, 1889, the St. Louis Republic (the former Missouri Republican) ran a story about a lost five-year-old boy. The paper's reporter placed the child's home in "the classic precincts of Dogtown, near Cheltenham" - a grand-sounding phrase that happens to be the earliest known appearance of the name in print. More than a century later, the name has outlived the mines that likely inspired it. Dogtown is the traditionally Irish section of St. Louis, tucked south of Forest Park, and its southeastern edge brushes against The Hill, the city's traditionally Italian neighborhood.

## Shelters by the Pits

Ask around and someone may tell you the name dates to the 1904 World's Fair. It doesn't. The name is older than the fair, and the likeliest explanation dates it to the mid-1800s, when this was a small mining community. Clay and coal mines were concentrated in the area, and miners of the era widely used "Dogtown" to describe a group of small shelters clustered around a mine. The label stuck to this patch of ground long before any fairgoers arrived, and by 1889 a newspaper could use it in passing, expecting readers to know exactly where it meant. The World's Fair story persists anyway, a tidier legend than the likeliest explanation - that the neighborhood takes its name from the small shelters of people who dug clay and coal for a living.

## A Neighborhood Off the Official Map

Look for Dogtown on the city's list of neighborhoods and you won't find it. St. Louis officially recognizes 79 neighborhoods, and Dogtown is not one of them. Instead it spans four of them - Clayton-Tamm, Franz Park, Hi-Pointe and Cheltenham - along with the eastern portion of a fifth, Ellendale. Its unofficial edges are precise all the same. Oakland Avenue forms the northern boundary, Macklind Avenue the eastern and McCausland Avenue the western. Manchester Avenue marks most of the southern line, except between Hampton and Dale Avenues, where Dogtown reaches down to Interstate 44. At the neighborhood's heart stands St. James the Greater Catholic Church, the parish that anchors the community. It is a place defined less by a city ordinance than by memory, faith and a shared sense of where home begins and ends.

## Green on Tamm Avenue

Once a year, Dogtown's Irish identity spills into the street. Since 1984 the Ancient Order of Hibernians has staged its St. Patrick's Day Parade here, running south down Tamm Avenue from Oakland to Manchester. The organizers hold firm on the date: whatever day of the week March 17 falls on, that is the day the parade steps off, starting at 11 a.m. and lasting about two hours. Alongside it runs the Dogtown Irish Festival, bringing live music and local vendors to celebrate the neighborhood's spirit and history. On a Tuesday or a Saturday alike, the old mining settlement turns out in green - proof that a name coined for miners' shacks can become something people are proud to claim.

## Flight Context

Dogtown is centered at approximately 38.6258 N, 90.2933 W in south-central St. Louis. From the air, the easiest reference is Forest Park, the large expanse of green directly to the north; Dogtown's residential grid spreads south of the park's southern edge along Oakland Avenue, with Interstate 44 running along its southern fringe. The Hill lies just to the southeast, and the Gateway Arch and downtown are about 6 miles due east. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 9 miles to the north-northwest, so the neighborhood sits beneath busy Class B terminal airspace; coordinate with ATC. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 8 miles to the east-southeast across the Mississippi, and Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) about 19.5 miles west. A viewing altitude of 2,000-2,500 feet AGL works well over the dense urban neighborhoods. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are the usual visibility concerns.
