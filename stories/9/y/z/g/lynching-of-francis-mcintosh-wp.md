# Lynching of Francis McIntosh

In April 1836 a white mob seized Francis McIntosh, a free Black steamboat cook from Pittsburgh, from the St. Louis jail and burned him alive near Seventh and Chestnut - a killing no one was ever punished for, which Elijah Lovejoy denounced and Abraham Lincoln later cited as a warning to the nation.

## Introduction

On the morning of April 28, 1836, a 26-year-old man from Pittsburgh stepped off the steamboat Flora at St. Louis. Francis L. McIntosh was a free man of color who worked aboard as a porter and a cook, and he went ashore to visit an African American chambermaid who worked on the Lady Jackson, which had docked the same day. Before long he was under arrest, and soon after that he was dead, killed by a white mob in a growing American city. What happened to him, and what the city's courts did afterward, would echo from a Missouri courtroom to an Illinois riverfront to a speech by Abraham Lincoln.

## An Arrest on the Levee

That afternoon, Deputy Constable William Mull arrested McIntosh, accusing him of helping two deckhands from the Flora escape Mull's custody. A justice of the peace charged him with breach of the peace. As Mull and Deputy Sheriff George Hammond escorted him to jail, McIntosh asked how long he would have to stay locked up. One of the officers told him he would serve five years in prison. McIntosh then stabbed both men, killing one and seriously injuring the other. An officer's death was a grave crime, and the courts had every means to try McIntosh for it. They never got the chance.

## Seventh and Chestnut

A white mob took McIntosh from the jail at Sixth and Chestnut and took him a block away, to what was then the outskirts of town, near the present-day intersection of Seventh and Chestnut streets. There they chained him to a locust tree, piled wood around him up to his knees and set it alight. He died within twenty minutes at most. During the night, an elderly African American man was paid to keep the fire burning, and the crowd drifted away. Francis McIntosh had been a working man with a trade, a home city and someone he had come ashore to see. He was denied a trial, a defense and any mercy.

## A Judge Looks Away

On May 16, Judge Luke E. Lawless addressed the grand jury. He told jurors that individuals should be prosecuted if they could be identified, but suggested that no judicial action was warranted if the killing was a "mass phenomenon." His charge went further, framing McIntosh's act as part of a threat posed by free Black people and blaming the rise of abolitionism for it - remarks that were openly racist. He also told the jury, inaccurately, that McIntosh had been a pawn of local abolitionists, above all Elijah Lovejoy, publisher of the St. Louis Observer. No one was ever charged or convicted for the lynching. Many people, both in the East and in St. Louis itself, condemned Lawless's conduct.

## Lovejoy and Lincoln

Lovejoy's Presbyterian newspaper printed abolitionist, temperance and anti-Catholic editorials, and on May 5, 1836, the Observer declared that the lynching had effectively ended the rule of law and the Constitution in St. Louis. Mob pressure and outright attacks on his press drove Lovejoy across the river to Alton, Illinois, in a free state. It did not save him. In November 1837, after he had acquired and hidden a new press, a white anti-abolition mob attacked the warehouse where it was stored, and Lovejoy was shot and killed, as was a man named Bishop in the mob. Back east, the abolitionist paper The Emancipator scorned the hypocrisy, writing that burning a man alive was "a practice nowhere except among free, enlightened, high-minded Americans." In January 1838, Abraham Lincoln pointed to the McIntosh lynching as an example in his address at the Lyceum.

## A Tree, a Handful of Soil

Shortly after the killing, the Missouri Republican reported that abolitionists were trying to gather McIntosh's remains to carry them east as a symbol of the evils of slavery. For years afterward, visitors to St. Louis, many of them from McIntosh's hometown of Pittsburgh, went to the locust tree and took pieces of it away as memorial keepsakes. Remembrance has continued into this century. On April 30, 2022, the Reparative Justice Coalition of St. Louis held a public soil collection ceremony in Kiener Plaza to commemorate him, near the corner where he died. Nearly two centuries on, St. Louisans have begun to say his name aloud.
