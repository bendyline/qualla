# Bloody Island (Mississippi River)

A wooded Mississippi River sandbar opposite St. Louis, claimed by neither Missouri nor Illinois, became the city's favorite dueling ground until Robert E. Lee's dikes joined it to the Illinois shore, where rail yards and the Poplar Street Bridge now cover it.

**Location:** 38.6384, -90.1738

**Categories:** Islands of the Mississippi River, East St. Louis, Illinois, River islands of Illinois, Landforms of St. Clair County, Illinois, Dueling grounds in the United States

## Introduction

Thomas Biddle was nearsighted, so when the choice of distance fell to him, he chose five feet. On August 26, 1831, he and Spencer Pettis faced each other with pistols on a wooded sandbar in the Mississippi River while, by some reports, about 2,000 people watched from the shore. At that range, both men received fatal wounds. The sandbar was Bloody Island, a stretch of river-born land opposite St. Louis that seemed to belong to neither Missouri nor Illinois. For roughly four decades that uncertainty made it the place where the region's politicians, lawyers and editors went to settle their quarrels by the rules of the Code Duello.

## Land That Belonged to No One

Bloody Island first rose above the water in 1798, a sandbar or "towhead" that kept growing until it became densely wooded. Its growth was more than a curiosity. The expanding island menaced the harbor of St. Louis. Its legal position made it notorious in another way. Because it was considered neutral ground, outside the control of either Missouri or Illinois, it lay technically beyond the reach of local authorities. For men bound by an honor code that demanded satisfaction, a patch of ground beyond the reach of the law was only a short trip across the river.

## Benton's Quarrel

One of the island's bloodiest feuds began with Thomas Hart Benton and Charles Lucas, two attorneys on opposite sides of a land dispute. Benton called Lucas a liar in court, Lucas later questioned Benton's right to vote over unpaid property tax, and Benton answered by dismissing him as a "little puppy." Lucas issued the challenge. At 6 a.m. on August 12, 1817, from 30 feet, Benton hit Lucas in the throat and was himself only grazed in the knee. That should have ended it, but when a recovered Lucas began to tell the story his own way, Benton challenged him again. At ten feet, Benton shot Lucas in the chest, and within minutes the young man was dead. The feud had a long reach. Joshua Barton, Missouri's first secretary of state, had served as Lucas's second. After Barton published charges in the St. Louis Republican against Rector's brother William, the surveyor general, Thomas C. Rector challenged him. On June 30, 1823, Barton died on Bloody Island, and Rector walked away unharmed. Rector himself was killed in a knife fight two years later.

## Politics at Point-Blank Range

The Biddle and Pettis affair became one of the most famous duels on the island and is often cited to illustrate that "all politics is local." Pettis, a Jacksonian Democrat and a congressman, challenged Biddle, brother of the banker Nicholas Biddle, after Biddle whipped him in his hotel room. Under the Code Duello the challenged man set the terms, and Biddle may have hoped that a five-foot distance would persuade Pettis to back down. It did not. Twenty-five years later to the day, on August 26, 1856, Benjamin Gratz Brown, editor of the St. Louis Democrat and a supporter of emancipation, faced U.S. Attorney Thomas C. Reynolds, who opposed it and issued the challenge. Brown was shot in the leg and limped for the rest of his life, while Reynolds was unhurt. Reynolds later became Missouri's Confederate governor and Brown was elected governor after the war, so the encounter came to be called the "Duel of the Governors."

## Lincoln's Broadswords

The most famous name linked to the island's legend is Abraham Lincoln. In 1842 he wrote letters to a Springfield newspaper under the name "Rebecca" mocking James Shields, the Illinois State Auditor, as a liar and a fool. Mary Todd, amused, sent more "Rebecca" letters of her own, and Shields challenged Lincoln to a duel. As the challenged party, Lincoln chose cavalry broadswords at a distance no closer than 10 feet, weapons that favored the taller man. On September 22, 1842, the two met, and their seconds persuaded Shields to accept a vague apology. Accounts differ on where that meeting took place. Some place it on Bloody Island opposite St. Louis, while others put it on a sandbar called Sunflower Island near Alton, farther up the river. Either way, Shields went on to serve as a general in the Mexican and Civil Wars.

## Swallowed by the Shore

The island's end came from engineering. In 1837 Lieutenant Robert E. Lee of the U.S. Army Engineers devised a system of dikes and dams that scoured out the western channel and ultimately joined Bloody Island to the Illinois shore. Nine years later, in 1846, the Miami people passed this way as the United States government forcibly removed them westward from their traditional homelands. They stopped on Bloody Island for about three days, and according to Miami oral history they buried an infant and an elderly member of their community on or near it. Commerce claimed the land as well. In the early 19th century Samuel Wiggins bought 800 acres around the island and ran a ferry between East St. Louis and St. Louis, at one point powered by a team of eight horses, and his ferry service developed rail yards that moved train cars across the river one at a time until the Eads Bridge opened. The south end of the old island now lies beneath the Poplar Street Bridge, in a train yard owned by the Terminal Railroad Association of St. Louis.

## Flight Context

Bloody Island no longer exists as an island; its former site, near 38.638N, 90.174W, is now part of the East St. Louis, Illinois, riverfront across the Mississippi from downtown St. Louis, northeast of the Gateway Arch. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look along the Illinois bank for the broad rail yards that cover the old island; the reference point lies just north of the Eads Bridge, and the Poplar Street Bridge crosses what was the island's south end, about a mile and a half downstream. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) lies about 5 miles to the south in Cahokia, Illinois, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 13 miles to the west-northwest; the riverfront sits under the Lambert Class B airspace. Early morning river fog can briefly hide the waterfront, but it usually burns off by midmorning.
