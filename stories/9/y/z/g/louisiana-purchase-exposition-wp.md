# Louisiana Purchase Exposition

The 1904 St. Louis World's Fair dazzled nearly 20 million visitors with palaces, inventions and new foods, while putting people on display to sell a young empire to its citizens.

**Location:** 38.6385, -90.2859

**Categories:** Louisiana Purchase Exposition, World's fairs, 1904 in Missouri, Human zoos, Forest Park, St. Louis, Washington University in St. Louis

## Introduction

It was said that no one could give even a hurried glance at everything in less than a week. Between April 30 and December 1, 1904, more than 1,500 buildings rose across 1,200 acres of what is now Forest Park and the Washington University campus, linked by some 75 miles of roads and walkways. The Palace of Agriculture alone covered about 20 acres. Nearly 19.7 million people came to the Louisiana Purchase Exposition, the St. Louis World's Fair, and what they found there ranged from the future of medicine to human beings exhibited as curiosities.

## A Centennial, One Year Late

The fair celebrated the hundredth anniversary of the 1803 Louisiana Purchase, and it took years of preparation. St. Louis raised $5 million through city bonds, private subscribers put up another $5 million, and Congress promised a final $5 million in 1900 once the first $10 million was in hand, then appropriated it in March 1901. Planned for 1903, the opening slipped to 1904 so more states and nations could take part; in the end 43 of the then-45 states exhibited. George Kessler drew the master plan, and chief of design Emmanuel Louis Masqueray produced the Palace of Agriculture, the Cascades and Colonnades and other buildings that were widely emulated in civic projects across the country during the City Beautiful movement. Not everyone got credit. Florence Hayward, the only woman on the Board of Commissioners, traveled to Europe and secured Queen Victoria's Golden Jubilee gifts and Vatican manuscripts for the exhibits, yet when fair president David R. Francis published his history in 1913, he left her out. She never forgave the slight.

## Tomorrow, Under Glass

Inside the Palace of Electricity, a telautograph recreated handwriting at a distance, an early ancestor of the fax machine. A perfected X-ray machine was exhibited, and a Finsen light, used to treat a form of tuberculosis, showed off work that had won its inventor the 1903 Nobel Prize. On The Pike, the fair's mile-long amusement strip, E. M. Bayliss displayed infant incubators in which about ten nurses cared for twenty-four newborns. An electric streetcar demonstrated its speed and braking on a 1,400-foot track, and the fair hosted the first "Airship Contest." Ideas were on display, too. Henri Poincaré gave a keynote on mathematical physics that included an outline of what would become special relativity, and a fruit specialist named J. T. Stinson gave an address that helped popularize the saying "An apple a day keeps the doctor away," which had appeared in print earlier. In Festival Hall, the French organist Alexandre Guilmant gave 40 recitals from memory on what was then the largest organ in the world.

## Breakfast in France, Dinner in Japan

Many foods claim a birth at the fair, and most of those claims are dubious. The waffle-style ice cream cone was probably popularized here rather than invented, and the same goes for iced tea and cotton candy. Dr Pepper and Puffed Wheat cereal did reach a national audience in St. Louis, and Jack Daniel's Tennessee whiskey won a gold medal after eight judges deliberated for four hours. What the fair really offered was something like America's first food court. As one historian put it, "one could breakfast in France, take a mid-morning snack in the Philippines, lunch in Italy, and dine in Japan." Scott Joplin wrote "The Cascades" for the waterfalls at the Grand Basin, and the song "Meet Me in St. Louis, Louis" outlived the fair by decades, becoming a focal point, along with the fair itself, of the 1944 Judy Garland film.

## The People Put on Display

The fair also put human beings on display. After the Spanish-American War, the United States took control of the Philippines, where Filipinos had fought annexation, and the fair exhibited 1,102 Filipinos, 700 of them Philippine Scouts and Constabulary. Igorot and Apache people were presented to visitors as "primitive," and organizers restaged ceremonies meant for special occasions day after day. The people had been trafficked under harsh conditions, and many did not survive. Burial plots in two St. Louis cemeteries were prepared in advance, but traditional burial practices were forbidden; when someone died, the body was removed at once, and mourners had to hold funeral rites without their dead, in front of an oblivious crowd. Geronimo, the former Apache war chief, was put "on display" in a teepee. Historians have argued that the fair's overriding purpose was to promote America's new role as an overseas imperial power. In 2025, after years of advocacy by Filipino American artist Janna Añonuevo Langholz, a historical marker was placed in the Wydown-Skinker neighborhood of Clayton, where the Philippine Village stood.

## What Stayed Behind

The fair also hosted the 1904 Summer Olympics, the first held in the United States, though the Games were overshadowed by the exposition around them. Pieces of the fair scattered widely. The huge bird cage at the Saint Louis Zoo dates from 1904. Birmingham's cast iron Vulcan statue first stood in the Palace of Mines and Metallurgy, the Swedish Pavilion survives in Lindsborg, Kansas, and the great organ became Philadelphia's Wanamaker Organ. Profits from the fair built the World's Fair Pavilion in Forest Park on the site of the Missouri State Building, which burned just eleven days before closing, and the Jefferson Memorial building, now the Missouri History Museum. For one visitor the fair was a final outing. The novelist Kate Chopin, who lived nearby and held a season ticket, suffered a brain hemorrhage after a visit on the hot day of August 20 and died two days later.

## Flight Context

The fairgrounds centered on 38.64 N, 90.29 W, in and around Forest Park on the western edge of the city of St. Louis, Missouri. The park is an unmistakable green rectangle amid dense city blocks, with the Grand Basin, Art Hill and the Saint Louis Art Museum inside it and the Washington University campus just west; the Gateway Arch and the Mississippi River lie 5-6 miles east. A view from 2,500-3,500 feet MSL shows the park's layout clearly. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 7 nm north-northwest and St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) about 7 nm east-southeast across the river; Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) lies about 17 nm west. The park sits beneath St. Louis Class B airspace, so coordinate with approach. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common.
