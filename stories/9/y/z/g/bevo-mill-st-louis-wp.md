# Bevo Mill, St. Louis

A south St. Louis neighborhood takes its name from a Dutch-style windmill August Busch Sr. named for a non-alcoholic malt drink, and in the 1990s it was revived by families fleeing war in Bosnia and Croatia.

**Location:** 38.5816, -90.2672

**Categories:** Bosnian-American culture in Missouri, Croatian-American history, Neighborhoods in St. Louis, Restaurants in St. Louis, Restaurants established in 1916, Buildings and structures completed in 1916, German-American culture in St. Louis

## Introduction

The Busch family made its name in beer, yet the windmill August Busch Sr. raised in south St. Louis was named for a drink with no alcohol in it. Bevo was a non-alcoholic malt beverage that Anheuser-Busch was producing when the Bevo Mill opened in 1917 at the corner of Gravois and Morganford roads, a Tudor Revival restaurant with a windmill and a beer hall. The mill gave its name to the neighborhood that grew up around it. Generations later, those same streets were remade again, this time by families who had fled war in Bosnia and Croatia and found a place to start over beneath the sails of a Dutch-style windmill.

## A Windmill for a Drink Without Alcohol

Construction began in 1916 on the former site of a blacksmith shop, and the restaurant opened on Tuesday, June 19, 1917. Busch had spent a year touring Holland beforehand, seeking authentic artifacts and studying Dutch windmills. He wanted to recreate a European beer garden, where drinks would be served outdoors amid music and dancing, a kind of country club in the city. The architects Klipstein and Rothman designed the building in the Tudor Revival style, and Grone Construction, owned by Louis Henry Grone, whose cousins owned the H. Grone Brewery, built it. The name came from Bevo, a non-alcoholic malt beverage that Anheuser-Busch introduced in 1916, as the push for Prohibition was gathering strength. When national Prohibition arrived, Bevo production rose significantly.

## Tiles from Germany, Blades of Aluminum

Among the Bevo Mill's famous features is a set of porcelain tile murals showing idyllic pastoral scenes. They were made in Germany around 1890, at the Berlin Porcelain Works, and originally belonged to the restaurateur Tony Faust. After Faust's downtown restaurant closed in 1916, Busch reportedly paid $50,000 for them. The windmill itself has had a more complicated life. Its original wooden blades, 60 feet in diameter, were eventually declared a hazard and removed, and aluminum replacements were fabricated and installed in 1954. The mill at 4749 Gravois became a City Landmark in 1971. It operated as the Bevo Mill until closing in 2009, and in 2017 a restaurant and event venue called Das Bevo opened in the old building.

## From Dutchtown's Neighbor to New Arrivals

The Bevo Mill area lies directly west of Dutchtown, which in the mid-19th century was a major center of German settlement in St. Louis. Like much of the city, the neighborhood lost significant population in the later 20th century. Its revival came in the 1990s, driven by immigrants fleeing war in Bosnia and Croatia, and today much of Bevo Mill is home to Bosnian Americans and Croatian Americans. The St. Louis area is now home to an estimated 60,000 to 70,000 Bosnians, and their arrival helped stem the decline in the city's population. Many bought homes in South St. Louis and opened businesses: bakeries, cafes, taverns, nightclubs, restaurants, neighborhood grocery stores and butcher shops.

## Streets Made New

Those storefronts did more than feed people. They stimulated the local economy and are credited with transforming an area once known for crime into a safe, thriving neighborhood. People who had lost the lives they knew to war built new ones here, a few blocks from a windmill that a brewer once built to evoke another part of Europe entirely. The neighborhood today is mixed. In 2020 its population was 58.3 percent White, 20.6 percent Black and 4.8 percent Asian, with 9.9 percent identifying as two or more races, and 11.6 percent of residents were of Hispanic or Latino origin. In Bevo Mill today, the Dutch sails over Gravois and the Bosnian bakeries of South St. Louis belong to the same story of people arriving and making a home.

## Flight Context

The Bevo Mill windmill stands at the corner of Gravois and Morganford roads, near 38.582N, 90.267W, in south St. Louis, Missouri. Gravois Road is a long diagonal arterial that cuts across the city's grid and makes a useful line to follow; the windmill itself is small and best spotted below 1,500 feet AGL. Tower Grove Park lies roughly 2 miles to the north, and the red brick complex of the Anheuser-Busch Brewery is about 3 miles to the east-northeast. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 6 miles to the east across the Mississippi River, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 13 miles to the north-northwest; the neighborhood lies under the Lambert Class B airspace. Clear winter days with bare trees make the mill's sails easiest to pick out.
