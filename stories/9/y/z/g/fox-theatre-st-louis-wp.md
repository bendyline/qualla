# Fox Theatre (St. Louis)

Opened in 1929 as one of William Fox's five spectacular movie palaces, St. Louis's "Fabulous Fox" survived closure and neglect to be restored in 1982 as a performing arts center - and to host Chuck Berry, who had once been turned away at its door.

## Introduction

As a boy growing up in St. Louis, Chuck Berry was denied entrance to the Fox Theatre to watch a film because he was Black. He remembered it. In 1986 the early rock and roll pioneer stood on the Fox's stage for his own 60th birthday concert, with Keith Richards of The Rolling Stones leading the backing band, and he told the story in the documentary that came out of that night. The Fox - "The Fabulous Fox" to St. Louisans - holds both memories: the excluding movie palace of 1929 and the restored arts center that finally gave Berry its stage.

## Five Palaces for William Fox

Movie pioneer William Fox built this theater in 1929 as a showcase for the films of the Fox Film Corporation and for elaborate stage shows. It was one of five spectacular Fox Theatres he raised in the late 1920s, alongside houses in Brooklyn, Atlanta, Detroit and San Francisco. When the St. Louis Fox opened on January 31, 1929, it was reportedly the second-largest theater in the United States, with 5,060 seats. It stands at 527 North Grand Boulevard in the Grand Center arts district of Midtown St. Louis, one block north of Saint Louis University, and for decades it ranked among the city's leading movie theaters, holding that place through the 1960s.

## Siamese Byzantine

The architect was C. Howard Crane, a specialist in theaters, and he designed an eclectic interior layered with Asian decorative motifs, a style sometimes called Siamese Byzantine. The interior is the architectural twin of another Fox Theatre, built in Detroit in 1928. Reporters in 1929 were effusive, calling the St. Louis and Detroit houses "bewildering in their richness and dazzling in their appointments." William Fox had his own name for the look. He called it the "Eve Leo Style," a tribute to his wife, who decorated the interior with furnishings, paintings and sculpture she had bought on her trips overseas. The result was a room designed to astonish before a single frame of film had rolled.

## The Mighty Wurlitzer

A movie palace needed a voice, and the Fox's was a Wurlitzer pipe organ that cost $75,000 in 1929. Tom Terry served as resident organist from 1929 to 1935. Then the organ went silent for the public, unplayed from 1935 until 1952. It survived to sing again, and during the 1980s renovation it gained a companion in the lobby: a second, smaller Wurlitzer with two manuals and 11 ranks. That instrument had its own history, having first been installed in 1930 in the Majestic Theatre across the river in East St. Louis, Illinois. It replaced the lobby's original Möller organ, which had been removed.

## Dark House, Second Act

The golden age did not last. The Fox closed in 1978, and in 1981 - the year John Carpenter's film Escape from New York, shot in St. Louis in 1980, briefly showed its facade, cast as an abandoned Broadway theatre - it was privately purchased by Fox Associates. A one-year restoration followed. On September 7, 1982, the Fabulous Fox reopened with the musical "Barnum" before a capacity crowd of 4,500. It has carried on since as a versatile performing arts venue. On July 23, 2006, the Fox hosted the final performance of the Third National Tour of Les Misérables, which had run for nearly 18 years and 7,061 performances before its curtain came down for good in St. Louis.

## Hail! Hail!

The 1986 Chuck Berry concert gave the restored theater one of its defining nights. Keith Richards served as the project's musical director and backing band leader, and director Taylor Hackford wove the concert into a feature documentary, Hail! Hail! Rock 'n' Roll. On screen, Berry recalled being turned away from the Fox as a child because of his race - a reminder that the grand palace had not been open to all of the city it served. Decades later, he filled it with the music he helped invent. The theater that once shut its doors to a Black boy from St. Louis ended up throwing him a birthday party.
