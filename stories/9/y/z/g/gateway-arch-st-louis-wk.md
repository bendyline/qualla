# Gateway Arch

Eero Saarinen's 630-foot stainless steel catenary on the St. Louis riverfront - completed in 1965, ridden by elevator-gondola hybrid, and built to commemorate an expansion the people already living west of the Mississippi did not consent to.

**Location:** 38.6247, -90.1848

**Categories:** missouri, monuments, architecture, national-parks, engineering

## Introduction

The geometry only resolves at a distance. Stand directly under the south leg of the Gateway Arch and you cannot quite see the top - the curve carries the eye sideways before it carries it up. Step back four blocks and the arch finally reveals itself as a single line of stainless steel rising six hundred and thirty feet, a perfect weighted catenary balanced beside the Mississippi. It is the tallest monument in the United States, the tallest arch in the world, and a piece of architecture that argues with itself: a triumphal gateway honoring a westward expansion whose triumph was, for the Indigenous nations already west of this river, a long catastrophe. The monument is honest enough to admit this now. It did not always.

## The Curve Eero Drew

Eero Saarinen won the 1947-48 design competition for a Jefferson National Expansion Memorial on the St. Louis riverfront with a sketch most jurors had never seen before in a public monument: an unadorned parabolic arch. By the time the engineering was finished it was not quite a parabola - it is a weighted catenary, the shape a chain would take if its links grew heavier toward the bottom, mathematically described by a hyperbolic cosine and refined to make the form look less pointed and more soaring. The cross-section is an equilateral triangle that tapers from fifty-four feet on a side at the base to seventeen feet at the apex. The arch was assembled from one hundred forty-two prefabricated double-walled sections; from the ground to three hundred feet, the space between the walls was filled with concrete and tensioned with steel rods. Its roughly nine hundred tons of stainless steel skin was billed as the most stainless steel ever used on a single project.

## The Architect Who Never Saw It

Saarinen spent the rest of his life refining the design after the competition - adjusting its proportions, and working with the engineer Hannskarl Bandel on the equations that fixed its slender catenary shape. He never saw it built. He died during surgery for a brain tumor on September 1, 1961, at the age of fifty-one, before the first steel section went up. Construction ran from February 1963 to October 1965 under the supervision of his successor firm and a consortium of structural engineers. The two legs were built upward independently, section by section, with workers riding creeper derricks that climbed tracks on the curving steel. The closing keystone segment was lifted into the gap at the top on October 28, 1965, while Vice President Hubert Humphrey watched from a helicopter. The crews had to spray the south leg with fire hoses that morning to cool it - the sun was expanding it just enough to refuse the keystone.

## Riding the Inside of a Curve

The arch is hollow, and there is a tram inside. Nothing else in the world quite works the way it does. Eight small capsules ride a track that climbs the inner curve of each leg, and because the track's slope keeps changing as it climbs, each capsule pivots like a Ferris-wheel car as it rises, keeping the five passengers inside roughly upright. The cars were designed in the early 1960s and still feel like it - cramped and faintly carnival. The ride takes about four minutes up and three minutes down. At the top, an observation room with sixteen small rectangular windows on each side looks east into Illinois and west across St. Louis. On a clear day the view reaches roughly thirty miles. The floor inside the apex is just wide enough for visitors to stand.

## The Gateway and What It Cost

The arch was commissioned as a memorial to westward expansion and to Thomas Jefferson, who authorized the Louisiana Purchase and the Lewis and Clark Expedition. For the people who lived west of the Mississippi in 1804 - the Osage, the Lakota, the Pawnee, the Comanche, the Shoshone, the Nez Perce, and dozens of other nations - that expansion did not feel like an opening. It meant treaties broken, lands seized, populations displaced and decimated. The original museum beneath the arch leaned heavily into the heroic frontier story. The current Museum at the Gateway Arch, redesigned and reopened in 2018, broadens the frame: its galleries trace how westward migration affected American Indian nations and how settlement cost them part or all of their homelands, and it shares a campus with the Old Courthouse, where the Dred Scott case began. The arch still says gateway. The museum underneath says it was also a closing door.

## Standing at the Riverfront

Gateway Arch National Park covers about ninety-one acres on the west bank of the Mississippi, from the Old Courthouse on Fourth Street down a long landscaped slope to the river. The grounds were redesigned along with the museum: a park built over Interstate 44 now links downtown to the arch grounds, bridging the highway that used to cut the monument off from the city. The arch is climbed by tram; its emergency stairs are not open to the public, and there is no public route up the outside. At night - except in the spring and fall bird-migration seasons, when the park switches them off - LED floodlights installed in 2023, successors to the lights first switched on in 2001, bathe the steel in white from shortly after sunset. The reflection on the river doubles the curve. From the Eads Bridge, a few blocks north, the geometry is at its cleanest: river, arch, sky.

## Flight Context

Located at 38.6247 degrees N, 90.1848 degrees W on the west bank of the Mississippi River in downtown St. Louis. The arch stands 630 ft AGL, finished in mirror-polished stainless steel - unmistakable from any altitude or direction, especially with sun on the metal. CAUTION: Lambert's Class B airspace overlies downtown, and drones are barred within 400 feet of the monument. Best photographic approach from the east at 3,000-5,000 ft AGL, with the arch silhouetted against downtown. Nearest airports: KSTL (St. Louis Lambert International, 11 nm NW), KCPS (St. Louis Downtown, 3.5 nm SSE), KSUS (Spirit of St. Louis, 22 nm W). Busch Stadium sits a few blocks west-southwest; the Eads Bridge crosses the river just north. Floodlit nightly from shortly after sunset, except during the May and September bird-migration shutoffs.
