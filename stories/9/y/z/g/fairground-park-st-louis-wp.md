# Fairground Park (St. Louis)

Once the grounds of the St. Louis Exposition, a Civil War camp and a racetrack, this North St. Louis park later built what was said to be the world's largest swimming pool - and in 1949 became the scene of violence against Black children who came to swim there.

**Location:** 38.6656, -90.2217

**Categories:** Parks in St. Louis, 1908 establishments in Missouri, Protected areas established in 1908, Parks established in the 1900s

## Introduction

The bears are long gone, but their pits still greet visitors. At the corner of Grand Boulevard and Natural Bridge Avenue, the facade of an old zoo's bear pits stands at the main entrance to Fairground Park - one of only two structures the city chose to keep when it turned a private fairground into a public park. Behind that facade lies more than 150 years of St. Louis history: agricultural fairs and horse races, a Union army encampment, a record-setting swimming pool and one of the most painful days in the city's struggle over segregation.

## Fair Days and Wartime

The story begins in 1856, when the St. Louis Agricultural and Mechanical Association began holding the St. Louis Exposition on this privately owned ground. The fair ran year after year until the Civil War broke the rhythm. During the war the Fairgrounds were used as a Union encampment known as Benton Barracks. The fair returned afterward and carried on for decades, until 1902, when the city's attention shifted to something far bigger. Preparations were underway for the 1904 World's Fair, and the old annual exposition quietly ended after nearly half a century.

## The Last Race

Horse racing gave the grounds a second life. In the early 1880s the agricultural association fell upon hard times and was replaced by the St. Louis Fair and Jockey Club, and among the jockeys who rode the Fairground's track was Vearne C. Babcock. Politics ended it. Joseph W. Folk was elected governor of Missouri in 1904, running as an anti-gambling, progressive reformer, and he signed the Anti-Breeders Act. That law led directly to the permanent closure of the St. Louis Fairground Track. The track went quiet, and the grounds sat abandoned while the city argued over what to do with them.

## A Park and a Record Pool

In 1908, after protracted political debate, St. Louis bought the abandoned 132-acre fairground from the association for $700,000, and the park was dedicated on October 9, 1909. Nearly every trace of the old fair and zoo was cleared away. Only the bear pits and the amphitheater survived, and in 1912 the amphitheater came down too. In its place the city built its first municipal swimming pool, then said to be the largest in the world. It was also a whites-only pool, and that would not go unchallenged forever.

## June 21, 1949

In June 1949 the city opened its municipal pools to Black swimmers. On June 21, about 30 Black children entered the Fairground Park pool. Some 200 white children and teenagers surrounded and attacked them, and the confrontation swelled into a mob of thousands of people in the park, including Cardinals fans who walked over from nearby Sportsman's Park to join it. Between 150 and 400 police officers, depending on the account, were sent to break up a riot that lasted all night. The next day Mayor Joseph Darst closed the pool. He conceded that segregation was not established in law, but argued that opening the pools had breached a "community policy" of segregation. Only after a federal court ruling did Darst reopen the pools as desegregated in 1950. The children who came to swim that day had asked for nothing more than a place in the water.

## What Remains

The pool was later closed for low attendance after white swimmers stayed away from the integrated facility. In the late 1950s a new, smaller pool replaced the original under the city's 1955 bond issue program, which also brought lighted ball diamonds and hard-surface tennis courts. Today Fairground Park fills the land between Grand Boulevard on the east, Natural Bridge Avenue on the south, Fair Street on the west and Kossuth Street on the north. Four neighborhoods ring it: Fairground to the northeast, O'Fallon to the northwest, Greater Ville to the southwest and Vandeventer to the southeast. The park has hosted gatherings such as an event of the Black Veiled Prophet organization, and the bear pit facade still marks the gate - a quiet reminder of how many lives this ground has held.

## Flight Context

Fairground Park is centered at approximately 38.6656 N, 90.2217 W in North St. Louis. From the air, look for a large rectangle of open lawn, trees, ball diamonds and a pool on the west side of Grand Boulevard, with Natural Bridge Avenue running along its southern edge; the Mississippi River lies to the east. Downtown St. Louis and the Gateway Arch are about 3.5 miles to the southeast. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 9 miles to the northwest, and the park lies under St. Louis Class B airspace, so coordinate with ATC. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 7.5 miles to the south-southeast across the river. A viewing altitude of 2,000-2,500 feet AGL shows the park's full outline against the surrounding street grid. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are the main visibility concerns.
