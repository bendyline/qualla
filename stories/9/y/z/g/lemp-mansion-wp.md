# Lemp Mansion

Built by the brewing dynasty behind Falstaff, St. Louis's Lemp Mansion stands over the cave country that once chilled their lager, and holds the memory of a family fortune undone by grief, Prohibition and loss.

**Location:** 38.5932, -90.2160

**Categories:** Houses in St. Louis, Falstaff Brewing Corporation, German-American culture in St. Louis, Reportedly haunted locations in Missouri, Restaurants in St. Louis

## Introduction

The Lemp fortune began in the cold. Beneath south St. Louis runs a cave that once gave a German immigrant brewer natural refrigeration for his lager, and above that cave country the family built a brewery that grew into the largest in the city. The Lemp Mansion at 3322 DeMenil Place, in the Benton Park neighborhood, was home to the family whose William J. Lemp Brewing Co. dominated the St. Louis beer market before Prohibition with its Falstaff brand. It is also where three members of the family died by suicide, and it is said to be haunted by the Lemps themselves. Today the house serves meals and welcomes overnight guests, though its owners listed it for sale in September 2026 and plan to keep hosting events through December 2027.

## A Grocer's Lager

Adam Lemp arrived in the United States in 1836, settled in St. Louis in 1838 and became a naturalized citizen in November 1841. He opened a family grocery, A. Lemp & Co., at Sixth and Morgan, a spot now covered by the south side of The Dome at America's Center, where he sold his own vinegar and beer alongside ordinary groceries. Before long beer was his whole business. At the Western Brewery on South Second Street, about where the south leg of the Gateway Arch now stands, Lemp became one of the first in the country to produce German lager, a sharp departure from English ales and porters, and the city's growing German population embraced it. When he needed storage, he turned to a cave in south St. Louis. By the 1860s, 40 area breweries were using the caves along the Mississippi, and the Western Brewery was among the most successful.

## The Brewer Who Refrigerated America

Adam's son William J. Lemp, born in Germany in 1835, studied at Saint Louis University and enlisted in the U.S. Army in 1861, rising to orderly sergeant. That December he married Julia Feickert; their daughter Annie Lemp Konta became a writer. When Adam died on August 23, 1862, William took over the Western Brewery, and in 1864 he began building a larger plant above the storage caves. Under his management it became the largest brewery in St. Louis and, by some accounts, one of the largest in the country. He brewed and bottled in the same facility, a rare practice at the time. In the late 1870s he installed mechanical refrigeration, among the first in an American brewery, then extended the idea to refrigerated railway cars, making his beer one of the first in the country with national reach. Soon Lemp beer was sold worldwide.

## A Father's Grief

William Sr. hoped his fourth son, Frederick, born in 1873, would one day run the company. When Frederick died of heart failure on December 12, 1901, his father became despondent and slowly declined, and on February 13, 1904, William Lemp took his own life. His son William J. "Billy" Lemp Jr. became president that November. Billy's marriage to Lillian Handlan, allegedly nicknamed the "Lavender Lady" for her lavender wardrobe and carriages, ended in a divorce she was granted in 1908, along with custody of their only child, William III. After the trial Billy built Alswel, a country home overlooking the Meramec River. Sorrow kept finding the family. William Sr.'s youngest child, Elsa Lemp Wright, divorced her husband and then remarried him in March 1920; later that month, on March 20, she died by suicide at their home on Hortense Place.

## Prohibition and the Last Lemp

Prohibition finished what grief had begun. The brewery closed, the Falstaff trademark went to the family friend "Papa Joe" Griesedieck, and in 1922 the complex was auctioned to the International Shoe Company for $588,000. On December 29 of that year, Billy died by suicide in his office. His brother Charles, who had left the brewery in 1917 for banking and finance and dabbled in south-side politics, moved into the house in 1929. He never married, living there with his dog and two servants, a married couple. In 1941 he wrote to a funeral home asking that, when he died, there be no funeral and no newspaper notice, and that his ashes be buried on his farm. In 1949 he, too, died by suicide in the house. Edwin Lemp died in 1970 at the age of 90, having ordered his caretaker to destroy his art collection and the family heirlooms.

## What the House Still Holds

After Charles's death the mansion became a boarding house in 1950 and lost much of its ornate charm over the following decade, and construction of Interstate 55 in the 1960s destroyed much of the grounds and one of the carriage houses. Yet pieces of the Lemps' world remain. Diners in the basement restaurant can see the decorative iron gates of the old open-air elevator. A hand-painted parlor ceiling survives, along with carved African mahogany mantels, the three vaults where the family stored its art, and the Italian marble mantel in the office where Billy died. The house's reputation for restless spirits has drawn television crews, including The Atlantic Paranormal Society, whose investigation aired on Ghost Hunters in 2010.

## Flight Context

The Lemp Mansion stands at approximately 38.5932 N, 90.216 W in the Benton Park neighborhood of south St. Louis, beside the Interstate 55 corridor. From the air, look for the large former Lemp Brewery complex on Cherokee Street close by, with the Mississippi River a short distance to the east. The Gateway Arch lies about 3 miles to the north-northeast. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is roughly 3.5 miles to the east-southeast across the river, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 13 miles to the northwest. A viewing altitude of 2,000-2,500 feet AGL makes the brewery's cluster of brick buildings easy to pick out. This is busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC; summer haze often softens visibility over the river.
