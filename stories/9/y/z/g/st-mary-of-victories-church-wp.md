# St. Mary of Victories Church

Founded in 1843 for German immigrants just south of today's Gateway Arch, St. Mary of Victories became a sanctuary for newcomers of many kinds, from exiled German sisters and Syrian families to Hungarian refugees of 1956.

**Location:** 38.6181, -90.1906

**Categories:** Roman Catholic church buildings in St. Louis, German-American culture in St. Louis, Hungarian-American culture, Churches on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Downtown St. Louis

## Introduction

In 1872, a small group of women arrived in St. Louis from Germany and found lodging in an attic in the church complex of St. Mary of Victories. Their front door was the church door. Led by Mother Mary Odilia Berger, they had been unable to live out their religious calling at home because of the German government policies known as the Kulturkampf. Two years later they organized as the Franciscan Sisters of St. Mary, taking their name from the church that had sheltered them. Their healing ministry continues today in SSM Health Care, a St. Louis-based health system, and their story is only one of many carried by this old church in the Kosciusko neighborhood, just south of the Gateway Arch.

## Named for a Victory at Sea

St. Mary of Victories was established in 1843, the second Catholic church built in St. Louis. It was raised to serve about 500 families who had emigrated from Germany and settled around the pioneer Chouteau's Landing district of Kosciusko in the years before the Civil War. The name recalls a feast day proclaimed by Pope Pius V to celebrate the Christian naval victory at the Battle of Lepanto in 1571. For roughly the next century, St. Mary's served as the first ethnic parish and spiritual home of the city's German Catholics, a place where newcomers could worship among their own.

## Walls Anointed with Oil

In 1866, at the direction of Pope Pius IX, St. Louis Archbishop Peter Richard Kenrick consecrated the church in a specific ritual, anointing each interior wall with chrism oil. Brass cross sconces still mark the places he touched. The distinction matters: while all Catholic churches are blessed, only a few in a diocese are consecrated, and consecration assures that the building may be used only for Roman Catholic worship. The main altar carries its own honor. Hundreds of relics of saints are entombed in the High Altar, and in the late 19th century Pope Leo XIII attached an indulgence to it, granting temporal remission of sins at the time of death to Catholics who say the specific prayers and die in the state of grace.

## An Immigrant Artist's Interior

Architects George I. Barnett and Franz Saler designed the church in the Mannerist style. The rectangular nave and the transept form a cross, with the sanctuary at its head, and the choir loft sits on the upper tier of a two-tier balcony at the rear. Its ornately carved wooden organ case and stenciled display pipes are among the oldest in St. Louis. Eight tall stained-glass windows with rounded frames line the nave and transept, and an Egyptian-style doorway, framed by two massive pillars, supports a heavy wooden cross above the entrance. Inside, the church was the first in St. Louis whose interior design was based on liturgical studies. It was the work of Max Schneiderhahn, the city's first professional church artist and himself a German immigrant, who conceived and executed the altars, statuary, steepled baptismal font, communion rail, carvings and frescoes, and painted the Stations of the Cross.

## A Preacher's Missouri Debut

From October 1 to 15, 1865, the Bavarian Redemptorist priest Francis Xavier Seelos preached a parish mission here in German with his fellow Redemptorists, Fathers Schneider, Jacob and Gulielm. It was his Missouri preaching debut. Seelos, since beatified, worked with German immigrants in St. Louis, Baltimore and New Orleans, and a side altar in the south middle portion of the nave honors his role in the parish. His statue, cast by the Vatican Statuary Foundry, stands on an altar rebuilt from the communion rail of the old St. Malachy's Church in the Mill Creek Valley, since demolished. A copy of his bronze death mask, one of five in the world, is on permanent display. On the back wall, to the left of the entrance, a bronze plaque honors the American founding of the Sisters of St. Mary.

## The Hungarian Church

St. Mary's kept opening its doors to new arrivals. After 1917 the parish diversified to include Syrian families and others. In the 1950s, many families whose German ancestors had worshiped here left the city, and refugees from Hungary, arriving after World War II and the 1956 Hungarian Uprising, took their place and gave the parish new life. It became known, unofficially, as the "Hungarian Church." A carved white marble plaque in the sanctuary marks a 1970s visit by Cardinal Joseph Mindszenty of Hungary, and the Hungarian Ladies Society has embroidered most of the altar linens. St. Mary's was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1980. In 2005 Archbishop Raymond Burke reduced it from a parish to the status of a chapel, and its website now lists an 11 a.m. Sunday Mass on the third Sunday of each month.

## Flight Context

St. Mary of Victories stands at approximately 38.6181 N, 90.1906 W in the Kosciusko neighborhood on the Mississippi riverfront, about 0.6 miles southwest of the Gateway Arch. From the air, use the Arch and its riverfront park as the primary landmark, then look just south of the Arch grounds for the church near the river. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) lies about 4 miles to the southeast across the Mississippi, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 13 miles to the northwest. A viewing altitude of 2,000-2,500 feet AGL gives a good view of the riverfront; this is busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. Morning river haze is common in summer.
