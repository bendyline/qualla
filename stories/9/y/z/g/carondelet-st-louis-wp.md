# Carondelet, St. Louis

A French village nicknamed for emptying visitors' pockets became an independent city, built Union ironclads, opened what is often called America's first public kindergarten and finally became a brick-lined corner of St. Louis.

**Location:** 38.5596, -90.2510

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in St. Louis, French colonial settlements of Illinois Country, Missouri populated places on the Mississippi River, Former municipalities in Missouri, Civil War history

## Introduction

Invite an early St. Louisan down to Carondelet for a Sunday afternoon, and the answer might have come back in French: "A quoi sert, c'est un vide poche." What's the use? It's a place that empties your pocket. The riverside village at the far southeastern edge of today's St. Louis collected nicknames the way its visitors lost coins, and behind each one sits a piece of its history. Carondelet has been a French frontier outpost, an independent city, a Civil War shipyard and an industrial riverfront, and since 1870 it has been a neighborhood of the city it once teased.

## Flattering a Governor

Clément Delor de Treget, born in Cahors in the Quercy region of southern France, founded the village in 1767 after obtaining a land grant from the St. Louis commandant, Louis Saint Ange. He built a stone house, and the settlement became Delor's Village. It was later called Catalan's Prairie, after an early settler named Louis Catalan, and then Louisbourg, most likely in honor of King Louis XVI. The final name came, it is said, from a bit of political flattery. After the territory passed from French to Spanish hands, Treget wanted his commission as captain of the militia renewed but feared that, as a Frenchman, he would be refused. So he named the village for the Spanish governor-general of Louisiana, the Baron de Carondelet. Treget got his commission.

## The Place That Empties Pockets

The nickname Vide Poche has more than one explanation. It is often mistranslated as "empty pocket," which in French would be "poche vide," and a folk story ties it to poverty: villagers had to buy flour from St. Louis and often could not pay. Another reading makes it "empties pocket," a place where visitors lost their money. Carondelet's people were known for their hospitality and their love of sports and amusements, and St. Louis guests went home lighter, perhaps because the locals had faster horses or played cards more skillfully. A third explanation traces the phrase to New France, where "Vide-Poche" was documented as a nickname for a mill; many settlers came from Canada, and the village had a mill from its very beginning. It was also called Sugarloaf. A 1799 census counted 181 white residents and three enslaved Africans "two leagues below St. Louis."

## Lead Shot and Ironclads

Industry arrived early. Beginning in the 1840s, Carondelet became closely associated with processing lead from the Southeast Missouri Lead District, starting with the production of lead shot. Then, during the Civil War, the riverfront turned to war work on a far larger scale. James Buchanan Eads, whose shipyards stood here, went on to build more than 30 river ironclads for the Union before the war ended. Among them were four of the first City class ironclads, built here: the St. Louis, the Carondelet, the Pittsburgh and the Louisville. Eads subcontracted the other City class gunboats to builders in Mound City, Illinois. One of the vessels launched here carried the town's own name into the war as USS Carondelet.

## Classrooms, Convents and Chemicals

Carondelet's influence reached well beyond its riverbank. It was the first foundation in America of the Sisters of St. Joseph of Carondelet, a congregation with French roots whose members arrived in 1836. In 1873 Susan Blow opened what is widely described as the first public kindergarten in the United States at the neighborhood's Des Peres School, open to all local children. The factories kept coming, too. The Hertz Metal Company made baling wire and ran a lead smelter from 1870 until 1930, and in 1876 the Provident Chemical Works began producing phosphates at its Carondelet plant, in a process that initially involved lead, and became a world leader in the field. Monsanto bought the plant in 1935, and in the 1930s a Works Progress Administration project rerouted the River des Peres through it.

## Brick Homes, Older Roots

Carondelet was incorporated in 1832, became an independent city in 1851 and was annexed by St. Louis in 1870. Its first residents were predominantly French, followed later by German immigrants. Because it grew up as a separate village, the neighborhood holds some of the oldest homes in St. Louis, though most of its housing went up between 1880 and 1930. Brick dominates, in forms ranging from modest single-story cottages to apartment buildings and larger single-family homes. Industry still lines the Mississippi, while homes and shops fill the blocks away from the water. In 2020 the neighborhood counted 7,734 residents, 43.1 percent of them White and 39.3 percent Black.

## Flight Context

Carondelet sits at approximately 38.5596 N, 90.251 W on the west bank of the Mississippi River at the southeastern edge of the City of St. Louis. From the air, look for the band of industrial plants along the riverfront with dense brick residential blocks rising behind them. The Gateway Arch stands about 6 miles to the northeast. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) lies about 5 miles east across the river in Illinois, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 15 miles to the north-northwest. A viewing altitude of 2,000-3,000 feet AGL gives a good look at the riverfront and street grid; the area lies within busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. Haze over the river is common on summer mornings.
