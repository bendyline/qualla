# Knights of Columbus Vatican Film Library

Thanks to a bold 1950 letter from two Jesuit priests and Knights of Columbus funding, Saint Louis University holds the principal collection outside the Vatican of microfilmed manuscripts from the Vatican Library - tens of thousands of works in Greek, Latin, Arabic, Hebrew and Ethiopic.

**Location:** 38.6367, -90.2352

## Introduction

One summer day in 1950, two Jesuit priests on the faculty of Saint Louis University sat down and composed an unprecedented letter to the Vatican. Historian Lowrie Daly and librarian Joseph Donnelly were asking permission to microfilm the manuscripts of the Vatican Library. In December, to the priests' own amazement, the Vatican said yes. The result sits today in the Pius XII Memorial Library on the university's campus in Midtown St. Louis: what the university's own exhibit calls the principal study center for Vatican manuscripts outside the Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana's reading room.

## Rome on a Reel

Daly, who lived from 1914 to 2000, is credited with creating the library, and the Knights of Columbus provided the money. The goal was simple to state and enormous to achieve: to make Vatican and other documents far more available to researchers in North America. Microfilming began in 1951. By 1955, with funds from the Knights, Daly and Donnelly had eight electric cameras running at the Vatican, along with two big developers, a printer and an electric splicer. According to the library, it was the largest microfilming project that had been undertaken up to that date. Between 1951 and 1957, the cameras recorded twelve million manuscript pages from 30,000 different works - roughly 75 percent of the manuscripts available in the language groups the project targeted.

## A Vatican Library in Missouri

The Film Library opened in 1953 and moved into the Pius XII Memorial Library on the Saint Louis University campus in 1959. Its first librarian, Charles J. Ermatinger, served until 2000. The films kept coming, too. Other projects in the 1950s captured Jesuit archival material from Rome and archives in North America, South America and the Philippines. By 2007 the library held microfilmed versions of more than 37,000 manuscripts, and the university now counts over 40,000 manuscripts spanning the 4th to the 17th centuries. The languages range from Greek, Latin, Arabic, Hebrew and Ethiopic to more familiar Western European tongues. There are reproductions from the Vatican's Biblioteca Palatina and Biblioteca Cicognara, and papal letter registers from the Archivio Segreto Vaticano, long known in English as the Vatican Secret Archives, covering the 9th through 16th centuries.

## Slides, Incunabula and Catalogs

The collection reaches beyond the Vatican. The library gathers manuscript catalogs and handwritten inventories, both of Vatican Library manuscripts and of other libraries' holdings. It keeps microfilm copies of more than 2,500 medieval and Renaissance manuscripts from other collections, and more than 20,000 incunabula - books printed in the earliest decades of European printing. For those who want color, there are 52,000 slides of illuminated manuscripts, preserving the color of medieval illumination on film. Shelves of printed books support the work, covering palaeography, codicology, illumination and related fields of manuscript study. Together they turn a university library in Missouri into a workshop for anyone trying to read the handwriting of the Middle Ages.

## Every October, the Scholars Come

The library is not only an archive but a meeting place. For decades it has hosted an annual conference on manuscript studies, traditionally held in St. Louis in mid-October, drawing researchers into the city to compare notes on texts copied by hand centuries ago. It also publishes Manuscripta, a biannual academic journal established in 1957 and devoted to the study of medieval and Renaissance manuscripts. The journal is printed and distributed by Brepols Publishers and edited by Gregory Pass. Some seventy-five years after two priests sent their letter to Rome, the reels they started still carry the Vatican's pages to readers an ocean away.

## Flight Context

The Knights of Columbus Vatican Film Library is housed in the Pius XII Memorial Library on the Saint Louis University campus, at approximately 38.6367 N, 90.2352 W in Midtown St. Louis. From the air, look for the university's campus of green quadrangles along Grand Boulevard; the Fox Theatre and the Grand Center arts district lie just to the north. Downtown and the Gateway Arch are about 2.8 miles to the east. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 10 miles to the northwest, and the campus lies beneath St. Louis Class B airspace, so coordinate with ATC. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 6 miles to the southeast across the Mississippi. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,500 feet AGL shows the campus and Grand Center together. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are the usual visibility concerns.
