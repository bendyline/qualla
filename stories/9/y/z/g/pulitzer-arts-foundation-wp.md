# Pulitzer Arts Foundation

Tadao Ando's first public building in the United States, the free Pulitzer Arts Foundation in St. Louis's Grand Center shows art without wall labels, in concrete galleries shaped by light and water.

**Location:** 38.6404, -90.2344

**Categories:** Art museums and galleries in Missouri, Tadao Ando buildings, Modernist architecture in Missouri, Museums in St. Louis, Midtown St. Louis

## Introduction

Walk into the galleries at 3716 Washington Boulevard and something is missing: the little cards on the wall. The Pulitzer Arts Foundation installs its art without wall labels, on purpose, to encourage unmediated encounters between visitors and the work. The building itself does much of the talking instead. Designed by the Japanese architect Tadao Ando, winner of the 1995 Pritzker Architecture Prize, it was his first public building in the United States, a study in poured concrete, daylight and still water in the heart of St. Louis's Grand Center Arts District. Admission is free, and the museum, known locally as simply the Pulitzer, has been called "one of the loveliest places in the country to look at art."

## A Gallery Delayed by Loss

The idea began as a private one. Emily Rauh Pulitzer and her husband, Joseph Pulitzer Jr., wanted a place to install works from their own collection, and in the early 1990s they commissioned Ando to renovate an abandoned automobile factory and showroom in midtown St. Louis, in an old entertainment district known as Grand Center. During the design phase, Joseph Jr. died of colon cancer, and that project was never realized. Emily Rauh Pulitzer later returned to Ando and commissioned something new: the architect's first freestanding public building in the United States. The foundation she established opened in 2001, and its first exhibition drew on the couple's private collections, with works by Roy Lichtenstein, Claude Monet, Pablo Picasso, Mark Rothko, Kiki Smith and Andy Warhol.

## Concrete, Light and Water

The building took nearly ten years of planning and four years of construction before it was completed in October 2001. It carries Ando's longstanding attention to natural elements such as light and water, and his signature material, concrete. Its concrete forms were cast on site over that nearly four-year build, using advanced techniques uncommon in America at the time. One description calls it "both a serene setting for the contemplation of art and a contribution toward revitalizing the urban landscape of historic St. Louis." In August 2014 the museum closed to begin an expansion that turned lower-level storage and office space into two new public galleries. Working with Ando and his office, the Pulitzer grew its public space from 6,800 to roughly 10,400 square feet and reopened on May 1, 2015, with solo exhibitions of Alexander Calder, Fred Sandback and Richard Tuttle.

## Three Works That Never Leave

Although the Pulitzer is a non-collecting museum built around temporary shows, three works stay on permanent view. Ellsworth Kelly's Blue Black, a 28-foot vertical wall sculpture, hangs beneath a skylight in the main gallery. In the courtyard to the west stands Richard Serra's Joe, the first in the sculptor's series of torqued spirals in Cor-Ten weathering steel. Both were commissioned by Emily Rauh Pulitzer and installed before the building opened, and both artists worked with her and with Ando on how their pieces would sit in the space. Ando wrote that Kelly and Serra "brought their own expression, conceiving a space for art that could exist only there." The third work, Scott Burton's Rock Settee, came later and faces the building's exterior reflecting pool.

## Shows Without Wall Cards

From its second exhibition onward, Selected Works by Ellsworth Kelly from Saint Louis Collections, curated by Kelly himself in 2002, the museum reached beyond the family collection, and nearly every show since has followed suit. Operating as The Pulitzer Foundation for the Arts until 2014, it has presented minimalism, Buddhist art, Old Masters and contemporary themes, along with solo shows by Dan Flavin, Ann Hamilton, Gordon Matta-Clark, Richard Serra and Hiroshi Sugimoto. Artists have often taken the curator's role: Gedi Sibony organized In the Still Epiphany in 2012, and Glenn Ligon curated Blue Black in 2017, inspired by his first encounter with Kelly's sculpture. The museum has also championed the rarely shown, from 19th-century Japanese ukiyo-e drawings to Donald Judd's late multicolored works and the sculpture of Medardo Rosso. Ando said he wanted a place where art is not exhibited "merely as specimens but can speak to us as living things."

## A House Moved Indoors

The Pulitzer's programs spill well beyond the galleries. A chamber concert series with the Saint Louis Symphony Orchestra pairs contemporary music with the art on view. In January 2014, a week-long series called Reset filled a gap between exhibitions with breakdancing, yoga, family activities, a drag show and the U.S. premiere of John Cage's "Thirty Pieces for Five Orchestras," which the Los Angeles Times had called "the most significant American orchestral work never played in America." In 2016 the German collective raumlaborberlin brought the building materials of a condemned two-story house from St. Louis's north side into the main gallery. The Staging program, developed with Prison Performing Arts and Washington University's George Warren Brown School of Social Work, invited homeless veterans and formerly incarcerated people into the galleries for theatrical training, employment counseling and arts education, ending in a public performance.

## Flight Context

The Pulitzer Arts Foundation stands at approximately 38.6404 N, 90.2344 W on Washington Boulevard in the Grand Center Arts District of midtown St. Louis. The low concrete building is modest from the air; use the Saint Louis University campus just to the south and the Grand Boulevard corridor as references. Downtown and the Gateway Arch lie about 3 miles to the east, and Forest Park's large green expanse is roughly 2 miles to the west. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 10 miles to the northwest and St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) about 6 miles to the southeast across the Mississippi. A viewing altitude of 2,000-2,500 feet AGL suits the dense urban setting; this is busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. Summer haze is common.
