# Gaslight Square, St. Louis

For a bright decade after the 1959 tornado, three gas-lit blocks at Olive and Boyle drew Miles Davis, Barbra Streisand, Lenny Bruce and Jack Kerouac - then crime fears, hostility to integration and commercialization dimmed Gaslight Square, and many of its lamps ended up lighting an amusement park.

**Location:** 38.6454, -90.2486

**Categories:** History of St. Louis

## Introduction

The gas lamps of Gaslight Square still glow - just not in Gaslight Square. As the St. Louis entertainment district faded, many of its lamps were sold to Six Flags, which was building its St. Louis park (it opened in 1971) and wanted them for its Missouri section, now called 1904 World's Fair. Most of them remain in use there. Back at the intersection of Olive and Boyle, where those lamps once lit about three blocks of clubs, cabarets and coffeehouses, a small memorial now recalls a district once known as a hub of music, comedy and dance.

## Out of the Storm

Before its heyday, the area was home to antique shops and, especially early on, a beatnik and countercultural crowd. Then came disaster. The city's 1959 tornado outbreak caused severe property damage, but it also brought an influx of attention and insurance money, and local business owners seized the moment. Jimmy Massucci opened several night spots, Jay Landesman chose the district as the new home for his Crystal Palace cabaret theater, and brothers Dick and Paul Mutrux ran the Gaslight Bar. On March 24, 1961, the St. Louis Board of Aldermen officially named the district Gaslight Square, though some knew it as Greenwich Corners. By 1962 property values had tripled. At its height the district held around 50 businesses, framed by gas streetlamps and ornate Victorian-style architecture inspired by the city's steamboat era.

## Who Played the Square

The roster of performers reads like a guide to the coming decades of American culture. Miles Davis, Bob Dylan, Barbra Streisand, Judy Collins and Peter Yarrow played here alongside Singleton Palmer, James Crutchfield, Bob Kuban and Jeanne Trevor. Comedy brought Lenny Bruce, Dick Gregory, Nichols and May, Woody Allen, Phyllis Diller, Jackie Mason and the Smothers Brothers. Jack Kerouac and Allen Ginsberg passed through. Traditional jazz filled Peacock Alley and the Opera House, while modern jazz lived at the Dark Side. Diners had Smokey Joe's Grecian Terrace and the Golden Eagle Saloon, and the Laughing Buddha coffeehouse served up live folk music. The square even reached television: a 1962 episode of Route 66, "Hey Moth, Come Eat the Flame," was set and filmed inside the Dark Side. The square also housed the studios of KDNA, an early community radio station that aired music, poetry and spoken word alongside anti-war and leftist politics - a predecessor of KDHX.

## Lights Out

The decline came in the late 1960s, and it had several causes: fear of crime, hostility to racial integration and excessive commercialization. One death came to symbolize the turn. On the evening of December 30, 1964, Lillian Heller, a legal secretary and artist, was murdered as she returned home from work, and her killing was later described as foreshadowing the district's end. The atmosphere literally dimmed. In April 1967 the gaslights that gave the area its name were shut off for two months as the bills went unpaid, and by May 1968 only 12 clubs were still operating. The last remaining pub, O'Connell's, moved from Boyle to Kingshighway in 1972. That December, the Board of Aldermen officially retired the name Gaslight Square.

## Empty Lots, New Rooftops

For the next two to three decades the district sat almost completely vacant, a stretch of empty lots and dilapidated buildings. By the late 1990s most of the old structures were long gone, and those that remained stood open and deteriorated fast. Renewal finally arrived in 2005, when the development company RJK Inc. bought many of the properties and planned 150 units, mostly condominiums, priced from roughly $280,000 to $600,000. By 2008 the vacant lots and condemned buildings had disappeared. Today the former square is a mixed-density residential community of new single-family houses, row homes, small apartments and condos - quiet streets where the clubs once roared.

## Keeping the Flame

Memory has proved more durable than the buildings. At Olive and Boyle, a small memorial gathers decorative columns like those that once lined the street, a wall with a stone plaque listing people and establishments from the square's past, and a few sections of modest cornices salvaged from former buildings. Other significant architectural elements were preserved by the National Building Arts Center in the Metro East. The Gaslight Theater, located in the former square, carries its name. Filmmaker Bruce Marren made two documentaries about the district, in 2002 and 2005, interviewing the Smothers Brothers, Jay Landesman, Phyllis Diller and others. And in 2024, Jazz St. Louis and St. Louis Dance Theatre staged a version of The Nutcracker set in Gaslight Square, danced to Duke Ellington's jazz interpretations of the score.

## Flight Context

Gaslight Square centered on the intersection of Olive Street and Boyle Avenue, at approximately 38.6454 N, 90.2486 W, near the eastern part of the Central West End and close to the Grand Center arts district. From the air, look for the residential blocks east of Kingshighway; Forest Park's eastern edge lies about a mile to the west, and downtown St. Louis with the Gateway Arch is roughly 3.7 miles to the east-southeast. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 9 miles to the northwest, and the area sits beneath St. Louis Class B airspace, so coordinate with ATC. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 7 miles to the southeast across the Mississippi. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,500 feet AGL works for picking out the Central West End and Grand Center. Expect summer haze and occasional afternoon thunderstorms.
