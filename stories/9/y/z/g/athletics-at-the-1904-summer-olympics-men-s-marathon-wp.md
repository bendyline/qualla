# Athletics at the 1904 Summer Olympics – Men's marathon

In the 1904 St. Louis Olympic marathon, the first man across the line had ridden in a car, the real winner was kept going on strychnine and brandy, and only 14 of 32 starters survived the heat, dust and two water stops to finish.

**Location:** 38.6264, -90.1994

**Categories:** Athletics at the 1904 Summer Olympics, Marathons at the Olympics, Marathons in the United States, Men's marathons, 1900s marathons

## Introduction

Frederick Lorz crossed the finish line first, and the crowd greeted him as the winner of the 1904 Olympic marathon. Alice Roosevelt, the president's daughter, was about to present him with his medal. Then the truth came out: Lorz had ridden a large part of the course in a car. Meanwhile the real winner was still out on the course, barely able to stand and hallucinating after his trainers dosed him with strychnine. Run on August 30, 1904, over 24 miles and 1,500 yards of dusty roads outside St. Louis, the race was so badly organized and so punishing that it has been remembered ever since as a bizarre chapter in Olympic history.

## Heat, Dust and Two Water Stops

The marathon started in the hottest part of the day, with the temperature around 92 degrees Fahrenheit, and wound through Clayton and out along unpaved country roads choked with dust. The route itself had to be changed at the last minute, after rain washed out roads in the Creve Coeur area. Nobody cleared the course, either. Runners dodged cross-town traffic, delivery wagons, railroad trains, trolley cars and even people walking their dogs. Water was scarce. The only sources along the entire course were a water tower at 6 miles and a well at about the halfway mark, 12 miles in. James Edward Sullivan, a chief organizer of the Games, set up no others, ostensibly so he could conduct research on "purposeful dehydration."

## An Unlikely Starting Line

Thirty-two athletes from seven nations lined up for what was only the third Olympic marathon. Arthur L. Newton of the United States was the lone returner from 1900, and the American contingent included the three most recent Boston Marathon winners: Sammy Mellor, John Lordon and Michael Spring. Cuba and South Africa made their first appearances in the event. Two of the starters made history simply by taking their places. Len Taunyane and Jan Mashiani, most likely Tswana men who were in St. Louis as part of the South African exhibit at the World's Fair, became the first two Black Africans to compete in the Olympics. Some accounts say both ran barefoot, but photographs taken during the event show Mashiani wearing shoes. The field also included Albert Corey, a French immigrant who arrived without correct documents and whose national affiliation is recorded inconsistently.

## Eleven Miles by Automobile

Lorz's race fell apart early. Overcome by cramps at mile 9, he accepted a lift from his manager and rode for the next 11 miles before continuing on foot toward the finish. Arriving first, he was hailed as the champion, and only as the medal was about to be presented did the truth about how he had gotten there emerge. He was disqualified. The episode captured everything wrong with the day: a course so loosely monitored that a runner could climb into a car and climb back out again without being stopped, and a finish line where nobody knew for certain who had actually run the distance.

## Strychnine and Brandy

The true winner, Thomas Hicks, had his own trouble. Ten miles from the finish he led by a mile and a half, but his trainers had to restrain him from stopping to lie down. Over the miles that followed they kept him moving with several doses of strychnine, a common rat poison that stimulates the nervous system in small amounts, mixed with brandy and egg white. By the end he was near collapse and hallucinating. He won in 3 hours, 28 minutes and 53 seconds, the slowest winning time in Olympic marathon history by about 29 minutes. That kind of aid would never be allowed today. Behind him the race produced other strange scenes, among them Andarín Carvajal, who ate spoiled apples along the way, lay down for a nap, and still finished fourth.

## Fourteen Finishers

When the dust settled, only 14 of the 32 starters had completed the course, the worst ratio of finishers to starters the Olympic marathon has recorded. Poor organization and poor officiating had combined with the heat and the missing water to turn a footrace into an ordeal. The marathon is one of 12 athletics events that have been held at every Summer Olympics, and the 1904 edition remains one of the strangest links in that unbroken chain. What St. Louis offered that August day was heat, dust, a car, doses of strychnine, and a group of runners who kept going across the Missouri countryside anyway, most of them never reaching the end.

## Flight Context

The article's reference point, 38.626N, 90.199W, falls in downtown St. Louis, Missouri, near the Mississippi riverfront and the Gateway Arch. The 1904 race itself was run from the World's Fair grounds at Forest Park, about 5 miles to the west, out through Clayton and into what were then rural roads in St. Louis County, including a last-minute detour around washed-out roads near Creve Coeur. Today that countryside is dense suburb; from 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL you can trace the westward spread from Forest Park's green expanse toward Clayton's cluster of office towers. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 12 miles to the northwest, and the whole area lies under its Class B airspace. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 4.5 miles south-southeast across the river in Cahokia, Illinois. Late-summer haze and heat, much like the conditions on race day, can limit visibility.
