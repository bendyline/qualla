# Chatillon–DeMenil House

A four-room farmhouse built by Francis Parkman's Oregon Trail guide grew into a celebrated Greek Revival mansion, sat atop brewery caves that yielded Ice Age fossils, and survived an interstate only because the highway was rerouted.

**Location:** 38.5933, -90.2165

## Introduction

In 1849 Francis Parkman published The Oregon Trail, a best seller that immortalized his guide, Henry Chatillon, as a "true-hearted friend." That same year, back in St. Louis, Chatillon and his new wife built a four-room brick farmhouse on a few acres of their own. The farmhouse did not stay modest for long. Enlarged by the businessman Nicolas DeMenil, it became the Chatillon-DeMenil Mansion at 3352 DeMenil Place in the Benton Park neighborhood, a house its National Register nomination describes as "the finest example of Greek Revival architecture in the Midwest." Beneath it lay a network of caves, and ahead of it lay a near miss with an interstate highway.

## The Guide's Farmhouse

Henry Chatillon's life stretched far beyond St. Louis. His first wife was Bear Robe, an Oglala Sioux woman and the daughter of the Oglala chief Bull Bear. She never came to St. Louis to live with him, and she died in 1846 while Chatillon was traveling with Parkman. In October 1848 Chatillon married Odile Delor Lux, who was also his cousin. She had bought 21 acres in the City Commons area at $26 an acre, and on five of them the couple built their home: a simple, two-story brick farmhouse with four rooms and a one-slope roof. The Chatillons sold three acres in 1850, and by the mid-1850s the rest of the land, house included, had passed to Nicolas DeMenil.

## A Greek Revival Makeover

DeMenil and his wife first bought the house together with another family. By the beginning of the Civil War they had decided to live there year-round, so they bought out the other family's share and hired Henry Pitcher, a carpenter and contractor, to remodel and expand it in the Greek Revival style. The western side was pushed out 12 feet, adding three stories, two porches and possibly the carriage house, and the work was complete by 1863. The neighborhood kept changing around it. Development, partly financed by DeMenil himself, eventually blocked the house's view of the river, so the front entrance was turned to face 13th Street, and in 1879 balustrades and a porch were added to dress it up. Nicolas DeMenil died shortly after the new entrance was finished. His son Alexander inherited the house and lived there until his death in 1928. A third generation moved in, found that the once bucolic setting had become a bustling urban neighborhood, and left by 1929, according to the museum.

## Caves Beneath the Floorboards

The next owner came partly for what lay underground. In 1945 Lee Hess, a local pharmaceutical manufacturer, bought the property and converted the house into apartments, keeping one for himself and his wife. He had bought the property partly for the extensive system of caves beneath it, which in the 19th century had served as lagering cellars for the nearby Lemp Brewery. Hess christened them "Cherokee Cave" and opened them as a tourist attraction. Then the caves revealed something older than any brewery: the remains of a peccary of the genus Platygonus. The discovery drew the noted paleontologist George Gaylord Simpson, who came to St. Louis and spent several weeks recovering, cleaning and cataloging more than 3,000 fossils from the caves below. For a time, the grounds of a Greek Revival mansion served as a field site for the study of the deep past.

## Saved by a Detour

By the 1950s the house was becoming derelict, and plans for Interstate 55 called for tearing it down. A route change in the early 1960s gave the Landmarks Association of St. Louis the opening to save it. The Union Electric Company contributed to the purchase and sponsored the renovation alongside the Landmarks Association, and the Missouri Department of Transportation agreed to the plan. Renovations began in the summer of 1964 under the architect Gerhardt Kramer, and the house was dedicated the following year. Cherokee Cave was not as lucky: its museum closed in 1960, and the entrance visitors used was imploded for Interstate 55, though most of the cave survives, closed to the public. Today the Chatillon-DeMenil House Foundation, created by the Landmarks Association, operates the mansion as a museum. Its neighbors tell the story of the area: the Lemp Mansion stands just to the north, the former Lemp and the still-operating Anheuser-Busch breweries are close by, and the house lies within Cherokee Street's Antique Row.

## Flight Context

The Chatillon-DeMenil Mansion stands at 3352 DeMenil Place in the Benton Park neighborhood of south St. Louis, Missouri, at 38.593N, 90.216W, beside the Interstate 55 corridor. From 1,000 to 2,000 feet AGL, use the large red brick complex of the Anheuser-Busch Brewery, less than half a mile to the northeast, as your reference; the mansion and the Lemp Mansion immediately to its north sit beside the interstate. The Gateway Arch is about 3 miles to the northeast. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) lies about 3.5 miles to the east-southeast across the Mississippi River in Cahokia, Illinois, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 14 miles to the northwest; the area sits beneath the Lambert Class B airspace. Clear winter days, with the trees bare, make the house easiest to pick out.
