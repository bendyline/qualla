# National Building Arts Center

In a former steel foundry in Sauget, Illinois, the National Building Arts Center keeps an estimated 200,000 fragments of demolished and remodeled buildings - from a St. Louis bank's rotating sign to a 30-foot Statue of Liberty replica from Manhattan - gathered by preservationist Larry Giles.

## Introduction

A 30-foot Statue of Liberty has found a new home in Sauget, Illinois. "Little Liberty" is a metal replica that came from the Liberty Storage & Warehouse Company building on Manhattan's Upper West Side. When that building was converted to condominiums, the statue was saved: it was donated to the Brooklyn Museum in 2002 and stood in the museum's sculpture garden from 2005 or 2006. More than two decades after that gift, in 2023, she was sent west for repairs and a new home at the National Building Arts Center - a 12.5-acre warehouse of rescued architecture just across the Mississippi from St. Louis.

## One Man's Salvage Mission

The Center grew out of the passion of Larry Giles, a pioneering historic preservationist in St. Louis. Giles grew up in the city, served as a Marine in Vietnam, and turned a personal collecting hobby and an architectural salvaging business into something much larger. The idea was simple and urgent: when an architecturally significant building in the St. Louis region was about to be demolished, save the best pieces of it before the wrecking crews finished. Over time the net widened. The Center now takes pieces from across the country and occasionally from abroad, and it collects artifacts before major renovations of historic structures, not only before demolitions. Giles died of leukemia on June 12, 2021, leaving behind a collection that stands as a record of buildings, many of which no longer exist.

## 350 Truckloads to a Foundry

Giles needed room, and he found it in an old industrial site. The former Sterling Steel Casting foundry property in Sauget was acquired for about $1 million, and the collection moved in. It was a monumental undertaking. By one account, more than 300,000 items were packed into 1,600 wooden crates of 30 cubic feet each and hauled over in about 350 semi-truck loads; St. Louis Magazine today estimates the architectural collection at about 200,000 artifacts, plus a library of more than 300,000 items. The foundry that once poured steel now shelters terra cotta, metalwork, signage and much more from buildings in several cities, with large holdings from New York City, Chicago and Philadelphia alongside the pieces of St. Louis that started it all.

## What the Crates Hold

Some of the saves are dramatic. The rotating illuminated sign of the State Bank of Wellston, with its support mast, was saved after Giles raised $19,000 to rent a crane and flatbed trucks to salvage it. An 8-foot terra cotta frieze from the Ambassador Theater Building is here, along with elements of the Missouri Pacific Building and of the remodeled Globe Building. The Cherokee Native Statue, removed from Cherokee Street in 2021 after a community vote concluded it did not appropriately honor the Indigenous communities of the land, found a home here too. The Center has also absorbed whole collections from other institutions, including most of the architectural collection of the Brooklyn Museum - which is how Little Liberty came to Illinois.

## A Library and a Lending Life

The collection is not just objects. Giles also compiled a large library of specialty books, manuscripts, periodicals, blueprints, drafts and other documents. The Center lends its materials to other museums and exhibitions, including the Pulitzer Arts Foundation, the Missouri History Museum and the World Chess Hall of Fame, so fragments of lost buildings keep reappearing in galleries. Keeping it all running takes money, and the Center raises it creatively. Alongside traditional fundraising, it sells duplicate artifacts and some library items, such as spare books, specialty periodicals, schematics and rare maps. Every sale helps preserve the rest.

## Flight Context

The National Building Arts Center occupies a 12.5-acre former foundry site in Sauget, Illinois, at approximately 38.5949 N, 90.1683 W, on the east side of the Mississippi River just south of downtown St. Louis. From the air, look for the cluster of industrial buildings in Sauget's riverside industrial district; the Gateway Arch is about 2 miles to the north-northwest across the river. The site lies less than 2 miles north-northwest of St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS), so expect traffic-pattern activity and contact KCPS tower before overflying at low altitude. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 15 miles to the northwest, and the area sits beneath St. Louis Class B airspace. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,000 feet AGL, coordinated with ATC, shows the site against the river and the downtown skyline. River fog and industrial haze can reduce visibility, especially on calm mornings.
