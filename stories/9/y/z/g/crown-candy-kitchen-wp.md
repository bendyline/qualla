# Crown Candy Kitchen

Opened in 1913 by two Macedonian immigrants in Old North St. Louis, Crown Candy Kitchen is considered the area's oldest operating soda fountain, still family-run and famous for handmade malts, homemade chocolates and a BLT stacked with fourteen strips of bacon.

## Introduction

Five malts, 24 ounces apiece, in 30 minutes. Finish them all and they are free, and your name goes up on the Wall of Winners. The five-malt challenge at Crown Candy Kitchen reached television audiences in 2009, but it is only one chapter in a story that began in 1913, when two immigrants opened a candy store and soda fountain on St. Louis Avenue in the Old North St. Louis neighborhood. More than a century later, the same family still runs the place, and Crown Candy is considered the oldest operating soda fountain in the St. Louis metropolitan area and one of the oldest in the country.

## Four Generations Behind the Counter

Crown Candy was founded by two Macedonian immigrants, Harry Karandzieff and his best friend Pete Jugaloff. As the family tells it, the pair brought their confectionery skills from Greece, along with a dream of creating a friendly, family place. Harry and Pete ran it first. In the early 1950s Harry's son George took over the business, and later George's three sons, Andy, Tommy and Mike Karandzieff, took charge, with some help from a fourth generation. Mike died in 2012, and his brothers kept the family business going. The candy in the name is not just nostalgia: Crown Candy still makes its own chocolate candy. Behind the counter today, you might find Andy Karandzieff working the milkshake machine, just as his family has kept the fountain running for well over a century.

## Old-Fashioned by Design

Walking in feels like stepping back several decades. The decor is deliberately old-fashioned, with Coca-Cola memorabilia from the 1930s, an antique cash register, four-person booths and a vintage jukebox. The menu keeps things simple: sandwiches, a section labeled "Chili, Tamales and Other Hot Stuff," and the desserts that made its name, especially the handmade malts and milkshakes. It is a popular lunch destination for office workers from downtown St. Louis. There is one small irony in the room. For all that Coca-Cola memorabilia, Crown Candy switched from Coca-Cola to Pepsi in 1999.

## Christmas Day Fire

Christmas Day 1983 brought disaster. On December 25, a fire caused by a space heater damaged the restaurant. Some of the Coca-Cola memorabilia was lost, along with $2,000 worth of candy. The fire came at the tail end of hard times: business had been at its slowest in the 1970s. Crown Candy recovered, and in the 1990s business picked up again. The booths filled back up, and the family carried on the work that Harry and Pete had started.

## Fourteen Strips of Bacon

Crown Candy's modern fame rests partly on excess. Its "Heart-Stopping BLT" is built with about 14 pieces of crispy, kettle-cooked bacon. It was first offered to Adam Richman, host of the Travel Channel series Man v. Food, whose 2009 visit also featured the five-malt challenge, and in 2012 the sandwich appeared on Best Sandwich in America. According to Andy Karandzieff, employees gradually increased the bacon over time. In 2025 the Missouri Restaurant Association's St. Louis chapter named Crown Candy Kitchen its Restaurateur of the Year, honoring the Karandzieff family for preserving the city's historic soda fountain culture and culinary traditions.

## Flight Context

Crown Candy Kitchen sits at 1401 St. Louis Avenue in the Old North St. Louis neighborhood, at 38.651N, 90.198W, about 2 miles north-northwest of the Gateway Arch. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, use the Arch and the downtown skyline as your reference and look north into the grid of older brick neighborhoods near the Mississippi River. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 6 miles to the south-southeast across the river in Cahokia Heights, Illinois, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 11 miles to the northwest; the area lies beneath the Lambert Class B airspace. The storefront itself is small, so this is a landmark best appreciated on the ground, ideally with a malt.
