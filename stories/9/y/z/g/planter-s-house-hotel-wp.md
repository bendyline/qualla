# Planter's House Hotel

Three St. Louis hotels shared the name Planter's House, and the grandest of them fed Charles Dickens fourteen dishes at a sitting and hosted the tense 1861 meeting that helped keep Missouri in the Union.

**Location:** 38.6264, -90.1886

**Categories:** Hotels in St. Louis, Demolished hotels in the United States, Buildings and structures demolished in 1976, Demolished buildings and structures in St. Louis, Buildings and structures completed in 1817

## Introduction

"Dining alone with my wife in our own room, one day, I counted fourteen dishes on the table at once." Charles Dickens wrote those words about the Planter's House in St. Louis. In his travel book American Notes, he called the hotel "an excellent house," run by proprietors with "most bountiful notions of providing the creature comforts." The hotel he described was the second of three to carry the name. Together they spanned more than a century of St. Louis history, from a hotel that opened in 1817 to one that closed in 1922, and one of them hosted a meeting that shaped the course of the Civil War in Missouri.

## The Hotel That Was Almost the Lucas House

The first Planter's House opened in 1817 on Second Street, run by Evarist Maury. He had intentions of expanding it, but instead the name moved to a new and larger building. In 1836 a group of prominent St. Louisans gathered to discuss building the grandest and largest hotel in the city, with Judge J.B.C. Lucas presiding and Bernard Pratte serving as secretary. A committee of Messrs. McGunnegle, Morton, Kerr and Brant set out to find a site and shareholders, and settled on land on Fourth Street, bounded by Chestnut and Pine, which Judge Lucas himself owned. On December 6, 1836, the shareholders elected their directors, and after applying to the Legislature the company obtained a charter. Ground was broken in March 1837, but the work took until 1841. The hotel was nearly named The Lucas House in honor of its patron, but the proprietors kept the original name, and the new Planter's House opened in April 1841.

## Fourteen Dishes and Long Passages

A room cost $4.25 per person and included four meals, and the hotel quickly became a gathering place for politicians and businessmen. Dickens left a vivid picture of it. He found it "built like an English hospital, with long passages and bare walls, and sky-lights above the room-doors for the free circulation of air." So many boarders filled it that when his carriage drove up, lights "sparkled and glistened from the windows down into the street below," as if the building "had been illuminated on some occasion of rejoicing." He was hardly the only famous guest. The hotel counted Jefferson Davis, Abraham Lincoln, U.S. Grant and William F. Cody among its reported guests. In March 1847, the statesman Henry Clay visited St. Louis, stayed at the Planter's House and was honored there with a soiree.

## Four Men and a State

The most consequential meeting in the hotel's history came on June 11, 1861, during the Civil War. Governor Claiborne Jackson and General Sterling Price, representing the cause of secession, sat down at the Planter's House with Brigadier General Nathaniel Lyon and Frank Blair. The question on the table was nothing less than Missouri's future. Lyon refused demands that Federal forces stay confined to St. Louis and that Unionist Home Guard companies be disbanded, and the talks ended without a settlement. What followed was a series of battles across Missouri, at Boonville, Carthage and Wilson's Creek, near Springfield, and later at Lexington. That meeting in a St. Louis hotel ultimately kept Missouri in the Union. Life at the Planter's House went on after the war. Governor Benjamin Gratz Brown stayed there on May 3, 1871, and his hotel bill from that visit survives.

## From Hotel Rooms to a Bank Tower

The second Planter's House operated until 1891. In 1882, near the end of its run, its proprietors were listed as J&J Gerardi and the property was assessed at $15,440. A third Planter's House followed, the last hotel to carry the name, and it closed in 1922. Rather than coming down at once, the building was converted into an office building and lingered for another half century. It was finally demolished in 1976 and replaced by Boatmen's Tower, which later became the Bank of America Building. The hotels are gone, but their story survives in a novelist's praise and the memory of a Civil War meeting.

## Flight Context

The Planter's House Hotel stood at approximately 38.6264 N, 90.1886 W in downtown St. Louis, on Fourth Street between Chestnut and Pine, just a short walk from the domed Old Courthouse and the Gateway Arch grounds to the east. The site was later taken by Boatmen's Tower, among the downtown high-rises. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, use the Arch and the Old Courthouse dome to find the block. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 4 miles to the south-southeast across the Mississippi, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 13 miles to the northwest. Downtown lies beneath busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. Summer haze over the river can reduce visibility.
