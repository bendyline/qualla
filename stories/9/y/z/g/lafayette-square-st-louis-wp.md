# Lafayette Square, St. Louis

Carved from a robber-haunted town pasture into one of St. Louis's most fashionable addresses, Lafayette Square was flattened by the 1896 tornado, eroded by zoning rulings and highways, and brought back by renovators starting in the 1970s.

**Location:** 38.6160, -90.2164

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in St. Louis, Historic districts, Parks, Gilbert du Motier, Marquis de Lafayette, Tornadoes

## Introduction

Before it became one of the most fashionable addresses in St. Louis, this ground was a place travelers feared. For decades after the French founded their village in 1764, the land was a common pasture where townspeople grazed livestock, owned by no one, and in time bands of criminals were camping on the commons and robbing passers-by. The city's answer was to sell the land, drive the robbers out and keep one tract for the public. Around that tract grew Lafayette Square, a neighborhood of grand houses facing what is counted as the city's oldest public park, now bounded by Chouteau Avenue, Interstate 44, Truman Parkway and South Jefferson Avenue.

## A Park Named for a Famous Visitor

In 1835 Mayor Darby won permission from the state legislature to begin selling the commons, and the Board of Aldermen set aside about 30 acres for community recreation. Streets framed the square on each side, and the southern one was named Lafayette, honoring the Marquis de Lafayette, the French volunteer who had served under George Washington in the Revolution and had visited St. Louis during his famous 1824-25 tour of the United States. Growth stalled almost immediately. A real estate panic in 1837 forced many buyers around the square to stop their payments, and the land reverted to the city. Only in the early 1850s, once the courts had settled ownership, did several prominent St. Louis families buy most of the land along the park's southern end.

## Mansions Behind an Iron Fence

Those families built expensive homes along Lafayette Avenue and secured state legislation barring "any nuisance within a distance of 600 feet from the Park." On November 12, 1851, the grounds were formally dedicated as Lafayette Square, and in 1854 the park itself was renamed Lafayette Park. Lots on the western side, along Missouri Avenue, went on sale by 1856, the eastern side along Mississippi Avenue followed by 1858, and Park Avenue on the north was built up by the 1870s. Residents and the city paid for trees, shrubbery, graveling, fencing and outdoor concerts. The first bandstand rose in 1867, the same year Benton Place opened as a private street off Park Avenue, and in 1869 an iron fence designed by architect Francis Tunica, winner of a design competition, enclosed the square.

## Crowds in Their Tens of Thousands

In 1868, a crowd estimated at 25,000 to 40,000 gathered to watch the unveiling of a bronze statue of Senator Thomas Hart Benton. The next year the park received one of six casts of Houdon's life-size sculpture of George Washington, who had fought alongside Lafayette. By 1870 the Daily Democrat was marveling that "some of the finest residences in the city" had been built there within two years, praising the elevation, the shade trees, the wide streets and the two horse-car lines into town. Through the 1870s, Thursday and Sunday concerts routinely drew thousands of listeners, sometimes more than ten thousand, and at one point thirteen gardeners tended the grounds. The 1880s and early 1890s brought steadier growth, as local churches and schools took on a larger role in neighborhood life.

## The Storm of May 27, 1896

Then the sky turned. On May 27, 1896, a tornado tore through Lafayette Square, killing many people and causing millions of dollars in damage. It uprooted nearly every tree in the park and on Benton Place, damaged the iron fence and destroyed the bandstand. The Union Club and the Methodist church at Jefferson and Lafayette Avenues were destroyed, Presbyterian and Methodist churches were crippled, the Unitarian church lost its roof, and many homes around the square were badly damaged or wrecked. For families who had built their lives here, it was a catastrophe. Some gave up and moved away. Others stayed to rebuild, and by 1904 the square had recovered enough to win special praise from foreign landscape architects visiting the World's Fair.

## Losing Ground, Winning It Back

The recovery did not last. In 1923 the Missouri Supreme Court struck down the city's 1918 residential zoning ordinance, and businesses began buying lots in the area. Gas stations and grocery stores crept in, the Great Depression sped the decline, and industrial encroachment and highway construction weakened the neighborhood further. The turnaround began in the 1970s, when St. Louis residents started buying and renovating the old homes, and in 1972 the city declared Lafayette Square a historic district. By 2006 most of the homes had been restored, and shops and restaurants had returned. The park today offers walking and biking trails, a duck pond with a fountain, a playground and a rentable gazebo, along with cannons from a British warship that bombarded Fort Moultrie in Charleston Harbor in June 1776.

## Flight Context

Lafayette Square is centered near 38.6160 N, 90.2164 W, just southwest of downtown St. Louis. From the air, look for the nearly square, tree-filled park framed by blocks of restored historic homes, with Interstate 44 along the neighborhood's southern edge. The Gateway Arch stands less than 2 miles to the east-northeast on the Mississippi riverfront. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) lies about 4.5 miles to the southeast across the river, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 12 miles to the northwest. A viewing altitude of 2,000-2,500 feet AGL shows the park and the street grid clearly; this is busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. Summer haze is common, while bare winter trees reveal more of the historic houses.
