# Eugene Field House (St. Louis)

The last survivor of Walsh's Row, this 1845 brick house sheltered both Roswell Field, the attorney who shaped Dred Scott's fight for freedom, and his son Eugene, who grew up to become the nation's "Children's Poet."

## Introduction

In September 1936, a St. Louis Post-Dispatch headline carried a son's memory of his famous father. Eugene Field's son, known as "Pinny," said the poet had been a child when with children. That same year, the brick house where Eugene Field spent part of his childhood opened as a museum in his honor, rescued from threatened demolition. It still stands at South Broadway and Cerre Street, just south of downtown St. Louis - a three-story brick survivor that holds two very different family legacies under one gabled roof.

## The Lawyer of Walsh's Row

The house went up in 1845 as one of a row of similar buildings known as Walsh's Row. Its most consequential resident was Roswell Field, an attorney who represented Dred Scott in the case that became Dred Scott v. Sandford. Field did more than argue. He formulated the legal strategy behind Scott's lawsuit for freedom, a case that went all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court and remains one of the landmark cases in American law. Behind the legal names was a man fighting for his own liberty. Few ordinary city rowhouses can claim a resident tied to a courtroom battle of that weight.

## A Poet Who Stayed Young

Roswell's son Eugene spent part of his early childhood here, and he grew up to become a noted writer of children's stories and verse. His poems, including "Wynken, Blynken and Nod" and "Little Boy Blue," earned him a lasting nickname: the "Children's Poet." His life was short. He died in Chicago of a heart attack at the age of 45. A plaque on the house honors him with a simple line: "Here was born Eugene Field, the Poet, 1850-1895." Historians doubt that he was actually born in this house, but the plaque still stands. Pinny Field's recollection, printed in the Post-Dispatch decades later, captures the quality that made his father's work endure - a grown man who never lost the ability to see the world at a child's height, and to write for those who still did.

## Rescued From the Wrecking Ball

Most of Walsh's Row fell during the 20th century, and this house nearly followed. Threatened with demolition, it was saved by a citizens' campaign that began in 1934 and passed to the St. Louis Board of Education. Local preservationists funded its restoration, and in December 1936 it opened as a museum dedicated to Eugene Field. Stewardship changed hands over the decades: the Landmarks Association of St. Louis took it over in 1968, and the Eugene Field House Foundation in 1981. For years it operated as The Eugene Field House & St. Louis Toy Museum, and its vast collection of historic toys suits a man remembered for writing to children. In March 2007 the house was designated a National Historic Landmark. Today it goes by a simpler name, the Field House Museum, honoring the whole family.

## Brick Among the Parking Lots

The setting has changed around the house far more than the house itself. It now sits largely surrounded by parking lots, with Interstate 64 a short distance to the north. The building is three stories of brick, three bays wide, topped by a side-gable roof whose end walls rise above the roofline. Walk up to it and the details reward attention. The entrance is set into a panelled recess in the leftmost bay, and each window rests on a stone sill beneath a stone lintel. Alone on its corner, it offers a glimpse of what the whole row once looked like, when a lawyer and a future poet lived behind those walls.

## Flight Context

The Field House Museum stands at approximately 38.6197 N, 90.1917 W, at the northeast corner of South Broadway and Cerre Street just south of downtown St. Louis. From the air, the Gateway Arch is about half a mile to the northeast on the riverfront, and Busch Stadium lies just north across the Interstate 64 corridor - the small brick house sits among surface parking lots south of the highway. The Mississippi River and its bridges are a few blocks east. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 4 miles to the south-southeast across the river, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) about 13 miles to the northwest; the downtown area lies beneath St. Louis Class B airspace, so coordinate with ATC. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,500 feet AGL keeps the Arch, stadium and riverfront in one frame. Watch for summer haze and river fog on cool mornings.
