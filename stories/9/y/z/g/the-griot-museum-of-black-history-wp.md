# The Griot Museum of Black History

A St. Louis wax museum named for the West African keepers of oral history, where the founder sculpted many of the figures herself and a real cabin where enslaved people lived stands among the exhibits.

**Location:** 38.6529, -90.2093

**Categories:** Museums in St. Louis, African-American museums in Missouri, Wax museums in the United States, Slave cabins and quarters in the United States

## Introduction

Many of the wax faces in this museum were shaped by the woman who founded it. Lois Conley made many of the sculptures at the Griot Museum of Black History herself, and the figures on display range from Josephine Baker and Miles Davis to Madame C.J. Walker and Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. The museum opened in 1997 as the Black World History Wax Museum. In 2009 it took a new name, one that explains its purpose better than any mission statement could.

## Named for the Keepers of Memory

In some West African countries, a griot is a historian, storyteller, praise singer, poet or musician, and often several of these at once. The griot is a repository of oral tradition, frequently seen as a leader who preserves and shares the cultural traditions of a community. The St. Louis museum set out to do the same work in a different medium. It collects, preserves and shares the stories, culture and history of Black people, with a particular focus on those who have a regional connection to St. Louis. That local lens sets it apart, but it has company in the wider field. The Griot is the second African American wax museum in the United States, following the National Great Blacks In Wax Museum in Baltimore.

## A Founder Who Sculpts

Lois Conley is a St. Louis native. She earned both a B.A. in Communications and an M.A. in Education at Saint Louis University, then completed a graduate certificate in Museum Studies at the University of Missouri–St. Louis. The museum she built displays wax sculptures alongside art and memorabilia, and walking its rooms feels like moving through a crowded, living roll call. Carter G. Woodson is here. So are Dred and Harriet Scott, along with Elizabeth Keckley, William Wells Brown and James Milton Turner. Clark Terry and Miles Davis share the company, as do York and Percy Green, among many others. Other exhibits trace important people in St. Louis's Black history through music, agriculture, civic service and advocacy. Because so many of the likenesses came from the founder's own hands, the collection carries a personal stamp, turning the galleries into something like a family album for an entire region.

## What Slavery Left Behind

Some exhibits are meant to be hard to look at. The museum preserves a slave cabin built on the Wright–Smith Plantation in Jonesburg, Missouri, a real dwelling where enslaved people lived. Nearby, a scale model of a section of a slave ship shows how Africans were transported to America during the Atlantic slave trade, and a documentary film on the Middle Passage accompanies it. Closer to home, a portrayal of auctions at St. Louis's Old Courthouse includes artifacts from the people who were enslaved at that time. These are not abstractions. The artifacts belonged to people who were sold like property, and the exhibits ask visitors to see them as people first. The effect is to pull a national history of slavery down to the scale of a Missouri plantation and a St. Louis courthouse.

## A Museum Without Walls

The Griot has never kept its work entirely indoors. In 2009 the Missouri Humanities Council formed the Urban Museum Collaborative, pairing the Griot with the Eugene Field House Museum and the Campbell House Museum to share resources and build educational programs on a "museum without walls" model that connects exhibits to the city around them. The museum also hosts gallery talks, education projects and cultural celebrations. Its annual art-show fundraiser is called 40 Acres and a Mule, after the unfulfilled promise of land and a mule to formerly enslaved Black farmers. One exhibit, Eminent Domain/Displaced, gathered stories of the historic Mill Creek Valley and of sections of St. Louis Place demolished for the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency site.

## Honoring the Overlooked

In 2019 the Griot marked the 50th anniversary of the death of Robert Rayford, a 16-year-old St. Louis teenager thought to be the earliest known case of HIV/AIDS in North America. It hosted events around National Black HIV/AIDS Awareness Day on February 7, invited community members to contribute memorabilia and offered health screenings. Two years later a Monument Lab grant launched the Black HerStory Initiative, honoring Black women who shaped St. Louis's cultural, social and political history, with monuments first planned for Pearlie Evans, a civil rights activist and aide to Congressman Bill Clay, and the abolitionist Mary Meachum. The project later evolved into a campus sanctuary around the museum.

## Flight Context

The Griot Museum of Black History is located at 38.6529°N, 90.2093°W, just north of downtown St. Louis. The Gateway Arch on the riverfront, about 2.4 miles to the southeast, is the easiest landmark for orienting over the city, with the Mississippi River running along the east side of downtown. A museum building is too small to pick out from altitude, so treat this as a city overflight at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) lies about 11 miles to the northwest and its Class B airspace covers the city, so coordinate with approach control. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is roughly six miles south-southeast across the river in Illinois. Summer haze and humidity along the river can reduce visibility.
