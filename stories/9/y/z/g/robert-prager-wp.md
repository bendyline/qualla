# Robert Prager

Robert Prager, a German immigrant who tried to enlist in the U.S. Navy, was lynched by a mob near Collinsville, Illinois, in April 1918, and lies buried in St. Louis's Bevo Mill neighborhood on a street now named in his memory.

**Location:** 38.5765, -90.2711

**Categories:** 1888 births, 1918 deaths, American coal miners, Anti-German sentiment in the United States, Emigrants from the German Empire to the United States, Lynching deaths in Illinois, People from Collinsville, Illinois, People from Dresden, People murdered in Illinois, People murdered in 1918

## Introduction

"Dear Parents I must on this, the 4th day of April, 1918, die. Please pray for me, my dear parents." Robert Paul Prager wrote those words, in German, for his mother and father in Dresden, Germany, shortly before he died. He was 30 years old, a German immigrant who had tried to join the U.S. Navy, and he was about to be lynched by a mob outside Collinsville, Illinois, during a period of heightened anti-German hostility in the United States during World War I. Today he lies in Saint Matthew Cemetery in the Bevo Mill neighborhood of south St. Louis, and since 2018 the street beside it has carried his name.

## A Baker From Dresden

Prager was born in Dresden on February 28, 1888, and emigrated to the United States in 1905, at the age of 17. His early years in America were unsettled. He worked as an itinerant baker and spent time in custody in Indiana for theft. By the time the United States declared war on Germany on April 6, 1917, he was living in St. Louis, and he made his loyalties plain. The day after President Wilson's war speech on April 2, Prager filed his first citizenship papers. He registered for the draft and tried to enlist in the Navy, since aliens were promised citizenship after successful military service, but the Navy turned him down for medical reasons. He kept an American flag in his window at all times, and when his St. Louis landlord objected, Prager reported him to the police.

## A Letter Near the Mine

In the late summer of 1917 Prager settled in Collinsville, in southern Illinois, a hub for mining activity, where he first worked as a baker for an Italian named Lorenzo Bruno. In early 1918, drawn by the high wages miners were earning in wartime, he took a job as a laborer at the Donk Brothers Coal and Coke Company Mine #2 in nearby Maryville. He hoped to join the United Mine Workers of America Local 1802, but on April 3, 1918, his application was rejected, possibly because of his argumentative personality or his suspected socialist beliefs. The next afternoon, Prager posted copies of a letter near the mine and nearby saloons criticizing the local's president and protesting the decision. It angered the miners. Members of the mob would later claim he had planned to blow up the mine, but no evidence supported that claim, and Prager had never been charged with any crime.

## The Night of April 4

Around 9:45 that night, a group of about six Maryville men arrived at Prager's home on Vandalia Street, bringing dozens of men who had been drinking at a saloon, and ordered him to leave town. They made him take off his shoes, wrapped him in an American flag and paraded him barefoot along Main Street, beating and harassing him as they passed saloon after saloon. At about 10 p.m., three Collinsville police officers took him from the mob and locked him in the basement of City Hall for his own safety. When the crowd reached City Hall, Mayor John H. Siegel tried to calm the men, urging them to let federal authorities handle the case, and the mob accused him of being pro-German. Then it seized Prager. Unable to find tar for a tarring and feathering, one of the leaders, 28-year-old Joe Riegel, himself of German ancestry, found a length of manila rope in a car and announced that Prager should be hanged. Some men were reluctant, but no one spoke out. Prager was allowed to write his note home, and at around 12:30 a.m. on April 5 he was hanged at a bluff outside town, in front of 100 to 200 people.

## Ten Minutes of Deliberation

On April 25 a Madison County grand jury indicted twelve men for Prager's murder. One, George Davis, was never identified or found, and eleven stood trial. The judge refused to let the defense try to show that Prager had been disloyal. The defense rested on three claims: that no one could say who had committed the crime, that half of them had not been there and that the rest were bystanders. Even Joe Riegel, who had already confessed his part, used that defense. In closing, the defense invoked an "unwritten law" that would not tolerate unpatriotic speech. The case went to the jury on June 1, 1918, and after deliberating for ten minutes, the jurors acquitted every defendant. The response split sharply. The Collinsville Herald's J. O. Monroe wrote that "the city does not miss him," while The New York Times warned that "the new unwritten law appears to be that any group of men may execute justice, or what they consider justice." The St. Louis Star wrote that "we cannot let ourselves go in such a way as was done in the Prager outrage and hold up our heads as civilized people."

## A Street Named for Him

Prager was not buried in the town where he died. His grave is at Saint Matthew Cemetery, in the Bevo Mill neighborhood of the city where he had once hung his flag in the window and filed papers to become an American. In 2018, a century after his death, the stretch of Bates Street between Morgan Ford Road and Gravois Avenue, where the cemetery lies, was given the honorary name Robert Prager Way. It is a quiet acknowledgment of a man who wanted to belong to his adopted country, and who was killed by men who decided he did not.
