# Soulard, St. Louis

Named for a refugee surveyor and the widow who gave the city its market, St. Louis's Soulard is a 19th-century neighborhood of row houses, blues bars and a market reputed to be the oldest farmers' market west of the Mississippi, with the Anheuser-Busch brewery at its southern edge.

**Location:** 38.6053, -90.2086

**Categories:** French-American culture in Missouri, Landmarks of St. Louis, Neighborhoods in St. Louis, French colonial settlements of Illinois Country

## Introduction

The market hall at the heart of Soulard was modeled on a Renaissance building in Florence. When St. Louis City Architect Albert E. Osburg designed the current home of the Soulard Farmers Market in 1928, he based it on Brunelleschi's famous Foundling Hospital, giving a Florentine pedigree to a market reputed to be the oldest farmers' market west of the Mississippi River. Around it spreads one of the oldest communities in St. Louis, a largely residential neighborhood of 19th-century houses, historic churches and bars that host live blues and jazz, with the Anheuser-Busch brewery on its southern side.

## A Surveyor in Exile

The neighborhood is named for Antoine Soulard and his wife, Julia Cérre Soulard. Antoine was a refugee from the French Revolution in the 1790s and a surveyor for the Spanish government, serving as the Surveyor of Upper Louisiana and becoming a major early landholder in the city. The land that would become Soulard was a family gift. Julia's father, Jean-Gabriel Cerré, gave it to his son-in-law, and Antoine began to develop it. Later chapters added new layers, not all of them peaceful. In April 1852, election-day violence broke out at the Soulard Market polling place between German immigrants and native-born Americans, and a mob also set out for the offices of the Anzeiger des Westens, once a large German-American daily newspaper in St. Louis.

## A Market in Perpetuity

Julia Soulard left the neighborhood its most lasting gift. In 1838, as Antoine's widow, she donated two half-blocks to the city on one condition: that the site be used as a public market in perpetuity. The promise has been kept through hard times. An 1896 tornado heavily damaged the market building, and for more than three decades the market carried on under makeshift sheds until the new hall went up in 1928. Today more than a hundred vendors fill it, among them farmers, produce sellers, meat shops, spice shops, florists and food stalls. The market has even found its way into children's literature, featuring in the opening scene of Alan Schroeder's picture book Ragtime Tumpie.

## Row Houses and a Brewery

Soulard has been a certified local historic district since 1972, and walking its streets shows why. Many of its houses date to the mid- to late-19th century, and stars are a common architectural motif from that era, turning up as anchor plates set into brick walls. Several 19th-century churches rise among the homes, including Trinity Lutheran. Lynch Street roughly divides the neighborhood in two. To the north lie mostly row homes and small apartments, while to the south stretch the Anheuser-Busch Brewery and the company's North American headquarters, home to its St. Louis Brewery. Highways have reshaped the map as well; LaSalle Park, once part of Soulard, was divided off by their construction. A neighborhood this old takes tending, and groups such as the Soulard Restoration Group and the Soulard Business Association organize events, work to keep the streets clean and safe and publish a newspaper, The Soulard Renaissance.

## Blues After Dark

When the market closes, Soulard's other life begins. Many of its bars host live music, especially the blues and jazz for which St. Louis is known, and the district hosts regular pub crawls. The barrelhouse blues pianist James Crutchfield called the neighborhood home from 1984 until his death in 2001 and played in many of its nightclubs, part of a musical thread that runs through the neighborhood. The calendar fills with celebrations through the year, including Mardi Gras and Oktoberfest, and a neighborhood with a working brewery on its southern edge makes a natural host.

## Flight Context

Soulard is centered at approximately 38.6053 N, 90.2086 W in south St. Louis, just south of downtown and close to the Mississippi River. From the air, the sprawling Anheuser-Busch brewery complex marks the neighborhood's southern side, and the Gateway Arch rises about 2 miles to the northeast. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,500 feet AGL shows the dense grid of row houses, the market hall and the brewery together. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 3.5 miles to the southeast across the river, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 13 miles to the northwest. The area lies beneath busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC; summer haze over the river can reduce visibility.
