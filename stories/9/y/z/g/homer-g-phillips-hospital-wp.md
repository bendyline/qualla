# Homer G. Phillips Hospital

Named for the civil rights lawyer who fought for it and was murdered before it was built, Homer G. Phillips Hospital in The Ville trained more Black doctors and nurses than any hospital in the world, until the city abruptly closed it in 1979.

**Location:** 38.6586, -90.2361

## Introduction

On the morning of June 18, 1931, Homer G. Phillips left his home and waited for the streetcar he regularly rode to his office. He never boarded it. Two men approached the 51-year-old attorney and shot him, and although two suspects were tried, a jury found them not guilty; his murder remains unsolved. Phillips had spent years fighting for a proper public hospital for Black St. Louisans. He did not live to see construction begin. Six years later, the hospital that bore his name opened in The Ville, and by 1961 it had trained more Black doctors and nurses than any other hospital in the world.

## A Hospital for Whites Only

Between 1910 and 1920, the Black population of St. Louis grew by sixty percent as rural migrants came north in the Great Migration to take industrial jobs. Yet the public City Hospital served only white patients and had no facilities for Black patients or staff. In 1919, Black community leaders persuaded the city to buy a 177-bed hospital in Mill Creek Valley, formerly owned by Barnes Medical College. Known as City Hospital No. 2, it was inadequate for the more than 70,000 Black residents it was meant to serve. Phillips, a local Black attorney and civil rights advocate, led the campaign for a civic improvements bond issue that would pay for a larger hospital, and he helped plan the institution that would eventually carry his name.

## Built in His Memory

Construction began in October 1932 at 2601 North Whittier Street, funded first by the 1923 bond issue and later by the new Public Works Administration. City architect Albert A. Osburg, who also designed the nearby Tandy Community Center, was the building's primary designer, and it rose in phases: the central building between 1933 and 1935, and the two wings between 1936 and 1937. On February 22, 1937, the hospital was dedicated with a parade and speeches by Missouri Governor Lloyd C. Stark, St. Louis Mayor Bernard F. Dickmann and U.S. Secretary of the Interior Harold L. Ickes. For the next eighteen years it was the only public hospital for African Americans in St. Louis.

## Training the World's Black Physicians

Homer G. Phillips was the first teaching hospital west of the Mississippi River to serve the city's Black residents, and its reach grew far beyond the neighborhood. By 1948 its residents included more than a third of all graduates from America's two Black medical schools. It trained nurses, x-ray technicians, laboratory technicians and medical records staff, and it offered training and work to foreign doctors who were being turned away by other hospitals because of their race. By 1961 it had trained the "largest number of Black doctors and nurses in the world." After Mayor Raymond Tucker ordered city hospitals desegregated in 1955, Homer G. Phillips began admitting patients regardless of race, color or religion, though it remained a primarily Black institution into the 1960s, by tradition and because of the character of its neighborhood.

## August 17, 1979

On August 17, 1979, the city abruptly closed every department at Homer G. Phillips except a small outpatient clinic in an adjacent building. The community responded with protests, and more than a hundred police officers were needed to control the crowd and escort the remaining patients out. William Lacy Clay Sr. led the opposition, and many in the community charged that the closure was racially motivated. Not everyone saw it the same way; local NAACP official Ernest Calloway said, "Giving up the hospital may be the price we have to pay for an integrated community." Picketing continued for more than a year, and a group called the Campaign for Human Dignity formed to carry the fight forward. In 1981, Alderman Vincent Schoemehl won the mayor's office promising to reopen the hospital. It never reopened as a full-service hospital.

## Dignity House

The building sat vacant, but it was not forgotten. It was named a St. Louis Landmark in 1980 and listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1982 for its significance in architecture, education and Black history. In 1998, Sharon Thomas Robnett signed a 99-year lease on the property, and renovations by her company, W.A.T. Dignity Corp., and Dominium Management Services ran from December 2001 to July 2003. The project cost tens of millions of dollars (reports range from $32 million to $42 million) and created a 220-unit supervised residence for older adults, Homer G. Phillips Dignity House, which opened in 2003. The corridors where generations of Black doctors and nurses once trained now house seniors, in the same neighborhood the hospital was built to serve.

## Flight Context

The former Homer G. Phillips Hospital stands at approximately 38.6586 N, 90.2361 W, at 2601 North Whittier Street in The Ville neighborhood of north St. Louis. From the air, look for the large 1930s hospital complex with a central block and two wings; Sumner High School lies just a few blocks to the west-northwest, and Fairground Park's open green space is less than a mile to the northeast. Downtown St. Louis and the Gateway Arch are about 3.5 miles to the southeast. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 9 miles to the northwest, and the neighborhood lies beneath St. Louis Class B airspace, so coordinate with ATC. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 7.5 miles to the south-southeast across the Mississippi. A viewing altitude of 2,000-2,500 feet AGL suits the dense street grid. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are the main visibility concerns.
