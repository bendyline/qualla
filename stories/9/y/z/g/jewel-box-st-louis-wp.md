# Jewel Box (St. Louis)

Born from a smoke-choked city's search for plants that could survive, Forest Park's Art Deco Jewel Box was engineered with glass walls and solid roofs, a gamble a 1938 hailstorm proved right.

**Location:** 38.6353, -90.2803

**Categories:** Greenhouses in the United States, Art Deco architecture in Missouri, Forest Park (St. Louis), National Register of Historic Places in St. Louis, Tourist attractions in St. Louis

## Introduction

In 1938 a hailstorm hammered Forest Park and shattered about 1,000 panes of glass in the park's other greenhouses. The Jewel Box, barely two years old, reportedly lost not a single pane. That was exactly the point. Its designer, city engineer William C. E. Becker, had given his conservatory tall glass walls under solid roofs, keeping glass off the horizontal surfaces. Nearly nine decades later his Art Deco greenhouse at Wells and McKinley Drives still glitters among the park's trees, listed on the National Register of Historic Places and still open to anyone who wants to walk in among the flowers.

## Flowers Against the Soot

The idea grew out of smoke. In the 1910s, as pollution and smog threatened much of St. Louis's plant life, public floral conservatories became popular in the city. City gardener John Moritz was tasked with exhibiting pollution-resistant plants where the public could enjoy the hardy survivors, and in the winter of 1916-1917 he turned part of an existing greenhouse into a formal display. According to one account, a visitor likened the displays to a jewel box in 1926, and the name stuck.

## A Depression-Era Showpiece

The building visitors see today belongs to the 1930s. Bernard Dickmann became mayor in 1933 and decided to build a new facility on a 17-acre site in Forest Park. The project cost about $117,000, with roughly 45 percent coming from the federal Public Works Administration. City engineer Becker drew the plans, and the Robert Paulus Construction Company did the work. The Jewel Box was dedicated on November 14, 1936, surrounded by a network of footpaths tying it into the park's existing walking routes and by three reflecting pools stretching away from its southern side.

## Walls of Glass, a Roof of Wood

Becker set himself three goals: minimize the risk from hail, admit as much sunlight as possible and keep maintenance costs low. His answer was unconventional for its time. The building measures 144 feet long and 55 feet wide, with about 7,500 square feet of floor space. Its vertical glass walls rise 50 feet, holding more than 16,000 square feet of plate glass in more than 4,000 panes, most of them framed in copper that has weathered to a green verdigris patina. Overhead, instead of a glass roof, five stepped roofs with clerestory windows let in the light, while the ceiling beneath is made of wood planking. Eight fixed arches carry the structure's weight, with triangular trusses between every other arch. Visitors enter through a limestone vestibule, and a concrete-floored balcony runs across the south end.

## Brought Back to Life

When city budget cuts arrived at the end of the 20th century, local volunteer groups formed partnerships with the city's parks department to care for the landscape around it, and a master plan for Forest Park set the stage for a long restoration that included bringing the Jewel Box back to life. An 11-month, $3.5 million renovation was completed in 2002, two years after the building joined the National Register.

## Seasons Under Glass

The renovation opened up the interior. Old vegetation came out, planting beds moved to the sides and sunlight now floods the central space. A large fountain and water feature fills the middle, designed to be removed for weddings and other events, with large pots lining both sides of the pond and baskets of plants hanging from the ceiling. New heating and air conditioning keep the space comfortable through the region's coldest and hottest weather, and a catering area serves the celebrations held here. Tropical trees, foliage and flowers fill the house all year, and special floral shows mark Christmas, early spring, Easter, Mother's Day, summer and fall.

## Flight Context

The Jewel Box stands at approximately 38.6353 N, 90.2803 W in the south-central part of Forest Park, St. Louis, at Wells and McKinley Drives. At 144 feet long it is small from the air; look first for Forest Park's large expanse of lawns, lakes and woodland, then for the stepped greenhouse roofs and reflecting pools among the trees. The Saint Louis Art Museum on Art Hill lies less than a mile to the west-northwest, and the Gateway Arch is about 5 miles to the east. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 9 miles to the northwest, placing the park inside busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. A viewing altitude of about 2,000 feet AGL gives the best chance of picking out the building. Summer haze is common.
