# St. Louis bullfight riot

On June 5, 1904, the authorities' cancellation of a promoter's bullfight near the St. Louis World's Fair left thousands without refunds, and the crowd burned his 16,000-seat arena to the ground.

**Location:** 38.6500, -90.3000

## Introduction

When the rioters finally reached the bulls, the animals gave the game away. The three bulls the crowd turned loose inside the Norris Amusement Company arena were emaciated and showed no aggression at all, hardly the fighting stock a bullfight needs. Their condition suggested that no fight had ever really been planned. By then it scarcely mattered. On June 5, 1904, thousands of people had come to an arena north of the St. Louis World's Fair expecting a Spanish bullfight, the authorities had stopped the fight, and nobody was getting their money back. Before the day was over the arena was ashes, and the World's Fair had been disrupted for a day.

## A Spectacle for the Fair

The promoter Richard Norris saw an opportunity in the crowds drawn by the World's Fair. He advertised bullfights for June 5, 1904, and built an arena bearing his name with seats for 16,000 people. He contracted the Spanish bullfighter Manuel Cervera Prieto and 35 others, and he sold more than 8,000 tickets at a dollar apiece. There was one large obstacle. Bullfighting was illegal in the United States and in Missouri, and opposition mounted quickly. The St. Louis Humane Society pleaded with Governor Alexander Monroe Dockery to "avert this flagrant outrage upon the civilization of the State of Missouri and of the United States," and religious organizations, including the Congregational State Association of Missouri, joined the appeal. Under that pressure, one day before the fight, Dockery ordered St. Louis County's prosecuting attorney to arrest anyone who violated the state's anti-bullfighting law.

## Rocks Through the Windows

The World's Fair itself was closed on that Sunday. The program opened with horsemanship from the fair's Wild West, followed by lacrosse, but the crowd grew restless waiting for the main event. It never came. As the announcer was introducing Cervera, a deputy sheriff arrived and declared the fight prohibited. While police and organizers negotiated in the office, the crowd, denied refunds, began throwing rocks through the windows. A man on the office porch who was demanding his money back was struck and injured by a rock, and others inside were injured by broken glass. Police with drawn pistols deterred the rioters, but there were too few of them to stop the mob from pouring into the arena grandstand.

## Pine, Tar Paper and Straw

Inside the arena, rioters released the three bulls, then set fire to the straw in the bullpen. The flames leapt to the grandstand, which was built of pine and tar paper. Police and firefighters responded from the nearby World's Fairgrounds, but they could not save the structure, and the arena burned to the ground. Several rioters were arrested for arson. The riot's toll was a handful of injuries, but the story did not end with the fire. Three days later, on June 8, Cervera was shot and killed by a fellow bullfighter, Carleton Bass, in a dispute over pay. Bass said Cervera had attacked him with a knife, and a coroner's inquest found he acted in self-defense, a grim coda to the whole affair. Afterward, the bullfighters asserted that Norris had never planned a bullfight at all.
