# St. Louis Stars (baseball)

Born as a bank messenger's St. Louis Giants and reborn as the Stars in a ballpark built for Negro league baseball, the club of Cool Papa Bell, Mule Suttles and Willie Wells won three pennants in four years before the Negro National League collapsed in 1931.

**Location:** 38.6322, -90.2260

**Categories:** Negro league baseball teams, St. Louis Stars (baseball), Defunct baseball teams in Missouri, Sports history, African American history

## Introduction

The left field wall at Stars Park stood just 250 to 270 feet from home plate, depending on the source, pulled in to make room for a trolley car barn. In some seasons, balls hit over the barn counted only as ground-rule doubles, and still the park was a paradise for power hitters. Home to the St. Louis Stars, it rose at the southeast corner of Compton and Laclede Avenues in July 1922, one of the few ballparks ever built expressly for the Negro leagues. Over the next decade the team that played there, fed by the talent of Cool Papa Bell, Mule Suttles and Willie Wells, became one of the great dynasties in Negro league history.

## A Bank Messenger's Ball Club

The story starts with a fan. In 1906 Charles A. Mills, an African American bank messenger who wanted to upgrade the St. Louis Giants, persuaded Conrad Kuebler, a white ballpark owner, to invest in the team. Mills then coaxed the Leland Giants into visiting St. Louis, discovered that their star third baseman, Dick Wallace, wanted a change of scenery, and signed him as playing manager. Wallace stayed for most of the club's existence and assembled a core of veterans, among them Ben Taylor, shortstop Joe Hewitt, first baseman Tullie McAdoo, pitchers Bill Drake and Bill Gatewood, and outfielders Jimmie Lyons and Charles Blackwell. The Giants won the St. Louis City League championship in 1912 and 1913, but they could never pry the unofficial western championship of Black baseball away from the Chicago American Giants or, later, the Indianapolis ABCs.

## Charleston's Summer

In 1920 the Giants joined the new Negro National League and finished sixth of eight at 25-32. Then they acquired center fielder Oscar Charleston from Indianapolis. In 1921 Charleston put together a historic season, with the Seamheads database crediting him with a .434 average, 12 home runs and 35 stolen bases in 62 games. Blackwell nearly matched him at .432, Bill Drake won 16 games, and the Giants surged to second place at 40-28. That October they met the St. Louis Cardinals, who had finished third in the National League, in a best-of-seven series at Sportsman's Park, losing four games to one even though Cardinals star Rogers Hornsby sat out. It was the club's high point and its farewell. That winter Mills gave up the franchise, and the club passed to Dick Kent and Dr. Sam Sheppard, who renamed it the Stars and built it a new home.

## Candy Jim Builds a Dynasty

The Stars inherited nearly the whole 1921 roster except Charleston, and without him they slipped to fourth in 1922 and to sixth, at 28-44, in 1923. Midway through that season they picked up several players from the folding Toledo Tigers, including a new manager, Candy Jim Taylor. A veteran third baseman in his late thirties, Taylor tied for the league lead with 20 home runs, 19 of them for St. Louis. Over the next few years he assembled one of the most impressive collections of talent the Negro leagues ever saw. He turned Cool Papa Bell from a left-handed pitcher into a brilliant defensive center fielder and leadoff man. He had Mule Suttles, second on the all-time Negro league home run list behind Turkey Stearnes, at first base; Willie Wells, whom many historians consider John Henry Lloyd's only serious rival as the greatest shortstop in Negro league history; and pitcher Ted Trent, with one of the league's most effective curveballs.

## Three Pennants in Four Years

In 1925 the Stars won the second-half title, only to lose the playoff to Bullet Rogan and the Kansas City Monarchs. Taylor left to manage elsewhere in 1926, but Suttles delivered a season for the ages: according to John Holway, he hit .498 and led the league with 27 doubles, 21 triples and 27 home runs, while the Seamheads database, which later audited the season, credits him with 32 home runs and 19 triples, both single-season Negro league records. In 1928 the Stars took over the league, posting the best overall record at 66-26 and beating the two-time Negro league world champion Chicago American Giants five games to four in a thrilling playoff. Wells hit 27 home runs in 1929, though Kansas City edged the Stars in both halves. In 1930 they won a second pennant, beating the Detroit Stars in the playoff, and in 1931 they were awarded a third as the league fell apart midseason. The Stars folded along with it.

## The Stars Remembered

The Giants name lived on for a while. Mills organized new independent teams under it, often signing former players, and they toured the East Coast in 1924 and 1928; in the late 1930s an African American industrial league team called the St. Louis Giants counted future major leaguer Luke Easter among its players. Recognition for the Stars' own era came much later. In December 2020, Major League Baseball classified the seven top Negro leagues of 1920 to 1948 as major leagues. The Cardinals have also honored the Stars by wearing their replica uniforms on several occasions, from a July 4, 1997, home game against Pittsburgh to a June 20, 2024, game at Rickwood Field in Birmingham, Alabama. The old ballpark site is now the Harris-Stowe State University baseball field, where a monument recalls Stars Park.

## Flight Context

The former Stars Park site lies at approximately 38.6322 N, 90.2260 W, at Compton and Laclede Avenues in midtown St. Louis, now the Harris-Stowe State University baseball field. From the air, look for the ball diamond on the Harris-Stowe campus amid the midtown street grid, with the Saint Louis University campus a short distance to the northwest. The Gateway Arch rises about 2.3 miles to the east. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 11 miles to the northwest, and St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) about 5.7 miles to the southeast across the Mississippi. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,500 feet AGL helps pick out the field; this is busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are the usual visibility concerns.
