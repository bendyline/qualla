# Floating Freedom School

When Missouri outlawed teaching Black people to read in 1847, Baptist minister John Berry Meachum moved his school onto a steamboat anchored in the middle of the Mississippi, beyond the state's reach, and taught hundreds of children there.

**Location:** 38.6214, -90.1786

## Introduction

The classroom sat in the middle of the Mississippi River on purpose. In 1847 Missouri made it illegal to teach Black people to read or write, so the Reverend John Berry Meachum turned a steamboat into a school, anchored it out on the river and placed his students under the authority of the federal government rather than the state. Students were ferried out to it in small skiffs. For more than a decade, the Floating Freedom School taught free and enslaved African Americans the skills the law on shore was designed to deny them.

## A Basement Closed by Law

Meachum was a freed man from Virginia who had been ordained into the ministry by a traveling Baptist missionary. As early as 1825 he had organized a day school in the basement of the First African Baptist Church at Third and Almond streets in St. Louis. Then, in 1847, the Missouri legislature passed a law that banned "the instruction of negroes or mulattoes, in reading or writing." Meachum was forced to close the basement school. The threat was not abstract: by most accounts the sheriff arrested Meachum and one of his teachers and threatened them. A lesser commitment might have ended there. Meachum's answer was to find ground the state could not claim.

## Class Is in Session Mid-River

He found it on the water. Meachum put a steamboat into service as a school and anchored it in the middle of the Mississippi, where it fell under federal authority instead of Missouri's. Inside, the "Freedom School" was outfitted with desks, chairs and a library - a real schoolroom, afloat. Students were ferried back and forth between St. Louis and the boat in small skiffs, and in time the school attracted teachers from the East. Exactly where it was moored remains unknown. It may have been near the spot where Meachum built steamboats, itself a location lost to history; it may have been upriver toward Alton, Illinois; or it may have floated somewhere else near St. Louis.

## Hundreds of Readers

Across the 1840s and 1850s, hundreds of Black children were educated aboard the Freedom School. Families who could afford it paid one dollar a month. Among the early students, by most accounts, was James Milton Turner, who went on to establish 32 new schools for African Americans in Missouri after the Civil War - a single student multiplying the boat's reach many times over. Another was John R. Anderson, who received much of his reading and religious training there. For these children, the skiff ride across open water led to something the state had tried to put out of reach: the ability to read and write for themselves.

## Passing the Oar

Meachum died in 1854, and the school passed to his former student. Anderson had been born enslaved and secured his freedom, and he became minister of the Central Baptist Church in St. Louis. He took over management of the Freedom School and carried on the work his teacher had begun. The years just before the Civil War were harder. Attendance dropped off, and by 1860 only 155 Black children were enrolled. The school closed sometime after that, its exact end as uncertain as its mooring. What it left behind was a generation of readers, and in Turner's case, thirty-two more schools.

## Flight Context
