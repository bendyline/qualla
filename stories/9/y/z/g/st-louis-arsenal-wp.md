# St. Louis Arsenal

Built on a Mississippi bluff in 1827, the St. Louis Arsenal armed the frontier and the Mexican-American War, then became the tinderbox of 1861 Missouri, when Nathaniel Lyon's secret shipment of rifles and the Camp Jackson Affair helped decide the state's fate.

**Location:** 38.5906, -90.2085

**Categories:** Missouri in the American Civil War, United States Army arsenals, History of St. Louis, Military installations in Missouri, Military history

## Introduction

On the evening of April 29, 1861, acting on orders from the Secretary of War, Captain Nathaniel Lyon loaded 21,000 rifles and muskets onto a steamer and sent them up the Mississippi to Alton, Illinois. The weapons came from the St. Louis Arsenal, a cluster of storage buildings on a bluff south of downtown that held more rifles and muskets than any other arsenal in the slave states. Missouri had voted to stay in the Union but to arm neither side, and everyone understood what those guns might decide. Lyon's move helped fuel the tension between secessionists and those loyal to the Federal government, and within weeks the city's streets would see blood.

## A Bluff Above the River

The arsenal was born of westward expansion. In 1827 the War Department decided to replace Fort Belle Fontaine, a 22-year-old post on the bluffs above the Missouri River some 15 miles north of St. Louis, with a larger facility to serve the fast-growing military forces in the West. Lieutenant Martin Thomas chose a 37-acre tract on a bluff overlooking the Mississippi, close to the main military base at Jefferson Barracks and within easy reach of the city and the river. The original grounds even included Arsenal Island in the Mississippi, which has since disappeared. By 1840 there were 22 buildings, manned by 30 ordnance soldiers and 30 civilian employees who assembled finished weapons and artillery from parts supplied by private contractors and armories.

## Arming the West

War brought the arsenal to life. During the Mexican-American War, more than 500 civilian workers were employed there at the peak. In two years they produced 19,500 artillery rounds, 8.4 million small-arms cartridges, 13.7 million musket balls, 4.7 million rifle balls, 17 field cannon with full attachments, 15,700 stand of small arms and 4,600 edged weapons. When peace came, the workforce shrank back to its usual 30, who spent much of their time refurbishing surplus arms returned from the war. Another surge came with the Utah War of 1857-58, when President James Buchanan sent Federal troops against the Mormon settlers of Utah Territory. Employment passed 100 workers, and the arsenal supplied much of the weaponry for William S. Harney's forces.

## The Prize of a Divided State

As secession loomed, several Southern states asked for their quotas of arms to be shipped from St. Louis to their own armories. Buchanan's Secretary of War, the Virginian John B. Floyd, was accused of aiding such transfers and resigned in December 1860; an investigation cleared him, though many still suspected he had helped arm the South ahead of secession. The arsenal held 38,141 muskets and rifles, the biggest collection in any slave state, yet it had traditionally been lightly guarded by a staff of about forty. In March 1861, Missouri's Constitutional Convention voted 98 to 1 to remain in the Union while supplying neither weapons nor men to either side. That made the arsenal an immediate flash point, and on April 20 a pro-Confederate mob seized the state's only other arsenal, at Liberty, carrying off about 1,000 rifles and muskets.

## Rifles to Alton, Blood in the Streets

When General Harney left for Washington on April 23, Lyon took temporary command and began enlisting Missouri Unionist volunteers into Federal service, orders Harney had refused to carry out. Cameron, General-in-Chief Winfield Scott and Midwestern Unionists remained worried about the more than 30,000 weapons at the arsenal, and within days the steamer carried most of them to Alton. Around May 1, Governor Claiborne Jackson, who favored the South while publicly pledging neutrality, called the Missouri Volunteer Militia out for "maneuvers" at Lindell's Grove, northwest of the arsenal and now the campus of Saint Louis University. Lyon suspected a thinly veiled attempt to seize the arsenal, and his suspicions deepened with the discovery that Jefferson Davis had sent artillery to the maneuvers. On May 10 he surrounded the militia, which surrendered. As the prisoners were marched through the streets, a riot broke out and troops fired into the crowd, killing about 28 people, including women and children, and wounding dozens more. The bloodshed became known as the Camp Jackson Affair.

## Lyon's Road and the Arsenal's Long Watch

The crisis moved fast. On June 11, Lyon and Jackson met at the Planter's House hotel, where Lyon declared that limiting Federal authority as Jackson demanded "means war." Lyon took the state capital without resistance and routed the Missouri State Guard at Boonville on June 17, securing most of the key strategic parts of the state for the Federal government. In August he was killed leading a charge at Wilson's Creek, the battle often called the "Bull Run of the West." The arsenal stayed in Federal hands throughout the war, supplying the Union armies of the Western Theater. In 1869, about 10 acres of its grounds became Lyon Park, and in 1871 its ammunition was moved to the better-secured Jefferson Barracks, though the Army kept the arsenal grounds, with major peaks in weapons and ammunition storage during both world wars. The Air Force took over the property in 1952, and much of the complex long stayed off limits as home to a major branch of the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency, which opened a new campus in north St. Louis in 2025, leaving the future of the old arsenal grounds to be decided.

## Flight Context

The St. Louis Arsenal lies at approximately 38.5906 N, 90.2085 W on a bluff above the west bank of the Mississippi River, just south of downtown St. Louis. From the air, look for the government complex beside the river, with the green square of Lyon Park adjoining the old arsenal grounds. The Gateway Arch rises about 2.7 miles to the north-northeast. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is roughly 3 miles to the southeast across the river, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 14 miles to the northwest. A viewing altitude of 2,000-2,500 feet AGL shows the bluff, the river and the complex together. This is busy St. Louis terminal airspace near a federal facility, so coordinate with ATC and observe any published restrictions. Morning haze over the river is common in summer.
