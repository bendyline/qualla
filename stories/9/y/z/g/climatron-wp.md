# Climatron

Opened in 1960 at the Missouri Botanical Garden, the Climatron was the world's first geodesic dome conservatory, a lattice of triangles that holds a steamy tropical rain forest inside a single, partition-free space.

**Location:** 38.6141, -90.2589

**Categories:** Buildings and structures completed in 1960, Geodesic domes, Historic American Buildings Survey in Missouri, Missouri Botanical Garden

## Introduction

Step through the doors of the Climatron and the air changes at once. Inside this geodesic dome at the Missouri Botanical Garden in St. Louis, the humidity averages 85 percent, daytime temperatures reach 85 degrees Fahrenheit, and waterfalls spill over rocky cliffs into a tropical rain forest. Outside, it may be a gray Missouri winter. When the dome opened on October 1, 1960, it was the first geodesic dome in the world used as a conservatory, enclosed in rigid Plexiglas panels, a bold experiment in whether a whole climate could be built and run under a lattice of triangles.

## Went's Experiment

The idea came from Frits W. Went, then director of the garden. The dome was designed by T. C. Howard of Synergetics, Inc., of Raleigh, North Carolina, working with the geodesic geometry that Buckminster Fuller had patented, and the garden credits the St. Louis architects Murphy and Mackey with developing the greenhouse design. Its ambition was unusual. Rather than dividing the interior into separate rooms for different plants, the builders relied on sophisticated climate controls to create a broad range of conditions within one open space, with no interior partitions at all. The aluminum-framed structure won the 1961 Reynolds Award, which honors architectural excellence in a building that uses aluminum.

## A World Inside a Dome

The numbers convey the scale. The Climatron rises 70 feet at its center, spans 175 feet across at the base, holds 1.3 million cubic feet of air and encloses roughly 24,000 square feet. Within that space, the planners set out to recreate a lowland rain forest, with conditions meant to range from the Amazon through Hawaii and Java to India. The builders are also said to have kept something old: a small neoclassical stone pavilion that predates the dome reportedly still sits inside it, a bit of formal garden architecture wrapped in jungle. Even the light was engineered. A bank of 24 floodlights, revolving at night in five-minute cycles, was designed to simulate noon light on one side of the dome and moonlight on the other.

## A New Skin

Controlling a rain forest took a toll on the building that contained it. Over time the original Plexiglas panels deteriorated, and the constant humidity damaged some of the metal elements. In 1988 the greenhouse closed for extensive renovations. The old Plexiglas was replaced with 2,425 panes of heat-strengthened glass, and the Climatron reopened in March 1990 with the gleaming faceted skin visitors see today. The makeover kept the original idea intact inside a new shell of glass, with the same rain forest thriving beneath it.

## Rain Forest in the Midwest

The collection inside has grown far beyond its beginnings. The dome once held over 400 varieties of plant life, and today the garden counts more than 2,800 plants, including 1,400 different tropical species, arranged around a rain forest theme. Temperatures swing from 64 degrees Fahrenheit at night to 85 during the day. Visitors follow paths past sparkling waterfalls and rocky cliffs to a river aquarium filled with exotic fish. More than six decades after it opened, the Climatron still carries visitors out of the Midwest and into the tropics in the space of a single doorway.

## Flight Context

The Climatron stands in the Missouri Botanical Garden at 38.614N, 90.259W in south St. Louis, Missouri. From 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, the 175-foot-wide geodesic dome is an unmistakable shape, a faceted glass hemisphere set among the garden's grounds just north of Tower Grove Park; Forest Park lies about 2.5 miles to the northwest and the Gateway Arch about 4 miles to the east. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 11 miles to the northwest, and the garden sits beneath the Lambert Class B airspace. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 6 miles to the east-southeast across the Mississippi River. On sunny days the glass panes throw back glints of light that can help you find the dome.
