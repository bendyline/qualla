# The Hill, St. Louis

Settled by Lombard immigrants who came to work the clay pits south of Forest Park, St. Louis's Hill neighborhood gave the world Yogi Berra, Joe Garagiola and most of a World Cup giant-killing team, and still keeps its Italian parish at its heart.

**Location:** 38.6170, -90.2780

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in St. Louis, Italian-American culture in Missouri, Little Italys in the United States, Sports history, Immigration

## Introduction

On Elizabeth Avenue, two boys who would become big-league catchers grew up across the street from each other. Yogi Berra went on to the Baseball Hall of Fame; Joe Garagiola became a popular broadcaster and television host. Their boyhood homes still face each other on the Hill, the historically blue-collar neighborhood on high ground south of Forest Park that Italian immigrants made their own. Bounded by Manchester Avenue, Kingshighway, Hampton Avenue and Columbia and Southwest Avenues, the Hill has produced more than its share of sporting heroes, and it wears its Italian heritage in its church and its language school.

## Clay, Rails and Lombardy

The neighborhood began as a mix. Immigrants from northern Italy, Germany and Ireland settled here alongside African Americans, drawn by the railroad connecting the area to downtown. In the 1830s Irish immigrants had discovered deposits of clay nearby, and plants sprang up to exploit them. Starting in the late 19th century, those jobs pulled in Italians, mostly from the north and especially from Lombardy, in such numbers that they formed the majority by the early 20th century. The name comes from the highest point in the city, once called St. Louis Hill, a few blocks south at Arsenal Street and Sublette Avenue. That corner borders Sublette Park, the former site of the Social Evil Hospital, where Josephine Baker was born, and sits beside the former St. Louis County Insane Asylum, opened in 1869 and now the St. Louis Psychiatric Rehabilitation Center.

## A Church Modeled on Milan

As Italian speakers multiplied, members of St. Aloysius Gonzaga Parish founded St. Ambrose in 1903 to serve the newly arrived Lombards. Their first church was wooden, and in 1921 it burned. Residents took pride in their parish and raised money for a replacement, completed in 1926. Architect Angelo Corrubia modeled it on the Sant'Ambrogio church in Milan, building in the Lombard Romanesque Revival style with brick and terra cotta, a piece of the immigrants' home region rebuilt on a Missouri hilltop. St. Ambrose served as a personal ethnic parish until 1955, when it became a territorial parish of the Archdiocese of St. Louis.

## Catchers and Goalkeepers

The Hill's sporting pedigree is remarkable. Yogi Berra spent 19 years in the major leagues with the New York Yankees and New York Mets, playing in 14 World Series and winning 10 of them. Garagiola caught in the majors before becoming famous for his colorful personality behind the microphone. Frank Crespi became the first Hill resident to play on a World Series champion when his hometown St. Louis Cardinals won in 1942. Soccer tells an even more improbable story. When the United States beat England at the 1950 FIFA World Cup, four of the five St. Louisans on the American team came from the Hill, a story told in the book and 2005 film The Game of Their Lives. Hill natives who played for the U.S. national team include goalkeeper Frank Borghi, Charlie Colombo and Gino Pariani. Singer Toni Carroll, another native, reached Broadway and the Copacabana.

## The Hill in the Blues

The neighborhood once carried an ugly nickname. According to Garagiola's book Baseball Is a Funny Game, the neighborhood was called "Dago Hill" in his youth, using a disparaging and offensive slur for people of Italian descent. The Hill was also well known to African Americans. During Prohibition and bootlegging, an African American enclave here inspired a string of blues songs. Luella Miller recorded "Dago Hill Blues" in 1926. In 1932 Tampa Red and Georgia Tom Dorsey sang of the Hill's link to illegal liquor in "You Can't Get That Stuff No More," and Charlie Patton mentioned the Hill in "Love My Stuff" in 1934, with "stuff" again meaning liquor. The next year, the North Carolina bluesman Blind Boy Fuller referenced it in "Log Cabin Blues," carrying the neighborhood's name far beyond St. Louis.

## Italian Roots, Present Tensions

The Hill today is overwhelmingly white; in 2020 its population was 90.4 percent White and 2.5 percent Black. That June, Black Lives Matter protesters marched through the neighborhood amid allegations that African Americans had been denied residency there. The Italian connection, meanwhile, has become more cultural than immigrant. By one recent estimate, only about 2,000 native-born Italians live across the St. Louis region, almost none of them on the Hill itself. The Italian Community of St. Louis holds popular Carnevale celebrations in February and Ferragosto in August, the St. Louis Italian Language Program makes its home at Gateway Science Academy on Fyler Avenue, and the city's Italian sister city is Bologna.

## Flight Context

The Hill is centered near 38.617 N, 90.278 W in southwest St. Louis, on high ground just south of Forest Park. From the air, look for Forest Park's large green expanse about a mile to the north, then scan south across the dense residential grid for the St. Ambrose church at the neighborhood's heart. The Gateway Arch lies about 5 miles to the east. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 10 miles to the north-northwest, placing the neighborhood inside busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 7 miles to the east-southeast across the Mississippi. A viewing altitude of 2,000-2,500 feet AGL shows the street grid well. Summer haze is common.
