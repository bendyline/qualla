# Missouri School for the Blind

Founded in 1851 by a blind teacher, the Missouri School for the Blind became the first school in the nation to adopt braille after its own students pushed past their director's objections - and its music teacher built one of the country's earliest braille printing presses.

## Introduction

The director did not want braille. Dr. John T. Sibley, who ran Missouri's state school for blind children in the late 1850s, objected that sighted teachers could not readily appreciate the raised-dot system. His students saw it differently. Working alongside a sympathetic music teacher, they pushed until the administration gave way, and in 1860 the Missouri School for the Blind became the first educational institution in the nation to adopt braille. It was a victory won by the very people the system was meant to serve.

## Founded by a Blind Teacher

The school opened in 1851 under a formal name that announced its mission: the Missouri Institution for the Education of the Blind. It began as a private charitable enterprise organized by Eli William Whelan, a blind teacher who had previously served as superintendent of the Tennessee Institution for the Blind. In 1855 the Missouri General Assembly placed it under state control, and it has operated as an agency of the State of Missouri ever since. The school has moved more than once over the decades, from its own building at 20th and Morgan Streets in downtown St. Louis in 1856 to its current site across from Tower Grove Park in 1906, but it has never closed. For more than 150 years it has served students from across the state out of the St. Louis area, and today it teaches children from kindergarten through twelfth grade at 3815 Magnolia Avenue in south St. Louis.

## The Students Make Their Case

Braille had been slow to take hold in the United States. It reached the Missouri school in the late 1850s through Dr. Simon Pollak, a member of the board of directors, who had seen the system's promise while in Europe. Sibley's opposition stalled it, but the students themselves took up Pollak's cause, and they found an enthusiastic spokesman in Henry Robyn, chairman of the music department. Together they overcame the administration's resistance, and braille was officially adopted at the school in 1860. Two years later the school reported that "great advantage has been derived from the use of the system of point writing known as Braille," adding that "in music its excellency is especially manifest."

## Dots Pressed From Metal

Robyn did not stop at advocacy. He supplied the school with his own custom-made braille printing press. It worked by means of metal slugs bearing braille dots in relief, which were placed by hand in a flat frame; soft rollers were then run over the frame to emboss the dots onto paper. The press went to work in 1865, with Robyn personally running it. More than one claim has been made to the title of first braille printing press in the United States, but some authorities award it firmly to Missouri. Harvard professor Gabriel Farrell wrote that "the first real printing press for the blind in the United States was invented and operated by Henry Robyn, head of the music department at the Missouri School for the Blind, in 1865."

## Music Under the Fingertips

It is fitting that a music teacher led the charge, because music runs through the school's story. Its alumni include the blind musicians Nat Brown, John William Boone, who lived from 1864 to 1927, and Louis Hardin, better known as "Moondog," who lived from 1916 to 1999. The school remains small. As of 2011 it enrolled sixty-seven students served by nineteen teachers, a ratio of about three and a half students per teacher. More than a century and a half after its students fought for braille, the school still teaches the blind and visually impaired children that fight was for.
