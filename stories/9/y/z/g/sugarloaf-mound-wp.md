# Sugarloaf Mound

The last of the roughly 40 earthen mounds that once earned St. Louis the name "Mound City" survived railroads, a World's Fair, a quarry and an interstate, and is now back in the hands of the Osage Nation.

**Location:** 38.5749, -90.2310

**Categories:** Middle Mississippian culture, Mounds in Missouri, Native American history of Missouri, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, History of St. Louis, History of the Osage Nation, National Register of Historic Places in St. Louis, Buildings and structures in St. Louis, Tourist attractions in St. Louis

## Introduction

For almost ninety years, a family house sat on top of the oldest human-made structure in St. Louis. Its mailing address was 4420 Ohio Street, and in the 1930s a widow dressed in black would still drive a horse and buggy from it around the neighborhood. Beneath her home lay Sugarloaf Mound, raised by Native Americans sometime between about 600 and 1200 AD. It is the sole remaining Mississippian culture platform mound in a city once called "Mound City" for its roughly 40 earthen structures. Every other one is gone.

## Mound City Loses Its Mounds

The nickname was earned honestly. Early St. Louis stood among dozens of mounds built by Woodland and Mississippian peoples, the latter the civilization that raised the great earthworks at Cahokia, just across the Mississippi River. One of the best known, called "Big Mound," was leveled in 1869 after its earth was sold to the North Missouri Railroad. Then, as the city prepared for the 1904 World's Fair, sixteen more mounds were destroyed. Across the river the outcome was different: about 80 mounds are preserved today at Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site. On the Missouri side, only Sugarloaf remains of the original forty or so. That makes this modest hill in south St. Louis the last physical link between the modern city and the people who built earthen monuments here centuries before St. Louis existed. It is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## A Loaf of Sugar

The name is a relic of another era's grocery shelves. Early St. Louisans noticed that the mound's profile resembled the tall loaves in which sugar was then transported, and the comparison stuck. Today the mound steps up in three tiers. Its interior remains a mystery in the most literal sense, because Sugarloaf Mound has never been excavated. It is assumed to have been used for burials, but no one has dug in to confirm it, and archaeologists are not certain whether it began as a Woodland-period burial mound or a Mississippian-period platform mound. That restraint has its own value. With so many of the city's mounds scraped away for railroads and fairgrounds, the one survivor still keeps whatever it holds, unexamined by any excavator's trowel.

## Neighbors on the Summit

The twentieth century brought houses. In 1928 Frederick and Nellie Adams built a residence abutting the mound's top level. Nellie outlived her husband and, still dressed in widow's black, kept driving her horse and buggy through the 1930s; she died in 1948, in her nineties. Oliver Schauenberg and his wife Anna came next, and Oliver held the house until 1962, when he sold it to Walter and Eileen Strosnider. The Strosniders stayed for nearly half a century. The mound did not escape the century unscathed, since a quarry and the construction of Interstate 55 both cut into portions of it. Yet the people living on it deserve some credit. Thanks to the stewardship of those homeowners, major destruction of the mound was avoided, and Sugarloaf endured while its neighbors vanished.

## Handing It Back

In 2009 the Strosniders sold their home and land to the Osage Nation, returning the summit to Native American ownership. The Osage count the mound as part of their own history: in 2015 the National NAGPRA Review Committee found the Osage people culturally affiliated with the Late Woodland and Mississippian peoples of the mound-building era. They bought the site with a stated goal of preservation: restoring Sugarloaf to something like its shape before later houses and razings altered it. The house on the summit, vacant since 2009 and deteriorating, was dismantled and removed by September 2017. In November 2024 came another step, when Joan Heckenberg, whose home sat on the second tier, transferred her property to the Osage. In July 2025 the pharmaceutical fraternity Kappa Psi, owner of the last residence on the lowest level, sold its parcel too, and the Osage Nation announced that the entirety of Sugarloaf Mound was back in Osage hands. The next steps are to remove the remaining houses, stabilize the mound and eventually build an interpretive center, which an earlier plan placed just north of the mound on land owned by the Missouri Department of Transportation.

## Flight Context

Sugarloaf Mound sits at 38.5749°N, 90.2310°W in south St. Louis, beside Interstate 55 and a short distance west of the Mississippi River. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL the three-tiered hill is small and wooded, so look for the I-55 corridor and the river as your guides; the Gateway Arch on the downtown riverfront lies roughly four miles to the northeast. Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site, where about 80 mounds survive, is across the Mississippi to the northeast and makes a natural pairing on the same flight. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is only about four miles east across the river, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 14 miles to the north-northwest, so expect busy Class B airspace overhead; coordinate with approach control. River haze can soften the view on humid summer mornings.
