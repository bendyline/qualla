# Laclede's Landing, St. Louis

On the riverbank where a fourteen-year-old Auguste Chouteau began building St. Louis in 1764, Laclede's Landing preserves the city's last stretch of 19th-century commercial riverfront - cobblestone streets and brick-and-cast-iron warehouses reborn as restaurants, bars and, increasingly, apartments.

## Introduction

In early February 1764, once the ice on the Mississippi had broken up enough to let boats through, Auguste Chouteau led a crew of 30 men back to a bluff on the river's west bank. He was fourteen years old. His stepfather, the French merchant Pierre Laclède, had given him the job of preparing the ground for a new settlement, and the men felled trees to build a warehouse and cabins. The place was named St. Louis. Its northern edge, where Laclède's landing on the riverside is still remembered in the name, survives today as Laclede's Landing - or simply "the Landing" to locals.

## The Fur Trader's Bluff

Laclède was chasing furs. He held a monopoly on trade along the Missouri River, and fur trading and trapping drove his interest in a landing here. In 1763 he had come upon a bluff rising almost 40 feet above the water, just downriver from the confluence of the Missouri and the Mississippi - a strategic spot for a trading post. A stone house went up as Laclede's home, and he named the village for King Louis IX of France. The street plan he and Chouteau laid out, with short city blocks and the Mississippi as its eastern spine, still shapes the district. Walk the Landing's cobblestones and you are tracing the bones of the original French town.

## Fire and Iron

The warehouses that define the Landing today are the children of a catastrophe. In 1849 the Great Fire broke out aboard the steamboat White Cloud and leaped to the watercraft docked beside it and on to the wooden warehouses along the shore, razing fifteen city blocks. St. Louis rebuilt quickly and more solidly. Cast-iron and brick warehouses sprang up at the Landing to serve the riverfront commerce that fueled the city's growth, and the buildings that stand here now date from the mid-19th to the early 20th century. Brick walls, cast iron and cobbled streets give the district a texture the rest of the riverfront has lost. It is the only part of St. Louis's riverfront that still has most of its 19th-century structures.

## Cobblestones Reborn

By the mid-1960s, local officials concluded that the district's compact footprint could not support modern industry and designated it for redevelopment. In 1976 the 22-acre district was listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and many of its warehouses were converted into shops, restaurants and bars. The nightlife has since faded, and developers have put more than $75 million into turning historic buildings into apartments with ground-floor retail, while in 2026 a broker was still working to lease about 55,000 square feet of empty storefronts. The Landing sits just north of Gateway Arch National Park, separated from it by the overland spans of the Eads Bridge, and the Landing's MetroLink station sits on the bridge's lower deck. Metro, the local transit agency, once kept its headquarters here. The Landing has found its way into song, too: Wilco mentions it in "Heavy Metal Drummer," from the band's 2002 album Yankee Hotel Foxtrot. Frontman Jeff Tweedy grew up in nearby Belleville, Illinois.

## Flight Context

Laclede's Landing lies at approximately 38.6303 N, 90.1833 W on the Missouri bank of the Mississippi River in downtown St. Louis. From the air, the Gateway Arch is the obvious reference - the Landing's tight grid of brick warehouses sits immediately to its north, bounded on the south by the Eads Bridge and its overland approach spans. The Mississippi runs along the district's eastern edge, with East St. Louis, Illinois, across the water. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 4.5 miles to the south-southeast across the river, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) about 13 miles to the northwest; downtown lies beneath St. Louis Class B airspace, so coordinate with ATC. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,500 feet AGL frames the Arch, the Eads Bridge and the Landing together. River fog can linger on cool mornings, and summer haze is common.
