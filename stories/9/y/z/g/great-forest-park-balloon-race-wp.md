# Great Forest Park Balloon Race

What began with a handful of balloons and a few spectators in 1973 grew into one of America's best-attended hot air balloon races, a hare-and-hound chase that sends invited pilots drifting over St. Louis rooftops in pursuit of a giant lead balloon.

## Introduction

In 1973, six or seven hot air balloons gathered in Forest Park, and a dozen to twenty people came to watch. That was the whole crowd. Nikki Caplan and John O'Toole had launched something small and a little improbable: a balloon race in the middle of a major American city. Half a century later, the Great Forest Park Balloon Race draws crowds of more than 100,000 over its weekend, among the largest at any hot air balloon race in the United States - and every year its pilots still face the same peculiar challenge of chasing a balloon across a city full of rooftops, streets and backyards.

## Four Novices Take the Reins

The race changed hands early. In 1977, Caplan and O'Toole passed its organization to a group of four novice balloonists - John Marlow, John Schaumburg, Ted Staley and Dan Schettler - who formed a ballooning group called the Mississippi River Balloon Transit Company. Those beginners stuck with it, and the company went on to host the race for decades, building it into one of the country's marquee ballooning events. The race's organizers describe it as the second oldest balloon race in the United States. One year went without it: no race was held in 2020. Today it is a September fixture, a free event staged on Forest Park's Emerson Central Fields, with race day preceded by an evening glow.

## Chasing the Hare

The format is called hare and hound. First the "hare" lifts off from the Central Fields - a lead balloon, a sponsor's hot air balloon in recent years. The pilots follow, trying to track it wherever the wind carries it. When the hare finally lands, its crew lays out an X on the ground as a target, each pilot tries to drop a birdseed or bean bag as close to it as possible, and whoever lands a bag nearest the target wins. It sounds whimsical, but the urban setting makes it hard. Because the race unfolds over the city of St. Louis, it is considered "particularly prestigious and challenging," and participation is by invitation only. Landings have come down in backyards, on baseball diamonds and golf courses, in street intersections, and even on the convent grounds of the School Sisters of Notre Dame.

## Night of the Glow

On the evening before the race, the balloons take part in the Balloon Glow. Crews inflate their envelopes and fire their burners, and spectators walk among the illuminated balloons as they light up from within - a field of giant lanterns flickering in the dark. The evening also brings a fireworks show. During the week of the event, the Saint Louis Symphony Orchestra performs a free public concert on nearby Art Hill, the grassy slope beside the Saint Louis Art Museum. On race day itself, the ballooning comes with a festival of food and drink, inflatable castles and a climbing wall for younger visitors, and the whole weekend is free to attend.

## A Local Legacy

Recognition came from Washington. On May 23, 2000, the American Folklife Center at the Library of Congress honored the race as a Local Legacy, following a nomination by Dick Gephardt, then the Minority Leader of the United States House of Representatives. The honor fit the event's character. The Great Forest Park Balloon Race is not really about winning. It is about a city looking up together - watching the hare balloon drift toward the rooftops and a sky full of pilots giving chase, much as the first handful of spectators did in 1973.

## Flight Context

The race launches from the Emerson Central Fields in Forest Park at approximately 38.6381 N, 90.2849 W. From the air, Forest Park is unmistakable: a vast green rectangle west of the Central West End, with the Saint Louis Art Museum crowning Art Hill above the Grand Basin and the park's lakes and golf course spread around it. Downtown St. Louis and the Gateway Arch are about 5.5 miles to the east. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 9 miles to the north-northwest, so the park lies beneath St. Louis Class B airspace; coordinate with ATC. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 8.5 miles to the east-southeast across the Mississippi. On race weekend, expect many low-flying balloons and possible temporary flight restrictions; check NOTAMs before flying over the park. A viewing altitude of 2,500-3,000 feet AGL keeps well clear of balloon traffic while showing the whole park. September usually brings good visibility, though morning haze is common.
