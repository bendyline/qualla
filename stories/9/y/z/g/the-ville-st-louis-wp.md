# The Ville, St. Louis

Hemmed in by racial covenants, forty-two blocks of North St. Louis became the center of the city's African American culture for four decades, home to a pioneering high school, a Black teaching hospital and a remarkable roster of residents.

**Location:** 38.6587, -90.2395

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in St. Louis, History of racial segregation in the United States

## Introduction

Charles Elleard was a horticulturist, and the rural settlement on the prairie northwest of early St. Louis came to be called Elleardsville after him. The city annexed it in 1876, and the long name eventually shortened to something friendlier. As the Ville, these forty-two square blocks of North St. Louis would become, from 1911 to 1950, the center of African American culture in the city. They became it not by invitation but by exclusion, and what residents built inside those boundaries still stands.

## Common Fields and Plantation Ghosts

After St. Louis was established in 1764, this land became part of the Grand Prairie Common Fields. The first Black residents arrived as enslaved laborers working for local farmers, and in the nineteenth century white settlers from Virginia and Kentucky followed. Some of those early landowners still speak through the street signs. Kennerly and Taylor Avenues carry their names, while Wash Street became Whittier and Goode became Annie Malone Drive. James Kennerly operated a substantial plantation called Cote Placquemine near present-day Kennerly and Taylor Avenues; it burned down in 1863. Those fields and farms eventually gave way to a dense city neighborhood. Today the Ville is bounded by St. Louis Avenue on the north and Martin Luther King Drive on the south, with Sarah and Taylor marking its eastern and western edges.

## A Home Drawn by Covenants

Beginning in 1911, restrictive covenants and other legal barriers kept African Americans from finding housing in many parts of St. Louis. In the early 1910s those racially restrictive covenants made the Ville one of the only places in the city where Black residents could own property. What followed was concentration, and concentration changed the neighborhood. The Black population drawn here by necessity transformed the old Elleardsville into a cultural epicenter, filled with African American businesses and anchored by public institutions. The neighborhood reached its peak in the 1920s and 1930s. For nearly four decades, this small grid of streets was the heart of Black cultural life in St. Louis, a community that took a boundary drawn to contain it and made it a foundation to build on.

## Brick, Stone and Ambition

The institutions tell the story. Sumner High School, described as the first high school for Black students west of the Mississippi River, occupies a building on Cottage Avenue that dates to about 1908 to 1910. Homer G. Phillips Hospital on North Whittier Street opened in 1937 as one of the few Black teaching hospitals in the United States and stayed active until 1979. Antioch Baptist Church, built in 1921 in red brick Gothic style, served as a cultural hub through segregation and beyond. The St. Louis Colored Orphans Home, built in 1922, lives on as the Annie Malone Children and Family Service Center. The Marshall School of 1918 and the Tandy Community Center of 1938 round out a remarkable cluster, and all six of these buildings are listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## What Remains, Who Came From Here

After 1948 the Ville lost nearly 40 percent of its residents, along with many of its businesses and public amenities. By the 2010 Census, 1,868 people lived in the Ville itself and 6,189 in the surrounding Greater Ville. Schools have closed, including Cote Brilliante Elementary in 2018 and the old Simmons Colored School, once known as Colored School #8. Still, the neighborhood's history was reviewed and its surviving historic resources assessed in a 1998 study, extended in 2010, and the roll of people linked to the Ville remains extraordinary. Chuck Berry, Tina Turner, the boxer Sonny Liston, Dick Gregory and Grace Bumbry are all counted among them. So is Annie Turnbo Malone, whose name now marks both a street and the former orphans' home. The neighborhood also took a heavy blow on May 16, 2025, when a tornado tore through north St. Louis: in the Greater Ville, whole stretches of blocks had every house damaged or destroyed, and Sumner High School's roof was badly damaged. A neighborhood plan for the Ville, the Greater Ville and Kingsway East, adopted by the city's Planning Commission in December 2025, aims to guide the recovery.

## Flight Context

The Ville is centered near 38.6587°N, 90.2395°W in North St. Louis, a compact grid bounded by St. Louis Avenue, Martin Luther King Drive, Sarah and Taylor. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the dense residential street grid about 3.8 miles northwest of the Gateway Arch, which stands on the Mississippi riverfront and makes the best reference point for the city. The neighborhood's historic buildings, including the Sumner High School and the former Homer G. Phillips Hospital, are difficult to identify individually from the air. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) lies roughly 9.5 miles to the northwest and its Class B airspace covers the area, so work with approach control. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is across the river in Illinois to the southeast. Expect summer haze near the river.
