# Anheuser-Busch Brewery

On the south side of St. Louis, a 142-acre brewery of red-brick Romanesque towers, a schoolhouse turned head office, and working Clydesdales became a National Historic Landmark for its place in the story of American beer.

**Location:** 38.5975, -90.2122

## Introduction

Light pours into the Brew House through its windows and falls across intricate ironwork, while chandeliers shaped like hops hang down through several stories of open space. The building went up in 1891 and 1892, and it is only one of 189 structures spread across the 142 acres of the Anheuser-Busch Brewery in St. Louis. Many of them are red brick Romanesque buildings with square crenelated towers and elaborate ornament, giving a working brewery the skyline of a fortress. In 1966 the complex was designated a National Historic Landmark, a recognition of the company's place in the history of beer brewing and distribution in the United States.

## A Tangled Beginning

The brewery's origins are messier than its polished brand suggests. An earlier operation on the site, the Bavarian Brewery, went bankrupt owing thousands of dollars to creditors, and around 1861 the building ended up in the hands of one of them, Eberhard Anheuser. Anheuser turned to Adolphus Busch, a German immigrant, to run it. By about 1879 the business had incorporated as the Anheuser-Busch Brewing Association. In between, in 1876, the first Budweiser was brewed here, in an Italianate brew house designed by Edmund Jungenfeld in 1869. The same architect designed an 1879 Romanesque Revival brew house, four stories tall with two more below ground, and that building still stands. Some accounts date the brewery's beginnings to 1852, but the details of those earliest years remain tangled.

## Castles of Red Brick

Walking the grounds feels less like touring a factory than exploring a small city. The landmarked district takes in all 189 structures, and many of the most striking are those red brick Romanesque buildings, their square crenelated towers and elaborate details repeated from block to block. The 1891-1892 Brew House is the showpiece, singled out for its multi-storied hop chandeliers, its intricate ironwork and the way it uses natural light. Brewing is industrial work, full of heat, steam and heavy machinery, yet here it was housed in buildings designed to impress anyone who walked through the doors.

## The Schoolhouse Head Office

One of the oddest buildings on the grounds was never meant for beer at all. The Lyon School House was built by the St. Louis Public Schools in 1868, and it is considered one of the oldest school buildings in St. Louis. After 1907 it served as the head offices of the brewery, so the brewery was managed from a former school. In 2015 the brewery opened the Old Schoolhouse Museum there, holding archive pieces from the company's founding onward, including a rare early Budweiser bottle and a bas-relief of the brewery as it looked around 1885 to 1890. Tour offerings change, so check what is currently open before planning a visit. It is a fitting place to see the scale of the change, from a struggling brewery to a complex of nearly two hundred structures.

## Horses with a Day Job

The most famous residents of the brewery are not people. The company keeps a rotation of its Budweiser Clydesdales at the St. Louis brewery, and these massive draft horses have a long history here: the hitch was introduced to the American public on April 7, 1933, to celebrate the repeal of Prohibition, and the St. Louis horses are housed in a brick-and-stained-glass stable built in 1885. They are now one of the brand's most recognizable symbols. Visitors can watch the Clydesdales in their exercise field and see their places in the carriage house. Not all of the herd lives at the brewery. Some are kept at Grant's Farm in St. Louis County, a property once farmed by Ulysses S. Grant, and since 2008 roughly half of the Budweiser Clydesdales have been kept at the Warm Springs Ranch near Boonville, Missouri.

## Flight Context

The Anheuser-Busch Brewery covers 142 acres at 38.598N, 90.212W on the south side of St. Louis, Missouri, a short distance west of the Mississippi River. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look for a dense cluster of large red brick industrial buildings and towers south of downtown; the Gateway Arch stands roughly 2.5 miles to the northeast and makes an easy reference. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) lies about 3.5 miles to the east-southeast across the river in Cahokia, Illinois, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 13 miles to the northwest. The whole area sits beneath the Lambert Class B airspace. River haze is common on summer mornings; the red brick stands out best in clear, low-angle light.
