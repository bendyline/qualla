# East St. Louis, Illinois

Across the Mississippi from the Gateway Arch, East St. Louis holds Mississippian mounds, a workers' commune, a devastating 1917 race riot and a long, unfinished fight to recover from industrial collapse.

**Location:** 38.6250, -90.1575

**Categories:** Cities in St. Clair County, Illinois, Illinois populated places on the Mississippi River, Mississippian culture, Labor history, Race riots in the United States, Urban decay in the United States

## Introduction

For nearly three decades, East St. Louis talked back to the Gateway Arch. From 1995, the Gateway Geyser on the city's riverfront, facing the Arch across the Mississippi, shot a column of water 630 feet into the air, exactly the Arch's height, in what was billed as the world's second-tallest fountain. It fell silent in 2023, closed over refurbishment costs and to prepare its park for handover to the National Park Service. The gesture suits a city that has always lived in its larger neighbor's shadow, and whose own story runs deeper, stranger and far more painful than the view from the Arch suggests.

## Mounds Beneath the Bridges

Long before any ferry crossed the river, rulers of the Mississippian culture organized thousands of workers to raise earthwork mounds on both banks. Settlers counted up to 50 here before the Civil War; most were lost to development and roadbuilding. Excavations ahead of new interstate construction in the early 1990s revealed what survived: the remains of a mound thought to have stood about 40 feet high and covered nearly a football field, surrounded by burial mounds, a large plaza and a wooden palisade. Only a handful of mounds remain visible today, and the 500-acre prehistoric site is now called the East St. Louis Mound Center. Excavations ahead of the Stan Musial Bridge, reported in 2012, uncovered something no one had mapped: a 900-year-old suburb of nearby Cahokia, with evidence of more than 1,000 dwellings and the base of a pyramidal mound, lying in present-day industrial wasteland.

## The Week the Workers Ran the Rails

Captain James Piggott, a Revolutionary War veteran, began running a ferry to St. Louis in 1797 from a village first called Illinoistown. On April 1, 1861, 183 residents voted to rename it East St. Louis. Coal, rail, meatpacking and the National Stock Yards made it an industrial city. After the wage cuts that followed the Panic of 1873, in late July 1877 representatives from almost every railroad line met here to form what became known as the St. Louis Commune. Their executive committee halted all rail traffic except passenger and mail trains, and Mayor John Bowman served as its arbitrator. At one packing plant, strikers allowed 125 cattle to be processed in exchange for 500 cans of beef. The workers held the railroads and the city for about a week without the violence seen in Chicago, and when federal troops took the Relay Depot on July 28, the strike ended peacefully. Nineteen years later, on May 27, 1896, a 20-minute tornado killed 118 people in East St. Louis and 137 across the river.

## The Summer of 1917

As the Great Migration brought Black families north from the Jim Crow South, the city's Black population nearly doubled between 1910 and 1917. When white workers struck the Aluminum Ore Company in April 1917, the company hired Black workers as strikebreakers. At a labor meeting in City Hall on May 28, amid rumors about Black men and white women, a speaker declared that "East St. Louis must remain a white man's town," and three thousand white men left to attack Black men on the streets. On July 1, whites drove past Black homes near 17th and Market and fired into several. When police arrived at a gathering of Black residents at 10th and Bond, their car was mistaken for the attackers', and shots from the crowd killed two detectives. The next morning thousands of white rioters swept through Black neighborhoods, beating, shooting and killing men, women and children. They burned whole sections of the city, shot people as they fled the flames and hanged several victims. Among the dead were 14-year-old Lurizza Beard and Ed Cook; Lena Cook, Lurizza's mother and Ed's wife, survived the attack and later testified against three white men. Officially 39 Black and 9 white people died; other estimates put the Black dead at 100 or more, some as high as 200. W. E. B. Du Bois came to investigate, and Congress held its own inquiry.

## Pittsburgh of the West, Unraveled

When East St. Louis celebrated its centennial in 1961, it was known as the "Pittsburgh of the West." What followed was a long hollowing-out. Middle-class residents left, and with them went the tax base. Sewers failed, garbage pickup ceased, police cars and radios stopped working, and the fire department went on strike in the 1970s. Structure fires destroyed so many consecutive blocks that scenes for the film Escape from New York were shot here. Carl Officer, elected in 1979 at 26, was one of the youngest mayors in the country at the time. In 1990 Illinois lent the city $34 million under its Financially Distressed City Law, and the Casino Queen riverboat brought East St. Louis its first new source of income in nearly 30 years. Nearby plants had incorporated as separate, nearly uninhabited jurisdictions such as Sauget, outside the city's tax base, while city residents lived with their contaminated air.

## Corn at the Edge of the Blocks

Today's population is less than a quarter of what the 1950 census counted. Where vacant buildings came down, whole blocks have grown over into "urban prairie," and old inner-city neighborhoods run up against corn and soybean fields. In 2010 a community gardening movement began developing plots for urban farming, with nonprofits and universities joining to spark green development. The city opened a new library and built a new city hall in 2001, and five MetroLink stations tie the city to St. Louis by light rail. Even the weather has a legend: on July 14, 1954, the temperature allegedly reached 117 °F, which would be the highest ever recorded east of the Mississippi, though with no weather station in the city, it was never a formal record.

## Flight Context

East St. Louis sits at 38.625 N, 90.158 W on the Illinois bank of the Mississippi River, directly opposite downtown St. Louis and the Gateway Arch. From 2,500-3,500 feet MSL the Arch, the Poplar Street and Stan Musial bridges, the riverfront park where the Gateway Geyser stood, and the rail yards and interstates of the American Bottom are easy to pick out; the Cahokia mound complex lies about 5 miles away. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) in Cahokia Heights is about 3 nm south, St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 12 nm northwest, and MidAmerica St. Louis Airport/Scott AFB (KBLV) is about 16 nm east. The area sits under busy St. Louis Class B airspace, so coordinate with approach. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common over the river valley.
