# St. Louis: Gateway to the West

The city where the Mississippi meets the Missouri, where the Gateway Arch rises 630 feet above the riverfront, and where Eero Saarinen's gleaming monument marks where western expansion began.

**Location:** 38.6270, -90.1994

**Categories:** missouri, st-louis, city, history, gateway

## Introduction

The Gateway Arch rises 630 feet above the St. Louis riverfront, the tallest monument in the United States, its stainless steel skin gleaming against Missouri sky. Eero Saarinen designed it as a monument to westward expansion - this was where Lewis and Clark departed in 1804, where wagon trains assembled, where the frontier began. St. Louis was the gateway, the last large city before the wilderness. That role ended long ago; the city shrank while its suburbs grew; the Arch now marks a downtown that struggles for relevance. But the monument remains: a perfect catenary curve reflecting the river where the Missouri joins the Mississippi, commemorating a moment when St. Louis was where America looked west and imagined what lay beyond.

## Where the Rivers Meet

The Missouri River joins the Mississippi ten miles north of downtown St. Louis - the confluence of the two mightiest rivers in North America. The combined waterway was America's western highway, the route Lewis and Clark followed when they departed St. Louis in May 1804 on their expedition to the Pacific. Fur traders, missionaries, and settlers followed. St. Louis became the outfitting point for western expeditions, the last place to buy supplies before the wilderness began. By 1850, it was America's second-largest city. The Civil War and railroad expansion shifted growth elsewhere, but the geographic fact remains: St. Louis sits where the rivers come together, the natural gateway between East and West.

## The Arch

Finnish-American architect Eero Saarinen won the Gateway Arch design competition in 1948, though construction didn't begin until 1963. The arch opened in 1965 - 630 feet tall, 630 feet wide at the base, forming a perfect weighted catenary curve. The structure contains no internal framework; it's essentially a hollow triangle that rises to a point. Tram cars carry visitors to the observation deck at the apex, where windows face both east and west - the symbolic view that gives the monument its meaning. The Arch is the centerpiece of the Jefferson National Expansion Memorial, now Gateway Arch National Park. The Museum of Westward Expansion lies underground beneath the monument. At night, lights illuminate the stainless steel skin, visible for miles across the Mississippi floodplain.

## The Shrinking City

St. Louis's population peaked at 856,000 in 1950; today, the city proper contains fewer than 300,000 residents. Unlike most American cities, St. Louis city is independent of St. Louis County - a political boundary frozen in 1876 that means suburban growth benefited the county while the city hollowed out. The results are visible: abandoned neighborhoods in North St. Louis, crumbling industrial districts, a downtown that empties after work. The metro area remains substantial (2.8 million), but the city itself struggled. Recent decades have brought selective revival - downtown lofts, Forest Park attractions, Cherokee Street's arts district - but the broader pattern of decline has been difficult to reverse. St. Louis is a case study in urban shrinkage, a gateway city that lost its way.

## Beer and Baseball

Anheuser-Busch, founded by German immigrants in 1852, made St. Louis synonymous with Budweiser - 'The King of Beers.' The brewery complex south of downtown still operates, though the company is now owned by Belgian-Brazilian conglomerate AB InBev. The Cardinals, St. Louis's beloved baseball team, have won 11 World Series titles, second only to the Yankees. Busch Stadium downtown fills with red-clad fans; the team's popularity extends throughout the Midwest. Stan Musial, who played for the Cardinals from 1941 to 1963, remains the city's sports saint. St. Louis also invented toasted ravioli, provel cheese (a processed blend found nowhere else), and the gooey butter cake. The regional food culture, like the regional beer, runs deep.

## Visiting the Gateway

St. Louis Lambert International Airport (STL) lies northwest of downtown, a former TWA hub now served by various carriers. MetroLink light rail connects the airport to downtown and the Arch grounds. Forest Park, site of the 1904 World's Fair, contains the free St. Louis Art Museum, the Missouri History Museum, and the St. Louis Zoo. The City Museum, in a former shoe factory, defies description - a maze of tunnels, slides, and architectural salvage that adults enjoy as much as children. The Delmar Loop offers restaurants and live music along a walkable strip. From altitude, St. Louis appears as development spreading from the river confluence, the Arch's gleaming curve marking downtown against the brown Mississippi - the Gateway to the West, where rivers meet and the frontier once began.

## Flight Context

Located at 38.63°N, 90.20°W on the Mississippi River just south of its confluence with the Missouri. From altitude, the Gateway Arch is unmistakable - a gleaming steel curve rising from the riverfront, visible for miles. The downtown cluster sits just west of the Arch. STL airport lies to the northwest. The Mississippi marks the border with Illinois; the rivers that made St. Louis strategic remain the dominant geographic features. What appears from the air as a Midwest river city with an iconic monument is the Gateway to the West - where Lewis and Clark departed, where America looked toward the Pacific, and where the Arch still commemorates the frontier.
