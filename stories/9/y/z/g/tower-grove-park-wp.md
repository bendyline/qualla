# Tower Grove Park

Henry Shaw's gift to St. Louis, Tower Grove Park is a 289-acre Victorian pleasure ground laid out by a Kew gardener, dotted with fanciful pavilions and marble composers, and alive with migrating songbirds each spring and fall.

**Location:** 38.6060, -90.2560

**Categories:** Parks in St. Louis, National Historic Landmarks in Missouri, Arboreta in Missouri, Urban public parks, Birdwatching

## Introduction

Beethoven and Wagner keep watch over the bandstand in white marble, joined on their columns by Mozart, Rossini, Gounod and Verdi. Just west of the Piper Palm House, two zinc stags lie on their pedestals, facing each other across Center Cross Drive. Tower Grove Park is full of such Victorian touches. Stretching 1.6 miles from Kingshighway Boulevard east to Grand Boulevard across 289 acres of the city's south side, it has been designated a National Historic Landmark as one of the nation's finest examples of a late 19th-century public park.

## A Botanist's Gift

The park began as part of a private estate. Henry Shaw, a 19th-century businessman and botanist, owned both this land and the neighboring ground that became the Missouri Botanical Garden. In 1868 he donated the land for the park to the City of St. Louis, and it opened to the public in 1872. Today it sits amid largely residential neighborhoods, including Southwest Garden, Shaw, Tower Grove East and Tower Grove South, a long green ribbon that gives the surrounding blocks their shared backyard. Elongated rather than square, the park is many times longer than it is wide, so a walk from one end to the other takes in its full parade of pavilions, lawns and trees.

## From Common Pasture to German Dairies

Long before Shaw, this land was part of La Petite Prairie, farmland of the area's early French settlers. Grazing land was held in common, while farmland was divided into long, narrow tracts. Around 1800 the commons system was abandoned and the land began passing into private hands. By the 1850s much of it belonged to German Catholics, newly arrived after the turmoil of 1848 in their homeland. These German dairy farmers found the ground ideal for pasture. They built comfortable homes, and toward the end of the 19th century a real community took shape as blocks were developed and prosperous German immigrants, now American citizens, built grand houses near the park.

## A Gardener from Kew

Tower Grove Park was designed in the gardenesque style by James Gurney Sr., who had been head of the aquatic plants section at Kew Gardens in London and who stayed on in St. Louis as the park's superintendent. His landscape is dotted with 32 pavilions of picturesque design, most from the Victorian era, among them the Turkish Pavilion, a Chinese pagoda and the Sons of Rest Pavilion. There is a lily pond with formal plantings, the Piper Palm House and a bandstand that still hosts music and special events. Modern needs have their place too, from tennis courts, softball diamonds and soccer fields to a wading pool for small children and paths for walkers, joggers and cyclists. Basketball courts, removed in the 1980s over maintenance costs, returned along Arsenal Street in 2023.

## Figures in Marble and Bronze

Several 19th-century statues line the park's central corridor. German sculptor Ferdinand von Miller created full likenesses of the German naturalist and explorer Alexander von Humboldt and of William Shakespeare, both from 1878, as well as the composer busts of Beethoven and Wagner in 1884. Howard Kretschmar sculpted Mozart and Rossini in 1882, and Carlo Nicoli added Gounod and Verdi in 1886. Near the Palm House stands a smaller-scale statue of Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben, the German-born volunteer of the American Revolution; the larger original, by Cuno von Uechtritz-Steinkirch, was commissioned for the 1904 World's Fair, and this version arrived in 1968. One statue is gone. Von Miller's Christopher Columbus, dedicated in 1886 near the Grand entrance and long honored by the local Italian American community, was vandalized in 2016 and 2017, reviewed by a commission formed in 2018 and removed in June 2020.

## Warblers and Market Stalls

About 7,000 trees of roughly 340 types grow here, along with shrubs and flowering plants, and the park is a well-known birdwatching spot. It lies along the Mississippi Flyway, a migration route used by roughly forty percent of North America's waterfowl and shorebirds, and during spring and fall migrating birds stop to rest among its tall specimen trees. Warblers, orioles and Canada geese are among the visitors, and the park's Gaddy Wild Bird Garden is an Audubon-recognized Important Bird Area with more than 200 recorded bird species. People gather here just as eagerly. Picnics and weddings fill the calendar, and large festivals include ones celebrating international cultures and paganism, as well as Tower Grove Pride in late September. Since 2006 a Saturday morning farmers' market has set up in the park, running from April into the fall in 2026, with a Tuesday evening market from May through September and local farmers, makers and food producers.

## Flight Context

Tower Grove Park is centered near 38.606 N, 90.256 W on the south side of St. Louis. From the air it is unmistakable: a long, narrow rectangle of green stretching 1.6 miles east-west between Kingshighway and Grand Boulevard, with the Missouri Botanical Garden immediately to the north. The Gateway Arch lies about 4 miles to the east-northeast. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 12 miles to the north-northwest, and St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) about 6 miles to the east-southeast across the Mississippi. A viewing altitude of 2,000-3,000 feet AGL shows the whole length of the park. The area is within busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. Autumn foliage adds color, while summer haze can soften visibility.
