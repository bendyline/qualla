# Apotheosis of St. Louis

Before the Gateway Arch, the symbol of St. Louis was a bronze King Louis IX on horseback in Forest Park, a World's Fair statue born of a sculptor's lawsuit, borrowed by soldiers and ballclubs, missing its sword more than once, and debated anew in 2020.

**Location:** 38.6398, -90.2941

## Introduction

For nearly six decades, if you wanted a picture that said "St. Louis," you did not reach for a steel arch. You reached for a king on horseback. The Apotheosis of St. Louis, a bronze statue of King Louis IX of France, the city's namesake, stands in Forest Park in front of the Saint Louis Art Museum. From its unveiling in 1906 until the Gateway Arch rose in the mid-1960s, it was the principal symbol of the city. Its story includes a World's Fair, a sculptor who sued over his own design, a sword that kept disappearing, and a 2020 standoff over whether a medieval king still deserves a place of honor.

## Born in Plaster

The statue began as a temporary work for the 1904 World's Fair. A plaster version by the sculptor Charles Henry Niehaus stood on the concourse of the Plaza of St. Louis, near the fair's main entrance, on the spot where the Missouri History Museum stands today. When the fair ended, the Louisiana Purchase Exposition Company decided to give Forest Park a permanent bronze replica. The inscription on the west side of the pedestal still records the gift: "Presented to the City of St. Louis by the Louisiana Purchase Exposition in commemoration of the Universal Exposition of 1904 held on this site." The north and south faces carry just two words, "Saint Louis," as if no further explanation were needed.

## The Sculptor Sues

Casting the bronze turned into a dispute over who owned the design. Niehaus offered to create a bronze version of his plaster model for $90,000. The exposition company passed on his price and accepted a lower bid of $37,500 from a local bidder named Hodges. Niehaus responded by suing the company for infringing his intellectual property rights, and he came away with $3,000 and a lasting form of credit: the words "designed by C. H. Niehaus" inscribed on the pedestal. With the matter settled, the finished statue was unveiled on October 4, 1906. Every visitor who reads the base today is reading, in part, the outcome of that lawsuit.

## The City's Face Before the Arch

Once it was installed, the king rode into nearly every corner of local identity. The statue in profile became the image on the arms and Distinctive Unit Insignia of the 138th Infantry Regiment of the U.S. Army and Missouri National Guard, a unit known as "The First Missouri" and "St. Louis' Own." In the 1930s and 1940s it appeared in the logo of the St. Louis Browns baseball club. The image proved durable: in 2008 St. Louis Soccer United revived it for its logo, and for the St. Louis Athletica the rider was reimagined as Joan of Arc. The statue has also attracted less official attention. Over the years, vandals have stolen Saint Louis' sword several times, and a local urban legend holds that university students used to dare one another to take it.

## A King Reconsidered

In 2020, during a period of national racial unrest, a coalition of activists called for the statue to be removed. They accused Louis IX of antisemitism, citing his burning of Jewish texts and the expulsion of French Jews from their homes, as well as his role in the Seventh and Eighth Crusades. Local Catholics gathered in counter-protest, praying the rosary in front of the statue. About 200 people gathered at the base of the statue on June 27, and the two sides faced each other, separated by several police officers. The Archdiocese of St. Louis, led by Archbishop Robert James Carlson, defended the statue in an unsigned statement, saying "We should not seek to erase history" and pointing to Louis IX's reforms and charity toward the poor. Historian Thomas Madden of Saint Louis University weighed the king's record and argued that the statue should stay. The bronze king still stands on the hill.

## Flight Context

The Apotheosis of St. Louis stands at 38.640N, 90.294W in Forest Park, directly in front of the Saint Louis Art Museum. From the air, the broad green expanse of Forest Park is easy to identify in the western part of the city; the museum at the top of Art Hill makes a good aiming point, though the statue itself is best picked out below 1,500 feet AGL. The Gateway Arch is about 6 miles to the east. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 9 miles to the northwest, and the park lies beneath its Class B airspace; St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is about 9 miles to the east-southeast across the Mississippi River. Clear days with a high sun give the best view of the statue against the lawn of Art Hill.
