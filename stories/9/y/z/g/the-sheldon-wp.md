# The Sheldon

Built in 1912 as a meeting house for the Ethical Society, this St. Louis hall passed through a church, a chamber-music lover and a near date with the wrecking ball before becoming a concert venue its admirers nickname "The Carnegie Hall of St. Louis."

**Location:** 38.6397, -90.2333

## Introduction

In recent summers, the art galleries inside a 1912 St. Louis concert hall have filled up with a fully playable nine-hole miniature golf course, with each hole designed by a different local artist or architect. "Golf the Galleries" is a playful tradition for a building that began life as the home of the Ethical Society of St. Louis, and that in the mid-1980s came close to being torn down for a parking lot. Today The Sheldon hosts hundreds of events a year and carries a nickname its admirers repeat with pride: "The Carnegie Hall of St. Louis."

## A Stage Built for Ideas

The hall began as a home for conversation rather than concerts. It was built in 1912 for the Ethical Society of St. Louis and named for Walter Sheldon, a founding member of the Society in the city. Its architect, Louis C. Spiering, was already noted for his work on the 1904 World's Fair. Margaret Mead spoke from its stage over the years, and so did Thurgood Marshall and Martha Gellhorn. The building's Green Room has its own claim on civic history, because the St. Louis Chapter of the League of Women Voters was founded there. Yet the feature that would ultimately define the place was its sound. Musicians and public speakers alike have long enjoyed the acoustics of the concert hall, and after the Ethical Society moved on, it was music that took over the room.

## Gospel Nights and a Near Miss

In 1964 the Ethical Society relocated to St. Louis County, and The Sheldon became primarily a music venue. In the 1970s a former singer with the Duke Ellington Orchestra bought the building and turned it into a church, and the hall became the setting for jazz and gospel concerts. The mid-1980s were precarious. The building was at risk of demolition to make way for a parking lot, and a group of community leaders and Ethical Society members launched a "Save Our Sheldon" campaign. Help also came from an unexpected direction. At the urging of the Paganini String Quartet, a California attorney with a love of chamber music purchased The Sheldon in 1984, and in 1986 he began operating it as a venue for concerts and community events. Public sentiment, along with growing recognition of The Sheldon as a cultural landmark, turned the tide. The building was saved.

## St. Louis's Own Carnegie Hall

To secure the hall for the long term, Walter F. Gunn formed the nonprofit Sheldon Arts Foundation in the late 1980s, and the Foundation bought the building in 1991. It is now governed by a board of more than 40 directors, and its stated mission is to preserve and operate the historic concert hall and galleries as an independent cultural institution. The calendar is crowded with jazz, folk and classical music. Dave Brubeck, Herbie Hancock and Wynton Marsalis have played here, as have Doc Watson, Joan Baez, Willie Nelson and B.B. King, along with José Carreras, Renée Fleming, Diana Krall, Judy Collins and Lyle Lovett. Local talent gets its due as well. The "Notes From Home" series showcases St. Louis musicians on weekday evenings, while Matinee Concerts, Coffee Concerts and school programs bring in audiences of every age.

## Glass, Galleries and a Garden Wall

Beginning in 1998 The Sheldon widened its mission with a $5 million expansion that added five art galleries, full wheelchair accessibility, new restrooms, two new lobbies, a sculpture garden and more parking. The 500-seat Louis Spiering Room, named for the original architect and now called The Konneker Room, followed in 1999. The galleries show exhibitions on jazz and music history, photography, architecture, St. Louis artists and children's art, changing with the seasons. In 2001 the artist Rodney Winfield installed five new stained glass windows, a series called "Theme and Variation" designed to be enjoyed both by day and by night, as a tribute to Father Maurice McNamee, S.J. The most recent addition arrived in 2019, when the Steward Family Plaza opened on the building's west side with a walkway and a vertical garden. More than a century after Spiering finished it, the hall still stands in the heart of St. Louis's Grand Center arts district.

## Flight Context

The Sheldon stands at 38.6397°N, 90.2333°W in the Grand Center arts district of Midtown St. Louis. The Gateway Arch on the Mississippi riverfront, roughly 2.8 miles to the east, is the obvious reference point, and the downtown towers between the Arch and Midtown help frame the approach. The concert hall itself is a modest building that is hard to single out from altitude, so plan a city overflight at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL and let the Arch and the river do the navigating. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) lies about 10 miles to the northwest and its Class B airspace blankets the city, so coordinate with approach control before overflying downtown. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is across the river in Illinois to the southeast. Humid summer air along the river often brings haze.
