# St. Stanislaus Kostka Church (St. Louis, Missouri)

Built by Polish immigrants north of downtown St. Louis, St. Stanislaus Kostka survived a dome-destroying fire and a vanishing neighborhood, then fought its own archdiocese over who owns a church, and won.

**Location:** 38.6423, -90.2057

**Categories:** Churches in St. Louis, Polish-American culture in Missouri, Independent Catholic denominations, Churches on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Landmarks of St. Louis

## Introduction

On Christmas Eve 2005, at least 1,500 people crowded into St. Stanislaus Kostka Church for Midnight Mass. Earlier that month, the Archbishop of St. Louis had declared the parish's lay board and its priest excommunicated, and the archdiocese soon moved to suppress the parish. The packed pews were the congregation's answer. The standoff that followed, over who controls a parish's property and money, drew wide publicity, and it ended with this Polish church north of downtown St. Louis standing on its own, run by its parishioners as an independent Catholic church.

## A Parish for Polish St. Louis

Until the late 1870s, the city's growing Polish Catholic community worshiped at St. Patrick's, at Sixth and Biddle Streets. In 1878 the Archdiocese of St. Louis authorized a parish of their own, and in 1880 construction began on St. Stanislaus Kostka, just north of downtown in a neighborhood home to many Polish immigrants. The first church was built in the Romanesque Revival style and administered by the Franciscans. It soon became a mother church, giving rise to three more Polish parishes: St. Casimir in 1889, St. Hedwig in 1904 and Our Lady of Czestochowa in 1907. In 1891 a land deed signed by Archbishop Peter Kenrick placed the parish property in a corporation run by a pastor and lay board members to be appointed by the archbishop, founding documents known as the Deed and Charter & Bylaws of 1891.

## A Cathedral-Style Church and a Lost Dome

A new and larger church, designed by the firm of Wessbecher and Hummel, was dedicated in September 1892. It has been called one of the best examples west of the Mississippi River of the opulent style known as Polish Cathedral architecture. In 1928 a fire destroyed the building's center dome and almost all of its original interior decoration, and the dome was never rebuilt. The parish carried on. In 1969 it welcomed a visitor from Poland, Cardinal Karol Wojtyla, Archbishop of Krakow, who would later become Pope John Paul II. The church was named a City Landmark in 1976 and added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1979. It stands on the edge of the former site of the Pruitt-Igoe housing complex.

## Keeping Faith as the Neighborhood Emptied

As the Polish community dispersed from the surrounding streets, St. Stanislaus kept serving it. By the 1950s the church had lost most of its local parishioners, yet it continued to receive support from the wider Polish community. By the 1970s the parish buildings were badly deteriorating, and the parishioners responded with a large-scale restoration of the church and began acquiring land around it. They later built a Polish Heritage Center on the grounds. By 2005 the parish held 8 acres, and its board of trustees estimated the total value of its assets at $9.5 million, all sustained, parish members noted, without financial help from the archdiocese.

## Who Owns a Church?

The dispute centered on an old question. Would the parish's assets be controlled by a pastor appointed by the archbishop, the current canonical norm in the United States, or by a lay board, a practice known as trusteeism? The tension stretched back to the era of Cardinal John J. Glennon and ran through several successors before coming to a head in December 2005, when Archbishop Raymond Leo Burke excommunicated the board members and their priest, Marek Bozek. The board argued that the archdiocese wanted control of the parish's assets and intended to close it. Under Bozek the congregation grew by roughly 500 families even as about 200 left. As the conflict shifted from property toward doctrine, some traditional members were alienated while new worshipers arrived. In 2010 the archdiocese offered a settlement that would have left the property with the congregation but restored church authority over the parish; members rejected it, 257 to 185.

## Its Own Property

On March 15, 2012, a St. Louis circuit judge ruled that the church's property and assets belonged to the St. Stanislaus Parish Corporation, not the archdiocese. "The archbishop may own the souls of wayward St. Stanislaus parishioners," Judge Hettenbach wrote, "but the St. Stanislaus Parish Corporation owns its own property." The two sides settled their legal dispute in 2013. The parish later explored affiliating with the Episcopal Church and the Polish National Catholic Church, and in 2019, citing property rights as a key factor, it decided not to join the Episcopal Diocese of Missouri, even though it had been chosen to host that diocese's consecration of a new bishop, a ceremony that was postponed and finally held in June 2020 at Christ Church Cathedral instead. Bozek returned to Poland in June 2022. Today the parishioners maintain and manage St. Stanislaus themselves as a not-for-profit corporation.

## Flight Context

St. Stanislaus Kostka Church stands at approximately 38.6423 N, 90.2057 W on the near north side of St. Louis, just north of downtown and beside the former Pruitt-Igoe site. From the air, look for the church and its parish grounds, including the Polish Heritage Center, just north of the downtown skyline. The Gateway Arch lies about 1.7 miles to the southeast. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 11.5 miles to the northwest, and St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) about 5.5 miles to the south-southeast across the Mississippi. A viewing altitude of 2,000-2,500 feet AGL works well; this is busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. Summer haze is common over the river.
