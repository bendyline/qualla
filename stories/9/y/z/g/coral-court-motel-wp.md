# Coral Court Motel

A glass-block Streamline Moderne motor court on Route 66 outside St. Louis, famous for private garages and the discretion they offered, faded after the interstate bypassed it and was demolished in 1995, leaving one rescued cabin in a museum.

## Introduction

At the Coral Court Motel, your car never had to sit in the parking lot. Each unit came with its own enclosed garage tucked into the center of the building, so a guest's automobile disappeared from the street the moment it pulled in. On U.S. Route 66 in Marlborough, Missouri, just outside St. Louis, that privacy was part of the design, wrapped in walls of glass brick and ceramic tile. It was also, in time, the source of the motel's reputation. For half a century the Coral Court was a Streamline Moderne jewel of the old highway, and then, despite a determined fight to save it, it was gone.

## Glass Block on Route 66

John Carr opened the Coral Court in 1942, in the middle of the nation's World War II mobilization. The original motor court had twenty rooms: a main office plus ten separate buildings with two units each, every one built in the Streamline Moderne style with a minimalist face of glass brick and ceramic tile and a garage at the heart of each building. After the war the motel kept growing. In 1946 Carr added 23 more two-unit cabins, bringing the total to 66 rooms. Three two-story buildings went up near the back of the property in 1953, raising the count to 77, and a swimming pool followed in the 1960s. It was the heyday of automobile tourism, and the Coral Court was built for it.

## Behind the Garage Doors

The same features that made the Coral Court elegant also made it discreet. Rooms could be rented for short periods with complete discretion, and the enclosed garages kept guests' cars out of sight from the street. The motel became known as a popular place for adultery. A darker episode brought it notoriety of another kind. On October 6, 1953, Carl Austin Hall briefly checked in after fleeing Kansas City in the aftermath of the abduction and murder of Bobby Greenlease. Hall was arrested that year, and he and an accomplice were sentenced to death. Only $288,000 of the $600,000 ransom was ever recovered. Whatever glamour the motel carried, its name was now also tied to the Greenlease case and to the loss suffered by a grieving family.

## Bypassed

Until the end of 1972, Route 66 was the main road through the area, and business was brisk. Then Interstate 44 bypassed the old highway in Missouri. The loss of traffic to freeway construction led to a broad decline among independent Route 66 businesses, though the Coral Court appears to have been kept in good condition until John Carr died in 1984. His widow, who later remarried, took ownership and ran the motel for several more years, but the buildings declined badly. By 1988 the site was at risk, as she had twice sold options on the land to developers planning a shopping mall. Local preservationists formed the Coral Court Preservation Society and succeeded in listing the motel on the National Register of Historic Places in 1989, recognized as a valuable example of Art Deco and Streamline Moderne design.

## One Cabin Saved

The listing could not save it. Unless public money has been invested in a historic property, National Register status offers no protection, and the Coral Court, in very poor condition after years of neglect, closed in 1993. It was demolished in 1995, and its site now holds a 45-unit housing development called Oak Knoll Manor. Not everything was lost. Volunteers carefully dismantled one of the original two-unit buildings, and in 2000 its facade became part of an automotive exhibit at the National Museum of Transportation in St. Louis, with a 1941 Cadillac parked in front of its garage. Nothing else remains at the old site, though local advocates have pushed for a historic marker to show where the motor court once stood.

## Flight Context

The Coral Court Motel stood at approximately 38.572N, 90.334W in Marlborough, a small suburb in St. Louis County, Missouri, along the old U.S. Route 66 corridor. Nothing of the motel remains; its grounds are now the Oak Knoll Manor housing development. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look for the path of old Route 66 running southwest through the suburbs, with Interstate 44, the highway that bypassed it, nearby. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 12 miles to the north, and the area lies beneath its Class B airspace. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is roughly 10 miles to the east across the Mississippi River, and Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) is about 18 miles to the west-northwest. Haze is common on summer afternoons.
