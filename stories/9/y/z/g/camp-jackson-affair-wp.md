# Camp Jackson affair

In May 1861, a Union capture of secessionist militia at Lindell's Grove in St. Louis turned deadly on the city streets and pushed a divided Missouri out of neutrality.

**Location:** 38.6366, -90.2367

**Categories:** Missouri in the American Civil War, 1861 riots, Secession crisis of 1860–61, History of St. Louis, German-American history

## Introduction

On May 9, 1861, the steamer J. C. Swon delivered a cargo to the St. Louis riverfront in crates marked as Tamaroa marble. Inside were two 12-pound howitzers, two 32-pound siege guns, 500 muskets and ammunition, sent by Confederate President Jefferson Davis from the captured federal arsenal at Baton Rouge. Militia officers hauled them several miles inland to a camp in Lindell's Grove, today part of the Saint Louis University campus. The next day, Union troops surrounded that camp, and the march that followed ended in gunfire, dead civilians and a state forced to choose sides.

## A Slave State Trying Not to Choose

Missouri was a slave state, and many of its leaders sympathized with the South, yet only a minority of Missourians initially favored secession. In March 1861, a state convention voted 98 to 1 against leaving the Union, while also voting to supply neither weapons nor men to either side. Neutrality was already fraying. Secessionists had organized as "Minutemen," often with help from state officials, and in February General Daniel M. Frost enrolled five companies of them into the state militia, while a new law forced pro-Union activists to organize in secret. Then, days after Fort Sumter, a pro-Confederate mob seized the Liberty Arsenal in Liberty, Missouri, and carried off about 1,000 rifles and muskets. All eyes turned to St. Louis, whose arsenal held somewhere between 30,000 and 40,000 rifles and muskets, depending on the account.

## Rifles Across the River

The federal commander in the West, William S. Harney, an elderly career officer, wanted to avoid open conflict. On April 23 the War Department replaced him with Captain Nathaniel Lyon, a passionate Unionist who immediately began enlisting and arming St. Louis Unionists. Many were "Forty-Eighters" and members of the Republican marching society called the Wide Awakes, and by one estimate 80 percent of the early volunteers were German-American immigrants, men whom pro-slavery native-born Missourians targeted with open prejudice. In the last days of April, nearly 21,000 rifles were loaded onto the steamer City of Alton and carried across the Mississippi to Illinois. Governor Claiborne Fox Jackson, elected on a pro-Union ticket but privately a secessionist, had other plans. He asked Davis for artillery to breach the arsenal's thick walls and called out militia under Frost for "maneuvers" at Camp Jackson, a few miles northwest of the arsenal.

## The March Through the Crowd

Lyon suspected the camp was plotting against the arsenal and, by one account, confirmed it by visiting in disguise as an older woman. On May 10 he marched on Camp Jackson with some 6,000 to 6,500 volunteers and regulars and forced the militia to surrender, taking 669 prisoners. He then marched them under guard toward the arsenal to be paroled, a procession that pro-secession residents saw as a humiliation. Crowds gathered, threw rocks and debris, and singled out the German units with shouts of "Damn the Dutch!" Exactly what triggered the shooting remains unclear. The most common explanation is that a drunken man stumbled into the column and fired a pistol, fatally wounding Captain Constantin Blandowski of the 3rd Missouri Volunteer Infantry. The soldiers fired over the crowd, then into it. Some 28 people were killed, among them women and children, and dozens more were wounded. Frost later reported that "several innocent lookers-on--men, women, and children" had died along with his men.

## Days of Fear in the City

The violence did not end that evening. On May 11, volunteers were fired on from windows at 5th and Walnut streets and returned fire into the crowd. Colonel Henry Boernstein, who commanded the 2nd Missouri Volunteer Infantry and published the German-language Anzeiger des Westens, recalled giving several of his men leave to visit their families that morning. Most, he wrote, came back only after dark, "with clothing torn, faces beaten bloody," and "two of them never returned and they were never heard of again." Fear ran in both directions. Rumors spread that the Germans planned to murder the city's American-born population, and many wealthy St. Louisans fled to Illinois or the Missouri interior. Only after martial law was imposed, and federal regulars arrived to relieve the German volunteers, did the rioting stop.

## This Means War

The day after the shooting, the legislature passed Governor Jackson's stalled Military Bill, creating the Missouri State Guard under Sterling Price; Unionists called it "a secession act in all but name." Unionists saw the truce Price then signed with the reinstated Harney as a capitulation, and on May 30 Harney was replaced for good. Lyon, now a brigadier general commanding all Union forces in Missouri, met Jackson and Price at the Planter's House hotel on June 11. After four hours of argument he ended the meeting with a vow that he would see "every man, woman, and child in the State, dead and buried" before conceding. "This means war," he declared. Within days his troops occupied Jefferson City and routed the State Guard at Boonville, leaving most of Missouri under federal control. The cost was a state torn open. Many who had hoped for compromise, Price among them, became full Confederate supporters, and Missourians who had wanted neutrality were forced to take sides.

## Flight Context

The Camp Jackson site, Lindell's Grove, lies at 38.6366°N, 90.2367°W along Lindell Boulevard in midtown St. Louis, on what is now the Saint Louis University campus, about 3.5 miles north-northwest of the old St. Louis Arsenal site near the Mississippi. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, follow the downtown grid west from the riverfront toward the university campus; the Mississippi River and the Illinois shore frame the view to the east. This is busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so plan the flight accordingly. Nearby airports: St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS), about 5.5 nm southeast across the river in Cahokia, Illinois; St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL), about 9 nm northwest; and Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS), about 19 nm west. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common over the river valley.
