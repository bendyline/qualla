# Old Courthouse (St. Louis)

Beneath an iron dome modeled on St. Peter's Basilica, St. Louis's Old Courthouse saw enslaved people sold near its East Door and heard Dred and Harriet Scott's suit for freedom, before becoming part of Gateway Arch National Park.

**Location:** 38.6258, -90.1893

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in St. Louis, County courthouses in Missouri, Federal architecture in Missouri, Museums in St. Louis, History museums in Missouri, Landmarks of St. Louis, Historic district contributing properties in Missouri, Tourist attractions in St. Louis, National Register of Historic Places in St. Louis, Downtown St. Louis

## Introduction

Two kinds of justice were dispensed under the same roof. Near the East Door of the Old St. Louis County Courthouse, the probate courts ordered enslaved people sold at auction when their owners died without a will or went bankrupt. In the west wing, in 1850, a jury heard the evidence in Dred and Harriet Scott's suit for freedom and decided that Scott and his family should be free. Both things happened in a building that still stands at the heart of downtown St. Louis, a combination federal and state courthouse that, from 1864 to 1894, was Missouri's tallest habitable building. Today it belongs to Gateway Arch National Park, and the National Park Service uses it for historical exhibits and events.

## Land Given Forever

The story begins with a gift and a condition. In 1816 Judge John Baptiste Charles Lucas and Auguste Chouteau, the founder of St. Louis, donated land for a courthouse, requiring that it be "used forever as the site on which the courthouse of the County of St. Louis should be erected." The Federal style courthouse built there was completed in 1828. It was designed by Laveille & Morton, among the first architecture firms west of the Mississippi River above New Orleans, which also designed the early buildings at Jefferson Barracks and the Old Cathedral. Joseph C. Laveille left a mark far beyond this one block. As street commissioner from 1823 to 1826, he devised the city's street name grid, giving the north-south streets ordinal numbers and naming the east-west streets for trees.

## A Dome Borrowed From Rome

Missouri became a state in 1821, and between the 1830 and 1840 censuses the population of St. Louis more than tripled, from about 5,000 to about 16,500. A new courthouse was soon needed. In 1839 ground was broken on a Greek Revival design by Henry Singleton, with four wings, including an east wing that took in the original courthouse, and a three-story cupola at the center. Robert S. Mitchell began a redesign in 1851 that tore down and replaced the east wing, and from 1855 to 1858 the west wing was remodeled. Then, in 1861, William Rumbold replaced the cupola with an Italian Renaissance dome of wrought and cast iron with a copper exterior, modeled on St. Peter's Basilica in Vatican City. The United States Capitol dome, going up at the same time during the Civil War, was modeled on the same basilica. Completed in 1864, the St. Louis dome was decorated with murals by Carl Wimar, whose four lunettes depict events in the city's history. Ettore Miragoli painted over them in 1880, but they were restored in 1888.

## Sold at the East Door, Freed in the West Wing

Slave auctions were held in the courthouse's probate courts near the East Door. When an owner died without a will or declared bankruptcy, the court ordered the enslaved people counted among the estate to be sold, a practice common in every Missouri courthouse. Each sale decided a person's future by court order. The last such auction here took place in 1861. In this same building, on April 6, 1846, Dred Scott and his wife Harriet filed suit against Irene Emerson for their freedom. Their case first came to trial in 1847 in the west wing's first-floor courtroom, and they lost because of hearsay evidence. They were granted a second trial, and in 1850 a jury decided that Dred Scott and his family should be free. That victory did not hold. In 1852 the Missouri Supreme Court reversed the decision, returning the Scotts to slavery, and in 1857 the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that no African Americans were entitled to citizenship and that the Missouri Compromise was void, a judgment the National Park Service says hastened the Civil War.

## From Courtroom to National Park

Other chapters of American law passed through these halls. Louis Brandeis was admitted to the bar here in 1878. When the city and St. Louis County split in 1877, the courthouse became city property, and in 1930 the city abandoned it after building the Civil Courts Building. The old promise of "forever" came back into play: descendants of Chouteau and Lucas sued to regain ownership. In the end, the courthouse's future was tied to a much larger plan. In 1935, during the Great Depression, St. Louis voted for a bond issue to raze nearly 40 blocks around it for what was then called the Jefferson National Expansion Memorial, now Gateway Arch National Park, and an executive order from President Franklin Roosevelt declared the area a national monument. The courthouse formally joined the monument in 1940. Its roof was replaced in 1941, restoration work followed in 1955, 1979 and 1985, and a renovation ran from 2020 to 2025. Inside, the National Park Service maintains four history galleries on St. Louis. The building once held up to 12 courtrooms, and two survive in period presentation: Circuit Court #13 in the east wing, restored to its 1910 appearance, and Circuit Court #4 in the west wing, restored to approximate 1850s detail.

## Flight Context

The Old Courthouse stands at approximately 38.6258 N, 90.1893 W in downtown St. Louis, just a few blocks west of the Gateway Arch, with the Mississippi River beyond. From the air, its dome stands out among the downtown towers just west of the Arch grounds, making it easy to spot once the Arch is in sight. A viewing altitude of 2,000-3,000 feet AGL gives a good view of the dome, the Arch and the riverfront together. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) lies about 4 miles to the south-southeast across the river, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 13 miles to the northwest. Downtown St. Louis lies beneath busy terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC and observe any published airspace restrictions downtown. Summer haze over the river can soften the view.
