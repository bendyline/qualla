# Mound 72

A modest ridgetop mound south of Monks Mound at Cahokia hides a solar post circle, an elite burial laid on a falcon of some 20,000 shell beads, and the graves of some 270 people, many of them sacrificed.

**Location:** 38.6506, -90.0634

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in Illinois, Middle Mississippian culture, Mounds in Illinois, Archaeoastronomy, Cahokia

## Introduction

About 20,000 marine-shell beads, laid out in the shape of a falcon, lay beneath one of the most important people ever buried at Cahokia. The bird's head sat beneath and beside the person's head; its wings and tail spread beneath the arms and legs. This was the "Beaded Burial," and it lies at the heart of Mound 72, a small ridgetop mound roughly 850 meters south of Monks Mound at Cahokia Mounds, near Collinsville, Illinois. From the outside, the mound is unremarkable. Inside, archaeologists found a solar calendar, the wealth of a continent-spanning trade network and the remains of some 270 people, many of whom did not die of natural causes.

## Posts That Tracked the Sun

Before there was a mound, there was a circle. Early in Cahokia's history this part of the site held a woodhenge: 48 upright wooden posts forming a ring 412 feet across. Archaeologists date the placement of at least one post to around 950 CE. Four posts stood at the cardinal points, the eastern and western ones marking where the sun rose and set at the equinoxes, and four more marked sunrise and sunset at the summer and winter solstices. The layout almost exactly matches Woodhenge III, another of Cahokia's timber circles, which was just 2 feet smaller in diameter, a trivial gap when single post holes could reach a meter across. Around 1000 to 1050 CE, two small platform mounds were raised around several of the posts, one of them the summer solstice sunrise marker. Over time they were expanded, merged and reshaped into the ridge seen today.

## The Falcon of Shell

Mound 72 began with the burial of several elite individuals oriented toward that summer solstice sunrise post. The bodies were laid with their feet to the northwest on a raised platform, and the central figure rested on the bed of shell beads shaped like a falcon. A small platform mound, which archaeologists call Mound 72sub1, was then built over the burial, with one ramp projecting east and another projecting west toward the summer solstice sunset post. Below the birdman lay a woman, buried face down. The falcon warrior, or "birdman," is a common motif in Mississippian culture, and it appears elsewhere at Cahokia on two small stone tablets carved with part-human, part-bird imagery; a Birdman sandstone tablet was excavated from Monks Mound in 1971. The central burial was long read as that of a male warrior chief, one of Cahokia's rulers, but a 2016 reanalysis found that the two central bodies were a man and a woman, surrounded by twelve bodies rather than the six first reported, and its authors read the burial as evidence of a nobility of both sexes. Either way, it clearly carried powerful symbolic meaning for the people who made it.

## Treasures from a Wider World

Other high-status burials surrounded the Beaded Burial. A group of seven women, nicknamed the "Exotic Cache burial," was accompanied by 1,674 marine-shell beads, a large amount of mica, copper-covered wooden scepters, 15 chunkey stones and 709 finely made arrowheads. Those arrowheads came in caches from across the Mississippian world, from Tennessee, southern Illinois and Wisconsin, and one batch from Caddoan Mississippian peoples in Oklahoma. Together they are strong evidence that Cahokia had extensive trade links across North America. Nearby, four young men were laid out on a platform with their arms interlocked, their hands and skulls missing. Some researchers have suggested that the four may represent the four cardinal directions.

## The Women in the Pit

The hardest discovery lay in a pit beside those four men. It held 53 young women, aged between 15 and 30, arranged in two layers separated by matting. Their remains show evidence that they were strangled before being placed in neat rows. Analysis suggests they did not belong to the same social class or ethnic group as others buried in the mound and may have been newcomers to the area. Their skeletons carry signs of poor health and nutritional stress, and isotope traces from maize show they ate more corn and less animal protein than the elite individuals nearby. Dating the burials is debated: radiocarbon dates on cedar litter poles from a neighboring mass grave were read as about 1030 CE, while other chronologies place the mound's burials between roughly 1050 and 1150 CE. Other pits held 24 women, another 19 probable women buried with 35,361 shell beads, and 39 men and women who appear to have been violently killed, some of them apparently trying to pull themselves out from among the bodies.

## A Hundred Years of Burials

After a few final burials cut into its southwestern edge, the builders covered everything in a blanket of earth and gave Mound 72 its distinctive ridgetop shape. In all, some 272 people had been buried here in six separate episodes over roughly a century, every one of them within the arc of the old woodhenge. Researchers theorize the mound may hold the lineage of a prominent family along with many retainers and sacrificial victims; scholars estimate that almost 62 percent of those buried here were sacrificed, based on signs of ritual execution, the manner of burial and other factors. A similar practice, the sacrifice of retainers and commoners on the death of an elite person, is attested in the historical record among the Natchez. Archaeologist Melvin L. Fowler began excavating the mound in 1967, with work continuing into the early 1970s and, in the area around the mound, again through the 1990s.

## Flight Context

Mound 72 lies at approximately 38.6506 N, 90.0634 W within the Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site near Collinsville, Illinois, about 850 meters south of Monks Mound. The low ridgetop mound is hard to pick out on its own; use Monks Mound, the site's dominant earthwork, as the reference and look south across the open grassland. The site sits in the flat Mississippi River floodplain east of St. Louis, with the Gateway Arch visible about 7 miles to the west on a clear day. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) is roughly 7.5 miles to the southwest, MidAmerica St. Louis Airport/Scott AFB (KBLV) about 14 miles to the southeast, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) about 18 miles to the west-northwest. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,500 feet AGL works well; stay alert for traffic in the St. Louis terminal area and for summer haze over the river bottoms.
