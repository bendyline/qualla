# Nicholas Jarrot Mansion

Built by masons unused to brick for a French merchant who hosted Lewis and Clark's winter camp, the slightly crooked Jarrot Mansion in Cahokia Heights is a rare Federal-style house that rode out the New Madrid earthquakes.

**Location:** 38.5700, -90.1874

## Introduction

The windows of the Nicholas Jarrot Mansion do not quite line up. Its builders were masons who were not used to working with brick, and the result is a facade that is noticeably asymmetrical, with windows set slightly askew. The house was meant to announce that its owner, a French-born immigrant, belonged in the new American order taking shape along the Mississippi. What it became instead is something more charming: a quirky, vernacular take on East Coast elegance, standing at 124 East First Street in Cahokia Heights, Illinois. Built between 1807 and 1810, it is an extremely rare example of Federal architecture in the upper Mississippi River valley, and in 2001 it became a National Historic Landmark.

## A Merchant Between Empires

Nicholas Jarrot lived from 1764 to 1820. Born in France, he reached Cahokia in 1794 and wore many hats along the way: fur trader, lawyer, county judge, businessman and developer. At the peak of his success he was estimated to own 25,000 acres of land. His community had begun as citizens of the French colony of Louisiana before passing under British rule, and then the ground shifted again. After the American Revolution, the Treaty of Paris in 1783 saw Great Britain cede the east bank of the Mississippi, including what is now southwestern Illinois, to the United States. Over the late 18th and early 19th centuries, this stretch of river country was transformed into what became known as the American Bottom. Jarrot tried to lead and manage his neighbors through that transition, a French-speaking town learning to live inside an English-speaking republic.

## A Winter Camp for Lewis

Jarrot's business ties to the new republic made him a natural contact for Americans heading west. In 1803 he was recommended to the explorer Meriwether Lewis as a pro-American merchant, and that December the two men met. Lewis needed somewhere for his men to spend the winter, and Jarrot gave permission for them to camp on one of his properties, a riverfront tract across from the mouth of the Missouri River. That winter encampment of 1803 to 1804 became Camp Dubois, the shakedown site of the Lewis and Clark Expedition. A French merchant's riverfront land, in other words, became the proving ground for a journey into the continent's interior.

## An East Coast House, Slightly Askew

When Jarrot built his home, he chose deliberately. Rather than follow the French Colonial style that French-speaking community leaders still used in places such as Sainte Genevieve, Missouri, he wanted an American-style house resembling those of successful families on the East Coast. It was a statement of business success and of his ability to fit in with the English-speaking frontiersmen arriving in the region. The unpracticed masons gave that statement an accent, so the house appears more vernacular than its owner may have wished. It was sturdily built, though, and the proof came quickly. Within a couple of years of its completion, the major New Madrid earthquakes of 1811 and 1812, centered at New Madrid, Missouri, shook the region, and the mansion survived. Beside it stands a spring house of cut limestone, where tubs of cool water once kept food from spoiling for short periods.

## Doors That Open by Season

The house has collected honors across the decades. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1974, and in 1980 a predecessor agency to the Illinois Historic Preservation Agency acquired it. The 2001 designation as a National Historic Landmark raised its standing further, and it is also a State Historic Site. Even so, it remains a largely inactive historic site. The Illinois Department of Natural Resources, which now runs the state's historic sites, keeps the mansion closed for restoration except for periodic special events, an autumn open house and a festival before Lent, and for tours by request, so on most days its crooked windows are best admired from outside. Jarrot's public life is still close at hand. Less than half a mile away stands the Cahokia Courthouse State Historic Site, where he served as judge of St. Clair County, and the two buildings together trace the arc of a man who bridged a French past and an American future.

## Flight Context

The Nicholas Jarrot Mansion stands at approximately 38.5700 N, 90.1874 W in Cahokia Heights, Illinois, on the Mississippi River floodplain known as the American Bottom, with the river a short distance to the west. It sits only about 1.7 miles west of St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS), so the airport's runways make the best visual reference; the Gateway Arch rises about 3.8 miles to the north.
