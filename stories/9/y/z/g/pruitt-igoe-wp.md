# Pruitt–Igoe

Hailed in the 1950s as a breakthrough, St. Louis's 33-tower Pruitt–Igoe housing project was imploded within two decades, and the argument over why it failed still shapes how Americans think about public housing.

**Location:** 38.6423, -90.2094

**Categories:** Public housing in St. Louis, Demolished buildings and structures in St. Louis, Urban decay in the United States, African-American history in St. Louis, Modernist architecture

## Introduction

One early resident called her 11th-floor apartment a "poor man's penthouse." Less than two decades later, on March 16, 1972, the first of Pruitt–Igoe's towers came down in an implosion, and critic Charles Jencks declared it "the day Modern architecture died." Between those two moments lies one of the most argued-over stories in American housing: 33 eleven-story buildings on St. Louis's north side, home at full occupancy to more than 10,000 people, and a failure that architects, sociologists and historians have been assigning blame for ever since.

## Clearing the Inner Ring

In the 1940s St. Louis was overcrowded and decaying. More than 85,000 families lived in 19th-century tenements, and a 1947 survey found 33,000 homes with communal toilets. Mayor Joseph Darst, elected in 1949, favored clearing slums and replacing them with high-rise, high-density public housing. "We must rebuild, open up and clean up the hearts of our cities," he said in 1951. The federal Housing Act of 1949 supplied the money. The new complex took the names of two St. Louisans: Wendell O. Pruitt, an African American fighter pilot in World War II, and William L. Igoe, a former congressman. The city originally planned Pruitt for Black residents and Igoe for white ones. A federal court ruling in 1955, in the NAACP's case Davis v. St. Louis Housing Authority argued by Frankie Muse Freeman, ended segregation in the city's public housing, and as many as 40 percent of the first tenants were white, but by the mid-1960s its residents were exclusively African American.

## Eleven Stories by Decree

Minoru Yamasaki, then early in his career, first proposed a mix of walk-ups, mid-rises and high-rises. It exceeded federal cost limits, so the Public Housing Administration imposed a uniform height of 11 floors. Density was set at 50 units per acre, higher than in downtown slums. The "river of open space" meant to flank each row of buildings lost its landscaping, and few trees were planted. What rose on 57 acres were 2,870 small apartments with undersized kitchen appliances and no balconies. To reduce congestion, "skip-stop" elevators halted only at the first, fourth, seventh and tenth floors, leaving many residents to climb the stairs, and ground-floor shops were cut to save money. The complex did not even have a public mailbox. Pruitt took its first tenants in November 1954 and Igoe in July 1955, at a cost of $36 million, 60 percent above the national average for public housing.

## Ordinary Lives in a Failing Building

Trouble came fast. By 1958 elevators were breaking down and vandalism was spreading, though a 1959 audit found most of it was done by transients rather than residents. A recession cut rent revenue, the housing authority trimmed maintenance, and fewer families applied, starting a loop in which vacancies drained the money needed to keep buildings livable. Residents bore the cost: minimum rent climbed from $20 a month in 1952 to $58 in 1968, forcing some families to pay as much as 75 percent of their income, and tenants were even charged for replacing fuses and door locks. In 1969 they joined a nine-month rent strike that ended with the housing authority's board resigning and tenant organizations gaining more influence. Through it all, most people simply lived their lives. Seventy-eight percent reported being satisfied with their apartments, and landings where neighbors kept their common areas clean often did relatively well.

## Coming Down

By 1970, only 10 of the 33 buildings were still occupied, and a report described the physical damage as "nearly unbelievable": broken windows and doors, walls stripped for wire and pipe, garbage across the grounds. Officials first hoped that removing a few towers might save the rest, since the project had already consumed $57 million. After the first implosion in March 1972, more followed that April, June and July. In August 1973 the Department of Housing and Urban Development decided to demolish everything, rejecting a neighborhood group's last-minute bid to buy and rehabilitate some buildings. The last tenant moved out in May 1974, and by 1976 the site was cleared, making Pruitt–Igoe one of the first major housing projects in the United States to be demolished. Footage of its fall appears in the film Koyaanisqatsi.

## Arguing Over the Rubble

For years the verdict blamed the architecture. Oscar Newman held up Pruitt–Igoe as a case study for his idea of defensible space, contrasting it with the neighboring low-rise Carr Village, which stayed fully occupied. In 1991, Katharine Bristol pushed back with an article titled "The Pruitt–Igoe Myth," pointing instead to underfunding, poverty and racial discrimination. The numbers support her emphasis on context: St. Louis, projected to grow to a million people by 1970, instead lost more than a quarter of its residents between 1950 and 1970 to suburbanization and white flight. Researcher Eugene Meehan called the whole approach "a set of policies programmed for failure," since federal law required housing authorities to cover their costs from rent. The Architectural Review summarizes today's consensus bluntly: the project was "doomed from the outset." Yamasaki personally regretted designing it. A public school, Gateway, opened on part of the site in 1995, but much of the ground remains vacant.

## Flight Context

The Pruitt–Igoe site lies at 38.6423°N, 90.2094°W on St. Louis's near north side, bounded by Cass Avenue, North Jefferson Avenue, Carr Street and North 20th Street, northwest of downtown. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for the large open tract of the former 57-acre site, now partly occupied by a school and newer development next to the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency's Next NGA St. Louis campus, which opened in 2025, with the downtown skyline and the Mississippi River to the southeast and east. This is busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so plan the flight accordingly. Nearby airports: St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS), about 5 nm south-southeast across the river in Cahokia, Illinois; St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL), about 10 nm northwest; and St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) at Alton, about 17 nm north-northeast. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common over the river valley.
