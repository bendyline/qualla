# Cahokia, Illinois

A French mission village on the Mississippi's east bank that grew into a fur-trading town, a Revolutionary War outpost and a territorial seat governing lands to the Canadian border, before folding into the new city of Cahokia Heights in 2021.

**Location:** 38.5619, -90.1728

**Categories:** French colonial settlements of Illinois Country, Illinois populated places on the Mississippi River, Former municipalities in Illinois, Villages in St. Clair County, Illinois, History

## Introduction

For a few years around 1800, a courthouse in this Mississippi River village was the legal center of a territory that stretched all the way to the Canadian border. Two centuries later, on May 6, 2021, the village itself disappeared from the map as a municipality, incorporated into the new city of Cahokia Heights. Between those two moments, and long before them, runs a story of Mississippian mound builders, French missionaries, fur traders, a Revolutionary War officer from Virginia and a string of territorial governors, all played out on the flat east bank of the Mississippi across from what became St. Louis.

## A Name Borrowed Twice

The name comes from one of the clans of the Illiniwek confederacy, the people who met the first French explorers in the region. European settlers later attached the same name to the vast, long-abandoned earthworks nearby in present-day Madison County, now the Cahokia Mounds UNESCO World Heritage Site. The borrowing is a little misleading. Archaeologists credit the mounds to the Mississippian culture, whose city peaked around 1100 AD and was abandoned by about 1350 to 1400, centuries before Europeans arrived. The builders are not believed to have been ancestral to the Illini, and the Cahokia people did not come together as a tribe in the Illinois area until close to the time of French contact. A link between the clan and the mounds is possible, but unknown.

## Logs Raised for the Holy Family

French missionaries arrived in the closing years of the 17th century. Accounts disagree on the exact year and on which priests led it, but a mission was founded here in the late 1690s, with Father Pinet among those credited, to convert the Cahokia and Tamaroa peoples to Christianity, and missionaries from Quebec raised a log church dedicated to the Holy Family. The congregation they began is said to be the oldest continuously active Catholic parish in the United States. The log church standing today came later: construction began around 1786, and it was formally dedicated on September 24, 1799. It is counted as the oldest church west of the Allegheny Mountains, a wooden survivor from the years when this riverbank still spoke French.

## Furs, Wheat and a Rough Main Street

Over the following century, Cahokia grew into one of the chief trading posts of the Illinois Country, though it stayed small, with at least 300 residents by 1770. Its central location made it a natural trading point for furs and goods from Native nations. Downriver, Kaskaskia, founded in 1703, became the region's leading shipping port, while Fort de Chartres, founded in 1718, served as the French military and governmental command center. In between, farming settlers known as habitants worked the bottomland, raising wheat as their main crop. Most were Canadien migrants whose families had already been in North America for some time, and relations between settlers and their Native neighbors stayed peaceful as settlement spread.

## New Flags on the East Bank

France's defeat in the French and Indian War in 1763 changed everything. The treaty ending the war handed the Illinois Country east of the Mississippi to Britain, and many French-speaking residents of Cahokia crossed to the west bank rather than live under British rule, helping populate new villages there, St. Louis among them. Cahokia itself stayed regionally important for another four decades. In 1769 the Odawa leader Pontiac was assassinated by other Native Americans in or near the town. Nine years later, during the American Revolution, the Virginian George Rogers Clark captured Kaskaskia and set up a court in Cahokia, and 105 of the village's heads of household pledged loyalty to the Continental Congress. The Treaty of Paris of 1783 made Cahokia part of the United States.

## A Courthouse for Half a Continent

After Congress passed the Northwest Ordinance in 1787, the Cahokia Courthouse, built around 1740 in the French Colonial style, was adapted as a U.S. territorial courthouse. Cahokia became the seat of St. Clair County, named by and for Arthur St. Clair, the first territorial governor. When the county was enlarged in 1801 and 1809, Governor William Henry Harrison and later acting governor Nathaniel Pope named the courthouse the legal and governmental center of an area reaching the Canadian border. By 1814, new counties and territories had trimmed that domain to St. Clair County's present size, and the seat later moved to more central Belleville after a local developer offered land for a new courthouse. Annexations in the late 1950s added more than 15,000 residents by 1960, and the 2010 census counted 15,241 people before the 2021 merger closed the village's long chapter.

## Flight Context

Cahokia lies at approximately 38.5619 N, 90.1728 W on the Illinois (east) bank of the Mississippi River, directly across from south St. Louis. St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS), a general aviation field that the village was home to, sits immediately to the east, making the area easy to spot on approach. The Gateway Arch rises on the St. Louis riverfront roughly 4 miles to the north, and the Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site lies about 9 miles to the northeast. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 17 miles northwest and Scott AFB/MidAmerica St. Louis Airport (KBLV) about 18 miles east. A viewing altitude of 2,000-3,000 feet AGL shows the river, the bottomland and the old village core well; the area sits within busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so coordinate with ATC. River haze and summer thunderstorms can reduce visibility.
