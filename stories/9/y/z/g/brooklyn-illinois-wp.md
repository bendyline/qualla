# Brooklyn, Illinois

Founded by people who crossed the Mississippi out of slavery, Brooklyn, Illinois, is the oldest majority-Black incorporated town in the United States, and its buried past is still being uncovered.

**Location:** 38.6567, -90.1653

**Categories:** Populated places established by African Americans, Underground Railroad, Villages in St. Clair County, Illinois, Illinois populated places on the Mississippi River, Archaeological sites

## Introduction

According to oral tradition, it began with a river crossing. By 1829, "Mother" Priscilla Baltimore led eleven families, some free and some fugitive, out of slavery in St. Louis and across the Mississippi into the free state of Illinois. In the American Bottoms they built a freedom village. That village became Brooklyn, popularly known as Lovejoy, three miles northeast of downtown St. Louis. Incorporated in 1873, it is the oldest majority-Black incorporated town in the United States, and its motto carries the whole history in six words: "Founded by Chance, Sustained by Courage."

## The Woman Who Bought Her Family Back

Priscilla Baltimore's life reads like a series of hard bargains. Born in Kentucky, she tracked her white father to Missouri and bought her mother's freedom from him. She is said to have purchased her own freedom as an adult, and she bought freedom for members of her family too. She became a Methodist preacher and a force in the religious life of the new settlement. The documentary record adds nuance to the oral history: archaeologist Miranda Yancey-Bailey found written evidence that Baltimore still lived in St. Louis in the 1830s, and the first record tying her to Brooklyn dates from 1839, so she may have traveled between the two for a time. In 1837, five white abolitionists platted the land, creating an unincorporated, nearly all-Black town. One of them, Thomas Osburn, lived in the area for decades, and Baltimore later built a house on his former land, living there from 1851 to 1872.

## Churches That Hid Travelers

In 1836 a missionary of the newly formed African Methodist Episcopal Church, William Paul Quinn, visited the settlement and helped found its AME church. Sources differ on the congregation's exact founding date, which some place as early as 1825, but it is considered the first church of the denomination west of the Appalachian Mountains and the first in Illinois; today it is Quinn Chapel AME Church. Antioch Baptist Church followed in 1838. From the town's beginnings, ministers of both churches turned their buildings into Underground Railroad stations for people escaping slavery toward Canada. Quinn and Baltimore visited enslaved people in neighboring towns to let them know the network existed, then sheltered freedom seekers in the churches before sending them on to the next station. The work was dangerous. On one missionary tour, Missouri slave-catchers seized Quinn, who talked his way free by presenting himself as a citizen of British India. He later became the fourth bishop of the AME Church. Quinn Chapel still stands, and it is part of a National Park Service registry of verified Underground Railroad locations.

## Self-Rule and Its Struggles

Brooklyn was incorporated on July 8, 1873. By 1880 it counted 371 African American and 203 white residents, and in the late 19th century many took new industrial jobs, commuting to East St. Louis and nearby areas. In 1886 the Black majority registered voters, won political control and elected John Evans as the community's first Black mayor. In 1891 Evans dedicated a new post office named Lovejoy, honoring the abolitionist Elijah P. Lovejoy, who had been assassinated in Alton, Illinois, in 1837; the high school later took the name too and, around 1900, enjoyed a strong reputation. But regional investment, and the vital railroad connection, went to East St. Louis instead, and the village soon became almost entirely Black. Autonomy did not guarantee unity. Class and color conflicts sharpened, and so did corruption: in 1915, rival gangs each claiming to be police chief fought a shootout that left three dead, and in 1933 a police chief running an extortion ring dissolved the city council at gunpoint.

## A Site Named Janey B. Goode

When the Stan Musial Veterans Memorial Bridge required realigning part of Route 3 near the village, a state archaeological survey came first. In 2002 it began turning up prehistoric artifacts in such numbers that researchers named the site "Janey B. Goode," after Chuck Berry's "Johnny B. Goode." By the end of the 2007 field season, the team had excavated nearly 7,000 prehistoric features, including hundreds of house structures, in a dense village area near Cahokia. The site was occupied by Native American peoples for about 750 years, between roughly 650 and 1400 CE, and points to a denser, more complex Indigenous community than researchers had known. The survey also turned toward Brooklyn's own story. A team led by Dr. Joseph Galloy found evidence of early African American occupation from 1830 to 1850, suggesting that the remains of Mother Baltimore's freedom village survive beneath the surface of Upper Brooklyn.

## Sustained by Courage

The village's modern hardships are real. Around 650 people live here today, and about a third meet the federal definition of poverty, down from nearly half of residents in 2000. Yet since the turn of the century, residents have rallied around their history, collecting oral histories and, in 2007, founding the Historical Society of Brooklyn, Illinois. With help from state archaeologists, the society located Priscilla Baltimore's unmarked grave at Bellefontaine Cemetery in St. Louis, and in September 2010 it installed a gravestone in her honor. The society and its partners are seeking national designation for the Janey B. Goode site, the town's historic cemetery and Quinn Chapel. Blues guitarist Albert King and saxophonist Hamiet Bluiett both count among Brooklyn's notable people, but the story still begins with the woman who, by tradition, led those first families across the river.

## Flight Context

Brooklyn sits at 38.6567°N, 90.1653°W on the Illinois side of the Mississippi River, about two miles north of East St. Louis and three miles northeast of downtown St. Louis. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for the Stan Musial Veterans Memorial Bridge crossing the river nearby and the Horseshoe Lake meander just beyond the town; the village covers less than one square mile of the flat American Bottoms. This is busy St. Louis terminal airspace, so plan the flight accordingly. Nearby airports: St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS), about 5 nm south in Cahokia; St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL), about 11 nm northwest; and St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) at Alton, about 15 nm north. River-valley haze and summer thunderstorms are common.
