# Tug of war at the 1904 Summer Olympics

Six barefoot teams pulled for Olympic medals on Francis Field in St. Louis in 1904, and the New York club that reached the final went home with nothing after failing to show up for the silver-medal match.

**Location:** 38.6478, -90.3136

**Categories:** Tug of war at the 1904 Summer Olympics, Events at the 1904 Summer Olympics, Tug of war at the Summer Olympics

## Introduction

The New York Athletic Club reached the final of the 1904 Olympic tug of war and lost it to Milwaukee. Silver was still within reach. Then the New York men did not turn up. With their team absent, the remaining medal matches were scratched, and the silver and bronze went instead to two squads from the Southwest Turnverein of St. Louis, one of which New York had beaten in the semifinals. The finalists finished fourth. It was a strange ending to a contest held on Francis Field in St. Louis, where thirty men from three countries took off their shoes, dug into the turf and pulled.

## Six Teams, One Rope

Tug of war was still new to the Olympics. It had first appeared at the 1900 Games, where the winners were a mixed Scandinavian team of three Danes and three Swedes. Four years later in St. Louis, six teams of five entered, for thirty athletes in all. The host nation supplied four of them. The Milwaukee Athletic Club sent a team, the Southwest Turnverein of St. Louis entered two, and the New York Athletic Club filled out the American field. The Pan-Hellenic Athletic Club represented Greece, and South Africa was represented by a squad known as the Boer Team. The rosters make a roll call of their own: Flanagan and Olsen and Magnusson for Milwaukee, Rodenberg and Upshaw for St. Louis, Dimitrakopoulos and Georgantas for Greece, Lombard and Visser for South Africa.

## Barefoot on the Turf

The rules were simple and punishing. Teams pulled on turf ground with no shoes, and each bout lasted up to five minutes. If one side dragged the other across a line six feet from its starting position within that time, it won outright. If not, the team that had pulled its opponents closest to the line when five minutes ran out took the match. Three local judges officiated: Clark Hetherington of the University of Missouri, and John Meyers and Myles McDonough, both of St. Louis. Six games were played in all, on August 31 and September 1, 1904. In the opening round, Milwaukee beat the Boer Team and St. Louis No. 1 beat the Greeks, while New York and St. Louis No. 2 drew byes. Both foreign teams were out after a single match.

## Milwaukee Takes the Rope

The semifinals set Americans against Americans. Milwaukee defeated St. Louis No. 1, and New York beat St. Louis No. 2. The bracket then used a repechage to settle the lower medals. Rather than a simple third-place match, the two semifinal losers pulled against each other, and the winner would earn the right to face the loser of the final for second place. In the final itself, the Milwaukee Athletic Club defeated New York to win the gold medal. Its winning five were Patrick Flanagan, Sidney Johnson, Oscar Olsen, Conrad Magnusson and Henry Seiling. Back in the repechage, St. Louis No. 1 beat St. Louis No. 2, which set up the match that was supposed to decide the silver: St. Louis No. 1 against New York.

## Medals by Default

That match never happened. The New York team withdrew after losing the final and failed to appear for the silver medal match, which went to St. Louis No. 1 by walkover. The silver went to Southwest Turnverein No. 1, with Max Braun, August Rodenberg, Charles Rose, William Seiling and Orrin Upshaw, and the bronze to Southwest Turnverein No. 2. The Americans swept the top four places, while the Greek and South African teams went unplaced. One bronze medalist stands out. Frank Kugler, a German-American on the St. Louis No. 2 team, also won a silver medal in heavyweight wrestling and bronze medals in two weightlifting events at the same Games, and he is recorded as the only athlete to medal in three different sports at a single Olympics. His teammates Oscar Friede, Charles Haberkorn, Harry Jacobs and Charles Thias shared the tug-of-war bronze with him.
