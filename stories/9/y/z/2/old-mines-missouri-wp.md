# Old Mines, Missouri

In the lead and barite hills north of Potosi, descendants of French colonial miners kept their own dialect, folk tales and way of life alive well into the 20th century.

**Location:** 38.0062, -90.7634

**Categories:** French colonial settlements of Illinois Country, French-American culture in Missouri, Unincorporated communities in Washington County, Missouri, Mining history, Languages and dialects

## Introduction

They called themselves Créoles. They were also known as "paw-paw French", a name they sometimes used for themselves. It has been described as a "fun-loving insult" for a French creole so poor "that he lived on pawpaw in the summer and possum in the winter." The people behind the joke were the descendants of French colonists who came to the hills of what is now Washington, St. Francois and Jefferson counties, Missouri, in the early 18th century, when the region belonged to the Illinois Country of New France. They came to mine lead. Through a combination of geographic and cultural isolation, their descendants in and around Old Mines kept a distinctive French culture alive well into the 20th century.

## Ore Known Before the French

The lead was here long before the French. Found first in surface deposits over a wide area, the ore was known to the region's Native peoples, and it was from them that early French explorers learned of it. In October 1700, Father Jacques Gravier noted in his journal the presence of rich lead ore some twelve or thirteen leagues from the mouth of the Meramec River. Early expeditions had little to show for their efforts, although Renaudière managed to smelt some poor-quality lead. Then, in 1720, Philippe François Renault arrived with professional miners and successfully worked large quantities of lead in what is now the Old Mines region, though the exact site of his mines is not known. He received a grant in 1723 along the Petit Merrimac, today's Big River. His mines were worked until the 1730s, closed for some years, and reopened in 1743. By 1748 there was a village of some sort here, recorded simply as village des mines.

## Thirty-One Ribbons of Land

In 1803, just before the Americans took over following the Louisiana Purchase, residents and outside mine operators joined in a single petition for land. Commandant François Vallé of Ste. Genevieve wrote it himself, and on June 4, 1803, Lieutenant Governor Carlos de Hault de Lassus granted 13,400 arpents straddling Old Mines Creek. In the rush to survey it, the concession was laid out as 31 long, narrow plots running the full width of the grant, with ownership assigned by lottery regardless of where anyone lived or dug. Most of the French simply ignored the result, staying in the village of Old Mines near the south end and at Racola near the center. Most sold their lottery lots to outside speculators within a year. Claims under the new American administration were tangled and often fraudulent, and it was 1833 before the original concession was officially recognized. Those long strips survive, superimposed on the American township grid.

## Flinty Soil and Tiff

Isolation protected Old Mines. The speculators who bought its lots had no permanent interest in the place, so the French went on digging lead when and where they wanted, as they always had. Few American farmers were drawn to the region's thin, flinty soil. Ste. Genevieve, a center of commerce, gradually became more American, but Old Mines did not. By the mid-19th century the easily mined surface lead was gone, and when lead production escalated after the Civil War through capital-intensive deep mining, it did so in new areas east of Potosi, pulling economic activity even further away. Salvation came from another mineral. By the 1870s, digging for "tiff", the local name for barite, had begun to replace lead. Like lead before it, tiff could be dug by hand from near-surface deposits. Men would dig a couple of days a week to support their families, with home gardens providing the rest.

## A Language Borrowing English

The Missouri French spoken here has been described as having a Cajun vocabulary with a Québécois pronunciation. Scholars came to document it. Carrière recorded 73 folk tales from local conteurs, or storytellers, and, like Miller before him, noted how heavily the dialect had been influenced by English, with many English words and even whole idiomatic phrases borrowed or translated into it. Both linguists saw the language fading. Miller reported that the children could not speak it and the young people would not. Carrière found that the influx of English and closer ties to the outside world had undermined the dialect's base, and that young people saw no use for French outside their homes. Linguists describe the language as having largely died out by the 1970s, and by the mid-2010s only a few elderly native speakers remained in and around Old Mines.

## Galleries and a Brick Church

Some of Old Mines' French character is still visible in its buildings. The distinctive Missouri French home had a single story and a broad roof that sloped gently down over a gallery running along the long side of the house, or all the way around for wealthier families. The style remains common for new construction in the area. The community was first served by a mission priest from Ste. Genevieve; a log church went up in 1820, replaced in 1831 by the brick St. Joachim parish church. Three years older than St. Louis' Old Cathedral and still a center of community life, St. Joachim is one of the oldest standing churches in Missouri. In the 20th century, paved state highways eroded the region's physical isolation in the 1920s, and young men left for work in St. Louis and elsewhere. Historian and musician Dennis Stroughmatt, who learned French in Old Mines, now promotes its language and folk music.

## Flight Context

Old Mines lies at approximately 38.0062° N, 90.7634° W, in the hills of Washington County north of Potosi, within a broader French-settled area that stretches roughly 15 miles either side of a line from Potosi to De Soto. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, look for the long, narrow strips of the 1803 Old Mines concession, which remain visible in aerial imagery across the regular township grid along Old Mines Creek. Washington County Airport at Potosi (FAA identifier 8WC, no ICAO code) is about 9 km to the south-southeast, Bonne Terre Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 1BT) about 19 km to the southeast, and Farmington Regional Airport (KFAM) about 40 km to the southeast. Valleys in this hilly country can hold morning fog.
