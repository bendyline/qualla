# Jefferson Barracks Military Post

Founded on a Mississippi River bluff in 1826, Jefferson Barracks trained dragoons and cavalry, held Black Hawk prisoner, nursed Civil War wounded from both sides, and processed draftees for two world wars before becoming parkland, a cemetery and a Guard base.

**Location:** 38.4947, -90.2819

**Categories:** Forts in Missouri, Military installations in Missouri, Missouri in the American Civil War, American Civil War hospitals, Mexican–American War forts, Military history

## Introduction

On March 1, 1912, Albert Berry jumped from an airplane flown by Anthony Jannus over the field at Jefferson Barracks and became the first person credited with a successful parachute jump from an airplane. By then the post on the Mississippi River, south of St. Louis at Lemay, Missouri, had already been an Army installation for 86 years. It remained an important, active post from 1826 through 1946, and it is described as the oldest operating U.S. military installation west of the Mississippi. From the Black Hawk War to World War II, America's wars passed through its gates.

## Four Men on the Riverbank

In 1826, General Edmund P. Gaines, Brigadier General Henry Atkinson, explorer William Clark and Missouri Governor John Miller spent several days searching the banks of the Mississippi for a site to replace Fort Bellefontaine. They recommended ground near Carondelet, also known as "Vide Poche", about 10 miles south of St. Louis, and Major General Jacob J. Brown, commanding general of the Army, approved it. On July 10, 1826, two days after the deed was signed, six officers and 245 enlisted men of the 1st Infantry Regiment arrived under Brevet Major Stephen Watts Kearny and began building temporary quarters they named Cantonment Miller, after the governor. In 1827 the post was formally named Jefferson Barracks in honor of Thomas Jefferson, who had died the year before. It became the Army's first "Infantry School of Practice", and even William Clark's son, Meriwether Lewis Clark Sr., joined its ranks.

## A Prisoner and the Dragoons

The post's first conflict was the Black Hawk War of 1832. Black Hawk's band, made up of Sauk, Meskwaki and Kickapoo people, included roughly 500 warriors and several hundred non-combatants when it entered Illinois that April. Troops from Jefferson Barracks were sent to push what the language of the time called "hostile Indians" back to their village in present-day Iowa. In August 1832, Black Hawk surrendered at Prairie du Chien, and a lieutenant named Jefferson Davis was among the officers who escorted him downriver to Jefferson Barracks, where he was held with Neapope, White Cloud and other leaders of the band. After eight months, in April 1833, Black Hawk and several others were taken east on President Andrew Jackson's orders. That same era saw the United States Regiment of Dragoons organized at the post. Trained to fight mounted or dismounted, the dragoons were the first permanent cavalry unit in the U.S. Army.

## Jeff Davis's Pets

During the Mexican–American War of 1846–1848, Jefferson Barracks served as a rest and supply station for most U.S. troops heading to Mexico and as the recruiting center for most of the regiments organized for the war. Many returning units were stationed here afterward because of its strategic location and healthful situation. In 1853, President Franklin Pierce named Jefferson Davis Secretary of War, and two years later the Army raised two new cavalry regiments. The 1st Cavalry, later the 4th, was organized at Jefferson Barracks in March 1855. The 2nd Cavalry, activated in Louisville with Albert Sidney Johnston as colonel and Robert E. Lee as lieutenant colonel, received its cavalry training here. The new regiments were derisively nicknamed "Jeff Davis's Pets" because their officers were considered the best in the Army, and the 2nd's roster included some of the ablest commanders of the Civil War.

## A Hospital for Both Sides

In the Civil War, Jefferson Barracks served as a recruitment depot for the North and a military hospital for soldiers of both sides. Construction of the Western Sanitary Commission's hospital began in 1862, and the finished complex could hold 3,000 patients; by the war's end, well over 18,000 soldiers had been treated there. The post kept answering each new call. Regular and volunteer regiments were formed and outfitted here for the Spanish–American War in 1898, and in 1906 it became a permanent recruiting depot. Soldiers trained here for Europe in World War I. In the 1930s, young men could spend a month each year at the Citizens Military Training Camp, and Works Progress Administration camps operated on the grounds. In World War II the post, at its peak covering 1,518 acres, became a major reception center for draftees and an important basic training site. It was decommissioned in 1946.

## Guardsmen, Graves and Museums

After the war, parts of the grounds were sold for housing, and some barracks became temporary low-cost homes before giving way to the Jefferson Townhomes in the 1960s. The old post theater is now St. Bernadette Catholic Church. The land today holds two St. Louis County parks, Jefferson Barracks National Cemetery, a Veterans Affairs health care campus and a 135-acre National Guard post that is home to Missouri Army and Air National Guard units. The county park keeps the post's memory alive. The Powder Magazine Museum traces its history from 1826 to 1946, and the Missouri Civil War Museum opened in June 2013 in the restored 1905 Post Exchange and Gymnasium Building, with over 22,000 square feet of exhibits. A telephone museum in a former Officers' Row duplex even preserves a portable switchboard set up for presidential visits to St. Louis.

## Flight Context

Jefferson Barracks lies at approximately 38.4947° N, 90.2819° W, on the west bank of the Mississippi River at Lemay, about 10 miles south of downtown St. Louis. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, look for the long rows of headstones of Jefferson Barracks National Cemetery beside the river, alongside the wooded county park and the Guard post. Sackman Field (FAA identifier IL91, formerly H49; private use, permission required) lies across the river in Illinois about 6 km to the southeast, St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS) about 14 km to the northeast, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) about 29 km to the north-northwest. The area sits within the busy St. Louis terminal airspace; expect river haze in summer.
