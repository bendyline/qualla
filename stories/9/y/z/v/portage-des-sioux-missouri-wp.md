# Portage Des Sioux, Missouri

A tiny Mississippi River town named for a Sioux escape route, founded as a Spanish answer to an American fort, host to the 1815 treaty councils that reshaped the frontier and guarded since 1957 by a fiberglass Madonna raised in thanks for surviving a flood.

**Location:** 38.9264, -90.3431

**Categories:** Cities in St. Charles County, Missouri, Missouri populated places on the Mississippi River, Portages in the United States, French colonial settlements of Illinois Country, Cities in Missouri

## Introduction

The name remembers a getaway. According to the story behind it, a band of Sioux fleeing their enemies reached the land between the Missouri and Mississippi rivers, hauled their canoes overland, and slipped back into the water on the other side. Their pursuers paddled the long way around to the confluence and fell behind. The French called the crossing a portage, and the town that grew beside it on the Mississippi, roughly opposite Elsah, Illinois, still carries the name: Portage Des Sioux.

## A Spanish Answer to an American Fort

The town itself was born of rivalry between empires. In 1799, while Spain still held the country west of the Mississippi, Spanish Lieutenant Governor Zenon Trudeau and François Saucier founded Portage Des Sioux in reaction to American plans to build a military post about 12 miles downstream. It became one of the French colonial settlements of the Illinois Country, a river village planted on the Mississippi within reach of the old portage route to the Missouri. The Sioux had used this ground to outrun their enemies. The Spanish used it to answer an ambitious new neighbor, planting a settlement of their own upriver from the planned American post.

## The Summer of Treaties

In 1815 the village hosted the councils that followed the War of 1812. President James Madison appointed three commissioners, William Clark, governor of Missouri Territory; Ninian Edwards, governor of Illinois Territory; and the St. Louis fur trader Auguste Chouteau, and the treaty signings at Portage Des Sioux were scheduled between July 18 and September 16. Tribes including the Potawatomi, Kickapoo, Omaha, Osage, Iowa, Sauk and Meskwaki, and several Sioux bands signed here. The treaties were meant, ostensibly, to settle the conflicts of the war, but they also consolidated and affirmed earlier cessions: the 1804 Treaty of St. Louis, in which the Sauk and Meskwaki gave up northeast Missouri and much of Illinois and Wisconsin, and the 1808 Treaty of Fort Clark, in which the Osage Nation ceded most of Missouri and northern Arkansas. Black Hawk refused the invitation and held out; he signed a peace treaty reaffirming the 1804 cession only in May 1816, and later said he had not understood that stipulation. He would resist those terms in the Black Hawk War, and in the end many of the tribes were forced to move west of Missouri.

## Our Lady of the Rivers

Living beside two rivers has always meant living with floods. In 1951, massive rainstorms threatened to flood the town, and Father Schlattman called on his parishioners to pray to Mary for their safety. The town survived. Six years later, in 1957, a statue of Mary as Our Lady of the Rivers was erected at the town's riverside shrine, made of Monsanto fiberglass. The designer, Norma McClory, deliberately gave the statue's face only minimal features, so that it would not look distorted when waves reflected off it. The figure has stood by the river ever since, a Madonna made for a town that has always had to live with the water.

## A Small Town on Big Water

Portage Des Sioux has stayed small. The 2010 census counted 328 people in 136 households, with a median age of 44.3, living in a city of just over half a square mile, of which 0.08 square miles is water; the 2020 census counted 335. Children attend schools in the Orchard Farm School District. The rivers still set the terms of life here. The town once had its own public library, a branch of the St. Charles City-County District Library, but it closed after flooding in 2019. The shrine, the river and the old portage route remain, a reminder that this tiny city has stood at the meeting ground of rivers, empires and nations for well over two centuries.

## Flight Context

Portage Des Sioux lies at about 38.926°N, 90.343°W on the Missouri bank of the Mississippi River in St. Charles County, on the low floodplain between the Mississippi and the Missouri just above their confluence. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, look for the small town grid and riverside shrine on the west bank opposite Elsah, Illinois; the Illinois River joins the Mississippi at Grafton a few miles upstream to the northwest. St. Charles County Smartt Airport (KSET) is only a few miles to the west, St. Louis Regional (KALN) at Alton lies about 14 nm east, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is roughly 11 nm to the south, with its Class B airspace beyond. River fog is common on calm mornings.
