# Piasa

A horned, scaled monster painted on the Mississippi bluffs at Alton frightened Marquette in 1673, was quarried away in the 19th century, and has been reimagined ever since, from a man-eating bird to the underwater panther of Native tradition.

**Location:** 38.8978, -90.1990

**Categories:** Rock art in North America, Legendary creatures of the Indigenous peoples of North America, Archaeological sites in Illinois, Madison County, Illinois, Mythological hybrids

## Introduction

In 1673, Father Jacques Marquette was skirting the limestone bluffs above the Mississippi River when he saw two painted monsters on the rock. They were as large as a calf, he wrote, with horns like a deer's, red eyes, a beard like a tiger's, a face somewhat like a man's and a body covered with scales, wrapped in a tail so long it passed over the head and back between the legs before ending in a fish's tail. The painting that unsettled him no longer exists. What visitors see on a bluff in Alton, Illinois, today is a 20th-century re-creation of a creature that has been reinvented more than once since Marquette passed by.

## Older Than Any Map

The original mural was created before any European explorers arrived in the region. It stood at the end of a chain of limestone bluffs, at a river-bluff terminus of the American Bottom floodplain, and it may have been an older iconograph from Cahokia, the great Mississippian city that began developing about 900 CE. At its peak around 1100 CE, Cahokia is estimated to have held some 15,000 to 20,000 residents, and it was the largest prehistoric urban center north of Mexico. Falcons, thunderbirds, bird men and monstrous snakes were common motifs of its culture. European mapmakers took note of the name early. A 1778 map by Thomas Hutchins shows "PIASAS" where Alton now stands, bounded by the Wood River to the east, one of the earliest documented references to the word. The 1797–98 map of French explorer Nicolas De Finiels labels the cliffs above the painting "Hauteurs De Paillisa."

## Through a Missionary's Eyes

The 1673 journal, an account edited by Claude Dablon and published under Marquette's name, records that green, red and black were the three colors of the picture, and that the monsters "at first made us afraid." He also wrote that they were so well painted that he could not believe any "savage" was their author, a judgment that says more about the prejudices of his era than about the Native artists who actually made them. Notably, his description never mentions wings. Around 1682, the French cartographer Jean-Baptiste-Louis Franquelin compiled a map from Louis Jolliet's account of the same 1673 journey, and the creature sketched on it, east of the Missouri River and south of the Illinois, resembles an underwater panther, wingless and with no likeness to a bird. The images did not age gently. By the late 1690s, the missionary St. Cosme reported them badly worn because passing local people would "discharge their weapons" at them, and around 1838 the author A. D. Jones described the damage firearms had done to the paintings, which he called "Piasau."

## The Bird That Devours Men

The creature got its wings in print. Around 1836, John Russell, a professor of Greek and Latin at Shurtleff College in Upper Alton, published "The Tradition of The Piasa," the article that first called it the "Piasa Bird." Russell said the name came from a nearby stream and meant, in the Illini language, "The Bird That Devours Men." In his telling, a huge bird living in the cliffs acquired a taste for human flesh from the dead of a war and began devouring villagers, until a chief named Ouatoga, guided by a dream from the Great Spirit, offered himself as bait while his warriors waited with poisoned arrows. Russell even claimed to have explored the monster's cave. In 1887, however, William McAdams wrote that he had contacted Russell, who admitted the story was fabricated. It is also possible the image was repainted between 1673 and 1836. Even so, historians, folklorists and tourism promoters often still rely on Russell's account. The Piasa Creek he named once ran through downtown Alton's main ravine; around 1912 it was completely covered by huge drainage pipes.

## The Panther Beneath the Water

Researchers have since looked to Native tradition itself. Esarey, Costa, Wood and others now link the Piasa to the Underwater Panther, both in its imagery and in its name. "Payiihsa" refers to a small supernatural being, often shown with large feet bearing four or six toes and frequently paired with underwater panther symbolism in pottery and rock art, and "Pa-i'-sa" appears among the "Little People" in the historian Jacob Piatt Dunn's account. Esarey and his colleagues believe the original folktale involved an aquatic pair of malevolent twin dwarves, the Payiihsaki, who took over a den that the culture hero known as the Illinois Trickster had conquered from a water manitou, and that Russell mangled that tale into his own story of a piasa bird. Dr. Mark J. Wagner of Southern Illinois University Carbondale's Center for Archaeological Investigations has also supported the underwater panther interpretation.

## A Monster Made of Quarry Stone

The original painting was on lithographic limestone, and quarrying destroyed it during the 19th century; accounts date its final removal anywhere from the 1840s to the late 1870s, when the Mississippi Lime Company is said to have quarried it away. Other images fared differently. A May 27, 1921, article in the Alton Evening Telegraph reported seven smaller painted images, believed to be of ancient Native American origin, at Levis Bluff about 1.5 miles upriver, authenticated in 1905 by George Dickson and William Turk. One finder removed four of them, among them an owl, a sun circle and a squirrel, and sold them to the Missouri Historical Society in 1921. Three of the pictographs, including the owl, were rediscovered in the Society's collection in the late 1990s. Today's Piasa, based partly on 19th-century sketches and lithographs, sits on a bluff several hundred yards upstream from the original site. The rock there is poorly suited to holding an image, so the monster must be regularly restored.

## Flight Context

The present Piasa painting is at approximately 38.90 N, 90.20 W, on the Mississippi River bluffs at Alton, Illinois, upstream of the original site. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, follow the pale limestone bluff line along the Illinois bank of the Mississippi westward from Alton; the painting itself is small at altitude, but the bluffs, the Great River Road at their foot and the Alton riverfront are clear landmarks. The confluence with the Missouri River lies just downstream and the confluence with the Illinois River lies upstream toward Grafton. St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) is about 7 nm east, St. Charles County Smartt Airport (KSET) about 11 nm west, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) about 12 nm southwest, so mind the St. Louis Class B airspace to the south. River haze is common on humid summer mornings.
