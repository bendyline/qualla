# Alton, Illinois

A bluff-top river town on the free side of the Mississippi, Alton saw an abolitionist martyred, Lincoln and Douglas debate for the last time, and a Civil War prison where smallpox killed hundreds.

**Location:** 38.9003, -90.1597

**Categories:** Cities in Madison County, Illinois, Illinois populated places on the Mississippi River, Underground Railroad, Lincoln-Douglas debates, American Civil War prisons

## Introduction

In the summer of 1937, two commercial fishermen from Alton noticed that something was wrecking their wood and mesh traps in the Mississippi. Deciding it had to be a fish, they built a strong wire trap and baited it with chicken guts. The next morning they hauled out a bull shark, five feet long and 84 pounds, and displayed it at the Calhoun Fish Market, where it drew crowds for days. It is a fitting story for Alton, a river town about 18 miles north of St. Louis that has spent two centuries being surprised by what the water brings to its door.

## A Border Made of Water

Rufus Easton laid out the town in January 1818, named it after his son, and ran a ferry across to the Missouri shore. That short crossing mattered enormously. Illinois was a free state and Missouri a slave state, so Alton became a destination for people escaping slavery, who crossed the river and moved on through Underground Railroad stations, some of them homes fitted with tunnels and hiding places. Slave catchers raided the city, and pro-slavery residents lived alongside abolitionists. On November 7, 1837, the Reverend Elijah P. Lovejoy, an abolitionist printer who had moved here from St. Louis, tried to stop a pro-slavery mob from destroying yet another of his presses. When one of the mob moved to set the warehouse on fire, Lovejoy went outside and was shot dead. The mob threw his press into the Mississippi. A 110-foot monument to him now rises in Alton Cemetery on the bluffs.

## The Last Debate

On October 15, 1858, Abraham Lincoln and Stephen Douglas met in Alton for the seventh and last of their debates before that year's Illinois Senate election. Oversized statues of the two men now stand on Lincoln Douglas Square downtown, and the Franklin House, where Lincoln dined and may have stayed the night, survives as the Lincoln Lofts. Alton's link to the end of slavery ran deeper still. Lyman Trumbull, an Alton resident who chaired the Senate Judiciary Committee, co-wrote the Thirteenth Amendment, and his Alton home, the Lyman Trumbull House, is a nationally designated landmark. Within weeks of the Civil War's start, amid reports that Missouri Governor Claiborne Fox Jackson meant to use the state militia to attack the St. Louis Arsenal and capture its tens of thousands of small arms, the Secretary of War ordered most of the weapons moved to Illinois. On a night in the last week of April 1861, about 21,000 guns were secretly evacuated to Alton by steamboat.

## The Prison Near Church Hill

Illinois built its first penitentiary in Alton, and during the Civil War Union forces used it to hold prisoners of war. Up to 12,000 Confederates were held there. The smallpox epidemic of 1863 and 1864 was catastrophic: an estimated 1,500 to 2,200 men died. Many of them lie in a mass grave on the north side of town, and a monument at the North Alton Confederate Cemetery honors 1,354 soldiers who died in the prison. Escapees often tried to cross the river back into Missouri. Only a corner of the penitentiary survives today, a few blocks from the river, though it once stretched nearly to Church Hill.

## Brick Streets and High Water

Alton climbs steeply from its waterfront, past massive concrete grain silos and railroad tracks to Queen Anne houses with sweeping views of the Mississippi. Several brick factories once operated here, and the city still has an unusually high number of brick-paved streets. The river gives and takes. Flood heights are marked on the big silos near the Argosy Casino, and the flood of 1993 is considered the worst of the last century; it cut off the city's water supply, leaving residents on bottled water for more than three weeks. In Upper Alton, Oakwood Cemetery holds the grave of Robert Pershing Wadlow, an Alton native listed by Guinness as the world's tallest man at 8 feet 11.1 inches. The earth over his grave is raised so visitors can compare its length with the graves around it, and a life-size statue of him stands on College Avenue.

## Where Three Rivers Meet

Alton sits near the confluence of three navigable rivers, the Illinois, the Mississippi and the Missouri, and its identity is tied to them. The cable-stayed Clark Bridge opened in 1994 after crews built it through the Great Flood of 1993, work featured in the Nova documentary Super Bridge; the city's children were handed pieces of cable so they could "take home a piece of the bridge." At Alton, Melvin Price Locks and Dam is the newest lock and dam on the Mississippi's main channel and one of its largest locks, and in winter bald eagles gather below it to feed on fish. North of town, a bikeway and the River Road run beneath limestone bluffs, where a reproduction of the Piasa bird, a cliff painting described by Father Jacques Marquette in 1673, still watches over the water. Alton once grew so fast that, by one local account, St. Louis businessmen backed a rival town, Grafton, upriver to check it. In 2021, voters elected David Goins as the city's first Black mayor.

## Flight Context

Alton sits at 38.900 N, 90.160 W on the Illinois bank of the Mississippi, about 18 miles north of St. Louis. From 2,000-3,000 feet MSL look for the cable-stayed Clark Bridge, the Melvin Price Locks and Dam nearby, the grain silos on the waterfront, and the pale limestone bluffs lining the river to the west-northwest toward Grafton, where the Illinois River joins the Mississippi. The Missouri River's confluence lies a few miles to the south. St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) is about 5 nm east, St. Charles County Smartt Airport (KSET) about 13 nm west, and St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) about 13 nm southwest under St. Louis Class B airspace. Low river fog can form on calm mornings, and summer brings haze and thunderstorms.
