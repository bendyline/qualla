# McPike Mansion

Built on one of the highest points in Alton by a mayor who grew prize grapes, the McPike Mansion was stripped down to its toilets during decades of abandonment before new owners began restoring it, and it now draws visitors for its history and its ghost stories.

## Introduction

By the time the scavengers were finished, even the toilets were gone. The McPike Mansion in Alton, Illinois, stood empty for years after its last long-term resident died, and people helped themselves to whatever was left, from the furnishings to the wooden banisters. It was an ignoble fate for a house built by a man who served as Alton's mayor, on a hilltop called Mount Lookout. Yet the red brick mansion survived its plunder, and today it is known for two things: a restoration funded by donations and tours, and a reputation as one of the area's reportedly haunted houses.

## The House on Mount Lookout

Henry Guest McPike, who lived from 1825 to 1910, chose his site well. The mansion stands on Alby Street at one of the highest points in Alton, a rise known as Mount Lookout, and the house itself sometimes goes by that name. The estate once covered 15 acres, of which about 4.4 remain. Construction began in 1869 under the architect Lucas Pfeiffenberger, and the mansion was completed in 1871. It rises three stories in red brick trimmed in white, with white pillars supporting the porch. Inside are 16 rooms, and below them a vaulted wine cellar. McPike was a man of standing in the river town: he served as mayor of Alton and was a notable local businessman, with interests in real estate and box making.

## A Grower's Hilltop

McPike was also an avid horticulturalist, and he surrounded the house with extensive gardens of orchards, shrubs and rare trees. He had a talent for grapes, too. In 1869, the year work on the mansion began, his Rulander, a name for the grape better known as pinot gris, was considered one of the finest in quality at an exhibition of the Mississippi Valley Grape Growers' Association, while his Diana grapes were judged the best on exhibition. It is tempting to connect those prize grapes to the vaulted wine cellar beneath the house, though the record does not say what McPike stored there. Later, in the late 1880s, he served as librarian of the Alton-Southern Illinois Horticultural Society. He died in 1910.

## Rooms for Rent, Then Ruin

The next chapter belongs to Paul A. Laichinger, who bought the mansion in either 1925 or 1936, depending on the account. He rented rooms to others and lived in the house until his death, in 1945 or 1951. After that, the mansion was abandoned for years. There was some interest in tearing it down and turning the land into a shopping center, but the plan fell through over zoning issues. Meanwhile, the house suffered. It was ransacked for everything left behind and became a victim of vandalism and neglect. Even recognition did not rescue it at first. The mansion was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on June 17, 1980, yet it stayed derelict for many more years.

## Restoration and Rumor

In 1994 Sharyn and George Luedke bought the mansion at auction and began a restoration funded through donations and tours. The work has been recognized: in 2017 the Alton Historical Commission gave the Luedkes a preservation award for their work on the front porch and the conservatory. The tours have a second draw. According to the owner, the mansion is allegedly haunted by the ghost of a former owner and a former domestic servant, and it is frequently part of the area's many haunted tours. Television has come calling as well. The house appeared in the series Scariest Places on Earth, in Season 1, Episode 7 of Fact or Faked: Paranormal Files, and in an episode of Ghost Lab. Whatever visitors believe about its spirits, the house itself is the real survivor.
