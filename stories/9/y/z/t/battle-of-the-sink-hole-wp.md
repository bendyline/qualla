# Battle of the Sink Hole

Five months after the Treaty of Ghent was signed, and three months after it ended the War of 1812, Missouri Rangers and Sauk warriors led by Black Hawk fought a deadly skirmish near the Cuivre River, where a limestone sinkhole became a fortress.

**Location:** 38.9334, -90.6871

**Categories:** Battles of the War of 1812, Battles in Missouri, May 1815, Battles of the War of 1812 in the Old Northwest

## Introduction

The War of 1812 had officially been over for three months. The Treaty of Ghent was signed in December 1814 and took effect in February 1815, but news traveled slowly to the frontier, and on May 24, 1815, men were still dying for it in a low spot near the mouth of the Cuivre River in Missouri. According to Robert McDouall, the British commander in the region, the Sauk had never received official word from the British that the war was over. What followed was a deadly fight with an uncertain outcome, and its most famous feature was a hole in the ground.

## Raid and Reprisal

The battlefield lies in what is now Lincoln County, near present-day Old Monroe and two frontier posts called Fort Howard and Fort Cap au Gris. The fight began with a raid by Sauk warriors on a local settlement. According to Black Hawk's own account, the raid was retribution for the murder of a child he had adopted earlier that spring. In that telling, this was not a campaign in a finished war but an answer to a personal loss. The Rangers at the nearby forts moved out to meet the raiders, and the two sides met near Fort Howard.

## Into the Sinkhole

Captain Peter Craig led a contingent of 50 Rangers and Regulars against the Sauk and Meskwaki roughly 300 meters south of Fort Howard. Captain David Musick joined the fight with 20 soldiers from Cap au Gris, arriving from the Cuivre River about three kilometers to the south. Under that pressure the Native American force split. Some warriors retreated north toward Bob's Creek, while Black Hawk and roughly 20 to 35 others took refuge in a sinkhole. The American troops partly encircled it, and for a time Black Hawk's group appeared trapped. The ground told a different story. The region's karst terrain, pocked with limestone sinkholes, gave the defenders natural cover, and though they were outnumbered by the American force, they held their ground.

## Two Stories of One Day

An ambush by the Sauk on a company of Rangers drew the Americans into a prolonged siege. Seven or eight Americans were killed, including their commander, Captain Peter Craig, and several more were wounded, against at least one Sauk warrior dead. Even those figures depend on who is telling the story. John Shaw and Black Hawk himself left conflicting accounts of the action, and tallies range from one to five Sauk dead. At nightfall both sides pulled back. The Americans returned to Fort Howard, and Black Hawk's warriors slipped away under cover of darkness and went back to Illinois. Neither side could claim a clear victory, and the outcome of the battle remained uncertain. Men had died on both sides of a war that had already ended on paper.

## Peace, Reaffirmed

The fight at the sinkhole was not the end of Black Hawk's story, but it closed this chapter of it. The war on this stretch of frontier had gone hard for the American posts: Fort Osage abandoned and Fort Madison defeated in 1813, Fort Shelby defeated and Fort Johnson abandoned in 1814, with battles at Rock Island Rapids and Credit Island that same year. The sinkhole near Cap au Gris was one of the very last. In 1816 he entered into negotiations with the United States government, ultimately reaffirming the Treaty of St. Louis. The battlefield lies near the present-day city of Old Monroe. Its terrain, though, has kept drawing attention: geologists have examined how the karst landscape shaped the fighting, a reminder that the hole which sheltered Black Hawk's warriors was as much a part of the battle as any soldier.

## Flight Context

The battle site lies near 38.9334°N, 90.6871°W, about 3 miles east of the center of Old Monroe in Lincoln County, Missouri, near the mouth of the Cuivre River north of St. Louis. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, the winding Cuivre River and the small grid of Old Monroe are the best references; the sinkhole itself is not identifiable from the air, so treat this as a landscape flight over the Cuivre valley. St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) lies about 21 miles to the southeast, so stay clear of or coordinate with the St. Louis Class B airspace when approaching from the south. St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) near Alton, Illinois, is roughly 35 miles to the east. Fog can form along the rivers on calm mornings in late spring, the season of the battle.
