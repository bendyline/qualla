# Warrenton Nursing Home fire

On a Sunday afternoon in February 1957, fire swept through the Katie Jane Memorial Home for the Aged in Warrenton, Missouri, killing 72 elderly residents and prompting a sweeping new nursing home law in Missouri.

## Introduction

On the afternoon of Sunday, February 17, 1957, the residents of the Katie Jane Memorial Home for the Aged were singing. In a first-floor room of the main building in Warrenton, Missouri, Lutheran minister Walter Schwane was leading the hymn "What a Friend We Have in Jesus" when a scream cut through the music. A visitor had noticed smoke down the hall. Moments later flames were visible, and a cry of "Fire!" ran through the building. In the end, 72 people were dead.

## A College Becomes a Home

The Katie Jane Memorial Home stood about fifty-five miles west of St. Louis in a two-and-a-half-story building that had once been the site of Central Wesleyan College. It had been converted into a home for the elderly (local histories date the conversion to 1943, other accounts to 1955), and by that winter it housed roughly 150 older men and women. The main building had three sections, and there was also an annex. Much of the wood in the building was more than fifty years old. Inside, staff often kept the thermostats between 85 and 90 degrees Fahrenheit to keep residents comfortable through the frigid Missouri winter, a kindness that would later be counted among the potential factors behind the disaster.

## Thirty Minutes

The fire is believed to have started at about 2:40 p.m. in a linen closet on the first floor of the annex. It moved with terrible speed. Within thirty minutes the annex was an inferno. Nurses and attendants saved whomever they could, moving patients out on mattresses and in wheelchairs, and people from the town rushed in to help. Many residents, though, were trapped on the second floor, and rescuers raised ladders against the walls in an attempt to reach them. Eventually the roof caved in. Flames shot high into the air, and the smoke could be seen from thirty miles away.

## Seventy-Two Lives

Seventy-two people died: forty-six men and twenty-six women, seventy-one in the fire itself and one in the hospital two days later. Many never had a chance to flee. Some were locked in their rooms, a common practice at the time, and others were bedridden on the upper floors, leaving them unable to escape the fast-moving fire. When the roof fell, the first floor collapsed into the basement, which delayed the recovery of the victims and the painful work of identifying them. They had come to the Katie Jane Home to be looked after in old age, and that promise of care is what makes their loss so hard to read about even now.

## What the Fire Changed

Investigators found that the home had been inspected just one week earlier. Early speculation about arson was investigated and dismissed, and the final determination was that the fire started from an undetermined cause. What the investigation did make clear was how exposed the residents had been. The facility was reportedly operating without a current license. Its fire escapes were inadequate, and it had no sprinkler system, no alarm system, and no evacuation plan. The response came quickly. On March 13, 1957, only weeks after the disaster, Missouri Governor James T. Blair signed a broad nursing home law, the Spradling Act, which put the state's Division of Health in charge of setting and enforcing minimum standards and added fire safety measures. The seventy-two who died in Warrenton changed how Missouri protected its elderly.

## Flight Context
