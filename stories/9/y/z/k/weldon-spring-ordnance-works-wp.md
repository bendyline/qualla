# Weldon Spring Ordnance Works

West of St. Louis, three small towns were cleared in 1940 for a World War II TNT plant that later processed uranium for the Atomic Energy Commission, leaving a contamination legacy that turned much of the land into Superfund sites and, eventually, conservation areas.

**Location:** 38.6958, -90.7467

## Introduction

In 1940 the U.S. government bought more than 17,000 acres of largely rural land in St. Charles County, Missouri, about 50 kilometers west of St. Louis, and the people who lived there had to go. Beginning that year and ending the next, the towns of Hamburg, Howell, and Toonerville and around 700 residents of the area were displaced. In their place rose the Weldon Spring Ordnance Works, a plant built to make explosives for a world at war. What happened on this land over the next quarter century would take decades more to clean up.

## Explosives for the War

Weldon Spring was government-owned but contractor-operated, and from 1941 to 1945 the Atlas Powder Company ran it, manufacturing trinitrotoluene, better known as TNT, along with dinitrotoluene, or DNT. Four TNT production lines and two DNT lines stood on the ground that would later become the Chemical Plant. A limestone quarry on the property supplied aggregate for building the works, and during wartime production the Army also used it to burn explosives-manufacturing waste and dump TNT-contaminated rubble. The work served the war effort, but it left a mark. The operations contaminated soil, sediments, and some off-site springs with nitroaromatic compounds.

## The Land Divided

After World War II, the government let most of the property go. More than 15,000 acres passed to the State of Missouri, including what became the 6,987-acre August A. Busch Memorial Conservation Area. Other parcels went to St. Charles County, the Francis Howell School District, and the University of Missouri, and much of the university's share became the 8,398-acre Weldon Spring Conservation Area. By 1959, with most of the site already out of government hands, 1,655 acres had been redeveloped for the U.S. Army Reserve as the Weldon Spring Training Area, which is still in use. But one small corner of the old ordnance works was about to begin a very different chapter.

## Uranium on the Old Production Lines

In 1955, after considerable cleanup by the Army and Atlas Powder, 205 acres were transferred to the Atomic Energy Commission for the Weldon Spring Uranium Feed Materials Plant, now called the Weldon Spring Chemical Plant. Mallinckrodt, Inc. operated it under contract from 1957 to 1966, converting processed uranium ore concentrates into uranium tetrafluoride, uranium trioxide, intermediate compounds, and uranium metal, along with a small amount of thorium. Wastes went into four raffinate pits on the plant property. During the 1960s the quarry served as a dump for uranium, thorium, and radium residues, both drummed and uncontained, and for contaminated rubble, equipment, and soil from a demolished uranium processing facility in St. Louis. When processing ended, the Army planned to make defoliants at the plant, but that project was canceled before any equipment was installed, and the site sat under caretaker status until 1985.

## A Superfund Legacy

Two portions of the old ordnance works became Superfund sites. The Environmental Protection Agency listed the Weldon Spring Quarry on the National Priorities List in July 1987 and added the adjacent chemical plant and raffinate pits in 1989. That site of about 228 acres includes the former uranium plant, an engineered 41-acre disposal cell and the nine-acre former quarry, where TNT and DNT residues once mingled with uranium, thorium, and radium, just three-quarters of a mile from a St. Charles County well field that an EPA summary says supplies drinking water to about 70,000 people. The former Army Ordnance Works followed, listed on February 21, 1990, with seven unlined lagoons that once held TNT wastewater and eight areas where explosive wastes were burned. In 1987, TNT was detected in surface water downstream of those lagoons. The Energy Department finished its active cleanup of the quarry and plant in the early 2000s and its Office of Legacy Management now monitors them, while the Army remains responsible for the ordnance works, where explosives contamination in groundwater is declining but still above drinking water standards.

## Prairie over the Past

Today, much of the land that was taken from Hamburg, Howell, and Toonerville is set aside in conservation areas, though portions of the Busch Conservation Area lie within the ordnance works Superfund site. The Department of Energy runs the Weldon Spring Site Interpretive Center, which opened to the public in April 2022 and whose exhibits trace the area's history, its contributions to World War II and the Cold War, the cleanup, and the ongoing work of long-term stewardship. Outside, gardens, prairie, trails, and a disposal cell viewing platform are open to the public, and from there visitors can look out across a landscape that once held three towns.

## Flight Context

The Weldon Spring site is centered near 38.6958°N, 90.7467°W in St. Charles County, Missouri, between the Missouri and Mississippi Rivers and about 50 kilometers west of St. Louis. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL, look for the broad Busch and Weldon Spring conservation areas and the DOE site with its interpretive center and disposal cell viewing platform. Spirit of St. Louis Airport (KSUS) is only about 5 nm east-southeast, so expect busy traffic in its vicinity, and St. Louis Lambert International Airport (KSTL) lies about 18 nm east. Washington Regional Airport (FYG) is about 13 nm west-southwest. Clear days offer the best views.
