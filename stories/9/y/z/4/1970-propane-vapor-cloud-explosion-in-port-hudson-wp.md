# 1970 propane vapor cloud explosion in Port Hudson

In December 1970 a broken propane pipeline near the Missouri crossroads of Port Hudson released a dense cloud of gas that did not merely burn but detonated, with the force of about 50 tons of TNT, making it one of the first confirmed accidental cases of an open-air vapor cloud turning from fire into shock wave.

## Introduction

In the technical literature on fuel hazards, a Missouri crossroads that most maps barely label carries unusual weight. On December 9, 1970, a propane pipeline owned by Phillips Pipeline Company broke near Port Hudson in Franklin County. The escaping gas spread into a large, dense vapor cloud, and when that cloud found a source of ignition, it did not simply burn. It exploded with tremendous force, releasing energy equivalent to about 50 tons of detonating TNT. More than half a century later, the accident is still quoted as one of the first confirmed cases of its kind.

## A Crossroads With a Borrowed Name

Port Hudson is an unincorporated community in western Franklin County, set along Missouri Route C about two miles north of Leslie and U.S. Route 50. Its name most likely came from Port Hudson, Louisiana. A post office served the community from 1859 to 1909, and after it closed, the community faded into the quiet of the Missouri countryside. What finally brought Port Hudson into the record was not anything on its surface. It was the pipeline running beneath the land, carrying propane for the Phillips Pipeline Company.

## A Cloud That Stayed Low

When the line broke, propane poured into the open air. Propane vapor is heavier than air, so instead of rising and dispersing, it gathered into a large, dense cloud. The U.S. Bureau of Mines, which investigated the accident at the invitation of the National Transportation Safety Board, estimated that about 750 barrels of liquefied petroleum gas escaped between the line break at 10:20 a.m. and the explosion at 10:40 p.m. By the moment of ignition, the propane-air mixture extended over roughly 10 acres and filled a volume of one to two million cubic feet. Nothing enclosed it. There were no tank walls or building to contain the fuel, only open ground and a spreading mass of flammable gas waiting for a spark.

## When Fire Becomes a Shock Wave

Most burning gas clouds deflagrate, meaning the flame front moves through the mixture slower than the speed of sound. A detonation is a different animal: a reaction front that travels as a supersonic shock wave, carrying far greater destructive pressure. At Port Hudson, damage both close to the blast and far from it indicates that the explosion may be attributed to the detonation of propane in air, with an energy release equivalent to about 50 tons of TNT. That is why the accident is widely quoted as one of the first, if not the first, confirmed accidental cases of an unconfined vapor cloud deflagration turning into a detonation, a process known as a deflagration-to-detonation transition.

## The Worst Case on Paper

The investigation looked at the pipeline as well as the blast. The NTSB cited past external and internal corrosion, along with poor welds on the uncoated line, as concerns. For anyone assessing the hazards of fuel transportation, Port Hudson represents a "worst-possible" sort of case history. Later disasters, including the 1989 Ufa train disaster in the Soviet Union and the 2005 fire at the Buncefield oil storage terminal in Hertfordshire, England, belong to the same family of vapor cloud hazards. Port Hudson's place in that history rests on one grim distinction: it showed that an open-air cloud could make the leap from fire to detonation.
