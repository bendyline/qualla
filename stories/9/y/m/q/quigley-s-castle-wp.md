# Quigley's Castle

South of Eureka Springs, a woman who had been promised a house of her own design built one instead: a rock-covered home with a two-story indoor garden, begun in 1943 and open to curious visitors since 1950.

**Location:** 36.3461, -93.7558

**Categories:** Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Arkansas, Houses completed in 1943, Houses in Carroll County, Arkansas, Museums in Carroll County, Arkansas, Historic house museums in Arkansas, Buildings and structures in Eureka Springs, Arkansas, National Register of Historic Places in Carroll County, Arkansas

## Introduction

Elise Quigley had been promised a house she could design herself. The story goes that one day, with the new house still unbuilt, she and her teenage children tore down the family's old one, leaving her husband Albert little choice but to start building. Whether or not every detail of that tale is exactly right, the house that followed is real enough. Quigley's Castle, off Arkansas Highway 23 south of Eureka Springs, is one of the most unusual homes in northwestern Arkansas, covered in stones and built around a garden that grows inside its walls.

## A Promise Kept, Eventually

The land came to Albert Quigley from his father, W. D. Quigley, who deeded it to him in 1930. Albert had promised his bride, the former Elise Fioravanti, a new house that she could design herself. Elise took the promise seriously. She drew up her plans, and in 1943 Albert and a neighbor began building the house to her design, using lumber cut from the property itself. It was a homemade project in the fullest sense. The design, the timber and eventually the decoration all came from the Quigleys' own hands and their own ground, which may be why the result looks like no house anyone would buy from a builder's catalog.

## A Lifetime of Stones

The outside walls are the first surprise. Elise had been collecting rocks since she was a child, and she used that collection to decorate the exterior, so the house wears her childhood hobby on its walls for everyone to see. Most people keep a childhood collection in a shoebox, if they keep it at all. Elise built hers into the walls of her family's home. The windows are the second surprise. The house has twenty-eight of them, along with French doors, all in keeping with Elise's wish to bring nature inside. With that many openings, the boundary between the garden and the living room starts to blur, which was precisely her intention. This is a house designed by someone who knew exactly what she wanted.

## The Garden Inside the House

Elise's most original idea was to let the outdoors grow indoors. She designed the house specifically to hold a two-story space for tropical plants. Using two foundations, she created a natural space between the outer and inner walls where plants could grow straight from the earth, so the greenery rises through the house rather than sitting in pots. Upstairs, one bedroom holds another of her creations: a collage she crafted from butterflies and moths collected in her own garden. Outside, meanwhile, that garden never stopped growing. Over the years Elise Quigley's plantings spread until they had taken over much of the property, wrapping the rock-studded house in greenery.

## Admission Since 1950

It did not take long for word to spread. In 1950 the family began charging admission to tour the home, turning a private dream house into a roadside destination, and the tours continue today, from March through November. The Quigley's Castle that visitors find now is still the house Elise imagined and Albert and his neighbor built from their own timber, with its stones, its many windows and its indoor garden. In 2003 it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. It is a monument to a particular kind of Ozark persistence: the conviction that a home should look exactly the way its owner dreamed it, and the willingness to spend a lifetime making that true.
