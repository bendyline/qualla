# Turpentine Creek Wildlife Refuge

In the Ozark hills south of Eureka Springs, a family's rescue of a single lion cub grew into a 459-acre sanctuary where more than a hundred abused, abandoned and neglected big cats, mostly tigers, live out their lives on grass instead of concrete.

## Introduction

Forty-two lions, tigers and cougars, crammed into three cattle trailers. That, according to the refuge's own history, is what a breeder in trouble with the law brought to the Jackson family's door in 1991. The Jacksons already knew something about big cats; a lion cub named Bum had come into their lives back in 1978. Now they faced a choice, and they made it quickly. They moved every one of those animals to a ranch in the Ozark hills near Eureka Springs, Arkansas, and out of that emergency grew Turpentine Creek Wildlife Refuge, a 459-acre sanctuary for abused, abandoned and neglected big cats.

## A Family's Choice

Don Jackson was a former employee of the Dallas Zoo, and with his wife, Hilda, and their daughter, Tanya Smith, he established the refuge in 1992. The decision was not a small one. Taking in dozens of large predators meant committing a family's money, land and years to animals that would need care every day for the rest of their lives. Bum, the cub who started it all, had shown the Jacksons how much a single big cat needs. The 1991 arrival showed them how many animals were out there needing it. From that start, the refuge became a permanent home for big cats that had been abused, abandoned or neglected, animals with nowhere else to go.

## Who Lives Here

The refuge houses a hundred or more animals across its 459 acres, which works out to about 186 hectares of Ozark hillside. Tigers are the heart of the population and the main focus of the sanctuary's care, but they share the land with a remarkable range of other species: lions and hybrid ligers, cougars, servals, bobcats, bears, jaguars, leopards, hyenas and caracals. Turpentine Creek holds itself to outside standards. In 2014 it achieved Verified status from the Global Federation of Animal Sanctuaries, and it was later fully accredited by the federation, an independent check on how a sanctuary cares for the animals in its charge.

## Rescue Ridge

In 2012 Turpentine Creek took on one of the largest rescues in its history. Between November 2012 and April 2013 the refuge relocated thirty-four big cats from a private breeding facility in Mountainburg, in Crawford County, Arkansas. Absorbing that many animals at once required a whole new section of the refuge, and a secondary area was built to hold them. It is now known as Rescue Ridge. Many of the cats that came from the breeding facility had never grown used to human contact, and being around crowds would only have added to the stress they already carried. So Rescue Ridge stays closed to the public. The rescues have not stopped. In 2023 the refuge took in more than thirty more big cats from Big Cat Rescue in Tampa, Florida, with the move finishing that December.

## Grass Instead of Concrete

For years, most of Turpentine Creek's animals lived in the original area now called the Compound, a collection of small cages with cement floors. The refuge set out to replace them. It built grassy habitats ranging from a quarter acre to half an acre, giving the cats grass underfoot and far more room to move. By September 16, 2015, every one of the small concrete cages had been emptied, and on September 17, 2015, demolition of the old Compound began. Care extends to medicine as well. The refuge has its own on-site veterinary hospital, the Jackson Memorial Veterinary Hospital, which opened in 2016. Treating animals on the property spares them stressful trips and reduces the risk of injury to the cats and to the people caring for them.

## Flight Context

Turpentine Creek Wildlife Refuge lies along Arkansas Highway 23 south of Eureka Springs, at 36.3105°N, 93.7571°W, in the forested Ozark hills of Carroll County. From about 2,000-2,500 feet AGL, look for the cleared habitat areas along the highway; please avoid low passes so as not to disturb the animals. Quigley's Castle lies about 2 nm north along the same road. Northwest Arkansas National Airport (KXNA) is about 27 nm west, and Branson Airport (KBBG) in Missouri is about 30 nm northeast. Morning valley fog is common in the Ozarks, especially in autumn.
