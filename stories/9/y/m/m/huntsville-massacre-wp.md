# Huntsville massacre

In the early hours of January 10, 1863, Union cavalrymen shot nine prisoners outside Huntsville, Arkansas, killing eight; the motive was never documented, no one was punished, and the killings were nearly forgotten for more than a century.

**Location:** 36.0876, -93.7366

## Introduction

For decades, people around Huntsville, Arkansas, returned each year to a patch of ground outside town and decorated it with flowers. Few of them said much in public about why. The site marked the place where, in the early morning hours of January 10, 1863, Union soldiers shot nine prisoners, killing eight. It took until 1974, when a historian came across accounts of the killings while researching a biography, for the story to be told in print, and until 2006 for a monument to stand where the men died.

## A Divided County

Madison County's war was a war between neighbors. Isaac Murphy of Huntsville, a former state legislator, was elected to the 1861 secession convention on a Unionist platform and held out as the lone delegate still voting against secession. In 1862, after the Battle of Pea Ridge, he received death threats and was forced to flee, taking a civilian post on General Curtis's staff. That fall his daughters visited him at Pea Ridge, and 25 Union soldiers escorted them home. Two miles from Huntsville the escort sent the girls ahead with a driver and made camp. Some 300 Confederate troops stopped the daughters and forced them, by threatening to kill the driver, to reveal the escort's size. The Confederates took the girls' money, then attacked the soldiers, killing eighteen; seven survived. Confederates also targeted Unionist families near Huntsville, stripping them, in one account's words, "of everything but what was on their bodies."

## Before Dawn, January 10

When Union General Francis Herron's 5,000 troops passed through Huntsville after the Battle of Prairie Grove, bound for the Mississippi and the push on Vicksburg, they heard that Murphy's daughters were being harassed and robbed. Within days, soldiers had arrested several men, but no charges were filed. Before dawn on January 10, members of Company G, 8th Regiment Missouri Volunteer Cavalry, under Lieutenant Colonel Elias Briggs Baldwin, took nine of the prisoners from where they were held. Their names were Chesley Hood Boatright, William Martin Berry, Hugh Samuel Berry, John William Moody, Askin Hughes, John Hughes, Watson P. Stevens, Robert Coleman Young and Bill Parks. Hugh Berry and Askin Hughes were Confederate captains home on leave; Moody, a former U.S. marshal, was a Confederate officer with the Fourth Arkansas Cavalry. William Berry was Hugh's father. All nine were shot. Bill Parks, left for dead, survived and later moved to Mississippi.

## A Dying Man's Words

Hugh Berry lived for three days, clear-minded enough to be asked what had happened. "Men of the 8th Missouri Regiment," he answered. "But Johnson, Ham and Murphy had it done." He meant the attorneys Isaac Murphy, E. D. Ham and Union Colonel James M. Johnson; he did not mention Baldwin, who commanded the unit that carried out the shooting. Why the men were killed has never been documented. It may have been over perjured oaths of allegiance to the Union, or revenge for the ambush of the escort or the harassment of Murphy's daughters, or something else entirely. The Union Army arrested Baldwin and charged him with the murder of prisoners of war, and he was held in Springfield, Missouri, for court-martial. But many witnesses were on active duty and civilian witnesses were displaced or unable to travel, so the Army dropped the charges and discharged him. Beyond that dismissal, no one was punished.

## The Long Silence

The killings split the town's institutions too. Several of the dead were Masons, and the local Masonic chapter believed Murphy had some part in the executions. It withdrew its support from the Plain View Female Seminary, run by Murphy's wife and daughters, and from the Huntsville Institute, which Murphy headed, and both schools closed. Murphy's career went on regardless: he became governor in 1864 and stayed in office through the first years of Reconstruction. Johnson was elected to Congress but never seated, and Ham became a district attorney and circuit judge. Meanwhile, the flowers kept appearing at the execution site. In 1974 historian John I. Smith, who had found accounts of the massacre while researching Murphy's life, published articles about it, and on September 30, 2006, a monument to the victims was dedicated where they fell.

## Flight Context

The massacre site lies about a mile northeast of Huntsville, Arkansas, which sits at about 36.088°N, 93.737°W, in the Ozark hills of Madison County east of Fayetteville. The town square and surrounding streets are easy to identify from 2,500-3,500 ft AGL; Huntsville Municipal's field elevation is about 1,750 ft MSL, so add that to AGL figures and keep ridge clearance in mind. Nearby airports: Huntsville Municipal (FAA identifier H34; no ICAO code), about 1 nm southwest; Drake Field at Fayetteville (KFYV), about 22 nm west. Morning fog often settles in the valleys.
