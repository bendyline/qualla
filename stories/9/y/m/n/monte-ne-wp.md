# Monte Ne

Coin Harvey built an Ozark health resort with Italian gondolas and an indoor pool, then poured his last fortune into a pyramid for a doomed civilization, and Beaver Lake swallowed nearly all of it.

**Location:** 36.2873, -94.0691

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Arkansas, Planned communities in the United States, National Register of Historic Places in Benton County, Arkansas, Towers in Arkansas, History of Arkansas

## Introduction

An expert from the Portland Cement Association studied the plans and pronounced that the Pyramid would not erode and would last for over a million years. It was never built. What William Hope Harvey managed to finish, an amphitheater meant to serve as its foundation, now lies beneath the waters of Beaver Lake east of Rogers, Arkansas, surfacing only when the lake level drops far enough. Around it, drowned in the same valley, are the bones of Monte Ne, a resort town that Harvey ran from 1901 into the 1920s with hotels, a golf course, bowling alleys, its own bank and a gondola imported from Italy.

## The Man They Called Coin

In the 1890s Harvey was a lawyer, businessman, author and lecturer with a national following. His 1894 pamphlet, Coin's Financial School, sold about a million copies in its first few years and gave him his nickname. His advocacy of Free Silver strongly influenced William Jennings Bryan, the 1896 Democratic presidential nominee, and campaigning for Bryan brought Harvey to northwest Arkansas. In 1900 he bought land near a small village called Silver Springs and renamed it Monte Ne, which he claimed combined Spanish and Native American words for mountain and water. He explained his choice plainly: "The people of Arkansas are to be congratulated on having no large city and no extremely rich people. For these reasons I have come here to make my home."

## Gondolas on the Lagoon

Harvey wanted a showpiece. He imported a 50-foot gondola from Italy and sold rides on the lagoon, while his son and brother-in-law opened a bathhouse with a heated 25-by-50-foot indoor pool, complete with slides and springboards, the first in Arkansas. A railroad line opened on June 19, 1902, with Bryan himself speaking at the ceremony, though service proved erratic and many guests came instead on Frisco excursion trains. The St. Louis architect A. O. Clarke drew up a grand Club House Hotel and four cottage rows, each to be named for a state bordering Arkansas. When workers were asked to put in longer hours for no extra pay in 1905, they struck, and Harvey fired them. The Club House Hotel never rose past its first floor. Harvey paid local merchants in his own scrip, and his Bank of Monte Ne lasted only until 1914.

## The Difficult Host

Harvey filled the calendar with dances, music, tennis, croquet and fox hunting, but his temperament often drove away more people than it drew. He enforced a lights-out at 10 p.m., and at least once he cut the town's main electricity to make the curfew stick. Disgruntled guests left the next day. Critics also objected to his Sunday events and to his ban on children and people who were ill. His most lasting success came from the road rather than the resort. The Ozark Trails Association, founded at Monte Ne in 1913, grew until its 1916 meeting in Oklahoma City drew 7,000 delegates, and it marked its sponsored routes with large obelisks listing their benefactors. By 1920 attendance had dwindled to 200, and the group disbanded in 1924, but many of the roads it championed became part of historic U.S. Route 66.

## A Letter to the Future

Personal losses piled up. Harvey's son Hal died in a railway accident in 1903, his wife and daughters had moved to Chicago, and his son Tom left in 1908 and never returned. The railroad was sold, the bank closed, and automobiles lured vacationers away from long stays at a single resort. Convinced civilization was ending, Harvey announced in 1920 that he would leave a message for whoever came next: a 130-foot obelisk whose sealed vaults would hold his books, a globe, a Bible, encyclopedias, newspapers and everyday objects "from the size of a needle and safety pin up to a Victrola." He built a 165-foot retaining wall and spent his remaining money on the amphitheater. The 1929 crash ended any hope of finishing. In 1931 the Liberty Party held its national convention at Monte Ne, reportedly the only presidential convention ever held in Arkansas, and chose the eighty-year-old Harvey as its candidate. He died in 1936.

## What the Lake Keeps

Some resort buildings found new lives until the 1960s, when the Army Corps of Engineers dammed the White River and Beaver Lake flooded almost the entire resort. Rumors of treasure hidden in the amphitheater persisted, and its last owner, W. T. McWhorter, planned to dynamite it the day before handing the deed to the Corps. Spectators gathered, but the Corps stopped the blast. For decades a crumbling hotel tower marked the site, until the Corps demolished it in February 2023, citing ongoing vandalism and health hazards. Harvey's tomb, moved to higher ground before the lake rose, can still be seen from the Monte Ne boat launch, and a concrete chair from the amphitheater is in the Rogers Historical Museum's collection.

## Flight Context

Monte Ne lies at about 36.29°N, 94.07°W on the western reaches of Beaver Lake, a few miles east of Rogers in Benton County, Arkansas. The long, branching reservoir is the dominant landmark; follow its arms southeast of Rogers and look for the shoreline near Highway 94. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL on a calm day, low water can reveal foundations and parts of the amphitheater near the shoreline; at normal pool the site is simply open water ringed by wooded Ozark hills. Rogers Executive Airport - Carter Field (KROG) lies about 5 nm north-northwest, with Northwest Arkansas National Airport (KXNA) about 12 nm west. Morning fog can settle over the lake, especially in fall.
