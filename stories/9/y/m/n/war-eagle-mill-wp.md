# War Eagle Mill

Washed away by a flood, burned in the Civil War and burned again in 1924, the mill on War Eagle Creek in northwest Arkansas has been rebuilt each time and still grinds grain under an 18-foot cypress water wheel.

**Location:** 36.2672, -93.9431

**Categories:** Watermills in the United States, Grinding mills in Arkansas, Buildings and structures in Benton County, Arkansas, Arkansas in the American Civil War, Tourist attractions in Benton County, Arkansas

## Introduction

Water slaps against an 18-foot cypress wheel, and inside the mill grain becomes flour, the same work done on this bank of War Eagle Creek since the 1830s. War Eagle Mill, about ten miles east of Rogers in Benton County, Arkansas, is the only working watermill in the state, and the building grinding today is the fourth to stand here. A flood took the first, soldiers burned the second, and fire claimed the third; each time, somebody came back and built it again.

## Flood and War

Sylvanus and Cathryn Blackburn moved to the War Eagle Valley in 1832 and by 1838 had built a mill beside the creek, next to a ford that travelers would use for decades. In 1848 a flood pushed that first mill into the water and carried it downstream, destroying it completely. The Blackburns rebuilt, added a sawmill, and prospered as settlers filled the valley. Then came the Civil War, which left northwest Arkansas caught between strongly Confederate Arkansans to the south and Union-supporting Missourians a few miles north. The Blackburns' five sons joined the Confederate Army while the rest of the family went to Texas. In 1862 the Union Army moved into the valley and used the mill to grind grain. When Confederates advanced and Union forces gathered near Pea Ridge, Confederate soldiers ran the mill for two days and then burned it so the Union could not use it again. After the Battle of Pea Ridge, retreating Confederates and bands of looters laid the countryside waste, and many families abandoned their homesteads.

## The Lumber King

When the Blackburns returned after the war ended in 1865, only their house still stood. Sylvanus's son, James Austin Cameron Blackburn, known as J.A.C., had the mill rebuilt by 1873, and he pushed it further, adding a more powerful grinding machine driven by a turbine instead of a water wheel. The family's sawmill, reportedly the largest in Arkansas, earned him the title "Lumber King" of northwest Arkansas. War Eagle lumber went into much of Fayetteville, including Old Main on the University of Arkansas campus. By the early twentieth century the mill was turning out 40 barrels of flour a day, creating a need for better access to markets, and in 1907 a one-lane steel truss bridge, the War Eagle Bridge, went up beside it across the old ford. J.A.C. eventually won a seat in the Arkansas Senate and sold the mill to the Kilgore family, who ran it until 1924, when it burned down, leaving only the foundation and a few remnants.

## The Medlins Bring Back the Wheel

Almost fifty years later, in 1973, Jewel Medlin bought the property, and his wife, Leta, and daughter, Zoe Medlin Caywood, found blueprints and rebuilt the mill once more. They changed the design slightly to restore the undershot water wheel used more than a hundred years earlier, the kind in which the current pushes against the bottom of the wheel. Today the mill grinds organic grain, cereal and flour products that are sold on site, and the building also holds a store and a restaurant. The mill has since changed hands, most recently in 2024, when Mark and Cristin Hall of Bentonville bought it from Marty and Elise Roenigk, who had owned it since 2004. The 1907 bridge still spans the creek beside it, 182 feet long and listed on the National Register of Historic Places, the only Parker truss bridge in northwest Arkansas. Just downstream, War Eagle Creek bends south and flows into Beaver Lake.

## Flight Context

War Eagle Mill sits at 36.267°N, 93.943°W on War Eagle Creek, about 10 miles east of Rogers, Arkansas, where the creek makes a sharp bend just before entering the upper arms of Beaver Lake. Beaver Lake's long, branching reservoir is the dominant landmark; the mill and bridge lie in the wooded valley just east of the lake. A pass at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL shows the creek bend and the steel truss bridge. Nearby airports: Rogers Executive-Carter Field (KROG), about 10 nm northwest; Springdale Municipal (KASG), about 10 nm southwest; Northwest Arkansas National (KXNA), about 18 nm west. Fog often forms over Beaver Lake and the creek valleys on cool mornings.
