# Dinosaur World (Arkansas)

For decades a roadside park in Beaver, Arkansas, filled 65 acres with life-size dinosaurs, cavemen and a roughly 40-foot King Kong named for a Hollywood actor who never owned a piece of it, until the park closed in 2005.

## Introduction

A roughly forty-foot King Kong once loomed over a roadside park in Beaver, Arkansas, bearing the name of a Hollywood actor who never owned a single share of the place. Around the great ape stood about a hundred life-size sculptures of dinosaurs, cavemen and other prehistoric creatures, spread across 65 acres in the Ozark hills. The park went by several names over its lifetime, Farwell's Dinosaur Park, then John Agar's Land of Kong, and finally Dinosaur World. At one time it was described as the largest dinosaur park in the world. It closed in 2005, leaving behind a trail of photographs, film cameos and roadside legend.

## Farwell's Dinosaurs

The park began in the 1960s (most accounts say 1967), when Ola Farwell hired the sculptor Emmet Sullivan to create somewhere between six and ten life-size replicas of dinosaurs. With those first giants in place, the attraction opened as Farwell's Dinosaur Park. Sullivan was the designer, but the hands that actually built the creatures belonged to two local men, A. C. McBride and Orvis Parker, who turned his plans into full-sized beasts. It was a proper roadside venture of its era, the kind of place built to make a family pull over on a summer drive, and it grew from that small first herd into something much larger. By the time it was finished growing, the park held about a hundred sculptures, from dinosaurs to prehistoric mammals to the cavemen who, in the logic of roadside attractions, lived alongside them.

## The Sculptor of the Ozarks

Emmet Sullivan's work reached well beyond Beaver. He also designed the dinosaur statues at Dinosaur Park in South Dakota and at Wall Drug, another landmark of the American roadside. Closer to home, he designed the Christ of the Ozarks statue in nearby Eureka Springs, a very different kind of monument that still stands on its hilltop a short distance from where his dinosaurs once roamed. The combination says something about the craft of monumental sculpture in mid-century America. The same artist could shape a dinosaur for a tourist park and a towering religious figure for a hillside overlooking a resort town, and both would draw travelers off the highway to stand and stare.

## Kong Comes to Beaver

In the late 1970s or early 1980s the park was sold to Ken Childs, and it took on a flashy new identity: John Agar's Land of Kong. A statue of King Kong, about 40 feet tall by most counts, promoted as the "World's Largest King Kong," was built for the occasion. The name came from friendship rather than investment. Childs was a friend of the film actor John Agar, who had appeared in the 1976 version of King Kong, and Agar gave permission to use his name for the park. Many articles over the years reported that Agar owned the park or held a share in it, but he was never either. Photographs from 1988 show what visitors found in that era: a shaggy woolly mammoth, a caveman beside a saber-toothed cat, and the rest of the prehistoric crowd.

## A Last Bow on Film

The park held one more oversized attraction: a mural of Noah's Ark by local artist Will Johnson, billed as the world's largest. Its sculptures also found their way onto the screen. A few of the dinosaurs, along with the nearby Beaver Dam, appear briefly in the opening scenes of the 1969 horror movie It's Alive!, and the park's tyrannosaurus is featured in the 2005 film Elizabethtown, where it appears on the film's cover. That same year, 2005, the park closed for good. There is a certain bittersweet symmetry in it: just as one of its dinosaurs reached a wider audience than ever through a Hollywood film, the place that had given it a home for decades shut its gates. What remains now is a closed site, the story, and the images of a roadside world where cavemen and King Kong kept company with dinosaurs.

## Flight Context

The former Dinosaur World site lies near Beaver, Arkansas, at 36.4420°N, 93.8422°W, close to Beaver Dam on the White River at the northern end of Beaver Lake. The park closed in 2005 and its grounds are private, so use Beaver Dam and the lake as your primary references from about 2,000-3,000 feet AGL. Eureka Springs lies about 6 nm to the east-southeast. Northwest Arkansas National Airport (KXNA) is about 25 nm west-southwest, and Branson Airport (KBBG) in Missouri is about 31 nm east. The Ozark terrain here is hilly and wooded, and morning fog often lingers over the lake and river.
