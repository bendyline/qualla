# Christ of the Ozarks

A 65.5-foot concrete Christ with arms flung wide above Eureka Springs, Arkansas, the Christ of the Ozarks was commissioned in 1966 by Gerald L. K. Smith, a notorious antisemite and white supremacist who is buried at its feet.

**Location:** 36.4069, -93.7232

**Categories:** Colossal statues of Jesus, Outdoor sculptures in Arkansas, Buildings and structures in Eureka Springs, Arkansas, Tourist attractions in Carroll County, Arkansas, 1966 sculptures

## Introduction

The figure on Magnetic Mountain holds its arms straight out, sixty-five feet from fingertip to fingertip, in the posture of the Crucifixion, yet no cross stands behind it. The face is nearly blank, the forms reduced to a few simplified lines. Christ of the Ozarks rises 65.5 feet above the hills outside Eureka Springs, Arkansas, a spare, modernist image of Jesus. The man who commissioned it spent many years preaching hatred.

## The Man Behind the Monument

Gerald L. K. Smith first rose to prominence during the Great Depression as an organizer for Huey P. Long's Share Our Wealth movement, which he led briefly after Long's assassination in 1935. What followed were many years of highly controversial, religiously charged activism characterized above all by Holocaust denial, virulent racism, antisemitism and pro-Nazi sympathies. Along the way he founded the America First Party in 1943 and ran as its presidential candidate in 1944, winning fewer than 1,800 votes. Late in life Smith retired to Eureka Springs, where he bought and renovated an old mansion. According to his biographer, Glen Jeansonne, Smith had only $5,000 to his name at the end of 1963, yet by the spring of 1964 he had raised $1,000,000 to build what he called his "Sacred Projects," a religious theme park on the grounds of his estate. His plans included a life-size recreation of ancient Jerusalem, which was never built. The statue, completed in 1966, was the centerpiece. Smith died in 1976, and he and his wife are buried beside it.

## Built by Hand to Outlast a Tornado

The sculptor, Emmet Sullivan, claimed to have assisted in the work at Mount Rushmore and also worked on the nearby attraction Dinosaur World. His Christ is modernist and minimal, with little facial detail or expression and forms pared down to their simplest lines. It was built entirely by hand from steel and mortar, sits on 320 tons of concrete, and was designed to withstand winds of 500 miles an hour, with reinforcement meant to survive a passing tornado. Standing on Magnetic Mountain at an elevation of about 1,500 feet, it overlooks the town. Not everyone admires the result. Critics have nicknamed it "Gumby Jesus" and "Our Milk Carton with Arms."

## A Stage on the Mountain

Smith also completed an amphitheater of roughly 4,000 seats near the statue, home to seasonal outdoor performances of The Great Passion Play, which he started with inspiration from the passion play staged every ten years in Oberammergau, Germany. The statue has since found its way onto screens. It appears briefly in the 1988 film Pass the Ammo and the 2005 film Elizabethtown, shows up in the opening titles of the third season of True Detective, filmed in the Arkansas Ozarks, and features in the 2018 documentary The Gospel of Eureka. In July 2021 the art collective Indecline hung a banner on it, which was taken down. The statue keeps its arms open over the hills either way, a monument that asks visitors to hold two stories at once: the faith it depicts and the hatred of the man who paid for it.

## Flight Context

Christ of the Ozarks stands atop Magnetic Mountain at about 36.407°N, 93.723°W, on the eastern edge of Eureka Springs, Arkansas, at roughly 1,500 ft elevation. The statue is small from the air; look for it on the wooded ridge with the Passion Play amphitheater nearby. Keep at least 1,000 ft above the ridge tops (around 2,500-3,000 ft MSL) over town. Nearby airports: Carroll County Airport at Berryville (FAA identifier 4M1, GPS code K4M1; no ICAO code listed), about 5 nm east-southeast; Rogers Executive-Carter Field (KROG), about 19 nm west; Branson Airport in Missouri (KBBG), about 26 nm northeast. Ridges and hollows can trap fog on cool mornings.
