# Eureka Springs, Arkansas

A stairstep Victorian spa town in the Ozarks, built on the promise of healing water, where streets refuse right angles and the whole city is a historic district.

**Location:** 36.4031, -93.7383

**Categories:** Cities in Carroll County, Arkansas, Hot springs of Arkansas, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Arkansas, Gay villages in the United States, County seats in Arkansas

## Introduction

At the Basin Park Hotel, the front doors open onto the floor below the first, and a ground-level emergency exit at the back of the building lets out from the fifth floor. Nobody made a mistake. The hotel simply obeys the ground it stands on, like the rest of Eureka Springs. This small Ozark city of roughly 2,200 people climbs the walls of a narrow valley in northwest Arkansas, its stone buildings stacked along limestone streets that curve, rise and fall in a five-mile loop. Few of those streets meet at right angles, and there is not a single traffic light. It has long been nicknamed the Stairstep Town.

## Water That Promised Miracles

Long before the Victorians came, Native American legends told of a Great Healing Spring here, and people of many indigenous cultures visited the waters. These hills are ancestral lands of the Osage Nation, and Delaware and Shawnee bands also lived in the area before federal removal pushed them west. Newcomers embraced the legend eagerly. In 1856 Dr. Alvah Jackson claimed that Basin Spring had cured eye ailments; during the Civil War he ran a hospital in a local cave, and afterward he bottled the water as "Dr. Jackson's Eye Water." In 1879 his friend Judge J. B. Saunders announced that the springs had cured his own crippling disease and began promoting them across the state. Thousands arrived, covering the hillsides in tents and shanties. Eureka Springs was incorporated on February 14, 1880, and the 1880 census counted 3,984 residents, more than Fort Smith or Pine Bluff.

## The Springs Not Shared

Not everyone was welcome at the healing waters. The city's early Black residents were freedmen who had come from farms where they had been enslaved; some arrived for work or for their health and stayed. Barred from whites-only lodging, Black visitors stayed at Black-owned hotels, and in the 1890s the community founded its own school and the Pilgrim's Chapel of the African Methodist Episcopal Church. Segregation hardened after the Supreme Court's Plessy v. Ferguson ruling, and African Americans were eventually banned from every spring in town except one, Harding Spring. The springs themselves have faded, too. The town counts more than 140 of them, all cold-water, but over-extraction has greatly diminished their flow. In 1970 the entire city, within its borders at the time, was listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Healers, Hucksters and a Giant Christ

A town founded on miracle cures has always attracted big promises. In 1937 Norman Baker, a charlatan, turned the grand Crescent Hotel into a hospital claiming to cure cancer, and he was later convicted of mail fraud. The Crescent's dark history later drew the television series Ghost Hunters to investigate its reputed hauntings. On a hilltop outside town, the clergyman and political organizer Gerald L. K. Smith, who retired here, commissioned the Christ of the Ozarks, a statue more than 60 feet tall erected in 1966. Nearby, the Great Passion Play has drawn an estimated 7.7 million people, which the Institute of Outdoor Theatre counts as the largest attendance of any outdoor drama in the United States. Its script has been revised; the original included a monologue blaming Jesus's death on the Jews. Filmmakers came as well, and the Auditorium still bears burn marks from the special effects of the movie Pass the Ammo.

## A Welcome Painted in Many Colors

Modern Eureka Springs has turned its eccentricity into an identity. In 2015, after Fayetteville voters repealed that city's similar ordinance, it became the only city in Arkansas with an anti-discrimination ordinance covering LGBT residents and tourists, and when a state law meant to invalidate such ordinances took effect on July 22, 2015, the mayor said the ordinance would become largely ineffective but promised the town would join a court challenge to the law. The calendar reflects that spirit. Four Diversity Weekends and a week-long Pride celebration in June share the year with a UFO conference, a month-long May Festival of the Arts, a New Orleans-style Eureka Gras, and, the weekend before Halloween, a Zombie Crawl that ranks among the largest zombie parades in the country. Walking these streets means climbing constantly, past Victorian cottages and manors built of local stone, through a town once nicknamed the Magic City and the Little Switzerland of the Ozarks.

## Flight Context

Eureka Springs sits at about 36.40°N, 93.74°W in western Carroll County, Arkansas, about 11 miles south of the Missouri state line via Arkansas Highway 23. Look for a compact town folded into a narrow valley at the headwaters of Leatherwood Creek, with U.S. Route 62 running along a ridgecrest on its south side toward Berryville (11 miles east) and Rogers (34 miles west). The Christ of the Ozarks statue stands on a hilltop near town, and the large Beaver Lake reservoir lies to the west. A pass at 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL shows the stairstep streets well. Terrain is hilly and wooded, so keep a safe margin over the ridges. Northwest Arkansas National Airport (KXNA) lies to the west, and Branson Airport (KBBG) is about 27 nm east-northeast in Missouri. Fall foliage and clear winter days offer the best views; valley fog can linger on cool mornings.
