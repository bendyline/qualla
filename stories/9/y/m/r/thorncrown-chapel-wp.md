# Thorncrown Chapel

Built in 1980 in the woods outside Eureka Springs, Arkansas, E. Fay Jones's Thorncrown Chapel is a soaring lattice of pine and 425 windows, raised from pieces no larger than two men could carry.

**Location:** 36.4169, -93.7703

**Categories:** Chapels in the United States, E. Fay Jones buildings, Churches in Arkansas, Buildings and structures in Eureka Springs, Arkansas, National Register of Historic Places in Carroll County, Arkansas

## Introduction

The rule was simple: no structural piece could be bigger than two men could carry through the woods. Architect E. Fay Jones set that limit to protect the forested hillside outside Eureka Springs, Arkansas, and out of it came Thorncrown Chapel, a building of slender pine members and glass that stands 48 feet tall and seems to dissolve into the trees around it. Completed in 1980, it looks like an open-air pavilion. It is actually an enclosed, air-conditioned room that seats up to 100 people, set among the trees.

## A Schoolteacher's Vision

The chapel began with Jim Reed, a retired schoolteacher who bought land near Eureka Springs in 1971. He wanted a non-denominational pilgrimage chapel, set apart from the town, where people could come to meditate in the forested landscape. For the design he turned to E. Fay Jones, an Arkansas architect who had apprenticed with Frank Lloyd Wright, and the finished building recalls the Prairie School style that Wright popularized. Jones had chosen his career in 1938, after a short film about Wright's Johnson Wax Headquarters caught his imagination, and he became a Taliesin Fellow in 1953. Jones would later become the only one of Wright's disciples to receive the Gold Medal of the American Institute of Architects, its highest honor, in 1990, and he spent 35 years teaching at the University of Arkansas School of Architecture, where he served as its first dean. Thorncrown is among his most famous buildings; a similar chapel of his, the Mildred B. Cooper Memorial Chapel, stands in nearby Bella Vista.

## A Crown of Thorns in Pine

Jones said his inspiration was Sainte-Chapelle in Paris, the Gothic church famous for its jewel-like interior, where many narrow stained-glass windows and different kinds of glass fill the space with light. Sainte-Chapelle once held relics of King Louis IX, among them the Crown of Thorns believed to have been worn by Christ, and that relic gave the Arkansas chapel its name. Jones translated the idea into Ozark materials. The frame is pressure-treated Southern pine, and flagstone, like the pine indigenous to northwestern Arkansas, forms the floor and the low surrounding wall. The chapel measures 24 feet wide and 60 feet long, with 425 windows that add up to 6,000 square feet of glass. A small ornamental skylight in the roof was later enlarged to bring more daylight into the room.

## Honored Before Its Time

Most buildings must wait fifty years before they can join the National Register of Historic Places. Thorncrown was listed in 2000, when it was just twenty years old, three decades ahead of that usual cutoff, in recognition of its architectural significance. In 2006 the American Institute of Architects gave it the Twenty-five Year Award, which honors works whose value has endured, and in a poll of the institute's membership it was ranked as the fourth most favored building. Travel writers have joined in: Budget Travel put it on a list of the "12 Most Beautiful Churches in America." The chapel is open to visitors daily from March through December, and non-denominational Sunday services are held in an adjoining worship center from April to the third week of December.

## Flight Context

Thorncrown Chapel sits in the woods at about 36.417°N, 93.770°W, just west of Eureka Springs, Arkansas. The chapel itself is hidden by tree cover and hard to spot from the air; use Eureka Springs as the reference at 2,500-3,500 ft MSL, which keeps you clear of the surrounding ridges. Nearby airports: Carroll County Airport at Berryville (FAA identifier 4M1, GPS code K4M1; no ICAO code listed), about 7 nm east-southeast; Rogers Executive-Carter Field (KROG), about 17 nm west. Fog and low cloud often settle in the Ozark hollows on cool mornings.
