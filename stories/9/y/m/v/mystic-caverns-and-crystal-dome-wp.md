# Mystic Caverns and Crystal Dome

Two Ozark caves 400 feet apart tell opposite stories: one scarred by lantern soot and souvenir hunters, the other found by a bulldozer and guarded so carefully that most of it is still growing.

**Location:** 36.1196, -93.1267

**Categories:** Caves of Arkansas, Show caves in the United States, Landforms of Newton County, Arkansas, Tourist attractions in Newton County, Arkansas

## Introduction

Somewhere inside Mystic Caverns, a formation carries a signature: Adam Kolbe, Wilcockson, April 16, 1919. Kolbe is the first visitor known by name, though settlers who came to this corner of the Arkansas Ozarks in the 1830s, and named it Wilcockson, likely found the cave well before the 1850s. Back then its entrance was simply a sinkhole with a 10-foot drop into darkness. Today the cave has a twin just 400 feet away, and the pair hold a steady 58 degrees Fahrenheit year-round. Their histories, though, could hardly be more different.

## Lanterns, Ladders and Soot

The cave's first name, Mansion Cave, came from its huge open chambers. Around 1928, owners Jim and Bob Gurley began charging admission, building a wooden ladder down the sinkhole, leveling the floor into trails and leading visitors by kerosene lantern. They renamed it Wild Horse Cavern and stationed a hand-carved horse beside the ticket booth. A new owner named Singer took over in 1930 and handed each guest a pair of coveralls and a lantern of their own. Those flames left most of the soot that still stains the cave. Concrete steps and a guide rope followed in 1937, but in 1938 an Arkansas state official halted commercial visits, most likely over the crumbling steps and the risk of rock slides near the entrance. For the next eleven years the cave sat unsupervised, and vandals broke off formations and sold them to commercial rock dealers.

## The Trout Farmer's Cave

Albert Raney Sr., who ran a trout farm nearby, bought the damaged cave in 1949, just as the local highway was being paved for the first time. Raney hauled out years of accumulated debris, cut a safer spiral path down the sinkhole, added hand rails, and spread lime on the trails so it would harden into firm footing. He also strung the first electric lights through the chambers. Renamed Mystic Caverns, the cave reopened in 1950, and in 1959 Raney handed it to his son, Albert Jr. In 1966 the family sold the cave, along with their trout pond, to the developers of Dogpatch USA, the Li'l Abner theme park rising next door. Jim Schermerhorn, an experienced caver and Dogpatch shareholder who supervised the work, replaced a rickety swinging bridge over Mill Creek canyon with a safer road, dug a new man-made entrance and laid stone trails.

## Found by a Bulldozer

Then the ground opened. While Schermerhorn was grading a new parking lot, a new sinkhole opened up as he worked the bulldozer, revealing an enormous cavern no one had ever seen. He understood at once what a pristine cave meant, and he camped at its entrance until it could be sealed. The park named it Old Man Moses Cave after a Li'l Abner character and promised to open it, but like many Dogpatch plans, that never happened while the park owned it. In 1981 the caves were sold, and under Bruce Raney, Albert Sr.'s grandson, the project was finally completed. Renamed Crystal Dome, it opened for tours that year. Because it was protected from the start, about 90 percent of the cave is still actively forming.

## Waiting in the Dark

Together the twin caves have been promoted as holding more formations per foot than any other caves in Arkansas, and Mystic Caverns, in business since the late 1920s, is said to be older than any other commercially operated cave in the state except Onyx Cave in Eureka Springs, and perhaps Diamond Cave near Jasper. A third cave on the site, Not Much Sink, has been deemed too dangerous for public tours. In late 2008 the longtime operator put the attraction up for auction on eBay, cutting the minimum bid from $1.2 million to about $900,000 and saying he wanted to devote more time to Christian ministries. At the time the owner put attendance at about 15,000 visitors a year. Since 2021 both caves have been closed to the public indefinitely, and their formations keep growing in the dark.

## Flight Context

Mystic Caverns and Crystal Dome lie at about 36.12°N, 93.13°W along the Arkansas Highway 7 Scenic Byway in Newton County, roughly a mile north of the former Dogpatch USA site at Marble Falls, between Harrison and Jasper. The caves themselves are invisible from the air; use Highway 7 as it threads through the steep, wooded hills of Mill Creek canyon as your reference. A pass at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL keeps a comfortable margin over the surrounding ridges. Boone County Airport (KHRO) at Harrison is the nearest airport with services, a short hop to the north. Valley fog can linger on cool mornings, and even in their open years the caves closed to tours during the January flooding season.
