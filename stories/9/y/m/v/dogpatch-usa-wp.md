# Dogpatch USA

A real estate agent looked at an Ozark trout farm and saw Li'l Abner's hometown, and for 25 years the comic-strip park rode the rise and fall of America's hillbilly craze.

**Location:** 36.1068, -93.1322

**Categories:** Defunct amusement parks in the United States, Amusement parks in Arkansas, Li'l Abner, Buildings and structures in Newton County, Arkansas, History of Arkansas

## Introduction

In 1966 Albert Raney Sr. listed his family's Ozark trout farm with O. J. Snow, a real estate agent in Harrison, Arkansas. Snow walked the property and saw something nobody had put on the listing: a comic strip. The canyon at the foot of a 55-foot waterfall could be the "bottomless canyon" from Li'l Abner. The Raneys' nearby show cave, Mystic Caverns, could become the hideout where shady characters brewed Kickapoo Joy Juice. Two years later, the farmland along Highway 7 between Harrison and Jasper had become Dogpatch USA, and the local post office had changed its name to match.

## Selling the Dream to Al Capp

Snow and nine or ten Harrison businessmen formed Recreation Enterprises to build the park, but first they needed the blessing of Al Capp, Li'l Abner's creator. Snow mailed him home movies of the land and promised a quiet, dignified park of craft shops, trail rides, paddle boats and costumed characters, with no roller coasters to spoil the rustic mood. Capp had turned down other theme-park offers. He accepted this one, became a partner, and claimed he had once driven through the Ozarks and pictured just such a place as Dogpatch. Not everyone cheered. Some state officials and residents feared the park would reinforce hillbilly stereotypes that Arkansas was already struggling to shed, and two visiting economists doubted it could succeed where other Disneyland imitators had failed. Harrison's Chamber of Commerce approved an 825-acre plan anyway, and Capp and his wife attended the groundbreaking on October 3, 1967.

## Opening Day in Dogpatch

Builders gave the fictional town real bones. Jim Schermerhorn, a board member and experienced caver, renovated Mystic Caverns, and while he was driving a bulldozer he broke into a second, pristine cave next door, which he sealed to keep it untouched. Genuine 19th-century log cabins were dismantled elsewhere in the Ozarks and rebuilt in the park, and a watermill that had run on the property around 1834 was restored to working order. On May 17, 1968, about 8,000 visitors streamed in. Capp unveiled the centerpiece, a giant statue of the town hero Jubilation T. Cornpone, before a crowd of about 2,000. Visitors could fish the overstocked trout pond, pay only for what they caught, and have the park restaurant cook it. Organizers predicted a million visitors a year within a decade, but the first season drew 300,000, and estimates for later years vary wildly, from no more than 200,000 to nearly a million a year.

## Riding the Hillbilly Wave

The timing looked perfect. Arkansas businessman Jess Odom bought the original partners' shares for $750,000 and took control in October 1968, and former governor Orval Faubus served as the park's president. In 1969 The Beverly Hillbillies, Green Acres and Petticoat Junction were popular on television, and Li'l Abner ran in more than 700 newspapers every day. Odom expanded boldly. A Swiss-built funicular costing $250,000 began lowering up to 1,700 guests an hour from the parking lot into the valley, narrating the park as they descended. In 1972 he opened Marble Falls, a sister resort billed as the first ski area in Arkansas, relying on snow machines to cover its slopes.

## When the Snow Would Not Come

The 1970s turned on him. Interest rates soared in 1973, an energy crisis kept tourists home, and the networks cancelled many of their country-themed shows in what became known as the Rural Purge. A Li'l Abner television pilot flopped. Mild winters left the ski slopes idle, and Odom, already $2 million in debt, borrowed another $1.5 million before closing Marble Falls' slopes for good. Capp's retirement ended the comic strip, the park's best advertisement, and after the heat wave of 1980 capped a second straight year of poor income, Dogpatch USA filed for bankruptcy. New managers fought back with bargain prices, concerts by Reba McEntire and Ike and Tina Turner, and Dukes of Hazzard actor Denver Pyle as spokesman. In 1986 Money magazine found a family of four paid $34 at Dogpatch versus $84 at Disney World. But one general manager lamented that most children no longer knew who Li'l Abner was. The owners eventually dropped the characters to save on licensing, and the park closed in 1993.

## Ruins, Revivals and a Quieter Future

On December 20, 1994, the park went up for auction on the courthouse steps in Jasper, overseen by a Harrison lawyer who had once played Earthquake McGoon there and whose wife had played Daisy Mae there from 1968 through 1970. In 1997 residents successfully petitioned to change the post office's name back to Marble Falls. Owners came and went. A 2014 buyer reopened the grounds briefly and drew more than 5,000 curious visitors in a single weekend, 21 years after the gates had closed, but his plans ended in foreclosure. In 2020 Johnny Morris, founder of Bass Pro Shops, bought the property for $1.12 million, and an announcement from his company described its next chapter as "an ode to the heritage of the Ozarks and the abundant wildlife and natural beauty found here." His team has since cleared debris and dilapidated structures and begun extensive earthwork, but as of the latest reports the grounds remain closed to the public and no opening date has been announced.

## Flight Context

The former Dogpatch USA site lies at about 36.11°N, 93.13°W in Newton County, Arkansas, in the Mill Creek valley at Marble Falls along Arkansas Highway 7, roughly midway between Harrison to the north and Jasper to the south. Highway 7 winding through steep, forested Ozark ridges is the best visual guide; the site sits in a narrow hollow that is easy to miss at altitude. Try 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL and stay well above the ridgelines, which rise sharply here. Boone County Airport (KHRO) at Harrison is the closest airport with services, a few minutes' flight to the north. The Buffalo National River corridor lies just to the south. Low clouds and morning fog can fill these valleys, so afternoon visibility is often better.
