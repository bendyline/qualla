# Marmaduke–Walker duel

On September 6, 1863, with a Union army closing on Little Rock, Confederate generals John S. Marmaduke and Lucius M. Walker settled a quarrel over courage with Colt Navy revolvers at fifteen paces, and Walker died of his wound the next day.

**Location:** 34.7536, -92.2186

**Categories:** Duels in the United States, Arkansas in the American Civil War, 1863 in Arkansas, Military history of North Little Rock, Arkansas, September 1863

## Introduction

The terms read like a contract. Six-shot Colt Navy revolvers. Fifteen paces, a short distance for those weapons. The firing would go on until five minutes had passed, every loaded round had been fired, or someone was hit. Each man would bring a second, another friend and two doctors. Dueling was illegal in Arkansas, and a Union army was bearing down on Little Rock, but on the morning of September 6, 1863, on a farm north of the Arkansas River, two Confederate generals met to settle a question of honor anyway.

## Two West Pointers

John S. Marmaduke, born near Arrow Rock, Missouri, in 1833, had studied at Yale and Harvard before graduating from West Point in 1857. Wounded at Shiloh, he was a brigadier general by late 1862 and fought at Prairie Grove and Helena. Lucius M. Walker, born in Columbia, Tennessee, in 1829 and a relative of President James K. Polk, had graduated from West Point in 1850 but left the Army for business in Memphis. After a falling-out with Braxton Bragg, who did not trust his generalship, Walker was sent west to the Trans-Mississippi Department in 1863. Their trouble began at the Battle of Helena on July 4. Walker's brigade was supposed to guard Marmaduke's left flank, but when Marmaduke's men were pinned by enfilading fire, Walker, worried about his own flank, did not move to help. An angry Marmaduke then failed to tell Walker when the Confederates retreated, and many of Walker's men were nearly captured.

## "The Damned Coward"

As Union Major General Frederick Steele advanced on Little Rock that summer, Major General Sterling Price put Marmaduke under Walker, who outranked him on the seniority list. It went badly. During the retreat after a fight near Brownsville on August 25, the two agreed that Marmaduke would halt and lure the Union troops into some woods where Walker would strike, but Walker kept retreating and Marmaduke was nearly captured. At Bayou Meto two days later, Marmaduke asked Walker to come to the front; Walker neither came nor answered. Marmaduke requested a transfer, and Price granted it. Then one of Marmaduke's aides reportedly said at Walker's headquarters that Marmaduke claimed he had "won the fight" and that Walker "had acted the damned coward." Another aide denied it, but Walker believed it. Notes passed between them through friends, John C. Moore for Marmaduke and Robert H. Crockett, a grandson of Davy Crockett, for Walker. Marmaduke denied using the word "coward" but wrote that Walker had shown "somewhat more than prudent care in the avoidance of all positions of danger."

## Fifteen Paces

Sources disagree over whether Walker proposed the duel or whether Crockett and Moore arranged it without first consulting the generals. Historian Dick Steward suggests the close distance was chosen because Marmaduke was nearsighted. The night before, Price learned of the plan and ordered both men to stay at their headquarters. Walker never received the order, and Marmaduke ignored it. That morning, at a farm 7 miles from Little Rock, both first shots missed. Marmaduke's second shot struck Walker, whose own pistol went off as he fell. Crockett knelt beside his friend, who told him he was dying. The bullet had hit Walker's right kidney and lodged in his spine, paralyzing him from the waist down. A doctor pronounced the wound mortal, and Walker was carried back to the city in an ambulance Marmaduke had brought. He died the next day.

## A City Lost, a Career Saved

Price arrested Marmaduke for murder and arrested both seconds, but with the campaign under way and Marmaduke's officers pleading for him, the arrest was canceled and he was given command of Price's cavalry. He was never tried for the killing. The duel damaged Confederate morale and soured relations between Marmaduke and Price, and it did nothing to save Little Rock. Within days Union cavalry crossed the river, Marmaduke fought a delaying action at Bayou Fourche while Price evacuated, and on September 10 the city's civilian government surrendered. Marmaduke was captured at Mine Creek in 1864, was promoted to major general while a prisoner, and after the war was elected Governor of Missouri in 1884, serving until his death in 1887. Historian Diana Sherwood called the encounter the "last duel of note" in Arkansas. Its full details emerged only when Crockett published his account in the late 1880s, and in 2015 a historical marker recalling the duel was placed in Jacksonville, on the Reed's Bridge battlefield near Bayou Meto.

## Flight Context

The duel site, as it is usually mapped (sources vary on the exact farm), lies at about 34.754°N, 92.219°W on the north side of the Arkansas River east of downtown Little Rock, directly across the river from Bill and Hillary Clinton National Airport/Adams Field (KLIT), about 1.5 nm north of the field. The site is inside Little Rock's Class C airspace, just across the river from the runways, so contact approach control and expect airline traffic; low-altitude sightseeing here is not practical. North Little Rock Municipal (FAA identifier ORK, GPS code KORK; no ICAO code listed) lies about 5 nm to the north-northwest. The river bend and airport runways are the obvious references. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common.
