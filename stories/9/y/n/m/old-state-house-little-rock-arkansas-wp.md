# Old State House (Little Rock, Arkansas)

The oldest surviving state capitol west of the Mississippi has seen a fatal knife fight on the House floor, a secession vote with one defiant holdout, a small war over the governorship and a future president's victory nights.

**Location:** 34.7490, -92.2726

**Categories:** Former state capitols in the United States, Greek Revival architecture in Little Rock, Arkansas, American Civil War sites in Arkansas, Brooks–Baxter War, National Historic Landmarks in Arkansas, Museums in Little Rock, Arkansas

## Introduction

On December 4, 1837, during an early session of the Arkansas General Assembly, the Speaker of the House, John Wilson, killed Representative Joseph J. Anthony in a knife fight on the floor of the chamber. The building where it happened was not even finished. Lawmakers had moved into Little Rock's new State House while construction was still underway, and they would keep working around the builders until 1842. That unfinished hall, now called the Old State House, is the oldest surviving state capitol building west of the Mississippi River, and its first bloodshed was far from its last drama.

## Greek Temple on a Territorial Budget

Governor John Pope commissioned the State House, and construction ran from 1833 to 1842. The architect, Gideon Shryock, had already designed the Kentucky State Capitol in Frankfort, and he chose the Greek Revival style for Arkansas as well. His original design proved too expensive for the territory. Shryock's assistant, George Weigart, revised the plans and oversaw the actual construction, so the building that rose near the Arkansas River was a revised version of the architect's design. It served as the capitol of Arkansas from statehood until 1911, when government offices moved to the present State Capitol. In those decades it hosted both the convention that took Arkansas out of the Union and the one that later wrote voting rights for freedmen and public education into the state's constitution.

## The Holdout

Secession came to Arkansas in two votes. At an initial convention in March 1861, after Abraham Lincoln's election, Unionists prevailed. Then South Carolina fired on Fort Sumter and Lincoln called up troops, and on the morning of May 6, 1861, a second secession convention assembled in the State House. After intense debate, an ordinance of secession passed with five delegates opposed. When the others urged unanimity, four of the five gave way. Isaac Murphy, a delegate from Madison County, would not. He held out under enormous pressure, a single dissenting vote recorded in a hall full of men determined to leave the Union. The war reached the building itself two years later. After the Union victory at the Battle of Bayou Fourche in September 1863, Federal troops occupied the State House for the rest of the war.

## A Constitution, a Cannon and Three Graces

Peace brought a different kind of fight. Another constitutional convention met in the State House to decide whether Arkansas would accept the Fourteenth Amendment, allow Black men over 21 to vote, and create public schools for both Black and white children. After contentious debate the proposals were approved in a new constitution that emerged in February 1868 and was ratified in March. During Reconstruction the building also became an object of contention in the so-called Brooks–Baxter War, and it was fortified. A cannon from that conflict, known as "Lady Baxter," has been displayed on the grounds. Later decorations were gentler. A three-tiered fountain shown at the 1876 Philadelphia Centennial Exposition was placed on the grounds in 1877, and a recast of it stands there today. In 1885 an iron statue of the Three Graces, representing Law, Justice and Mercy, was set atop the building; it came down in 1928.

## From Capitol to Campaign Stage

After the government moved out, the old building found new work. For a time it housed a medical school. Later it served as an Arkansas war memorial, as offices for federal and state agencies, and as a meeting place for patriotic organizations. In 1947 the General Assembly designated it a museum. The State House had one more political act to play, though. On October 3, 1991, Bill Clinton announced his presidential campaign from its front entrance, and he returned to the same spot for his election night celebrations on November 3, 1992, and November 5, 1996. A major renovation followed in 1996, and in 1997 the building was designated a National Historic Landmark. Today its collections include battle flags, the inaugural gowns of Arkansas's First Ladies, art pottery and African American quilts.

## Flight Context

The Old State House stands at 34.7490°N, 92.2726°W on the south bank of the Arkansas River in downtown Little Rock. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL, look for the Greek Revival building and its grounds just south of the river near the downtown bridges; the modern State Capitol, its successor, lies to the west. Bill & Hillary Clinton National Airport/Adams Field (KLIT) is about 2.7 nm to the southeast; expect airline traffic and coordinate with approach control. North Little Rock Municipal Airport (FAA identifier ORK; no ICAO code listed) is about 5 nm to the north. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common, while clear winter days give the best views of the river.
