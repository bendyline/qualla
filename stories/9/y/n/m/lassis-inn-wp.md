# Lassis Inn

Founded around 1905, Little Rock's Lassis Inn fed the city fried catfish and buffalo fish ribs for more than a century and gave civil rights leaders a safe place to plan.

**Location:** 34.7228, -92.2684

## Introduction

The ribs at Lassis Inn came from a fish. Buffalo fish is bony and white-fleshed, and cooks tend to pass it over because of its many small bones, but the rib section is free of them. Butchered with enough flesh on either side, it resembles a rack of spareribs, and fried it comes out, in Southern Living's words, "crunchy, hot, and meaty." For generations, that improbable dish and a plate of fried catfish drew people to a modest building on East 27th Street in Little Rock, Arkansas. Some came for the food. In the 1950s and 1960s, some came to plan the fight against segregation.

## Sandwiches from the Watson Home

Joe and Molassis Watson founded the business around 1905, making it one of the oldest restaurants in Arkansas. It began humbly, with sandwiches sold from the couple's home. As customers kept coming, the Watsons added fried catfish to the menu and put up a separate structure for the carryout trade. The name came from Molassis herself. The couple had apparently meant to call the place the Watson Inn, but they settled on a shortened form of her first name because they thought it sounded better. In 1931 the Watsons moved both building and business to East 27th Street. In the 1960s it shifted once more, only slightly, to make way for the construction of Interstate 30 near Roosevelt Road.

## A Safe Haven on 27th Street

In the 1950s and 1960s, Lassis Inn became a meeting place for Little Rock's civil rights leaders. Daisy Bates was among them, and in these rooms activists planned efforts that included the desegregation of Little Rock Central High School. The restaurant was considered a "safe haven" for them. It is worth pausing on what that phrase meant in the segregated South. People organizing to challenge the city's schools needed somewhere they could talk freely, among people they could trust, and a Black-owned catfish restaurant gave them that. The plates of fish on the tables were ordinary. The conversations over them were anything but.

## Catfish, Buffalo and Hushpuppies

The menu stayed focused. Lassis Inn specialized in catfish, served both fried and as catfish steaks, along with its signature "fish ribs" and hushpuppies. The ribs came from buffalo fish, a local specialty, and they turned an overlooked species into the dish people remembered. Buffalo fish is not a preferred fish because of its many small bones, and the rib is the only part of the fish that is free of them. Cutting it so that the bone carries meat on each side creates something shaped like a sparerib. Arkansas Online called the restaurant's catfish and buffalo "some of the state's best fried fish," and it is easy to see how a dish built on thrift and skill became a destination.

## Honors and an Uncertain Door

Recognition arrived late in the restaurant's long life. In 2017 Lassis Inn was one of three inaugural inductees into the Arkansas Food Hall of Fame, alongside Rhoda's Famous Hot Tamales and Jones Bar-B-Q Diner. In 2020 the James Beard Foundation named it an America's Classic, and in 2022 Southern Living included it among the South's Best Soul Food. Elihue Washington Jr. and Maria Washington had bought the business in 1989. By May 2024, though, the restaurant had closed while Washington received medical treatment. A sale announced that summer ended in a lawsuit, which was settled in Washington's favor in August 2024. After that settlement the restaurant's future remained uncertain.
