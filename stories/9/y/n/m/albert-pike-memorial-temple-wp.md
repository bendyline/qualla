# Albert Pike Memorial Temple

Behind nineteen forty-foot limestone columns on Scott Street, Little Rock's Scottish Rite Masons built a temple for ceremony, and their predecessors are remembered as pioneers of staging Masonic degrees as drama.

**Location:** 34.7417, -92.2706

**Categories:** Freemasonry, Neoclassical architecture in Arkansas, Buildings and structures in Little Rock, Arkansas, National Register of Historic Places in Little Rock, Arkansas, Buildings and structures completed in 1924

## Introduction

In 2015 the local company Opera in the Rock went looking for a place to stage Mozart's The Magic Flute, and it settled on a building where ceremony had long been performed under stage lights. Behind a colonnade that fills a whole city block of Scott Street in downtown Little Rock, the Scottish Rite Masons of the Valley of Little Rock are known as the first in their Southern Jurisdiction to use lights, props, theater drops and robes, and to perform their degrees as drama. The Albert Pike Memorial Temple has been called "The Jewel of the Southern Jurisdiction," and it looks the part.

## A City Block of Indiana Limestone

The temple announces itself at 700–724 Scott Street with sheer mass. It is a Classical Revival structure of 156,000 square feet, three stories tall with a basement below and a rigging loft above, finished in pale Indiana limestone. Nineteen Ionic columns, each forty feet high, march the length of the front facade. Visitors pass through massive bronze doors flanked by cast concrete double-headed eagles, the emblem of the Scottish Rite. Completed in 1924, the building was designed by George R. Mann and Eugene Stern, local Masons who are best known as the architects of the Arkansas Capitol Building. It was not the first Masonic home on this ground. The present temple is the third consistory building raised on the site, which gives the colonnade a sense of continuity as well as grandeur.

## The Name Above the Door

The temple honors Albert Pike, a man who packed several careers into one life. He was an author, poet, orator, editor, lawyer and jurist, and a Confederate general who commanded the District of Indian Territory. Within Freemasonry his influence was enormous: from 1859 until his death in 1891 he served as Sovereign Grand Commander of the Supreme Council of the Scottish Rite, Southern Jurisdiction. The man who saw the Little Rock temple built was Charles E. Rosenbaum, a Sovereign Grand Inspector General and Lieutenant Grand Commander of the Rite. Rosenbaum is reported to have been known for another landmark as well, having chaired the building committee for the Scottish Rite's House of the Temple in Washington, D.C.

## Ritual as Theater

The rigging loft is the clue to what happens inside. The Little Rock bodies are known as the first in the Southern Jurisdiction to present them as staged drama, complete with lighting, props, painted theater drops and robes. They were also the first to institute a "Camp Guard" for degree and ceremonial work. Arkansas holds another distinction as one of five states to originally have a Grand Consistory. The temple remains the only building in Arkansas, and one of very few in the country, that regularly confers the 33rd degree and performs the Investiture of the Knight Commander of the Court of Honor. Offices and meeting rooms for much of the state's Masonic fraternity fill the building, from the Grand Lodge of Arkansas to Western Star Lodge No. 2 and Albert Pike Lodge No. 714.

## Fire, Then an Open Door

In 1952 a near-catastrophic fire broke out in the oldest part of the building and burned it. Afterward the building was "modernized," with new climate control systems and updated facilities installed inside the historic shell. The temple was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1986. For most of its life the building served its members, but in 2014 it opened to the public for rentals, and the idea proved a success. A year later Opera in the Rock brought The Magic Flute into the temple, filling a house built for Masonic ceremony with Mozart. The columns on Scott Street have not changed since 1924. What happens behind them has grown a little more public.

## Flight Context

Albert Pike Memorial Temple stands at 34.7417°N, 92.2706°W on Scott Street in downtown Little Rock, a few blocks south of the Arkansas River. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL the long pale limestone colonnade reads as a full city block of white stone amid the downtown grid. Bill & Hillary Clinton National Airport/Adams Field (KLIT) lies about 2.4 nm to the east-southeast, so expect busy traffic and coordinate with Little Rock approach. North Little Rock Municipal Airport (FAA identifier ORK; no ICAO code listed) is about 5.5 nm north across the river. Summer thunderstorms and haze are common; clear winter days give the sharpest views.
