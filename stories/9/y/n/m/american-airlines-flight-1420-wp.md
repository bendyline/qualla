# American Airlines Flight 1420

On a stormy June night in 1999, an MD-82 from Dallas/Fort Worth ran off the end of Little Rock's Runway 4R, and eleven people who had boarded a routine short flight never came home.

**Location:** 34.7363, -92.1995

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in 1999, Airliner accidents and incidents in Arkansas, Airliner accidents and incidents caused by weather, Airliner accidents and incidents involving runway overruns, American Airlines accidents and incidents, 1999 in Arkansas

## Introduction

Every holiday season from 2000 to 2022, a house in Vallejo, California, disappeared under Christmas lights and decorations. The display drew attention from local and national media, and the man behind it had a reason for every string of lights. His daughter, Debra Taylor-Sattari, was one of eleven people who died after American Airlines Flight 1420 overran the runway at Little Rock on the night of June 1, 1999. Her father's annual tribute kept one of those eleven lives in public view for more than two decades after a short evening flight from Dallas/Fort Worth ended in the dark beyond the runway.

## Racing the Storm

Flight 1420 was a McDonnell Douglas MD-82, a member of the MD-80 series descended from the DC-9. The evening started late. The plane originally scheduled for the trip arrived late at Dallas/Fort Worth, so the airline substituted another MD-80, registration N215AA, and the flight finally departed at 10:40 p.m. with 145 people aboard. Captain Richard Buschmann, 48, was an experienced pilot, and First Officer Michael Origel was 35. By the time they neared Little Rock National Airport, today named Bill & Hillary Clinton National Airport, severe thunderstorms had moved into the area. Winds at the field were reported from 330 degrees at 25 knots, beyond the MD-82's 20-knot crosswind limit for landing in reduced visibility on a wet runway. The crew continued toward Runway 4R.

## Beyond the Runway's End

The airliner touched down shortly before midnight, at 11:50 p.m. local time, but the spoilers did not deploy automatically, and the brakes proved ineffective. The MD-82 kept rolling past the end of the runway, through a security fence and an ILS localizer array, and then struck a structure built to hold the approach lights for Runway 22L. The captain and eight passengers died at the scene. Two more passengers died in the hospital in the weeks that followed. First Officer Origel, three of the four flight attendants and 41 passengers were seriously injured, while the remaining flight attendant and 64 passengers had minor injuries. Twenty-four passengers escaped without injury.

## What the Investigators Found

The National Transportation Safety Board placed the probable cause on two failures: the flight crew's failure to discontinue the approach when severe thunderstorms and their hazards had moved into the airport area, and the crew's failure to ensure that the spoilers had extended after touchdown. Investigators also looked hard at how storms shape pilots' judgment on approach, drawing on a study by experts from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology that recorded the behavior of pilots landing at Dallas/Fort Worth. Fatigue entered the report too. The NTSB cited it as a contributing factor, noting that the captain had been awake for 16 hours that day.

## Verdicts and Remembrance

American Airlines admitted liability for the crash, and individual trials were scheduled to decide compensatory damages. The airline then reached settlements with most of the domestic plaintiffs, who gave up their punitive damages claims as part of those agreements. The only liability trial arising from the crash was brought by Captain Buschmann's family against Little Rock National Airport. In 2005 a federal jury in Little Rock awarded the family more than $2 million in wrongful-death damages, faulting the airport and a runway that did not fully meet safety guidelines; the NTSB stood by its own finding that the crew's actions were a probable cause. The story reached television in the Canadian series Mayday, in a 2003 episode titled "Racing the Storm," as well as on The Weather Channel's Storm Stories and the Biography Channel's Flightmares. American still flies the Dallas/Fort Worth to Little Rock route, usually with Embraer E175 jets operated by its subsidiary Envoy Air.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies at 34.7363°N, 92.1995°W, beyond the northeast end of Runway 4R/22L at Bill & Hillary Clinton National Airport/Adams Field (KLIT), the field formerly called Little Rock National Airport. This is active airspace for an air carrier airport, so view it only as ATC permits, for example from the traffic pattern at 1,000–1,500 ft AGL. Downtown Little Rock lies about 4.5 nm to the west. Other nearby fields include Little Rock Air Force Base (KLRF), about 11 nm to the north-northeast, and North Little Rock Municipal Airport (FAA identifier ORK; no ICAO code listed). Late spring and summer thunderstorms can build quickly here, the same hazard that brought down Flight 1420.
