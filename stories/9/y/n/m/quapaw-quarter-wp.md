# Quapaw Quarter

Little Rock's Quapaw Quarter gathers many of the city's oldest homes and streets into as many as fifteen historic districts, where antebellum mansions, a general's birthplace and a sitcom's favorite facades share the same shady blocks.

**Location:** 34.7344, -92.2730

## Introduction

On the CBS sitcom Designing Women, its exterior stood in for Sugarbaker Designs, the fictional Atlanta interior design firm where the show was set. The real house, the Villa Marre, stands on Scott Street in Little Rock, Arkansas, and it once held the offices of the Quapaw Quarter Association, the chief sponsor of historic preservation in the neighborhood around it. Some of Little Rock's oldest streets have a habit of playing other roles while quietly remaining themselves.

## A Name From 1961

The Quapaw Quarter takes in some of the city's oldest and most historic business and residential neighborhoods, but its name is relatively new. It was first given in 1961, honoring the Quapaw people who lived in the area centuries ago. As many as fifteen separate historic districts make up the Quarter, and more than 200 of its homes and buildings are listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Trapnall Hall, on East Capitol Avenue, was among the first of these homes, built in 1843 for early state legislator Frederic Trapnall and his wife, Martha. Antebellum and Victorian houses, large and small, stand throughout the Quarter, along with Craftsman-style homes. Scott, Center and Spring streets hold especially rich concentrations, while commercial buildings line Main Street and Broadway south of Interstate 630.

## The Governor's Block

Designing Women borrowed a second Quapaw Quarter landmark. The front exterior of the Arkansas Governor's Mansion also appeared on the show. The real mansion and its grounds fill an entire city block, splitting Center Street in the 1800 block, and anchor the Governor's Mansion Historic District along lower Broadway. It was the first official residence of Arkansas's governors, and when it opened on January 10, 1950, the state threw a week-long open house for all Arkansans. The Georgian Colonial Revival home was renovated under Governor Mike Huckabee beginning in 2000, and a new Grand Hall and glass atrium opened in the spring of 2003.

## A General's Birthplace and SoMa

Other districts carry their own stories. The MacArthur Park Historic District, listed on the National Register in 1977, adjoins MacArthur Park along East 9th Street and takes in the Arkansas Museum of Fine Arts, formerly the Arkansas Arts Center, and the Tower Building of the Little Rock Arsenal, which dates from about 1840. That building contains the birthplace of General Douglas MacArthur, one of the foremost American commanders in the Pacific during World War II. Farther south, the South Main Residential Historic District runs along South Main Street between 19th and 24th streets. Added to the National Register in 2007, and nicknamed SoMa by some locals, it is known for its quality late-19th and early-20th-century houses in Queen Anne, Craftsman and Colonial Revival styles.

## Graves, Newsprint and Bread

The Quarter holds its past close. Mount Holly Cemetery, at 12th and Broadway, dates from 1843 and keeps the graves of many notable Arkansans, from governors, senators and mayors to Confederate spy David Owen Dodd and William E. Woodruff, founder of the Arkansas Gazette. That newspaper's descendant lives on at the Quarter's edge. The Arkansas Democrat-Gazette, the state's newspaper of record, traces its roots to the Gazette of 1819 and the Arkansas Democrat of 1878, and it is headquartered in a building from around 1904 at East Capitol Avenue and Scott Street that was built for the YMCA. Daily life goes on around the history, too, with shops such as the Boulevard Bread Company outlet on Main Street.
