# Hoga (YT-146)

A small Navy harbor tug that fought fires for 72 hours at Pearl Harbor and later spent four decades as an Oakland fireboat, Hoga now rests at the Arkansas Inland Maritime Museum in North Little Rock.

**Location:** 34.7523, -92.2669

**Categories:** Ships present during the attack on Pearl Harbor, Museum ships in Arkansas, Fireboats of California, National Historic Landmarks in Arkansas, Tourist attractions in North Little Rock, Arkansas

## Introduction

Her name is the Sioux word for "fish," and she has ended up, improbably, on the Arkansas River in North Little Rock, a long way from salt water. Hoga is a small Navy harbor tug, built to push cargo lighters and nudge ships into their berths. On the morning of December 7, 1941, she was tied up in Pearl Harbor when the Japanese attack began, and for the next three days her crew fought fire almost without stopping.

## A Tug for the Yard

Hoga was authorized on June 18, 1940, and built by the Consolidated Shipbuilding Corporation in Morris Heights, New York, where her keel was laid that July. She was launched on the last day of 1940 and placed in service at Norfolk, Virginia, on May 22, 1941. Assigned to the 14th Naval District at Pearl Harbor, she made the trip by way of the Panama Canal, San Diego and San Pedro. At Pearl she was berthed at the Yard Craft Dock and did a harbor tug's ordinary work, moving cargo lighters and helping ships in and out of their berths. Like other yard tugs, she also carried firefighting equipment, a detail that would soon matter. On the morning of the attack she was moored with other yard craft near the drydocks at 1010 Dock.

## Battleship Row

Hoga was underway within ten minutes of the first strike. Steaming into the harbor, she pulled two men from the water and headed for the burning ships along Battleship Row, where USS Arizona lay shattered. At 8:30 she helped pull the damaged repair ship USS Vestal away from Arizona, then went to aid the minelayer USS Oglala, which had to be towed to clear the field of fire for the cruiser USS Helena. As she reached Oglala at 8:50, the battleship USS Nevada steamed past, making a run for the open sea. She never reached it. Hit by a torpedo and then by waves of bombs, she was sinking by 9:10, and if she went down in the channel she could bottle up the harbor. Rear Admiral William Rea Furlong sent the two tugs working on Oglala to help. Hoga and YT-130 nosed Nevada toward Hospital Point and moved her to the western side of the harbor entrance, where she settled by 10:45. Tied to the battleship's port bow, Hoga fought a raging forecastle fire with her pilothouse monitor and four hose lines for more than an hour.

## Seventy-Two Hours of Fire

Then Hoga went back to Battleship Row and fought fires on USS Maryland, USS Tennessee and finally Arizona, working the Arizona fire from 4 p.m. on Sunday until 1 p.m. on Tuesday, December 9. After 72 continuous hours of firefighting, she stayed on duty through the rest of the week, patrolling the harbor, helping to recover the bodies of the dead, and searching for Japanese submarines believed to be hiding in the harbor. In February 1942 Admiral Chester Nimitz commended her skipper, McManus, his men and their tug; the commendation recalled that when another ship was disabled and burning, "you moored your tug to her bow and assisted materially in extinguishing the fires." Through the rest of the war Hoga helped clear debris and salvage sunken and damaged ships as Pearl Harbor became a springboard for the Pacific campaign.

## Forty Years on the Oakland Waterfront

In 1948 the Navy transferred Hoga to Oakland, California, where a $73,000 refit turned her into the fireboat Port of Oakland, later City of Oakland. The day after her commissioning she was already fighting a shipboard fire on the freighter Hawaiian Rancher. Over some forty years she battled ship and waterfront fires and pulled people from the water, and on July 3, 1980, she carried President Jimmy Carter on a 35-minute tour of the port, during which he playfully aimed her bow monitor at the press boat. In 1984 she answered the burning tanker SS Puerto Rican in rough seas outside the Golden Gate. As big wooden piers and warehouses disappeared and shipboard fire control improved, the port began to consider a smaller, more maneuverable boat.

## Bookends on the Arkansas

Hoga was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1989, and in 1994 Oakland returned her to the Navy, which moved her to the Suisun Bay Reserve Fleet. After several years on offer for donation, the Navy chose the City of North Little Rock over four other applicants, and ownership passed to the city in 2005. She stayed at Suisun Bay until 2012, when she went to the Mare Island drydocks at Vallejo to be made seaworthy for the long trip east. She arrived at the Arkansas Inland Maritime Museum on November 23, 2015, joining the submarine USS Razorback, present in Tokyo Bay at Japan's surrender. Together they bookend America's war. Of the vessels that took part in the Pearl Harbor attack that morning, the few survivors are museum ships, among them Hoga and the Coast Guard cutter once called Taney, which fired on the attackers from her berth in Honolulu and now sits in Baltimore's Inner Harbor.

## Flight Context

Hoga is moored at the Arkansas Inland Maritime Museum on the north bank of the Arkansas River in North Little Rock at 34.752°N, 92.267°W, across the river from downtown Little Rock and near the submarine USS Razorback. From 1,500-2,500 ft AGL the river bridges and downtown skyline make the location easy to find, though the tug herself is small. The site is only about 2.5 nm northwest of Bill and Hillary Clinton National Airport/Adams Field (KLIT) and within Little Rock's Class C airspace, so contact approach control. North Little Rock Municipal (FAA identifier ORK, GPS code KORK; no ICAO code listed) lies about 5 nm north. Expect summer haze and thunderstorms.
