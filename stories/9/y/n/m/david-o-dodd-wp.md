# David O. Dodd

A 17-year-old caught carrying notes on Union troop positions was hanged in occupied Little Rock in January 1864, and later generations turned the dark-haired boy into a golden-haired saint in stained glass.

**Location:** 34.7376, -92.2785

**Categories:** American Civil War, Civil War in Arkansas, Burials at Mount Holly Cemetery, History of Little Rock, Arkansas, People executed for espionage

## Introduction

In a stained-glass window commissioned in his memory, David Owen Dodd has curly blond hair and the bearing of a saint. The only known photograph of him shows a boy with straight black hair. Somewhere between the two lies the real teenager, born in 1846, who rode a mule into Union-held Little Rock at Christmas 1863 and was hanged there as a spy two weeks later, at 17. He was buried in Mount Holly Cemetery, and he has been remembered ever since as the "Arkansas Boy Martyr of the Confederacy."

## A Boy Sent on Business

Dodd's parents were Baptists who had married in a village south of Little Rock and moved to Texas with their first daughter, Senhora. David was born there, as were his sisters Leonora and Ann Eliza; Ann Eliza died before the Civil War. By late 1863 the family was at Camden, Arkansas, and David's father, Andrew Dodd, had a plan to buy tobacco and store it until it could be sold for a higher price. On December 24, 1863, he sent David to Little Rock to deliver letters to former associates, seeking investment for the deal. His age mattered: as a minor, the boy was assumed to be neutral. He carried a pass issued by either Confederate General James F. Fagan or Colonel William A. Crawford, along with a birth certificate showing he was 17.

## The Notebook

Dodd delivered his letters in the Union-held city, then set out to rejoin his family at Camden. On the way he mistakenly re-entered Federally held territory. He had no pass for it, and the U.S. soldiers who stopped him found a notebook recording the locations of Federal troops in the area. He was arrested and tried by a military tribunal, and the court-martial lasted four days, with only minimal defense offered for his actions. The verdict was guilty of spying and the sentence was death, and the Union's General Steele set the execution for Friday, January 8, 1864. Dodd never named a source for his information. Legend says General Steele offered to free him in exchange for his informant's name and that Dodd refused, saying he could give his life for his country but could not betray a friend. He had met a teenage girl named Mary Dodge, whose family hosted Union officers, at holiday parties; she and her father were reportedly escorted back to their home in Vermont.

## Five Minutes on the Schoolgrounds

The hanging took place on the grounds of St. John's, Dodd's former school, just east of the Little Rock Arsenal. He stood on the tailgate of a wagon beneath the noose while the executioner, a man named Dekay, fixed the rope around his neck. Then the prop was knocked from under the tailgate. According to legend, the rope stretched, and the boy dangled, strangling for a full five minutes before he died, though the record is unclear. Onlookers, Union soldiers among them, are said to have become ill at the sight. A Little Rock resident donated a grave, and Dodd was buried in the southeast part of Mount Holly Cemetery, the cemetery that also holds many of the state's governors, senators and newspaper founders.

## Martyr in Glass

Dodd's short life became a legend in the decades after the war. The Arkansas Division of the United Daughters of the Confederacy raised money for a stained-glass window in his honor, presented to the Confederate Museum in Richmond, Virginia, in 1911. The Charles F. Hogeman Company of New York City made it, portraying Dodd as a Southern saint and martyr, a golden-curled figure far from the dark-haired teenager of the photograph. The gap between the two images is its own piece of history. A 17-year-old on a family tobacco errand, caught with a notebook of troop positions, was remade in glass as the idealized hero of a defeated cause. His grave still lies in Mount Holly Cemetery in the Quapaw Quarter of Little Rock.

## Flight Context

Dodd's grave lies in Mount Holly Cemetery at 34.7376°N, 92.2785°W, in the Quapaw Quarter just south of downtown Little Rock at 12th and Broadway. Look for the green, tree-covered cemetery block standing out from the surrounding street grid; 1,500–2,000 ft AGL gives a good view. Downtown's towers and the Arkansas River lie a short distance to the north. Bill & Hillary Clinton National Airport/Adams Field (KLIT) is about 3 nm to the east, so expect airline traffic and coordinate with approach control. North Little Rock Municipal Airport (FAA identifier ORK; no ICAO code listed) is across the river to the north. Haze and summer thunderstorms are common; winter offers the clearest air.
