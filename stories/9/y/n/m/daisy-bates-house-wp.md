# Daisy Bates House

A modest brick ranch house south of downtown Little Rock became the command post, refuge and target of the 1957 fight to integrate Central High School.

**Location:** 34.7217, -92.2833

**Categories:** Civil rights movement, Little Rock Nine, African-American history in Little Rock, Arkansas, National Historic Landmarks in Arkansas, Houses in Little Rock, Arkansas

## Introduction

On August 27, 1957, a rock crashed through the front window of a new brick ranch house at 1207 West 28th Street in Little Rock. Tied to it was a note: "STONE THIS TIME. DYNAMITE NEXT." The house belonged to Daisy Bates, president of the Arkansas chapter of the NAACP, and nine Black students were about to try to walk through the doors of Little Rock Central High School. Over the months that followed, this ordinary suburban home served as their meeting place, their shelter and their headquarters, and it drew the hatred of people determined to keep them out.

## A House Like Its Neighbors

Nothing about the building suggests history. It is a typical 1950s ranch house in a mid-century neighborhood south of downtown, one story high and roughly 39 by 51 feet, built of wood frame with a brick veneer. A hip roof with broad eaves also shelters a carport along the north and west sides, and the main entrance to the living room opens from that carport, beside a second door to the kitchen. Two picture windows with sidelights face the street. Inside, a living room, dining room and kitchen give way to a hallway, two bedrooms and two bathrooms, with a partly finished basement recreation room below and a finished attic reached by pulldown stairs. Daisy Bates and her husband, Lucius Christopher Bates, bought the newly built house in 1955.

## The Publisher and the Plan

Daisy Lee Gatson Bates, born in 1914, had been elected president of the Arkansas NAACP in 1952, and she and her husband had founded the Arkansas State Press to advance the civil rights of African Americans in the state. After the Supreme Court's 1954 decision in Brown v. Board of Education required public schools to integrate, Little Rock's superintendent, Virgil Blossom, published a plan in 1955 for integrating the city's schools. The NAACP sued in 1956 because the plan set no detailed timetable, and the case made Mrs. Bates widely known in the city. Under the Blossom Plan, the NAACP selected nine students to enroll at Central High in 1957, the first of Little Rock's schools to be integrated. Their first day was set for September 4.

## Command Post on West 28th Street

Racial tension and legal maneuvering built through the days before school opened. On September 2, Governor Orval Faubus called out the Arkansas National Guard to keep the students from enrolling. Mrs. Bates attended a school board meeting on the students' behalf the next day, then arranged for a group of ministers, white and Black, to escort them to school. On September 4 the Guard turned the nine back as a hostile white crowd surrounded them. The confrontation drew worldwide attention, and reporters seeking what they called the "black perspective" converged on the Bates house. Elizabeth Eckford, whose image among the crowd became especially well known, is reported to have spent that night there under arranged security. On September 23 the students and their parents gathered at the house and went to the school, where they entered under police escort but were taken out through a side door about three and a half hours later, as a crowd of more than 1,000 white protesters gathered outside. They returned to the Bates home. President Dwight D. Eisenhower then federalized the Arkansas National Guard and sent a detachment of the 101st Airborne Division to Little Rock. On September 25, the nine assembled at the house once more, and soldiers escorted them into Central High.

## Crosses in the Yard

Enrollment did not end the danger. Throughout that school year, the nine returned to the Bates house to talk through how they were being treated at Central and how best to respond. The home had formal police protection, and Mr. Bates organized informal security of his own. Even so, crosses were burned in the yard, and shots were fired through the windows from passing cars. After the school year ended, Mrs. Bates was hanged in effigy in Ouachita County. The school board found another way to delay integration by closing all of Little Rock's high schools for the 1958–59 year, and in July 1959, as that year ended, an incendiary bomb was thrown onto the Bates property. When the schools reopened in 1959 with a small number of African American students, the Bates house became their meeting point once again. Today it is a National Historic Landmark.

## Flight Context

The Daisy Bates House stands at 34.7217°N, 92.2833°W in a residential neighborhood south of downtown Little Rock. A single-story ranch house is too small to pick out reliably from altitude, so use the downtown towers and the Arkansas River to the north as references and orbit at 1,500–2,000 ft AGL over the gridded streets south of downtown.
