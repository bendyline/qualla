# Mosaic Templars Cultural Center

On the site of the headquarters of a Black fraternal order founded by two formerly enslaved men in 1883, Little Rock's Mosaic Templars Cultural Center tells the story of African American Arkansas.

**Location:** 34.7407, -92.2769

**Categories:** African-American museums in Arkansas, Museums in Little Rock, Arkansas, Fraternal orders, African-American history of Arkansas, Museums established in 2008

## Introduction

When the Mosaic Templars Cultural Center opened in September 2008, some of its guests had traveled from Barbados. They belonged to the last surviving lodge of the Mosaic Templars of America, an organization born more than a century earlier on the streets of Little Rock. Their presence closed a long loop. The order had once reached as far as the Caribbean, then collapsed, and its old headquarters had burned to the ground. Now a new four-story building stood on the same site, dedicated to the history the Templars had helped to make.

## Insurance Against Hard Times

The Mosaic Templars of America was founded in Little Rock in 1883 by John E. Bush and Chester W. Keatts, two formerly enslaved men. It was a Black fraternal order with a practical purpose. In an era when few basic services were available to Black people, the Templars provided illness, death and burial insurance, protection that many Black families could not find anywhere else. The Great Depression undid it. In the 1930s the Mosaic Templars began to feel the economic collapse, and the order eventually ceased operations everywhere except for a single chapter that still survives in Barbados.

## Headquarters on the Line

The Templars' building stood on West Ninth Street, the heart of Little Rock's African American business district. That stretch, known as the "Line," was the center of Black commerce and culture in the city and also a boundary separating its Black and white communities. During the racist riot that followed the lynching of John Carter in May 1927, a group of white women occupied the steps of the Templars' headquarters while a white mob looted businesses and terrorized the neighborhood. After the order folded, various businesses rented the building into the late twentieth century. Then the neighborhood changed around it. Shifting business conditions, urban renewal and a highway built through the old West Ninth Street district left the building empty and falling apart.

## Fire and a Second Life

Preservationists saw what was at stake. On January 19, 1993, a group of them incorporated the Society for the Preservation of the Mosaic Templars of America Building to lobby against its destruction. The building survived another twelve years, until it burned to the ground on March 16, 2005. The loss did not end the project. A new building rose on the same site, the center was turned over to the state, and the four-story museum opened in September 2008 under the Department of Arkansas Heritage, with members of the Barbados lodge in attendance. What visitors enter today is not the Templars' original headquarters but its successor, built on the same ground to carry the same name forward.

## Four Floors of Memory

The center's mission is to collect, preserve, interpret and celebrate African American history, culture and community in Arkansas from 1870 to the present, with particular attention to Black achievement in business, politics and the arts. The ground floor holds the permanent exhibit, an interactive children's gallery and a gallery for temporary shows. The second floor houses the Bush-Remmel genealogical research center and library, where visitors can trace their own family histories. The Arkansas Black Hall of Fame occupies the third floor, along with an auditorium used for public forums, conferences and performances. In all, the center offers more than 8,000 square feet of interactive exhibit and education space. In 2016 the center hosted a panel on the lynching of John Carter, whose murder in 1927 brought terror to the street outside.
