# Lynching of John Carter

On May 4, 1927, a white mob murdered John Carter outside Little Rock and burned his body in the heart of the city's Black business district, terror that drove Black residents from the city and that no one was ever charged for.

**Location:** 34.7411, -92.2767

**Categories:** Lynching in the United States, Racially motivated violence against African Americans in Arkansas, History of Little Rock, Arkansas, African-American history of Arkansas, 1927 in Arkansas

## Introduction

The Mosaic Templars Cultural Center stands on Little Rock's West Ninth Street, and in 2016 it hosted a panel that included the descendants of a man named John Carter. It is the same street where, on the night of May 4, 1927, a white crowd of perhaps 5,000 people burned his body in the street. Almost nothing about Carter's life survives in the accounts of his death. What is recorded is what was done to him, and how much of the city chose not to speak of it. It has been called "one of the most notorious incidents of racial violence in the state's history."

## A City Primed for Violence

The prelude began on April 30, 1927, when the body of a young white girl was found in the First Presbyterian Church. The church janitor, a man named Dixon, discovered her, and the next day police arrested him and his son, Lonnie Dixon. Fearing unrest, authorities moved the two to the city jail in Texarkana. That night a crowd of thousands gathered at Little Rock's city hall hoping to kill them. On May 1 people searched nearby Arkansas jails looking for the Dixons, and on the night of May 2 crowds were still massing outside the state penitentiary demanding that they be handed over. The tension did not break. It went looking for another target.

## May 4, 1927

On May 4, a Black man named John Carter was accused of assaulting a white woman and her daughter. He was never tried. An armed posse went looking for him and found him on the outskirts of Little Rock. They pistol-whipped him and hanged him from a telephone pole, and then fired at him with rifles; accounts say he was hit more than 200 times. The mob then took down his body and drove it into the city in a caravan of cars, strapped to the front of one of them. For several hours they paraded him through the streets, roped to the back of another car, until they reached West Ninth Street, the heart of Little Rock's African American business district.

## Fire on West Ninth Street

There the crowd, up to 5,000 white people, rioted. They looted flammable materials from businesses and took pews from the nearby Bethel African Methodist Episcopal church, and with them they set Carter's body on fire. The sheriff and his men were present and watched the lynching without intervening. Photographs of the body were sold the next day for fifteen cents each, yet no one was ever charged. The city police did nothing to stop the rioting, reportedly because police chief Burl C. Rotenberry was still out of town after moving the Dixons. Hours passed before the National Guard arrived to end it.

## A Neighborhood Emptied

For the Black residents of the district, the night was a siege. Rioters occupied the entire neighborhood and attacked people at random. Those who could leave fled, and those who could not went into hiding; the Arkansas Gazette reported that all "business houses and residences in the vicinity appeared deserted throughout the night." A group of white women occupied the steps of the headquarters of the Mosaic Templars, the Black fraternal order, while other whites took over the porches of people's homes. Black residents were chased. One man was caught, beaten and tossed from car to car until a group of 20 young men rescued him and brought him to police headquarters, from where an ambulance took him to a hospital. He escaped being burned alive only because a white man promised the rioters action against the Dixons. People in hiding emerged only after soldiers arrived and assured them it was safe, and one businessman refused to go home until a soldier offered to escort him there.

## The Long Silence

Local and state leaders condemned the lynching and the riot, among them John Netherland Heiskell in a front-page editorial in the Arkansas Gazette. The case drew national attention at a time when the Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 had already turned the country's eyes to the South, and Arkansas's image, along with national flood relief, seemed at stake. Unrest lasted for weeks. Black residents fled the city in what was reportedly a mass exodus, and the Little Rock Board of Censors banned two Black newspapers from outside the state, the Pittsburgh Courier and the Chicago Defender. Lonnie Dixon was tried on May 19, quickly sentenced to death and executed. Carter's killing is said to have been the last lynching in Little Rock. Arkansas Times columnist Jay Barth has written that the white community kept the story under wraps, but it was as important to the people of Little Rock as Emmett Till's murder later was in Mississippi, because it instilled fear in the Black citizens of a white-dominated town. Barth has argued that the city should erect a physical monument. In 2021 the Equal Justice Initiative and the Arkansas Peace and Justice Memorial Movement dedicated a historical marker to Carter near the entrance of Haven of Rest Cemetery, where some historians believe he was buried in an unmarked grave.

## Flight Context

The West Ninth Street district where Carter's body was burned lies at approximately 34.7411°N, 92.2767°W, on the south side of downtown Little Rock near the Mosaic Templars Cultural Center. A highway built through the old business district runs nearby; from 1,500–2,000 ft AGL, the downtown towers to the north and the Arkansas River beyond them make good references. Bill & Hillary Clinton National Airport/Adams Field (KLIT) is about 2.7 nm to the east-southeast, so expect airline traffic and coordinate with approach control. North Little Rock Municipal Airport (FAA identifier ORK; no ICAO code listed) lies across the Arkansas River to the north. Summer thunderstorms and haze are common.
