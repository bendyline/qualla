# Taborian Hall

Built by a fraternal order in 1916, Little Rock's Taborian Hall housed businesses, a wartime service club and the Dreamland Ballroom, where stars of the Chitlin' Circuit played for audiences on Ninth Street.

**Location:** 34.7417, -92.2800

**Categories:** African-American history of Arkansas, Music venues, Chitlin' Circuit, Commercial buildings in Little Rock, Arkansas, National Register of Historic Places in Little Rock, Arkansas

## Introduction

Climb to the third floor of Taborian Hall on Little Rock's Ninth Street and you reach the Dreamland Ballroom, a room that drew some of the greatest musical stars of its era. Louis Armstrong, Cab Calloway, Count Basie, Duke Ellington, Ella Fitzgerald, B.B. King, Ray Charles and Sammy Davis Jr. are all remembered as having performed there. For Black performers on the road, Dreamland was a safe stop on the Chitlin' Circuit. For Black Little Rock, the building beneath it was something more: a business address, a club, a refuge and a landmark.

## A Temple for the Tabors

The building began in 1916 as Taborian Temple, built by the Arkansas chapter of the Knights and Daughters of Tabor. At the time, the Ninth Street area was a major hub of African American commerce, and the Temple rose in the middle of it. It is a Classical three-story masonry building with stone trim. Its recessed main entrance is framed by Ionic columns supporting a segmented-arch pediment, and brick pilasters divide the upper floors, rising to a projecting cornice and parapet. Through the 1920s and 1930s the Temple housed Black-owned businesses, a community center and an officers and veterans club, and it was Arkansas's major stop on the Chitlin' Circuit.

## Depression and Dreamland

The Great Depression broke the Tabors, as it broke many fraternal organizations of the time. They lost their assets, and in the years that followed the building changed hands again and again, divided and sold and divided once more. During this period its third floor opened as the Dreamland Ballroom. The name spread. Dreamland became known throughout the South, and in cities like Chicago and New York, as a magnet for Cotton Club artists. The Chitlin' Circuit was a network of venues across the southern and eastern United States known to be safe and welcoming for African American musicians, performers and comedians, and Dreamland was a regular stop. Its audiences were often a mix of Black and white listeners.

## Service Club for Soldiers

In the early years of World War II, the USO bought the building. Taborian Temple became a regular stopping point for Black soldiers and war workers training across the Arkansas River at Camp Robinson, in North Little Rock. It was the only service club for African Americans in Little Rock during both World Wars. By the mid-1950s the building was known as Taborian Hall and held three nightclubs stacked floor by floor: the Twin City Club in the basement, the Waiters Club on the second floor, and Dreamland, renamed Club Morocco, at the top. Together they anchored the Ninth Street "Line," the center of Black business and culture in Little Rock and also the boundary that separated the city's Black and white communities.

## Bringing Back the Music

As Little Rock slowly began to integrate in the 1970s and 1980s, the Line lost its luster, and Taborian Hall eventually fell into disrepair. Recognition came before revival: the building was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1982. In 2009 a group of devoted citizens formed Friends of Dreamland, a nonprofit dedicated to building community through the music, history and culture of the ballroom. In March 2018 the U.S. Department of the Interior and the National Park Service awarded the group a $499,668 African American Civil Rights Grant to help open the historic building to the public, and two more such grants followed. They paid for a three-story elevator addition on the west side of the hall, a central heating and cooling system, repaired windows and a rebuilt stage. The ballroom opened to the public at full capacity in 2023, and it is now available for live music, dances and weddings.

## Flight Context

Taborian Hall stands at 34.7417°N, 92.2800°W on Ninth Street on the south side of downtown Little Rock, near the Mosaic Templars Cultural Center. From 1,500–2,000 ft AGL, use the downtown towers and the Arkansas River to the north as references. Robinson Army Air Field (KRBM), a military field, lies across the river in North Little Rock. Bill & Hillary Clinton National Airport/Adams Field (KLIT) is about 2.9 nm to the east-southeast, so expect airline traffic and coordinate with approach control. North Little Rock Municipal Airport (FAA identifier ORK; no ICAO code listed) is about 5.5 nm to the north. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common.
