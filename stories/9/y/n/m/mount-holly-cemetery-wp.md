# Mount Holly Cemetery

Called "The Westminster Abbey of Arkansas," Little Rock's Mount Holly Cemetery holds governors, senators, poets and the state's first newspaperman, along with enslaved people whose modest stones tell a quieter story.

**Location:** 34.7376, -92.2785

**Categories:** Cemeteries in Little Rock, Arkansas, Cemeteries on the National Register of Historic Places in Arkansas, Quapaw Quarter, History of Arkansas, Monuments and memorials in Little Rock, Arkansas

## Introduction

Each October, crowds move between the headstones of Mount Holly Cemetery. Student guides lead audiences from grave to grave, and at each stop a student in period costume performs a short monologue or dialogue about the person buried there. The drama students of Little Rock's Parkview Arts and Science Magnet High School are each assigned one life to research, and the event they perform, "Tales of the Crypt," is held on a Sunday afternoon in October. Its purpose is history rather than fright. In a cemetery nicknamed "The Westminster Abbey of Arkansas," there is no shortage of lives to bring back.

## A Capital's Burial Ground

Mount Holly dates from 1843 and occupies ground at 12th and Broadway in the Quapaw Quarter, home to some of Little Rock's oldest neighborhoods. Its roll of the dead reads like a civics lesson. Eleven former Arkansas governors lie here, along with United States senators, 14 justices of the Arkansas Supreme Court and 22 mayors of Little Rock, plus Confederate generals and a long list of the state's literary figures. Among the governors are Elias Nelson Conway, Henry Rector, Thomas James Churchill and Jeff Davis. Augustus H. Garland is here as well, a man who served as governor and U.S. senator before becoming the 38th Attorney General of the United States. The cemetery was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1970.

## Founders, Poets and a Traveller

Some of Mount Holly's residents built Little Rock from the ground up. Dr. Matthew Cunningham was both the city's first physician and its first mayor. William E. Woodruff founded the Arkansas Gazette and served as the state's first treasurer, and a later editor of the same paper, John N. Heiskell, is buried here too. George R. Mann, the architect of the Arkansas State Capitol, is buried here as well. Culture has its corner as well. Sandford C. Faulkner, who died in 1874, is remembered as the original "Arkansas Traveller." The Pulitzer Prize-winning poet John Gould Fletcher is buried with his wife, the children's book author Charlie May Simon. Even a mayor of another city ended up here: Allison Nelson, ninth mayor of Atlanta, Georgia, and later a Confederate brigadier general.

## Stones Without Titles

Not every grave at Mount Holly announces an office. Several enslaved people are buried in the cemetery, their graves marked by modest gravestones. Those plain stones share the same ground as the monuments of the men who governed the state, and they carry lives that the official honor roll leaves out. Another grave belongs to a teenager. David O. Dodd, hanged by Union authorities in January 1864 at the age of 17 for carrying notes on Federal troop positions, was buried here in a plot donated by a Little Rock resident. In later years he became known as the "boy martyr of the Confederacy." Some graves hold no body at all: the physician and U.S. Senator Solon Borland is honored with a cenotaph.

## The Night of Broken Stones

Old cemeteries are fragile, and Mount Holly learned how fragile in the overnight hours of April 20, 2016. Vandals moved through the grounds, toppling and smashing numerous headstones. Among the damaged monuments were some of the best known in the cemetery, the statues of a mourner beside two little girls. It was heavy damage for a place that had kept its dead since the 1840s. Yet the cemetery's life continues. The students still come each autumn in their period costumes, and the dead of Mount Holly still have living people willing to learn their stories and speak them aloud.

## Flight Context

Mount Holly Cemetery lies at 34.7376°N, 92.2785°W at 12th and Broadway, just south of downtown Little Rock in the Quapaw Quarter. From 1,500–2,000 ft AGL, look for a tree-shaded rectangle of green set into the street grid, with the downtown towers and the Arkansas River to the north. Bill & Hillary Clinton National Airport/Adams Field (KLIT) is about 2.7 nm to the east, so expect airline traffic and coordinate with approach control. North Little Rock Municipal Airport (FAA identifier ORK; no ICAO code listed) is across the river to the north. Autumn and winter bring the clearest air; summer often means haze and thunderstorms.
