# Pine Bluff Arsenal

Founded in 1941 to make incendiary bombs, the Pine Bluff Arsenal in Arkansas went on to produce and store chemical weapons, hosted biological weapons work, and then spent years destroying its stockpile; today it is described as the only facility in North America able to fill white phosphorus munitions.

**Location:** 34.3325, -92.0867

**Categories:** Military installations in Arkansas, Chemical weapons destruction facilities, Biological warfare facilities, Cold War military installations of the United States, Military history of Arkansas, 1941 establishments in Arkansas

## Introduction

In the 1950s, workers at the Pine Bluff Arsenal tried to destroy a stock of captured German rockets, filled with chemical agents, by burning them in a pit. Rockets were ejected from the pit and scattered around the site. Nobody meant for that to happen, and it is a strange, alarming footnote in the history of a place built to make fire and poison for war. Spread across more than 13,000 acres of Jefferson County, about 8 miles northwest of Pine Bluff and 30 miles southeast of Little Rock, the arsenal would spend its first half-century building chemical weapons and much of the next two decades taking them apart.

## Magnesium, Thermite and Gas

The U.S. Army established the arsenal on November 2, 1941, to manufacture incendiary grenades and bombs. Its foundation was 5,000 acres bought from a local physician, James W. John Sr. It opened as the Chemical Warfare Arsenal and was renamed four months later, and its mission widened to the production and storage of pyrotechnic, riot-control and chemical-filled munitions. At the height of the war, the plant moved beyond magnesium and thermite incendiaries to become a chemical warfare factory as well, producing lethal gases and chemical compounds that went into artillery shells and specially designed bombs. Pine Bluff became one of nine Army installations in the United States that stored chemical weapons. Mustard agent, VX and G-series nerve agents were kept in large bunkers on the grounds, and for decades the arsenal also stored chemical agent rockets.

## Germs, Hallucinogens and Binary Shells

The Cold War brought new work. Biological weapons operations ran at the arsenal from 1953 to 1969, when they ceased after President Nixon banned biological weapons; between 1954 and 1967 at least seven different biological agents were produced here, and all of them were destroyed between 1971 and 1973. In 1971 its biological facilities were formally transferred to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration; the following year the National Center for Toxicological Research was established at nearby Jefferson. The arsenal also had a BZ Fill Facility, which loaded munitions with BZ, an incapacitating agent that acts as a hallucinogen. The arsenal was also home to the Integrated Binary Production Facilities, designed to make binary weapons that mix two non-lethal chemicals into a chemical agent while in flight to a target. Only one part ever ran: from 1988 to 1990 it produced the precursor methylphosphonic difluoride, known as DF, sealing it into coffee-can-sized canisters for the M687 155mm binary artillery shell. The Bigeye bomb fill plant and other facilities were built but never operated.

## Taking the Weapons Apart

Under the Chemical Weapons Convention, the arsenal's job reversed. According to the Associated Press, Pine Bluff held 12 percent of the nation's chemical weapons stockpile, in the form of rockets, land mines and ton containers, and between March 2005 and November 2010 all of it was destroyed. Separate crews dealt with recovered weapons that were never part of the official stockpile. Starting in June 2006, a system of three explosive destruction units, each inside a vapor containment structure, used cutting charges to open more than 1,200 recovered chemical munitions, the largest such inventory in the nation, including 4.2-inch mortars and German Traktor rockets captured in World War II. The last was destroyed in April 2010. Workers baked more than 4,300 empty 1,600-pound ton containers at 1,000 degrees Fahrenheit for an hour, recycling more than 6.5 million pounds of steel. On December 28, 2006, demolition of the last building of the binary complex marked the destruction of the last former chemical weapons production facility in the United States, four months ahead of the treaty milestone.

## Smoke and Light

The arsenal is still very much at work. Today it designs, manufactures and refurbishes smoke, riot-control and incendiary munitions, along with equipment for chemical and biological defense, and it serves as a technology center for illuminating and infrared munitions. It is described as the only facility in North America able to fill white phosphorus munitions, and the material has its dangers. On June 6, 2005, a leak in a white phosphorus container was suspected of starting a fire that destroyed a warehouse; white smoke was visible six miles away, and by the time the fire was out, roughly 19 hours later, more than 7,500 canisters of white phosphorus had been lost. The installation covers 13,493 acres, with 665 buildings and 271 storage igloos, and most of the people who work there are civilians employed by the Department of Defense. It also trains first responders to use their equipment.

## Flight Context

Pine Bluff Arsenal occupies a large tract at about 34.333°N, 92.087°W, roughly 8 miles northwest of Pine Bluff and 30 miles southeast of Little Rock in Jefferson County, Arkansas. From altitude look for the installation's road grid and its rows of storage igloos (271 of them). This is an active Army installation that handles munitions; check current charts and NOTAMs for restricted or special-use airspace and do not overfly low. Nearby airports: Pine Bluff Regional, Grider Field (KPBF), about 12 nm southeast; Bill and Hillary Clinton National Airport/Adams Field at Little Rock (KLIT), about 25 nm north-northwest. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms in this part of Arkansas.
