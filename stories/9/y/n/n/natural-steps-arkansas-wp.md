# Natural Steps, Arkansas

A pair of sandstone walls stepping down into the Arkansas River gave this farming hamlet its name, its steamboat trade, its picnic crowds and a legend of Confederate gold.

**Location:** 34.8619, -92.4758

**Categories:** Census-designated places in Pulaski County, Arkansas, Arkansas populated places on the Arkansas River, Landforms of Pulaski County, Arkansas, Archaeological sites in Arkansas, 1822 establishments in Arkansas Territory

## Introduction

The steamer Maumelle left Little Rock at 8:30 sharp on a May morning in 1878, loaded with church picnickers. After about thirty miles of what the Arkansas Gazette called "varied and picturesque scenery," the boat reached its destination and the excursionists poured ashore. They spread out in the shady groves, ate in "true and time-honored style," and then took on the main attraction, a giant stone staircase rising out of the river. The newspaper summed up the climb with evident delight: "such getting up stairs you never did see." The place was Natural Steps, and it had been drawing visitors for more than half a century.

## A Staircase Nobody Built

The steps are pure geology. When state geologist David Dale Owen described and sketched them in his 1859 survey report, he found two perfectly parallel vertical walls of sandstone, twenty feet apart, jutting out of softer, crumbling slate. They descend steplike for fifty-one feet from the top of the bank to the river's low-water mark, and they can be seen continuing beneath the water. Boatmen and travelers on the Arkansas River used them as a landmark, and in 1870 an Army Corps of Engineers survey of the river drew them onto its charts. Most of the steps still stand, about 13 miles northwest of downtown Little Rock in a straight line (roughly 18 by road) on the river's southern bank, and river runners still use them as a marker, although they are not open to the public. Around them, Natural Steps has become a small farming community of 413 people.

## Landing for the Steamboats

The first inhabitants of the area were Native Americans, whose presence here may date back 10,000 years; from about 1500 to the late 1700s the local people were the Quapaw, whose name translates as "downstream people." Beginning in 1822, the steps offered visitors from Little Rock a convenient place to go ashore for a hike to the mountain. Deep water ran right up to the bank, making Natural Steps a natural port, and by 1849 the Arkansas Gazette reported fifteen to twenty steamboat arrivals and departures each week. Cotton, corn and firewood went out from the landing. The most spectacular gathering at the steps came on June 28, 1876, when three Masonic lodges arrived by steamboat for a joint installation ceremony. A century later, on June 27, 1976, the same lodges returned by boat and car, buried a time capsule meant to be opened a hundred years later, and marked the spot with a granite monument.

## Gunfire on the River

Not every boat that stopped here left in peace. On May 8, 1874, during the Brooks-Baxter War, a struggle between rival claimants to the Arkansas governorship, the sternwheeler Hallie took on fuel wood at Natural Steps and headed upstream. Near Palarm, a small community across the river, a "terrific volley" of gunfire erupted from behind the rocks on the far bank. Riflemen aboard returned fire for ten to fifteen minutes until a stray bullet pierced the pipe between the boiler and the engine. The powerless Hallie drifted downriver, out of range, and lodged against the opposite shore. One rifleman was dead, her captain was mortally wounded, a pilot would die of his wounds weeks later, and several others lay wounded. Accounts of the attackers' losses conflict, ranging from one man killed to five. Palarm lent its name to the brief, bloody fight.

## Gold, Outlaws and a Woman in White

A place this old collects stories. According to local legend, a Confederate gunboat was sunk at the steps to keep its gold out of Union hands after Little Rock fell, and three soldiers killed in the blast lie in the Natural Steps Cemetery. In the 1940s residents took dynamite to the rocks looking for the treasure. They found no gold, only damage, and the steps were partially destroyed. Bart Moreland Sr., born in Natural Steps in 1899, remembered hearing from his parents that Jesse James and his gang once spent a night here before robbing a stagecoach in Benton. Older residents also tell of a woman in white who walks from the cemetery down the stone steps into the river on the darkest October nights. They name her as Martha Sanders, whose husband Gustavus died of a sickness that swept the community in October 1880, only days after the couple married at the top of the steps.

## Flight Context

Natural Steps lies at about 34.86°N, 92.48°W on the south bank of the Arkansas River, roughly 13 miles northwest of downtown Little Rock in a straight line (about 18 by road along Arkansas Highway 300). The unmistakable landmark is Pinnacle Mountain, a steep, isolated 1,011-foot peak just to the south in Pinnacle Mountain State Park, rising sharply above the river valley. Follow the broad river upstream from Little Rock and look for the farm fields of the bottomland beneath the peak. A pass at 2,000 to 2,500 feet AGL gives a good view of the river bend; the steps themselves are small and not open to the public. North Little Rock Municipal Airport (KORK) and Bill and Hillary Clinton National Airport (KLIT) lie to the east-southeast. River fog can form on cool mornings.
