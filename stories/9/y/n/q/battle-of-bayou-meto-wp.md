# Battle of Bayou Meto

On August 27, 1863, Confederate cavalry held the line at Bayou Meto against the Union drive on Little Rock, then abandoned the field that night, while a feud between two of their generals ran toward a fatal duel.

**Location:** 34.8425, -92.1200

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Arkansas, Advance on Little Rock (American Civil War), Confederate victories of the American Civil War, History of Pulaski County, Arkansas, 1863 in Arkansas

## Introduction

In the middle of the fighting along Bayou Meto on August 27, 1863, Confederate Brigadier General John S. Marmaduke sent a note to his fellow cavalry commander, Brigadier General Lucius M. Walker. He wanted a conference on the battlefield, since he did not feel he could leave his own men while the guns were working. Walker neither came nor replied. That silence would outlast the battle itself, which the Confederates won and then walked away from within hours. Also remembered as the Battle of Reed's Bridge, the clash unfolded near present-day Jacksonville, Arkansas, about 12 miles northeast of Little Rock, and the grudge it deepened would end ten days later in a duel between the two generals.

## A Capital in the Crosshairs

By the summer of 1863, Confederate Arkansas was losing ground on every side. Union armies had won two significant victories in the state's northwest the year before and had occupied the Mississippi River town of Helena. Arkansas Post fell in January, and a Confederate attempt to retake Helena on July 4 was bloodily repulsed. With the Union victory at Vicksburg freeing up troops, a new objective came into view: Little Rock, the state capital and a stronghold on the Arkansas River.

Major General Frederick Steele set out from Helena on August 10 and 11. Disease tore through his ranks. After reaching Clarendon on August 17, he moved most of his force upriver to DeValls Bluff, believed to be a healthier location, and sent the cavalry of Brigadier General John W. Davidson across the river. Davidson pressed ahead and drove outnumbered Confederate horsemen back near Brownsville, a town on the road to Little Rock.

## A General Who Knew the Odds

On the Confederate side, command had changed hands when Lieutenant General Theophilus Holmes fell ill and Major General Sterling Price replaced him. Price ordered defenses built across the river from Little Rock, but he understood their weakness. His position was only really tenable if the Union attacked head-on, and that was unlikely when the Arkansas River could easily be crossed at many places downriver from the city. With the Confederate post at Fort Smith, on the western side of the state, also threatened, he began removing stored materials from Little Rock and preparing to evacuate.

Price's screen of cavalry rested on two brigadiers who were openly at odds. Marmaduke and Walker had both fought at Helena, and an intense feud had formed between them there. After the setback at Brownsville, the two fell back together to the far side of Bayou Meto.

## Smoke Over the Bayou

A small skirmish flared at the bayou on August 26. The next day Davidson came on with his main force. Five miles out, troopers of the 10th Illinois Cavalry ran into Confederate soldiers and pushed them back two miles before the rest of Colonel John Montgomery Glover's brigade deployed. Waiting between them and the water were Shelby's Iron Brigade, commanded that day by B. Frank Gordon, and a brigade under Colonel William L. Jeffers.

A Union attack drove the Confederates back to the defenses they had built in front of the bayou. Then the 3rd Missouri Cavalry and the 32nd Iowa Infantry pushed them across the bridge, and once his men were over, Marmaduke had it burned. A charge by the 1st Iowa Cavalry failed, and both sides settled into lines stretching along the water. What followed was mostly a back-and-forth artillery duel. When a Confederate artillery officer was killed, Marmaduke massed his guns against the Union batteries, hoping to exact revenge. Though outgunned, his crews placed their shots well enough to significantly reduce the accuracy of the Union fire.

## Winning the Field, Leaving It

Davidson missed his chance to strike Marmaduke's weak right flank and pulled back to Brownsville at sunset. Depending on the account, the Union lost 45 or 46 men, among them seven killed and 38 wounded. Full Confederate losses are not known, but at least two men were killed and several wounded.

The Confederates held the ground at day's end, yet during the night of August 27 they left it, taking up a new position less than five miles from Little Rock. The historian Albert E. Castel writes that Price forfeited his best opportunity to defeat Steele by withdrawing from the Bayou Meto line.

Steele rejoined Davidson at Brownsville on September 2, and the Union army crossed the bayou on September 6. On the morning of September 10, Davidson's cavalry crossed the Arkansas River on pontoon bridges. Marmaduke fought him that day at Bayou Fourche, buying Price time to withdraw, and at 7 p.m. the city's civil government surrendered Little Rock. The Confederate army had already gone, eventually withdrawing to Arkadelphia.

## The Note Nobody Answered

Walker's silence widened the rift between the two men, and the relationship kept worsening until September 6, when Marmaduke and Walker met in a duel and Walker was killed. A Confederate general was lost not to Union fire but to a quarrel inside his own army's high command, while the enemy was crossing the very bayou where the note had been sent.

Part of the ground where the two armies traded fire was added to the National Register of Historic Places on December 31, 2002, as the Bayou Meto Battlefield. The bridge that Marmaduke ordered burned is long gone, but the bayou still winds past Jacksonville, marking the line where a Confederate victory was won and then given away.

## Flight Context

Site at 34.8425°N, 92.12°W, along Bayou Meto near Jacksonville, Arkansas, roughly 12 miles northeast of downtown Little Rock. A viewing altitude of 2,500-3,500 ft AGL shows the wooded ribbon of the bayou threading between Jacksonville's suburbs and open farmland; the Arkansas River and the Little Rock skyline lie to the southwest. Little Rock Air Force Base (KLRF) is about 5 nm north of the site; it is an active military field, so respect its airspace and traffic pattern. Nearby civil options: North Little Rock Municipal Airport (KORK), about 7 nm west, and Bill and Hillary Clinton National Airport/Adams Field (KLIT), about 8.5 nm southwest. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common in central Arkansas; cooler months usually bring the clearest views.
