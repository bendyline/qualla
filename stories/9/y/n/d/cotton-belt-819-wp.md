# Cotton Belt 819

The last locomotive ever built in Arkansas ran 804,000 miles, rusted in a Pine Bluff park, and was brought back to steam by the very craftsmen who once built engines like it.

**Location:** 34.2269, -91.9847

**Categories:** Preserved steam locomotives of Arkansas, Individual locomotives of the United States, St. Louis Southwestern Railway, Arkansas Railroad Museum, Railway locomotives on the National Register of Historic Places in Arkansas

## Introduction

"Every time I come out here, I can see my daddy standing right on that step," James Norris said. "It kinda gets down in your heart." His father had taken Cotton Belt 819 out on her first run in 1943. Decades later, with the great steam engine parked in a Pine Bluff city park, James carried one of the two keys to her gate. Late in his fifties and in failing health, he came every week to knock off bird nests and replace broken glass in the cab, and on Sundays he swung the gate open so children could climb aboard, ring the bell and pepper him with questions.

## The Last Engine Built in Arkansas

The St. Louis Southwestern Railway, better known as the Cotton Belt, bought its first ten 4-8-4 "Northern" locomotives from the Baldwin Locomotive Works in 1930 at $110,849 apiece. In 1937 it began building more in its own shops in Pine Bluff, adding modern touches such as Boxpok driving wheels and Timken roller bearings. No. 819, completed in December 1942, was the last engine the Cotton Belt ever built and the last locomotive built anywhere in Arkansas. She earned her keep. In ten and a half years of service she ran more than 804,000 miles before diesels retired her in 1953. In 1955 the railroad donated her to the city of Pine Bluff on one condition, that the locomotive receive proper care.

## A Runaway in the Park

The city did not keep its promise for long. In October 1959 the Pine Bluff Commercial reported the engine rusting, vandalized and ringed by weeds that visitors had to trudge through to get close. Members of the Brotherhood of Railroad Trainmen and Cotton Belt employees stepped in, since restoring a locomotive demanded know-how only railmen possessed, and they arranged to shift her about 150 yards to a better spot between Townsend and Oakland Parks. During the 1960 move, No. 819 broke loose on her temporary track, coasted down an incline, rammed the park lake's embankment and finally stopped against a large black oak tree. Workers spent the next day coaxing her back onto the rails. A green and white steel shed, designed by an International Paper engineer and paid for with small donations from the Jaycees, the city and the mayor himself, was completed over her in 1965.

## Stripped of Its Dignity

Norris's doctor eventually ordered him to stay away, and he died in 1970. By 1983 a newspaper described the engine as covered in graffiti, with vandals having carried off anything light enough to lift. Sally Miller Perdue, Miss Arkansas of 1958 and a Pine Bluff native from a railroad family, said the locomotive "had been abandoned and stripped of all its dignity." Under her slogan, "Let's Put the Steam Back in Pine Bluff," a campaign began. On December 1, 1983, between 50 and 100 Cotton Belt employees, most of them volunteers, set No. 819 back on railroad track for the first time in nearly three decades and hauled her to the shops where she had been built 40 years earlier. A "no questions asked" appeal brought back many missing parts, including the whistle and an emblem, but the original bell never turned up. Her current bell came from another 800-class engine, after years of service at Wesley United Methodist Church in Pine Bluff.

## Steam Returns to Pine Bluff

Seven volunteer crews worked six days a week under restoration director Bill B. Bailey, and about a fifth of them had worked on, or apprenticed on, the 800-class engines when they were built in these very shops in the early 1940s. On April 26, 1986, No. 819 steamed into Fordyce on her first full trip out of Pine Bluff in 31 years, as schoolchildren in Pine Bluff, Rison, Kingsland and Fordyce cheered and carloads of railfans followed with cameras. That June she carried Hillary Clinton and state dignitaries to Little Rock for Arkansas's 150th birthday, with Governor Bill Clinton climbing aboard as the train slowed to a crawl behind Barton Coliseum. She spent the celebration weekend on display at Little Rock's Union Station, a few blocks north of the state capitol, before heading home.

## Arkansas's Own Locomotive

Hollywood came calling that same summer. Arkansas-born actress Mary Steenburgen, executive producer of the film End of the Line, worked to secure the newly restored engine for a small role, alongside 35 Pine Bluff residents turned actors, many of them volunteers who had helped bring her back. For seven years No. 819 pulled excursions, including a trip to a 1990 railroad convention in St. Louis, where she stood beside other celebrated steam engines. Her last excursion ran to Tyler, Texas, and back in October 1993, and her fire was last lit on March 11, 1994. She joined the National Register of Historic Places in 2003, and in March 2021 the legislature named her the official state locomotive of Arkansas, hoping the honor would help fund her protection and an eventual return to steam. She sits at the Arkansas Railroad Museum in Pine Bluff, partly disassembled for a federally required inspection whose funding ran out; on September 22, 2026, she was rolled outside for the first time in about thirty years so a steam-shop contractor could evaluate her, and no return-to-steam date has been announced.

## Flight Context

Cotton Belt 819 is displayed at about 34.23°N, 91.98°W in Pine Bluff, Arkansas, the railroad town where she was built. Pine Bluff is easy to find: the Arkansas River curves along the city's northern edge in the flat Delta farmland. The locomotive itself is too small to pick out from altitude, but a pass at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL shows the rail lines threading through town. Grider Field (KPBF), Pine Bluff's municipal airport, sits southeast of the city, and Bill and Hillary Clinton National Airport (KLIT) at Little Rock lies about 37 miles to the north-northwest. Visibility is usually good outside of summer haze and occasional winter fog.
