# Battle of Marks' Mills

On April 25, 1864, a Union supply train bound for Pine Bluff walked into a Confederate ambush in a small Arkansas clearing, a disaster that cost more than a thousand men yet may have saved the rest of Steele's army.

**Location:** 33.7811, -92.2569

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Arkansas, Camden Expedition, Battles of the Trans-Mississippi theater of the American Civil War, Confederate victories of the American Civil War, History of Cleveland County, Arkansas

## Introduction

During the night, Major Wesley Norris of the 43rd Indiana tried to warn his commander about movement in the woods ahead. Lieutenant Colonel Francis M. Drake laughed off the concern and told Norris, a combat veteran of the Mexican War, that he "got scared too easily." Out in the dark, thousands of Confederate cavalrymen were racing to get ahead of the Union wagon train camped beside the flooded Moro bottom in what is now Cleveland County, Arkansas. By the afternoon of April 25, 1864, nearly all of Drake's command would be dead, wounded or marching into captivity.

## A Train Stuck in the Mud

The trouble began with hunger. After the Union defeat at Poison Spring on April 18, Major General Frederick Steele held Camden while Confederate forces kept up an improvised siege from the surrounding countryside, and his supplies were running low. Steele sent Drake east toward Pine Bluff with 240 army wagons and more than 1,400 infantry, cavalry and artillery; reinforcements brought the escort to roughly 1,800 men of the 43rd Indiana, 36th Iowa and 77th Ohio. About 75 civilian wagons belonging to cotton speculators trailed behind. Heavy spring rains had turned the Moro bottom into a nearly impassable swamp, so Drake halted in mid-afternoon and sent several dozen Black refugees traveling with the train, people the army called "contrabands," ahead to cut timber for a corduroy road across the mud. Captain Samuel Swiggett of the 36th Iowa later argued the whole train could have crossed that evening and been well on its way toward a Union escort posted at Mount Elba. Instead the men lay in camp, and Swiggett recalled a shared feeling of foreboding.

## Ambush at the Clearing

They had reason. The Confederates had crossed the Ouachita River below Camden and made an all-night forced march of 52 miles to get ahead of the train, arriving at the ambush site barely in time. On Monday morning the 43rd Indiana led the way across the Moro, passing abandoned Confederate campsites. Drake again dismissed the danger, cursed Norris and ordered a faster pace. When the Hoosiers stepped into the small clearing called Marks' Mills, Brigadier General James F. Fagan's dismounted brigade opened fire. The Indianans drove them back, only to be struck on the right flank by Brigadier General William Cabell's men. They fell back toward a few log cabins at the center of the clearing, where Union guns traded fire with an Arkansas battery. Then Jo Shelby's cavalry hit the left flank. Outnumbered and attacked from three directions, the Federals were surrounded. Of the 33 men of the 43rd's Company G who entered the fight, 23 were killed or wounded within half an hour.

## Hours to Surrender

The fighting lasted four to five hours. One Union artillery battery was reportedly wiped out to the last man, its mortally wounded lieutenant firing a final gun before he died. Drake, whatever his judgment the night before, directed his men until a minie ball tore into his hip and he fell from his horse, badly wounded, and was captured. According to Sergeant John Moss, the 43rd never surrendered all at once; each Confederate charge swept up a few more men until only about 50 remained. When those survivors asked Norris to surrender them, he refused, saying he would surrender no one but himself, and only if forced. They ran for the woods. Norris's horse was shot out from under him, he lost a boot, and he and his comrades were finally taken just 100 yards from Shelby's command. Even the enemy was impressed. "Men," Cabell wrote, "never fought better."

## The Human Cost

Cabell's command reported 41 killed, 108 wounded and 144 missing, and a park marker puts total Confederate losses at fewer than 500. Union losses are harder to count because nearly the whole column was captured; estimates range from 1,133 to 1,600. Among the African Americans traveling with the train, the toll was grim: Confederates seized 150 and were accused of killing at least 100 others during or after the assault. Union survivors described prisoners, even the wounded begging for mercy, being stripped of clothing, some marched into captivity entirely naked, and the dead left unburied for three days. Most captured soldiers were marched to Camp Ford, a prison stockade in Tyler, Texas, where many died over the next year of malnutrition and disease. Most of those who survived were released in 1865.

## The Defeat That Saved an Army

Some historians consider Marks' Mills the worst defeat Federal forces ever suffered west of the Mississippi. Combined with the arrival of more Confederate troops under General Kirby Smith, the loss of men and wagons forced Steele to abandon Camden on April 26 and march north toward Little Rock. Yet the disaster carried an irony. Fagan and Shelby had been ordered to get between Steele and his base at Little Rock, a move that, joined with the forces advancing under Kirby Smith, could well have trapped his entire army. By stopping to attack the wagon train instead, they lost just enough time for Steele to slip away. Drake recovered and later won election as governor of Iowa, but the men of the 43rd Indiana, according to the 36th Iowa's regimental historian, held him in contempt long after the war.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lies at about 33.78°N, 92.26°W in Cleveland County, Arkansas, where Marks' Mills Battleground State Park now preserves part of the site at the junction of Arkansas Highways 8 and 97, north of New Edinburg. From the air this is largely forested, gently rolling country, so the highway junction is the best reference; the Moro Bayou bottomlands lie a few miles to the west. A pass at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL gives a good sense of how enclosed the clearing was. Pine Bluff lies to the northeast along the old supply route, and Grider Field (KPBF) at Pine Bluff is a convenient nearby airport. Spring storms and summer haze are common; late fall and winter usually bring the clearest views.
