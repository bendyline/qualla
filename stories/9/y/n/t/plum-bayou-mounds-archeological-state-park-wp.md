# Plum Bayou Mounds Archeological State Park

Beside an oxbow lake southeast of Little Rock, eighteen earthen mounds built between the 7th and 11th centuries spent more than a century credited to the Toltecs before Arkansas gave them back to the people who raised them.

**Location:** 34.6469, -92.0653

## Introduction

The Toltecs never came to Arkansas. For well over a century, though, their name was attached to one of the state's most important ancient sites, on a railway station, a township and eventually a state park. The mounds beside Mound Lake, an oxbow of the Arkansas River, were in fact raised by people archaeologists now call the Plum Bayou culture, who lived here from the 7th to the 11th century. Today the site carries their name: Plum Bayou Mounds Archeological State Park, a correction that took more than 140 years to complete.

## Earthworks by the Oxbow

The park protects an 18-mound complex that includes the tallest surviving prehistoric mounds in Arkansas. The mounds stand on the banks of Mound Lake, a curving remnant of an old channel of the Arkansas River, and they are enclosed on three sides by a ditch and an earthen embankment wall. Archaeologists date the occupation to roughly AD 650 to 1050, placing it in the Late Woodland period. The mounds were raised by hand over several generations, and the scale of the place still registers from the ground: a planned landscape of earthworks set against the still water of the lake. In the state's archaeological site files it is recorded simply as 3 LN 42, a bland label for a place that took lifetimes of labor to build.

## A Culture of Their Own

The builders were not an outpost of some other civilization. Their culture was distinct from those of other Native American groups living in the Mississippi Valley at the same time. Plum Bayou sites turn up throughout the floodplains of the White and Arkansas rivers in central and eastern Arkansas, and as far west as the eastern Ozark Mountains. This site is the largest of them all. How the Plum Bayou people related to their neighbors, such as the Coles Creek culture to the south and the Fourche Maline culture to the southwest, is still under investigation. Much of what is known traces to careful fieldwork, including the Toltec Mounds Research Project, whose preliminary findings archaeologist Martha Ann Rolingson published in 1982.

## Mary Knapp's Letters

The site's modern story runs through Mary Knapp, who co-owned the land from 1849 to 1905. She and her first husband bought it in 1849, and it passed to her after his death. With her second husband she later bought more land nearby. Knapp was interested in archaeology, and she came to know Dr. William Barry, who shared her fascination with Native American artifacts and customs. She wrote to him about the mounds, and Barry passed her letter on to Joseph Henry, secretary of the Smithsonian Institution. Knapp went on to correspond with Henry directly and sent the Smithsonian artifacts she had collected at the site. Her letters put the mounds in front of the nation's leading scientific institution, even though her family's theory about who built them was wrong.

## Giving Back the Name

The Knapp family believed Mesoamericans had built the mounds, and they named the township Toltec. In 1888 they established a railway station there under the same name, and the site became known by it. The irony is that the science had already moved on. Archaeologists from Harvard's Peabody Museum and the Smithsonian's Bureau of Ethnology investigated the mounds in 1879 and 1882, showing that they were built by the ancestors of local Native Americans. The name stuck anyway. It survived the site's designation as a National Historic Landmark in 1978 and stayed on the state park that came to protect the mounds. On November 2, 2022, Arkansas renamed it Plum Bayou Mounds Archeological State Park, to help the public understand who actually built the mounds. In 2025 the site was also declared an Arkansas State Archeological Landmark.

## Flight Context

Plum Bayou Mounds lies at 34.6469°N, 92.0653°W in Lonoke County, southeast of Little Rock. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL, look for Mound Lake, a crescent-shaped oxbow on the flat Arkansas River floodplain; the mounds and the park's open grounds sit along its bank amid surrounding farmland. Low sun angles in the morning or late afternoon help the mounds stand out from the flat terrain. Bill and Hillary Clinton National Airport (KLIT) is about 9 nm to the west-northwest, so the site sits near Little Rock's Class C airspace, and Little Rock Air Force Base (KLRF) lies roughly 17 nm to the north-northwest. Expect haze and summer thunderstorms.
