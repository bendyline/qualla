# 2013 Mayflower oil spill

In March 2013 a decades-old pipeline, reversed to carry Canadian oil sands crude, split open beneath a quiet Arkansas subdivision and sent heavy oil down its streets toward Lake Conway.

**Location:** 34.9819, -92.4450

**Categories:** Oil spills in the United States, ExxonMobil oil spills, Disasters in Arkansas, Environment of Arkansas, Petroleum in Arkansas

## Introduction

The pipe that ruptured under Mayflower, Arkansas, was built between 1947 and 1954 to move oil out of Corsicana, Texas. Over the decades its flow was turned around more than once. Its northern section was abandoned in 2002, then recommissioned in 2005 and 2006 and reversed to carry diluted bitumen from Canada's oil sands south to refineries in Texas. By 2013 ExxonMobil's Pegasus Pipeline was moving about 95,000 barrels a day across roughly 850 miles, from Patoka, Illinois, to Nederland, Texas. On March 29, 2013, in a small town about 20 miles northwest of Little Rock, it split open.

## Crude on a Suburban Street

Early images from local media showed crude oil running along a suburban street and across front lawns. Twenty-two homes were evacuated. The pipeline was shut down once the leak was discovered, but oil was already flowing into storm drains that led toward Lake Conway, a fishing lake nearby. Firefighters, city employees, county road crews and police threw up dikes to block the culverts and keep the crude out of the water, while ExxonMobil strung 3,600 feet of containment boom around the lake. By early the next morning, the company said, no more oil was spilling from the pipe. Evacuated families were later allowed back only briefly, under police escort, to collect personal belongings. The spill was eventually estimated at about 3,190 barrels, and the Environmental Protection Agency classified it as a major spill.

## What Was in the Pipe?

Even the name of the substance became a dispute. Company officials described it simply as heavy oil. On April 5 the EPA asked ExxonMobil whether the product, known as Wabasca Heavy, could accurately be described as oil sands oil or diluted bitumen. In an April 10 reply, an ExxonMobil attorney wrote that "Canadian producers report their production of Wabasca Heavy as bitumen," and the product's safety data sheet confirmed it was bitumen mixed with hydrocarbon diluents. The distinction mattered to critics of oil sands pipelines. Massachusetts Representative Ed Markey tied Mayflower to the debate over the proposed Keystone XL pipeline, calling this kind of fuel corrosive and polluting. An industry report prepared for the Canadian Energy Pipeline Association countered that diluted bitumen is no more corrosive than other heavy crude.

## A Closed Sky

On April 1 the Federal Aviation Administration closed the airspace over Mayflower from the ground to 1,000 feet within a five-nautical-mile radius. According to the Arkansas Democrat-Gazette, only relief aircraft operating under the direction of an ExxonMobil aviation advisor were permitted inside. Two days later the FAA changed the restriction to allow media access, saying reporters should not have been kept out of this type of incident and taking responsibility for the error. The restriction was lifted entirely on April 5. On the ground, federal pipeline regulators ordered ExxonMobil not to restart the damaged segment until repairs were complete, warning that continued operation "would be hazardous to life, property, and the environment." Arkansas Attorney General Dustin McDaniel opened a state investigation and asked the company to preserve its records.

## Questions That Lingered

Official reports indicated that no oil had reached Lake Conway, and a state wildlife official pointed out that there were no dead fish or other animals to suggest oil in the main body of the lake, though an independent study claimed to have samples showing oil beneath the surface. The EPA's 2015 settlement notice later said the oil had spread from the neighborhood into a nearby creek, wetlands and a cove of Lake Conway. State health officials said air readings were below levels of concern for the general public outside the cleanup zones, while a citizens' advisory group reported residents still showing symptoms of chemical exposure more than four weeks later. With little media coverage, residents gathered and shared information themselves, and in July 2013 a crowdfunding drive raised over $25,000 to fund two reporters investigating the spill. At the time, ExxonMobil still had not explained the cause of the 22-foot gash in the pipe. Investigators eventually traced the failure to the pipe's seam: hook cracks, extremely low impact toughness and poor elongation properties, weaknesses long associated with the low-frequency electric resistance welding used to make it. In 2015 ExxonMobil settled federal and state environmental charges for $5.07 million, without admitting liability.

## Flight Context

Mayflower sits at about 34.98°N, 92.44°W in Faulkner County, Arkansas, about 20 miles northwest of Little Rock, where Interstate 40 runs between North Little Rock and Conway. Lake Conway, the fishing lake the responders fought to protect, spreads out close to town and is the easiest visual reference, with the Arkansas River to the west. There is nothing left to see of the spill itself; a pass at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL shows the residential streets and drainage paths leading toward the lake. During the 2013 response the FAA briefly closed the airspace here below 1,000 feet within a five-nautical-mile radius. North Little Rock Municipal Airport (KORK) lies about 13 nm to the southeast, with Bill and Hillary Clinton National Airport (KLIT) farther southeast, about 19 nm away. Spring thunderstorms are common in central Arkansas.
