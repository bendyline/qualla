# Battle of Jenkins' Ferry

In fog, smoke and knee-deep mud on the flooded Saline River, a retreating Union army fought off its pursuers on April 30, 1864, in one of the Civil War's costliest fights for its size.

**Location:** 34.2125, -92.5475

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Arkansas, Camden Expedition, Battles of the Trans-Mississippi theater of the American Civil War, Massacres in 1864, History of Grant County, Arkansas

## Introduction

Soon after the shooting started on April 30, 1864, soldiers along the Saline River could barely see each other. Fog lay over the bottomland, gunpowder smoke thickened it, and the only way to spot the enemy was to crouch low and peer beneath the haze. Rain had fallen in torrents, turning the approaches to Jenkins' Ferry into a quagmire of mud and standing water. Behind a line of breastworks, a Union rearguard held on while the rest of Major General Frederick Steele's exhausted army struggled to drag wagons and cannon across a pontoon bridge. The fight that followed, in what is now Grant County, Arkansas, would be counted among the Civil War's bloodiest for the numbers engaged.

## A Campaign Unravels

Steele had not set out to retreat. In the spring of 1864 he was supposed to march south from Little Rock in support of Major General Nathaniel Banks, whose army and Admiral David Porter's gunboats were driving up the Red River toward Shreveport, Louisiana, headquarters of the Confederate Trans-Mississippi Department. Steele left with about 8,000 men, marching through days of rain on reduced rations across poor country where the people were destitute and there was little food or forage to be had. Then Banks was turned back at the Battle of Mansfield on April 8. Confederate commander General E. Kirby Smith decided that Steele was now the bigger threat and the bigger prize, and over the vehement objections of his subordinate Richard Taylor, he sent infantry north into Arkansas to destroy him.

## Camden Becomes a Trap

Steele occupied Camden hoping to live off supply trains and the countryside. Instead the trains were lost. At Poison Spring on April 18, Confederates captured 170 wagons from a 198-wagon train, and field reports said they killed Black soldiers of the escort, men of the 1st Kansas Colored Infantry, as they tried to surrender. A week later at Marks' Mills, another train and most of its escort were captured, and there were reports that wounded African American soldiers were killed at the end of the fight. Short of food, outnumbered and in danger of being surrounded, Steele and his senior officers agreed there was no reasonable choice but to leave. The army slipped out of Camden overnight on April 26 and 27, crossing the Ouachita River on a pontoon bridge it had carried through the whole campaign. The Confederates lost time building a raft bridge across the swollen, bridgeless river, but the rain soon slowed Steele too.

## Fighting in the Mud

Steele's column reached Jenkins' Ferry at 2 p.m. on April 29 and found the Saline swollen by heavy rain. The cavalry crossed overnight, but the tired, hungry infantry could not get its wagons and artillery out of the mud in the dark. Brigadier General Samuel Rice took charge of the rearguard with 4,000 infantry, placing them behind breastworks, abatis and rifle pits, while about 10,000 Confederates under Kirby Smith and Sterling Price closed in, roughly matching Steele's remaining strength. Before dawn, John Marmaduke's dismounted cavalry opened a skirmish about two miles from the crossing. The Confederate assaults that followed came piecemeal, frontal charges across Kelly's field that were cut apart by Union fire. Mud kept cavalry and artillery largely out of the fight. When the 2nd Kansas Colored and the 29th Iowa charged and captured three Confederate cannon, the Kansans, whose officers had sworn after Poison Spring to take no prisoners while Black soldiers were being murdered, killed Confederate artillerymen trying to surrender, and wounded men, in retaliation.

## A Victory That Settled Nothing

By about 3 p.m. the Union army was across, burning the wagons it could not pull free. Exhausted, and facing guns now posted on the far bank, the Confederates did not renew the attack. Casualty reports were incomplete, but historians have estimated roughly 1,000 Confederate and 700 Union losses. The Union lost General Rice; the Confederates lost General Scurry and Colonel Randal. Among the grieving was Sophronia Smith Hunt, a woman who had disguised herself as a soldier and whose first husband, a soldier in her regiment, lost a leg here and later died as a prisoner of war. Tactically the Union had won, holding off its pursuers and escaping to Little Rock. Strategically the victory was hollow. Over the whole expedition Steele lost roughly 2,750 to 3,000 men, 635 wagons and 2,500 animals, and the Confederates held southwest Arkansas. Today the site is preserved as Jenkins' Ferry Battleground State Park, part of the Camden Expedition Sites declared a National Historic Landmark in 1994. The battle reached a wider audience in 2012, when the opening scene of Steven Spielberg's Lincoln dramatized the fight and two fictional Black Union soldiers spoke of it to Abraham Lincoln.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lies at about 34.21°N, 92.55°W on the Saline River in Grant County, Arkansas, roughly 40 miles south-southwest of Little Rock. From the air, follow the Saline's winding, wooded floodplain; the ferry site sits in low bottomland beside the river. A pass at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL shows how the river and surrounding wetlands channeled both armies. Bill and Hillary Clinton National Airport (KLIT) at Little Rock lies about 35 nm north-northeast, and Grider Field (KPBF) at Pine Bluff is to the east. Spring storms can bring rain and low ceilings, much like the weather that trapped Steele's army; fall often offers clearer skies.
