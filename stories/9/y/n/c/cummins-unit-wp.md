# Cummins Unit

Built on two former cotton plantations in the Arkansas Delta in 1902, the Cummins Unit prison farm became notorious for an armed-trusty system and abuses that federal courts found unconstitutional, and it still houses the state's execution chamber.

**Location:** 34.0514, -91.5839

**Categories:** Prisons in Arkansas, Buildings and structures in Lincoln County, Arkansas, Capital punishment in Arkansas, Prison reform, Arkansas Delta

## Introduction

Most prisons are measured in cells. Cummins is measured in acres, more than 16,000 of them, spread across the flat cotton country of the Arkansas Delta along U.S. Route 65, about 28 miles south of Pine Bluff. For more than a century those fields have been worked by people serving time, and what happened on them eventually drew national attention.

## Plantation Ground

In 1902 the State of Arkansas paid $140,000 for about 10,000 acres, the former Cummins and Maple Grove plantations, both of which had grown cotton. The purchase was a political fight: Governor Jeff Davis had wanted the state to buy a Jefferson County farm owned by his friend Louis Altheimer, a Republican Party leader, and when the legislature chose the Cummins land instead, Davis tried to force it to cancel the deal. The purchase stood, and prisoners began occupying the site in December 1902. From the start, Cummins held African-American men and women. When Governor Junius Marion Futrell closed the old state penitentiary, known as "The Walls," in 1933, some of its prisoners moved here, and from 1936 white men with disciplinary problems were sent here too. White women followed in 1951. The barracks were racially segregated, with separate quarters even for white and Black trusties.

## Guarded by Prisoners

For decades the farm ran on inmate labor and inmate power. Prisoners once worked the fields ten hours a day, six days a week, excused only when the temperature fell below freezing, and they had no fixed quotas, only orders to do as much as possible. Those judged not to be working hard enough were beaten. The prison system had very few paid employees. Armed trusties, prisoners trusted by the administration, guarded the rank-and-file inmates, and at one time a trusty shooter manned the main gate. Because trusties could come and go without being searched, they smuggled in alcohol, drugs and weapons and sold them inside. Other prisoners had to pay trusties for food, medicine, access to medical staff and visitors, and protection from arbitrary punishment. Federal cases in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Arkansas found that inmates at Cummins had been held in conditions amounting to unconstitutionally cruel and unusual punishment.

## Skeletons and Scandal

The worst revelations came in 1968, when Tom Murton alleged that three human skeletons found on the farm were the remains of inmates who had been subjected to torture, though an official state report found no signs of violent death and said they came from a pauper's grave, a conclusion Murton disputed. The publicized investigation that followed found that "a prison hospital served as torture chamber and a doctor as chief tormentor." Among the allegations were electrical devices attached to inmates' genitals. Unrest ran through those years: on September 5, 1966, riots broke out and 144 prisoners attempted a strike that Arkansas State Police ended with tear gas, and in 1970 another riot, begun by prisoners demanding segregated housing, brought state police in again. Outsiders came, too. In 1969 Johnny Cash performed a concert at Cummins and offered $5,000 toward a chapel, though the money went to a chapel at the Tucker unit, and Cummins's own chapel was not finished until 1977. The ethnographer Bruce Jackson gained access to photograph the prisoners after his friend T. Don Hutto became Arkansas commissioner of corrections in 1971, and his Cummins pictures were exhibited in 2010 at the Albright-Knox Art Gallery in Buffalo and at Duke University's Center for Documentary Studies.

## The Farm Today

Cummins is one of the state's "parent units" for male prisoners, with a rated capacity of about 1,875 (it often holds more), and it serves as one of several units where newly processed male prisoners are first assigned. Prisoners working the fields have been organized into the Hoe Squad, and those who refuse work have been sent to solitary confinement. As of 2001, prisoners harvested corn, cotton and rice under guards on horseback. Staff and their families live on the "Free Line," where prisoners work as house servants. A new execution chamber opened here in 1978; it sits next to the Varner Unit, home of the state's male death row. In 2000 the state's first lethal electrified fence, built by inmates, opened at Cummins. The pandemic hit hard in 2020: by late April, 800 prisoners and 33 correctional employees had COVID-19, and by mid-June, 11 Cummins prisoners had died.

## Flight Context

The Cummins Unit lies at about 34.051°N, 91.584°W in flat Delta farmland along U.S. Route 65 in Lincoln County, Arkansas, near the small towns of Grady and Gould, about 28 miles south of Pine Bluff. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL look for the prison compound amid the broad fields along Route 65. Do not circle or loiter low over the facility. Nearby airports: Pine Bluff Regional, Grider Field (KPBF), about 19 nm northwest; Billy Free Municipal at Dumas (FAA identifier 0M0, GPS code K0M0; no ICAO code listed), about 10 nm south. Expect agricultural aircraft working the fields at low altitude, and summer haze and thunderstorms over the Delta.
