# Salvation Mountain

Leonard Knight spent nearly three decades building a fifty-foot mountain of adobe, tires, and thousands of gallons of paint in the California desert, driven by nothing more than a desire to tell the world that God is love.

**Location:** 33.2540, -115.4730

**Categories:** Folk art in California, Imperial County, California, Tourist attractions in California, Outdoor sculpture in California, Desert art

## Introduction

Leonard Knight arrived in the desert near Niland, California in 1984 with a simple message he wanted the world to read from the air. What he built over the next twenty-seven years — sleeping in a shack on the back of a flatbed truck, welcoming strangers, accepting donations he converted almost entirely into more paint — became one of the most improbable and genuinely affecting works of folk art in the American West. Salvation Mountain is fifty feet tall. It is made from adobe bricks, discarded tires, car windows, and thousands of gallons of latex paint donated by churches, schools, and individuals who heard about the project. And it says, over and over, in letters large enough to read from the road: God is Love.

## The Man Who Built a Mountain

Leonard Knight was born in Burlington, Vermont on November 1, 1931. He spent most of his adult life moving through the country, working odd jobs, and carrying a quiet religious conviction that intensified after a conversion experience in 1967. By the early 1980s he had a specific ambition: to build something large enough that people — perhaps even airline passengers — could see his message. He first attempted a hot air balloon, which failed to ascend properly. Then he found the desert near Slab City, the informal community that occupies the concrete slabs left behind when the military abandoned Camp Dunlap. He began building in 1984. His first construction collapsed in a rainstorm in 1989, and he began again. The second version has endured.

## Material and Method

Knight built Salvation Mountain from whatever was available and donated. Adobe mud from the surrounding desert formed the base material, mixed with straw and shaped by hand. Car windows, wheel rims, discarded tires, and salvaged building materials were incorporated into the structure as both reinforcement and decoration. Paint was the primary medium — thousands of gallons of it, applied with brushes, rollers, and occasionally with his hands, covering the surface in the saturated colors that make Salvation Mountain visible from a significant distance. The Folk Art Society of America visited the site in 2000 and declared it worthy of preservation. Senator Barbara Boxer entered it into the Congressional Record in 2002 as a national treasure. The Annenberg Foundation donated $32,000 in 2013 for continued maintenance.

## The Lead Controversy and the Community

In 1994, soil tests around Salvation Mountain found elevated lead levels, attributed to the older oil-based paints that Knight had used in the early years of construction. Imperial County briefly considered demolishing the site and held public hearings on the matter. The response from the folk art and wider cultural community was overwhelming — letters, petitions, and media coverage made it clear that Salvation Mountain had acquired a constituency that extended far beyond the desert. The county ultimately abandoned the demolition plan, and Knight continued building with lead-free latex paint. The mountain attracted a steady stream of visitors, artists, musicians, and filmmakers drawn by its strangeness and its sincerity. Coldplay filmed a music video here. Kesha filmed her video for "Praying" on these slopes. Both were drawn to a place where someone had decided, against all practical logic, to make something beautiful.

## After Leonard

Knight's health declined in his early eighties, and he moved from his desert shack to a care facility in 2011. He died on February 10, 2014, at the age of 82, having never made money from his creation and having spent nearly three decades ensuring that it existed and was maintained. The Salvation Mountain organization continues to care for the site after his death, relying on volunteer labor and donations. The mountain weathers. The paint fades. New coats go on. The message remains legible from the road, which was, from the beginning, the entire point. Leonard Knight built a mountain because he had something to say and nowhere else large enough to say it.

## Flight Context

Salvation Mountain is located at approximately 33.254°N, 115.473°W east of Niland near the Slab City community. The installation is visible from low altitude as a splash of vivid color on the desert floor — bright reds, yellows, blues, and greens against the tan-brown of the surrounding landscape. From cruising altitude it is a small but distinctive feature near the southeastern shore of the Salton Sea, which is visible to the northwest. The nearest airport is Imperial County Airport (IPL) approximately 25 miles to the south.
