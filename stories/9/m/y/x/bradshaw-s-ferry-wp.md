# Bradshaw's Ferry

William Bradshaw found gold at La Paz, blazed a trail through the California desert to bring miners to it, and then established a ferry on the Colorado River to complete the journey — dying in 1864 under circumstances that history recorded as suicide but contemporaries suspected was murder.

**Location:** 33.6047, -114.5306

**Categories:** Colorado River history, California Gold Rush, Arizona Territory, Bradshaw Trail

## Introduction

The Colorado River, in the early 1860s, was both a barrier and a destination. On the Arizona side, at a place called La Paz, gold had been discovered — the first major strike of what would become the Colorado River Gold Rush. On the California side, miners needed a way to get there. William D. Bradshaw provided two essential pieces of that puzzle: a trail through the desert from San Gorgonio Pass to the river, and a ferry to cross it. Bradshaw's Ferry operated at several points along the Colorado between 1862 and 1867, its rates fixed by the Arizona Territorial Legislature, its operation left largely to others while Bradshaw himself roamed, and its final chapter written by his brother Isaac after William died in 1864 under circumstances that official records called suicide and that some people at the time called murder.

## The Trail and the Crossing

Bradshaw had scouted the route that bears his name — the Bradshaw Trail — specifically to connect the Southern California settlements accessible through San Gorgonio Pass with the gold fields at La Paz. When he reached the Colorado River, he needed a way across. He partnered with William Warringer of La Paz to establish a ferry at Providence Point, across from Olive City, near the road to La Paz. Bradshaw returned to Los Angeles and published an announcement in the Los Angeles Star describing both the new road and the ferry waiting at its end. It was, in essence, the first advertisement for a complete route to the goldfields — trail on one side, ferry on the other, profit on both.

## The Price of Crossing

On November 7, 1864, the Arizona Territorial Legislature formalized what Bradshaw's Ferry could charge. A wagon and two horses cost four dollars. A carriage with one horse cost three dollars. A saddle horse cost one dollar. A man on foot paid fifty cents. Cattle and horses were fifty cents a head; sheep a quarter. The legislature also authorized the ferry to operate at any point between Mineral City and five miles above La Paz for up to twenty years — a generous geographic and temporal mandate. In practice, the ferry moved with the settlements: first at Olive City, then at Mineral City in 1864, and finally at Ehrenburg from 1866 onward, when that settlement eclipsed the others. The ferry followed commerce, as ferries do.

## A Man Who Did Not Live to Collect

William Bradshaw died in 1864, the same year the legislature set his ferry rates. The official finding was suicide. The unofficial whisper was murder. He had left daily operations of the ferry to his brother Isaac before his death; whatever he was doing instead, he was not running the crossing he had established. Isaac Bradshaw sold out his interest in the ferry in 1867, three years after William's death, and the enterprise passed to others. The ferry itself eventually became irrelevant — the Southern Pacific Railroad bridged the Colorado River in 1877, and the economics of ferrying wagons across the desert river collapsed. What Bradshaw left behind was a trail that continued to bear his name and a river crossing that, for a few critical years in the 1860s, was the gateway to Arizona Territory.

## The Crossing Today

Nothing physical remains of Bradshaw's Ferry. The Colorado River has shifted its banks since the 1860s; Ehrenburg, Arizona, on the opposite shore from where the ferry once ran, is a small community today rather than the booming terminus it briefly was. The Bradshaw Trail, from which the ferry was the essential conclusion, can still be traced through the desert by those willing to follow it on unpaved roads through the Chocolate Mountains to the river. The historical marker at the site where Bradshaw and his partners once collected fifty cents from travelers heading toward gold is now the river itself — brown, managed, carrying far less water than it carried in 1862, but still crossing from California into Arizona at roughly the same latitude where men once paid to float across.
