# Blythe Intake

On July 17, 1877, a Welsh-born San Francisco financier named Thomas Blythe filed the first legal claim for Colorado River water rights — setting in motion an irrigation project that would give the city of Blythe its name and the Palo Verde Valley its future.

**Location:** 33.7318, -114.5158

**Categories:** California Historical Landmarks, Colorado River history, Irrigation history, Blythe California

## Introduction

The man who gave Blythe its name never lived there. Thomas Henry Blythe — born Thomas Williams in Mold, Wales, before reinventing himself as a San Francisco financier — visited the valley by the Colorado River only twice in his life, the second time in November 1882, just five months before he died. But on July 17, 1877, he did something that outlasted him by more than a century: he filed the first legal claim for Colorado River water rights, staking a claim to an irrigation project that would eventually make the Palo Verde Valley bloom with alfalfa, cotton, and citrus. The spot where that claim was made is now called the Blythe Intake. It is a California Historic Landmark. The Palo Verde Dam stands there today, on the same ground where the first canal was dug by men who believed the desert could be made to yield.

## The Engineer and the Financier

The idea did not originate with Blythe. It originated with William Calloway — sometimes called Oliver Calloway in historical records — a former captain of the 1st California Infantry Regiment who explored the land across the Colorado River from Ehrenberg, Arizona, in the early or mid-1870s and looked at the flat alluvial land on the California side. He saw potential. He made preliminary surveys, filed land claims under the Swamp Land Act of 1850, and then went looking for money. He found it in Thomas Blythe, a Welsh immigrant who had come to San Francisco and prospered. Blythe put up the capital; Calloway would do the engineering. Together they planned a diversion dam and canal to bring Colorado River water west into the valley. The canal opened in 1877, the same year Blythe filed his formal water rights claim.

## The Chemehuevi Conflict

The project did not proceed without violence. On March 28, 1880, Calloway died in a Chemehuevi attack. The circumstances reflected a broader pattern of conflict between settlers and Indigenous communities who had lived along the Colorado River for generations, communities whose relationship to the land and water operated under entirely different principles than the legal frameworks Blythe and Calloway were invoking. After Calloway's death, a man named C.C. Miller took over management — the father, incidentally, of Frank Augustus Miller, who would later become famous for building the Mission Inn in Riverside. George Irish had also been appointed manager to assist Calloway. The project continued, and Calloway's is the death the record remembers; what the valley's transformation cost the Chemehuevi and other river peoples went largely unrecorded.

## The Man Who Came Back Once

Thomas Blythe died on April 4, 1883, in San Francisco. His estate became the subject of a long probate battle in which nearly 200 people claimed to be his heirs, until a court ruled in 1890 that his daughter, Florence, was his sole heir. He had visited the valley he was transforming only twice, and his death did not halt the work. The irrigation system he funded kept expanding, and eventually the town that grew up around it took his name. The Blythe Intake — the point where water was diverted from the Colorado into the canal — was designated California Historic Landmark No. 948 on March 1, 1982. The marker on U.S. Route 95, at milepost 10.5 heading north, reads with the stripped-down economy of official history: on July 17, 1877, Thomas Blythe, a San Francisco financier, filed the first legal claim for Colorado River water rights. Oliver Callaway planned a diversion dam and canal which opened in 1877 to irrigate the Palo Verde Valley. This made possible the settlement and development of the valley.

## Water and Consequence

The Palo Verde Dam, which now occupies the Blythe Intake site, was built between 1956 and 1958 by the Bureau of Reclamation — a necessity created in part by the upstream dams that had cut off the sediment load of the Colorado, causing the riverbed to degrade and making the original diversion works increasingly ineffective. The dam can divert roughly 1,800 cubic feet of water per second into a district of about 121,000 acres, some 90,000 of them farmed. The water still flows where Calloway first surveyed it. What it irrigates, who benefits, and what was lost to make it possible are questions the landmark marker does not address. That is the nature of landmarks: they fix a moment, not its full meaning.

## Flight Context

Located at 33.73°N, 114.51°W, approximately 9 miles northeast of Blythe at the Palo Verde Dam site on the Colorado River. From the air, the Colorado River is clearly visible as the California-Arizona border; the Palo Verde Dam appears as a low earthen structure spanning the river. The broad agricultural expanse of the Palo Verde Valley stretches to the west and south. Blythe Airport (KBLH) is about 14 miles to the southwest. U.S. Route 95 is visible running north along the river's edge.
