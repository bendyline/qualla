# Palo Verde Dam

The Palo Verde Dam exists because of a problem created by dams that came before it — Hoover and Davis blocked the Colorado's sediment, starved the riverbed, and made a new structure necessary to keep the water flowing west into California's agricultural valley.

**Location:** 33.7329, -114.5107

**Categories:** Colorado River, Bureau of Reclamation, California irrigation, Blythe California

## Introduction

There is a certain irony embedded in the existence of the Palo Verde Dam. The dam — officially the Palo Verde Diversion Dam — was built between 1956 and 1958 by the Bureau of Reclamation, not because the river needed taming, but because two earlier dams had already tamed it too well. Hoover Dam and Davis Dam, upstream on the Colorado, had blocked the river's natural sediment load, Hoover for two decades by then. Without that sediment, the riverbed below had degraded — dropped lower, become more uneven — and the existing diversion works that sent Colorado River water west into the Palo Verde Valley had become increasingly difficult to operate. The solution was another dam: earthen and rockfill, 1,850 feet long, 46 feet high, built to raise the water level just enough to keep the canals full.

## The Problem Upstream Creates

The Colorado River, before Hoover Dam closed in 1936, carried enormous quantities of sediment — silt, sand, gravel — that it had been depositing in its lower reaches for millennia. That sediment built the river's banks, filled its channel, and maintained the elevation relationships between water surface and canal intake that irrigation systems depend on. When Hoover Dam went up, then Davis Dam in 1951, the sediment stopped. The water kept flowing, but it ran clear and hungry, picking up new material from the riverbed below the dams rather than depositing what it carried. Over the following years, the channel at Palo Verde — where water had been diverted since 1877, when Thomas Blythe filed the first Colorado River water rights claim — began to sink. The original Blythe Intake had been built when the river was higher. By the 1950s, that elevation advantage was gone.

## The Structure That Filled the Gap

The Bureau of Reclamation's solution was pragmatic. The Palo Verde Dam does not store water — it has no reservoir, no lake behind it, no electricity generation. It exists solely to back up the river enough to push water into the diversion canal's four intake pipes, each 12 feet wide and 8 feet tall. A gated spillway with three 50-foot gates handles excess flow. The dam crest sits at an elevation of 283.5 feet; the structure contains roughly 175,000 cubic yards of earthen and rockfill material. Construction began in 1956 and finished in 1958. The result was that the Palo Verde Irrigation District could again divert the approximately 1,800 cubic feet of water per second it needed for a district of about 121,000 acres, some 90,000 of them farmed.

## The Valley the Water Feeds

The Palo Verde Valley — flat, hot, and improbably green — exists because of the diversion that began at this site nearly 150 years ago. The crops change with commodity prices and water agreements: alfalfa for decades, cotton when prices warranted, various vegetables and citrus depending on the year. The canal system that branches west from the dam feeds a farming economy in one of the most arid parts of California, a place where rain is genuinely rare and the river is genuinely everything. The dam is nine miles northeast of Blythe, straddling the California-Arizona border about nine miles upriver from Ehrenberg, where Bradshaw's Ferry once carried prospectors across in the 1860s. The geography is consistent; only the infrastructure changes.

## A Consequence of Consequences

The Palo Verde Dam is a useful case study in the cascading logic of water management in the American West. Each intervention creates conditions that require the next one. Hoover Dam brought power and flood control; it also eliminated sediment. Davis Dam balanced water deliveries to Mexico; it also deepened the problem for downstream diversions. Palo Verde Dam corrected what those upstream decisions had done to the riverbed near Blythe. What problems the Palo Verde Dam's existence creates for systems further downstream — or what conditions in the river itself — is a question the Bureau of Reclamation was still answering when the dam was built, and is still answering now.

## Flight Context

Located at 33.73°N, 114.51°W, approximately 9 miles northeast of Blythe on the Colorado River, which forms the California-Arizona border here.
