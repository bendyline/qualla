# Yuma Project

The first dam and reclamation project on the Colorado River, begun in 1903, transformed barren desert into one of America's most productive farming regions — but only after displacing the Quechan people who had called the land home.

**Location:** 32.8275, -114.5000

**Categories:** Irrigation, Colorado River, Bureau of Reclamation, Arizona history

## Introduction

Three and a half inches. That is the average annual rainfall in the Yuma region of southern Arizona and California — less than some cities receive in a single afternoon thunderstorm. Yet the land has a secret: a long growing season that never really ends, and a river that carries water from a thousand miles of snowpack. The Yuma Project was the federal government's wager that these two facts together could conjure a farming empire from the desert.

## A River Worth Taming

The Colorado River at Yuma is not the docile stream it appears on maps. Before dams controlled it, the river was a wandering, flooding, channel-changing force that drove steamboat captains mad and made agricultural planning nearly impossible. The Bureau of Reclamation, newly created in 1902, saw in Yuma exactly the kind of challenge its engineers had been built to solve: reliable water in a place of unreliable nature. In 1903 the project began, and by 1915 the core infrastructure — the Laguna Diversion Dam, a 53-mile system of primary canals, and 218 miles of lateral canals — was substantially complete. It was the first dam and reclamation project on the entire Colorado River, making the choices made here a template for everything that followed.

## The Land and Its People

The Quechan people had lived along the lower Colorado for centuries before American engineers began surveying the land for irrigation potential. In 1854, the United States purchased the Yuma region in the Gadsden Purchase. By 1884, the government had created the Fort Yuma Indian Reservation to settle the Quechan, confining them to a fraction of their ancestral territory. Then, in 1910, the Dawes Severalty Act opened reservation lands to white settlers, prompting years of legal disputes that the federal government ultimately resolved in its own favor. The Reservation Division of the Yuma Project was built on what had been Quechan land — a fact the project's boosters seldom mentioned and its beneficiaries rarely considered.

## Engineering in the Heat

Building levees in the Sonoran Desert summer was brutal work. Temperatures regularly exceeded 110 degrees Fahrenheit; wage strikes slowed construction; flash floods threatened everything that had been built. The Bureau of Reclamation responded to flooding by constructing rail lines along the levee tops so maintenance crews could rush to problem areas by railcar. During a major flood in 1912, those rail lines proved their worth. When the Southern Pacific Railway declined to build a similar line on the Yuma Valley levee, the Bureau simply built its own — the Yuma Valley Railway, completed in February 1915.

## What the Project Became

The Laguna Diversion Dam served the project for decades, but by the mid-twentieth century, upstream dams — Hoover and Davis — had changed the river's sediment load so significantly that the riverbed was degrading, making water diversion harder. Between 1941 and 1948 the Laguna was replaced as the project's water source by the Imperial Dam. Today the Yuma Project serves 275 farms and supplies water to over 94,000 people. The green patchwork of fields visible from the air on the Arizona-California border — winter lettuce, melon, citrus, cotton — represents more than a century of infrastructure layered atop a desert that, left to its own devices, would grow none of it.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.83°N, 114.50°W, centered on the lower Colorado River where it forms the Arizona-California border. From cruising altitude, the Yuma Project is unmistakable: a grid of irrigation canals and green agricultural fields sharply interrupts the surrounding tan desert. The Colorado River's channelized corridor is the spine of the system. Yuma International Airport (KNYL) is nearby to the south. Best viewed from 5,000 to 10,000 feet where the full geometric scale of the canal network is visible.
