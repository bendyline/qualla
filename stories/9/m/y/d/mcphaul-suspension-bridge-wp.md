# McPhaul Suspension Bridge

Built in 1929, this graceful span across the Gila River was too narrow to be legal even on the day it opened — yet it carried Arizona's traffic for nearly forty years before becoming a bridge to nowhere.

**Location:** 32.7594, -114.4206

**Categories:** Suspension bridges in Arizona, National Register of Historic Places, Abandoned structures, Bridges to nowhere

## Introduction

Even when the McPhaul Suspension Bridge was new, it was already out of date. The year was 1929, and the engineers who built it over the Gila River knew perfectly well that US highway standards required two nine-foot lanes — a minimum of eighteen feet of road surface. The McPhaul was sixteen feet wide. It was too narrow before a single car crossed it, yet there it swayed, elegant and impractical, waiting for the next four decades of desert traffic to prove the point.

## A Bridge Named for a Local Man

Henry Harrison McPhaul was a Yuma resident, one of many desert people whose lives are now footnotes to the structures that bear their names. The bridge that carries his name was built to carry Arizona Route 95 across the Gila River, connecting Yuma to communities to the north along what would eventually become US 95. At 1,184 feet in total length, it was an ambitious project for the time — a Warren-type pony truss bridge suspended from cables, its main span stretching 798 feet across the broad, muddy river. Two steel cables, each 5.75 inches in diameter and more than 1,300 feet long, did the work of keeping it aloft. For the Sonoran Desert, it was engineering on a considerable scale.

## The Logic of a Narrow Span

How does a bridge get built at a width that violates the very standards it was meant to serve? The most likely answer is cost. The Gila River corridor in the late 1920s was remote, sparsely populated, and not exactly flush with infrastructure money. A two-lane bridge — properly wide, properly certified — would have cost significantly more than the narrow span that was actually built. Traffic was light enough that a sixteen-foot deck, which functioned as a de facto one-lane bridge with wide shoulders, worked well enough for daily use. Trucks would slow. Cars would edge past each other. The desert improvised, as it always does.

## Replaced, But Not Forgotten

By 1968, the Gila River corridor had changed enough that the McPhaul Bridge's quirks were no longer charming. Modern transportation demands — heavier trucks, faster speeds, highway standards that meant something — meant the bridge had to go. A dam was built, a replacement bridge was constructed, and the river itself was rerouted. The old span was left behind, stranded from the water it had once crossed, earning its unofficial title: the Bridge to Nowhere. Rather than demolish it, preservationists and highway historians made sure it earned a spot on the National Register of Historic Places. The McPhaul Suspension Bridge still stands in the Arizona desert, a testament to practical limitations and impractical charm.

## What Remains

Abandoned bridges have a particular quality in the desert. Without traffic, without the constant vibration of passing vehicles, they become still in a way they never were during their working lives. The McPhaul's cables and towers hold nothing but desert air now; the river is somewhere else. Roadside America has catalogued it as a curiosity, and the Arizona Department of Transportation has occasionally featured it in historical writing. It is a bridge with no practical purpose, which may be why it endures — structures that serve no function become purely themselves, objects of memory and form rather than utility.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.76°N, 114.42°W, just east of Yuma along the Gila River corridor. From the air, look for the distinctive silhouette of the suspension cables and towers rising above the flat desert plain. The rerouted Gila River channel is visible nearby. Yuma International Airport (KNYL) lies about 12 miles to the southwest. Best viewed at low altitude in clear conditions, particularly in morning or late-afternoon light when the cables cast long shadows on the desert floor.
