# Blythe Mesa Solar Power Project

What began as a $6 billion dream for the world's largest solar plant collapsed into bankruptcy before rising again as a 235-megawatt photovoltaic array — one of California's major renewable energy facilities.

**Location:** 33.6500, -114.7200

**Categories:** Solar energy, California renewable energy, Mojave Desert, NextEra Energy

## Introduction

Someone had a very large idea for the desert west of Blythe. In 2010, the California Energy Commission unanimously approved a plan for a 1,000-megawatt solar thermal plant — at the time, the largest solar energy project ever proposed anywhere. Four units, 242 megawatts each, spread across 7,025 acres of Bureau of Land Management land. The project would have been a landmark of renewable energy ambition. Then the company developing it collapsed into bankruptcy, the dream shrank, and a different technology took its place. What emerged was still significant: a 235-megawatt photovoltaic power plant that hums quietly in the desert sun.

## The Rise and Fall of Solar Trust

Solar Trust of America was the company behind the original vision — a subsidiary of Germany's Solar Millennium, formed to bring European solar thermal technology to the American desert. The concept was parabolic trough technology: curved mirrors focusing sunlight onto fluid-filled tubes to generate steam and drive turbines. Chevron Energy Solutions was involved as a partner. The Department of Energy offered a $2.1 billion conditional loan guarantee in April 2011. Solar Trust rejected the offer, believing it could finance the project on better terms. This proved to be a serious misjudgment. By August 2011 the company was reconsidering its technology, by 2012 its parent company Solar Millennium was in trouble, and in April 2012 Solar Trust filed for bankruptcy.

## NextEra Takes Over

NextEra Energy Resources, a subsidiary of Florida's NextEra Energy, emerged as the top bidder for the bankrupt project's assets, acquiring them in June 2012. The new owners scaled back the plan considerably: instead of 1,000 megawatts of solar thermal on 7,025 acres, they planned a photovoltaic project — solar panels rather than mirrors — on a more modest footprint. The switch from thermal to photovoltaic reflected a broader industry shift happening at the same time: photovoltaic technology had dropped dramatically in cost while solar thermal remained expensive and operationally complex. By 2015, construction had begun on the first units.

## First Solar and Thin Film

The technology that eventually went into the Blythe Mesa project came from First Solar, a US firm specializing in cadmium telluride thin film panels — a different approach from the conventional silicon panels used in residential solar installations. Thin film panels can be manufactured at lower cost and perform better in high-heat conditions, making them well-suited to the Mojave Desert environment. Two units were commissioned in 2016: a 110-megawatt unit in April and a 125-megawatt unit in October, totaling 235 megawatts. The majority of the output was sold under 20-year power purchase agreements to Kaiser Permanente and Southern California Edison.

## The Larger Picture

The Blythe Mesa Solar Power Project sits adjacent to the 250-megawatt McCoy Solar Energy Project, together forming a combined 485-megawatt complex on the desert landscape west of Blythe. From the air, the scale is striking: thousands of acres of solar panels arranged in geometric precision on land that, a generation ago, was unremarkable desert scrub. The same Bureau of Land Management land that once seemed fit only for military maneuvers and grazing now generates power for homes across Southern California. It is not what Solar Trust envisioned. It is also not nothing.

## Flight Context

Located at 33.65°N, 114.72°W, approximately 8 miles west of Blythe. From the air, the solar arrays are unmistakably visible as large geometric fields of blue-black panels contrasting with the tan desert. The adjacent McCoy Solar Energy Project expands the visible complex further. Blythe Airport (BLH) is a few miles to the southeast. Interstate 10 runs to the north of the array.
