# Blythe Airport

Started as a Depression-era emergency strip on the LA-to-Phoenix air route, Blythe Airport became a wartime air base for General Patton's Desert Training Center and then a bomber crew school that, at its peak, held nearly 8,000 military and civilian personnel, twice the population of the town.

**Location:** 33.6147, -114.7130

**Categories:** Aviation history, World War II, California airports, Desert Training Center

## Introduction

The Civil Aeronautics Administration built the field in the late 1930s for a practical reason: pilots flying the Los Angeles to Phoenix airway needed somewhere to land in an emergency. The landscape between those cities offers almost nothing in the way of landmarks or safe landing zones — just flat desert, the Colorado River, and the occasional town too small to be useful. Blythe, at least, had a strip. A commercial airport opened there in April 1940. Within two years, General George Patton had other plans for it.

## Patton's Desert Training Center

When the United States entered World War II, Patton was tasked with preparing American soldiers to fight in the North African desert. His solution was the Desert Training Center — a vast military exercise zone spread across the California and Arizona desert, roughly 18,000 square miles of some of the most unforgiving terrain in North America. Soldiers would learn to fight in heat, dust, and deprivation before encountering those conditions in North Africa. Blythe Army Air Field was the only airstrip in the area with construction already underway when the DTC was established, making it the default air support base. For six months in 1942 it served as the sole aerial hub for Army maneuvers across an enormous desert theater.

## A City in the Desert

The Army did not do things halfway. Between 1942 and 1944, the War Department acquired more than 4,500 acres around the Blythe field, constructing over 650 buildings: hangars, barracks, warehouses, a hospital, ordnance storage, fuel depots. The base was marketed, with some military bravado, as the 'World's Healthiest Air Base.' At its December 1943 peak, nearly 8,000 uniformed and civilian personnel were stationed there. The civilian city of Blythe at that time held approximately 4,000 people. The airfield, in other words, housed twice the population of the town it was named for — a temporary city of bombers and bombardiers in the Sonoran Desert.

## B-17s and B-24s Over the Desert

Multiple bombardment groups trained at Blythe in succession. At various points, up to 75 Boeing B-17 Flying Fortresses operated from the field, their four-engine roar a constant presence over the quiet desert. Later in the war, Consolidated B-24 Liberators flew from the same runways. The field served several distinct purposes as the war progressed — first as part of Patton's desert maneuver exercises, then as a heavy bombardment crew training base for the Second Air Force, sending combat crews toward Europe and the Pacific. Through it all, 126 airmen died in accidents related to the base. By April 1944, the field was winding down; by July 1944, the Army had declared it surplus.

## The Airport That Remained

After the war, the military infrastructure was stripped away or repurposed, but the airfield itself survived. Blythe Airport covers 3,904 acres at an elevation of 399 feet, with two asphalt runways — the longer measuring 6,543 feet. Bonanza Air Lines and its successors, Air West and Hughes Airwest, flew Fairchild F-27 turboprops to Blythe, service that ended by 1979, when Hughes Airwest retired its F-27s; small commuter carriers came and went in the 1980s, and Pacific Southwest Airlines used the field for jet training flights. Riverside County has owned the airport since 1948, and today it is a general aviation facility; in 2010 it averaged about 68 aircraft operations per day. The desert silence that returned after the wartime roar is now the dominant sound — the occasional single-engine Cessna, the wind, the heat.

## Flight Context

Located at 33.61°N, 114.71°W, seven miles west of Blythe. ICAO: KBLH. Elevation 399 feet. Two asphalt runways: 8/26 (6,543 x 150 ft) and 17/35 (5,800 x 100 ft). The airport's large footprint — remnant of its wartime scale — is clearly visible from the air, contrasting with the modest general aviation operations it now supports. The Colorado River and the Arizona border lie to the east. The airport sits at the western edge of the Palo Verde Valley agricultural landscape.
