# 1974 Blythe, California Bus Crash

Before sunrise on January 15, 1974, a farm worker bus fell into a drainage canal near Blythe, California, killing 19 people — a tragedy that prompted Cesar Chavez and the United Farm Workers to demand safer conditions for agricultural laborers.

**Location:** 33.5594, -114.7094

**Categories:** Transportation disasters, Farm workers, California history, United Farm Workers

## Introduction

It was still dark when the 1955 GMC bus left Calexico, heading north through the pre-dawn cold of the California desert. The passengers — 46 farm workers, most of them green card holders, heading to a High and Mighty Farms field near Blythe — had begun their workday long before dawn. At approximately 6:30 a.m. on January 15, 1974, still before sunrise, the bus approached the intersection of 20th Avenue and Rannells Boulevard, where a sign advised 20 miles per hour. Traveling an estimated 45 to 55 miles per hour, the driver, Pablo Navarro Arellanos, missed the 90-degree turn. The bus fell into the drainage canal and landed on its left side. Nineteen people, the driver among them, drowned. Twenty-eight more were injured. The light had not yet come up on the valley.

## Who Was on the Bus

The passengers were farm workers doing what farm workers in the California desert had always done: traveling long distances before dawn to reach fields that would only open for picking during daylight. Many held green cards; some were American citizens. They came from families, not statistics — and among the nineteen who died that morning was a father and his three teenage children, whose deaths Cesar Chavez mentioned specifically in a public commentary on the crash. The bus was owned by a labor contractor named Jesus Ayala. It was a nineteen-year-old vehicle, and the seats were fastened with what investigators later described as a flimsy anchorage system. When the bus fell, the seats broke loose and slid toward the front, contributing to the deaths of Arellanos and several passengers.

## The Investigation

The National Transportation Safety Board examined the crash carefully. It found the probable cause was the driver's failure to slow the bus enough to make the turn despite the posted warning and advisory speed signs, with fatigue contributing. Arellanos also had a blood alcohol content of 0.03 percent — low, but detectable — though investigators could not determine when he had consumed alcohol. The equipment mattered too: most of the seats were torn from their mountings, and the flimsy anchorage contributed to the high death toll. Had the seats held, some of the nineteen deaths might have been prevented. Most of the injured were treated at Palo Verde Hospital in Blythe; one required care at the more distant Loma Linda facility. The canal's water depth inside the bus contributed directly to the drownings.

## What Changed After

Cesar Chavez and the United Farm Workers did not let the Blythe crash pass quietly. The accident became a focal point for their campaign demanding inspections and safer conditions on the buses used to transport agricultural laborers. By early 1975, new state rules required annual Highway Patrol inspection and safety certification of farm labor buses, limited bus drivers' working hours, and required working speedometers, with a seat-securement rule to follow. Farm worker transportation safety had long been an issue in California's fields, where labor contractors operated aging vehicles under minimal regulatory oversight. The 1974 crash, like the 1963 Chualar bus crash that killed 32 bracero workers, exposed the systemic vulnerabilities of what Chavez called "a farm labor system that treats workers like agricultural implements and not as important human beings." The people on that bus were not cargo. They were workers with families, futures, and names that the investigation recorded and history should not forget.
