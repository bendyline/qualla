# Camp Gaston

An advance outpost of the Colorado River frontier, Camp Gaston served the US Army's operations against the Mohave people in 1859 before becoming a forgotten footnote 80 miles upriver from Fort Yuma.

**Location:** 33.4332, -114.7325

**Categories:** Military history, Colorado River, California frontier, Mohave War

## Introduction

In April 1859, two companies of the 6th Infantry made camp on the west bank of the Colorado River, some 80 miles upriver from Fort Yuma. They were the advance force of the 2nd Mohave Expedition, moving against the Mohave people who had attacked a wagon train crossing their territory the previous year. The camp they established had no town nearby and no particular future — just a strategic position on a river that was, at that moment, the edge of American expansion.

## The Mohave War

The Mohave War grew from the collision of two worlds at the Colorado River crossing. The Mohave people had controlled the river valley around that crossing for centuries, and they viewed the wagon trains pushing through on their way to California with growing alarm. After a Mohave attack on an emigrant party in 1858, the US Army organized a military response. Camp Gaston was the advance base and a temporary supply post for the expedition's push upriver toward Mohave country. The 80-mile river journey by boat translated to only 45 miles overland — but overland travel in that country, without reliable water, was its own kind of gamble.

## Life on the Lower Colorado

The camp sat on the west bank of the Colorado in what is now the Palo Verde Valley, near modern Palo Verde, California in Imperial County. The river here was broad and slow, flanked by cottonwood and mesquite. Summer temperatures in the low desert regularly exceed 110 degrees Fahrenheit; the small detachments who garrisoned this outpost faced heat, dust, insects, and the particular boredom of a frontier posting far from any American settlement. Supplies and reinforcements came by steamboat from Fort Yuma — a journey that took time the desert did not make comfortable.

## Intermittent Service, Then Silence

Once the 2nd Mohave Expedition's supplies moved on to the new Fort Mojave, Camp Gaston was abandoned, but not for good. It served as an intermittent outpost of Fort Yuma until 1867, used when the Army needed a forward position on the Colorado above Fort Yuma, and garrisoned by small detachments. Its history is complicated by a naming tangle: a different military post in Northern California, on the Trinity River in the Hoopa Valley Indian Reservation, was also briefly called Camp Gaston from 1866 to early 1867, before being redesignated Fort Gaston. The Colorado River Camp Gaston is the shorter-lived and less-remembered of the two — a ghost on a riverbank that has since been reshaped by dam-building and irrigation, its exact location remembered now mainly in historical records.

## Flight Context

Located at approximately 33.43°N, 114.73°W, west of the Colorado River's present channel near Palo Verde, California. From the air, the Palo Verde Valley spreads in irrigated agricultural green east of the low desert mountains. The Colorado River is the Arizona-California border visible to the east. Blythe Airport (KBLH) lies about 13 miles to the north. The historic camp location is within the broader agricultural valley landscape.
