# Camp Gaston

An advance outpost of the Colorado River frontier, Camp Gaston served the US Army's operations against the Mohave people in 1859 before becoming a forgotten footnote 80 miles upriver from Fort Yuma.

**Location:** 33.4332, -114.7325

**Categories:** Military history, Colorado River, California frontier, Mohave War

## Introduction

In April 1859, two companies of the 6th Infantry stepped off a Colorado River steamboat and made camp on the west bank of the river, 80 miles north of Fort Yuma. They were the advance force of the 2nd Mohave Expedition, moving against the Mohave people who had attacked a wagon train crossing their territory the previous year. The camp they established had no permanent structures, no town nearby, and no particular future — just a strategic position on a river that was, at that moment, the edge of American expansion.

## The Mohave War

The Mohave War grew from the collision of two worlds at the Colorado River crossing. The Mohave people had controlled this stretch of the river for centuries, and they viewed the wagon trains pushing through on their way to California with growing alarm. After a Mohave attack on an emigrant party in 1858, the US Army organized a military response. Camp Gaston was the advance base: close enough to Mohave territory to be useful, far enough from Fort Yuma that resupply by steamboat was the only practical option. The 80-mile river journey by boat translated to only 45 miles overland — but overland travel in that country, without reliable water, was its own kind of gamble.

## Life on the Lower Colorado

The camp sat on the west bank of the Colorado in what is now the Palo Verde Valley, near modern Palo Verde, California in Imperial County. The river at this point is broad and slow, flanked by cottonwood and mesquite. Summer temperatures in the low desert regularly exceed 110 degrees Fahrenheit; the soldiers who garrisoned this outpost endured months of heat, dust, insects, and the particular boredom of a frontier posting far from anything resembling civilization. Supplies and reinforcements came by steamboat from Fort Yuma — a journey that took time the desert did not make comfortable.

## Intermittent Service, Then Silence

After the 2nd Mohave Expedition concluded, Camp Gaston did not immediately close. It remained as an intermittent outpost of Fort Yuma until 1867, used when the Army needed a forward position in the upper Colorado River corridor. Its history is complicated by a naming tangle: a different military post in Northern California, on the Trinity River in the Hoopa Valley Indian Reservation, was also briefly called Camp Gaston from 1866 to early 1867, before being redesignated Fort Gaston. The Colorado River Camp Gaston is the earlier and less-remembered of the two — a ghost on a riverbank that has since been reshaped by dam-building and irrigation, its exact location now marked only in historical records.

## Flight Context
