# Wiley's Well

In the Colorado Desert west of Blythe, a stagecoach-era well deepened by a hopeful storekeeper has become a campground, a rockhound paradise, and a desert landmark where summer temperatures can reach 130 degrees Fahrenheit.

**Location:** 33.4939, -114.8900

**Categories:** Colorado Desert, Rockhounding, California desert, BLM campgrounds

## Introduction

A.P. Wiley was a storekeeper and postmaster in Palo Verde who understood something essential about the desert: water draws people, and people spend money. In 1876, a stagecoach company digging along the Bradshaw Trail had sunk a shallow well in the Colorado Desert west of Blythe. By 1907, Wiley had deepened it, hoping to attract business to his remote store. The water table, as it turned out, had its own ideas. Within a dozen years the well had dropped to 60 feet and kept going, eventually becoming unfit for drinking. The store did not outlast the water. But the name stuck, and the place endured.

## Old Well, New Drilling

When the Bureau of Land Management decided in 1985 to develop a campground at Wiley's Well, they drilled a new well — not 60 feet, but 965 feet into the desert floor. What they found was hot: 90 degrees Fahrenheit when it surfaced, heavily mineralized, requiring a cistern to cool it to drinking temperature. The campground became one of only two developed camping areas in the Mule Mountains Long-Term Visitor Area, a sprawling BLM recreational zone in the California desert east of the Chuckwalla Mountains. Seasonal visitors from colder climates — snowbirds, in the parlance of the desert — began arriving each winter, setting up camps in the mild temperatures that make the Colorado Desert's off-season genuinely pleasant.

## Rockhound Country

The real draw for many visitors is not the water but what lies beneath the surrounding desert. In the 1930s, rockhounds discovered geode beds near Wiley's Well, and the area has been picked over and visited ever since — yet it remains surprisingly rich. Chalcedony, citrine, quartz crystals, rhyolite, and jasper all turn up in the washes and desert flats surrounding the campground. The formations here are volcanic in origin, remnants of ancient eruptions that left mineral-rich rock in the ancient lakebeds and alluvial fans of the Colorado Desert. For people who find satisfaction in splitting open a rock to find a purple crystal pocket inside, Wiley's Well is a kind of pilgrimage site.

## Summer and Winter

The desert's dual nature defines Wiley's Well. In winter, the campground fills with visitors who have fled snow and cold for mild evenings and comfortable days. In summer, the same place becomes nearly uninhabitable. Recorded temperatures at Wiley's Well have reached 130 degrees Fahrenheit — not theoretical high temperatures, but actual recorded figures. The Colorado Desert in summer is a place of serious hazard. The well's water, hot enough to need cooling before drinking, feels like a metaphor: even the water carries the heat. The Wiley's Well Road exit off Interstate 10, sixteen miles west of Blythe, leads nine miles south to the campground entrance — a drive that feels much longer in July.

## Flight Context

Located at 33.49°N, 114.89°W, in the Colorado Desert west of Blythe. From the air, the campground area sits in a flat stretch of desert east of the Chuckwalla Mountains, accessible via a road running south from Interstate 10. The surrounding landscape is typical Colorado Desert: tan flats, scattered desert scrub, dry washes. Blythe Airport (BLH) is approximately 16 miles to the east. The Wiley's Well Road exit is visible as a small interchange on I-10.
