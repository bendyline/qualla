# Sundesert Nuclear Power Plant

The Sundesert nuclear plant was proposed for the California desert near Blythe in 1976 — it was rejected in 1978, after $100 million had been spent, becoming the last major attempt to build a nuclear plant in California.

**Location:** 33.4498, -114.7830

**Categories:** Nuclear power, California energy history, Cancelled projects, Blythe California

## Introduction

Fifteen miles southwest of Blythe, California, where the desert is flat and the sky is very large and the Arizona border is close enough to see, San Diego Gas and Electric Company once planned to build the future. Two Westinghouse pressurized water reactors, each generating 974 megawatts, scheduled to come online in 1985 and 1988 — a $3 billion investment in atomic power at a site cleared by state and federal regulators for both general and earthquake safety. The site was purchased in 1975. The application went to the Nuclear Regulatory Commission in 1976. And then California said no.

## The Anti-Nuclear Road to Sundesert

California's resistance to nuclear power did not begin with Sundesert. It began in 1958 with a proposed plant near Bodega Bay, a small fishing town north of San Francisco. Pacific Gas and Electric spent six years fighting activists who objected to the location before abandoning the project — the first successful anti-nuclear campaign in the history of nuclear power, according to historian Thomas Wellock. A second fight stopped a proposed facility in Malibu's Corral Canyon in 1970. By the time Sundesert was proposed, California had already built something unusual: a functioning, organized, and experienced anti-nuclear movement with real political influence.

## Jerry Brown and the Nuclear Question

Jerry Brown was elected governor of California in 1975, the same year San Diego Gas and Electric purchased land for Sundesert. Brown was explicit about his views on nuclear power: he was against it, and he intended to act accordingly. California had also passed a state law in 1976 requiring that a solution to nuclear waste disposal be demonstrated before new plants could be constructed — a requirement that the Carter Administration's ambitious nuclear expansion plans could not easily satisfy. In April 1978, a year before the Three Mile Island accident would transform American public opinion on nuclear power, Brown stated flatly that he would never sign a bill permitting nuclear plant construction in California.

## The End of Sundesert

With the governor opposed and state law requiring answers to questions that had no answers yet, the California Energy Commission voted to halt the Sundesert project. The stated reason was the "absence of federally demonstrated and approved technology for permanent disposal of radioactive wastes" — a condition that, in 1978, no nuclear project in the country could meet. The project was cancelled in May 1978. By that point, approximately $100 million had been spent. The land sat empty in the desert southwest of Blythe, and no nuclear plant has been built in California since. Sundesert was the last attempt.

## What Might Have Been

The site where two nuclear reactors were supposed to rise by 1985 is today desert. The broader Blythe area has instead become a focus for solar energy development — the Blythe Mesa Solar Power Project and the adjacent McCoy Solar Energy Project together occupy thousands of acres of the same flat desert terrain that San Diego Gas and Electric once envisioned glowing with reactor cores. The energy source changed; the scale did not. Both the nuclear plant that was never built and the solar fields that replaced it in the regional imagination draw from the same logic: that the California desert, with its vast flat land and relentless sun, is too good an energy resource to leave alone.

## Flight Context

Located at 33.45°N, 114.78°W, approximately 15 miles southwest of Blythe. The proposed plant site lies in the flat desert terrain between the Colorado River valley and the California lowlands. From the air, compare the site to the visible solar panel arrays of the Blythe Mesa Solar Power Project nearby — the desert's energy potential finally realized in photovoltaic rather than nuclear form. Blythe Airport (BLH) is about 15 miles to the northeast.
