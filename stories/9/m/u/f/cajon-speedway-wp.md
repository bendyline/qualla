# Cajon Speedway

A quarter-mile dirt oval that grew up beside Gillespie Field and ran for four decades before a murder and an expiring lease ended it together.

**Location:** 32.8221, -116.9682

**Categories:** Motorsport, El Cajon, San Diego County, Sports History

## Introduction

The man who built Cajon Speedway also founded the club that governed it, which meant that when Steve Brucker was murdered at his El Cajon home in 2003, the track lost more than its owner. The El Cajon Stock Car Racing Association he had inherited from his father Earle Brucker Sr. was now headless, the lease was running out, and there was no obvious path forward. The speedway ran one more season without him, then went quiet. What remained was forty-three years of Saturday nights on a quarter-mile oval wedged between a county airport and a freeway — a working-class institution in a working-class corner of San Diego County.

## Dirt, Then Pavement

Earle Brucker Sr. opened the track in 1961 on land between Gillespie Field and the San Vicente Freeway, a piece of geography that gave the place its character: aircraft overhead, freeway noise in the distance, the smell of racing fuel threading through both. He called it Cajon Speedway and built the El Cajon Stock Car Racing Association to run it. The original configuration was a 0.25-mile dirt oval — intimate enough that spectators in the front rows could feel the grit on their faces when the field came through. In 1964 the track was expanded to 0.375 miles and eventually paved, which changed the racing but preserved the essential feel of the place: local drivers, local crowds, local rivalries playing out week after week.

## Champions and Regulars

Over four decades, Cajon Speedway produced a recognizable cast of regional champions. Ron Esau won the ECSCRA Super Stocks title in 1978. John Borneman Jr. — father of Johnny Borneman III, who would carry the family tradition forward — took the track championship in 1992. Ricky Johnson followed in 1995. Between 1986 and 2004, local drivers also competed for the Whelen All-American Series crown, the NASCAR-affiliated program that gave regional tracks a connection to national sanctioning. The track hosted USAC National Midget events, NASCAR Winston West Series and Southwest Series races, and USF2000 competition — a range that made it something more than a purely local operation while keeping it rooted in the community that sustained it.

## The Final Seasons

Steve Brucker had inherited both the track and its organization from his father. When he was murdered at his home in 2003 — the result of a robbery attempt, with two men eventually charged — the speedway lost the person who had been holding its future together. He had managed to call 911 before succumbing to his wounds at Sharp Memorial Hospital. The lease was set to expire in 2005, and without Brucker, there was no one positioned to negotiate a renewal or find an alternative path. The track ran its 2004 season — one last summer of modified stock cars and late-model sedans on the paved oval — and then closed. The property was eventually demolished in 2007, absorbed into the suburban sprawl that had been advancing on it from every direction.

## What the Oval Left Behind

Cajon Speedway existed in the specific tradition of American short-track racing: unpretentious, regional, sustained by people who showed up every week because they loved the noise and the competition and the community that formed around both. It was never going to produce a championship-caliber driver who became a household name, and it never did. What it produced instead was forty years of Saturday nights, a few thousand local champions whose names meant something to a few thousand people who were paying attention, and a sense — still held by those who were there — that the patch of ground between the airport and the freeway was, for a long time, where the real racing happened.
