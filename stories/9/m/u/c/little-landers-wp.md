# Little Landers

A utopian cooperative farming colony established in San Ysidro in 1909 by reformer William E. Smythe attracted about 100 families to small irrigated plots before a flood in 1916 destroyed many of the farms and hastened the experiment's end.

**Location:** 32.5530, -117.0460

**Categories:** History of San Diego, Cooperative communities, Agricultural history of California

## Introduction

William E. Smythe believed that small plots of irrigated land could liberate working people from wage labor. His idea was simple: give families an acre or two of carefully prepared ground with water rights, and they could grow enough to eat, perhaps enough to sell, and enough to build lives independent of the industrial economy that had made so many of them dependent on employers who could dismiss them without consequence. On January 11, 1909, the first Little Landers colony was inaugurated in San Ysidro, at the southern edge of California. About one hundred families had arrived by 1912. By 1918, the colony was an evident failure.

## The Man Behind the Dream

William E. Smythe was a journalist and reformer who had become convinced that irrigation was the key to democratizing American land ownership. He had written extensively about reclamation and cooperative agriculture, arguing that the technologies of controlled water distribution made it possible to farm small plots intensively — that an acre with reliable water could produce more than many acres of dryland farming.

His Little Landers concept applied these ideas to an accessible scale: plots of one to three acres near cities, where families could maintain an urban income while developing agricultural self-sufficiency. The San Ysidro colony, on the former Belcher Ranch, was the first of several Little Landers colonies, close to San Diego on the flat floor of the Tijuana River valley. The site was irrigated, platted into small plots, and advertised to working families seeking an alternative to urban tenancy.

## A Hundred Families Try

The colony grew steadily in its early years. By 1912, about a hundred families occupied the Little Landers plots, growing vegetables, flowers, and fruit and raising poultry. A clubhouse with a library and assembly room became the center of community life, with weekly meetings, lectures, and songs. The colony had the character of a genuine settlement rather than a temporary experiment: families had invested in the land, built structures, and committed to the project.

The cooperative dimension — shared infrastructure, cooperative marketing of produce, and a colony government modeled on the New England town meeting — was central to Smythe's vision. Little Landers was not simply a collection of individual farms; it was meant to demonstrate that cooperation could make small landholdings viable in ways that isolated individual effort could not. The colony attracted reformers, idealists, and ordinary working families who simply wanted a different kind of life.

## The January That Destroyed Everything

January 1916 brought rain to San Diego County in quantities that the region's landscape could not absorb. The Tijuana River, which ran through the Little Landers colony's adjacent land, flooded. The floodwaters swept across the valley floor, destroying many farms and the colony's water pumping plant.

The flood did not merely set the colony back — it broke it. The farms were destroyed at a moment when families had invested their savings and their labor in them. A new pumping plant was installed, but recovery would have required the same investment again, on land that had just demonstrated its vulnerability, and many families did not stay. In 1917 the colony's corporation, Little Landers, Incorporated, was disestablished for failure to pay taxes, and by 1918 the colony was described as an evident failure. Higher-paying wartime work also drew people away from this and the other Little Landers colonies.

## What Remained in San Ysidro

The Little Landers colony was one of several cooperative agricultural experiments established in California in the early twentieth century, and like most of them, it failed — destroyed by a combination of climatic reality, undercapitalization, and the gap between the theory of cooperative small farming and the practice of it in an agricultural landscape that rewarded scale and mechanization.

San Ysidro grew into a different kind of community, not the cooperative agricultural village that Smythe had envisioned but a dense urban neighborhood anchored by one of the world's busiest international border crossings. The flat irrigated land where Little Landers families grew vegetables is now streets, houses, and commercial buildings, a transformation that would have been difficult to imagine for the reformers who inaugurated the colony in January 1909. The name persists in the area's local memory, a reminder of an experiment in living that arrived at the wrong time in the wrong place.
