# China Airlines Flight 006

On February 19, 1985, a China Airlines Boeing 747 plunged more than 30,000 feet over the Pacific after an engine failure — surviving a dive that subjected everyone aboard to about 5 Gs before the captain regained control. The same jet was later parked for good at Tijuana's airport, just across the border from San Diego.

**Location:** 32.5440, -116.9660

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents, China Airlines accidents, 1985 in California

## Introduction

At 41,000 feet over the Pacific, about 300 nautical miles northwest of San Francisco, on the morning of February 19, 1985, engine number four on China Airlines Flight 006 flamed out. The Boeing 747SP began to lose speed. The autopilot held the wings level as long as it could, then ran out of authority. Plunging into cloud and disoriented, the crew allowed the aircraft to roll into a steep dive. By the time the captain recovered, the 747 had plunged about 31,500 feet, descending from cruise altitude to about 9,500 feet in roughly two and a half minutes, at speeds approaching the structural limits of the airframe. The aircraft had been subjected to forces of about 5 Gs. It was still flying.

## The Spiral That Nearly Ended It

China Airlines Flight 006 was a scheduled service from Taipei to Los Angeles, flying across the North Pacific in the standard route that takes aircraft north toward Alaska before curving south toward California. The 747SP — a special performance variant of the 747, with a shorter fuselage and extended range — was at cruise altitude, ten hours into the flight, when engine four lost power.

The crew attempted to restart the engine. During this process, the aircraft's speed decayed, the autopilot attempted to maintain altitude by increasing pitch, and the plane entered a runaway sequence: one wing dropped, the nose pitched down, and the aircraft entered a high-speed spiral dive. Inside the clouds over the Pacific, spatially disoriented, the captain took the attitude indicators' alarming readings for an instrument failure and did not recognize the dive for what it was. By the time he broke out of the clouds at about 11,000 feet, saw the horizon and arrested the descent, the aircraft had fallen nearly six miles.

## The Passengers and the G-Forces

Inside the cabin, the dive was violent. The aircraft was subjected to structural loads far beyond its normal operating envelope — up to five times the force of gravity — as the crew pulled out of the dive.

Twenty-four people were injured, two of them seriously: a passenger with a broken foot and a flight attendant with an acute back strain. That no one died is largely a testament to the structural engineering of the 747, which was built with safety margins that could absorb extraordinary loads. The aircraft did sustain structural damage — the wings were permanently bent upward, large parts of the horizontal stabilizer were torn away, and the left outboard elevator was lost — but it held together through forces that were never supposed to occur in commercial service.

## Diversion to San Francisco

Having leveled off at about 9,500 feet, the crew assessed the aircraft. Engine four had been restarted, and they initially planned to continue to Los Angeles. Then they noticed that landing gear was hanging down and one hydraulic system was empty; with the extra drag, there was not enough fuel to reach Los Angeles. They declared an emergency and diverted to San Francisco International Airport.

The flight landed without further incident. Post-flight inspection revealed that the aircraft had sustained damage consistent with the extreme forces it had experienced. The structural assessment required significant repair before the aircraft could return to service. The 24 injured people received treatment; none of the injuries were fatal.

## What the Investigation Found

The National Transportation Safety Board investigated and identified the probable cause: the captain's preoccupation with an inflight malfunction and his failure to monitor properly the airplane's flight instruments, which resulted in his losing control of the airplane. Attention in the cockpit had gone to the engine and the falling airspeed rather than to the aircraft's attitude and trajectory.

The NTSB specifically cited the captain's over-reliance on the autopilot after the loss of thrust on the No. 4 engine — the autopilot masked the approaching loss of control — as a contributing factor. The case became a reference point in aviation training for crew resource management and for the dangers of automation complacency: the tendency of pilots to trust automated systems so completely that they fail to monitor what those systems are doing. Flight 006 survived because the 747's engineering margins were extraordinary. It came close enough to the edge that no such margin should have been needed.

## Flight Context

This story is pinned at 32.544°N, 116.966°W, Tijuana International Airport (MMTJ), just south of the border, where the incident aircraft, N4522V, was ferried in December 2005 after a later operator was grounded, and where it was still parked as of 2024. The upset itself happened about 300 nautical miles northwest of San Francisco. Brown Field (KSDM) lies just across the border to the north, and San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 18 miles northwest.
