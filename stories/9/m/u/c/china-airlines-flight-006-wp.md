# China Airlines Flight 006

On February 19, 1985, a China Airlines Boeing 747 plunged 30,000 feet over the Pacific after an engine failure — surviving a dive that subjected passengers to 5 Gs before the crew regained control near San Diego.

**Location:** 32.5440, -116.9660

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents, China Airlines accidents, 1985 in California

## Introduction

At 41,000 feet over the North Pacific on the night of February 19, 1985, engine number four on China Airlines Flight 006 flamed out. The Boeing 747SP began to lose speed. The autopilot compensated, then overcompensated. In the darkness and disorientation, the crew allowed the aircraft to enter a spiral dive. By the time they recovered, the 747 had plunged 30,000 feet, descending from cruise altitude to 11,000 feet in approximately two and a half minutes, at speeds approaching the structural limits of the airframe. The aircraft had been subjected to forces of nearly 5 Gs. It was still flying.

## The Spiral That Nearly Ended It

China Airlines Flight 006 was a scheduled service from Taipei to Los Angeles, flying across the North Pacific in the standard route that takes aircraft north toward Alaska before curving south toward California. The 747SP — a special performance variant of the 747, with a shorter fuselage and extended range — was at cruise altitude in the early morning hours when engine four lost power.

The crew attempted to restart the engine. During this process, the aircraft's speed decayed, the autopilot attempted to maintain altitude by increasing pitch, and the plane entered an unrecoverable sequence: one wing dropped, the nose pitched down, and the aircraft entered a high-speed spiral dive. In the darkness over the Pacific, in conditions of spatial disorientation, the crew did not immediately recognize the dive for what it was. By the time they took manual control and arrested the descent, the aircraft had fallen nearly six miles.

## The Passengers and the G-Forces

Inside the cabin, the dive was violent. Passengers not buckled in were thrown against the ceiling and the overhead compartments. Luggage fell. Serving carts were pinned against the cabin wall by the acceleration. The aircraft was subjected to structural loads far beyond its normal operating envelope — up to five times the force of gravity — as the crew pulled out of the dive.

Twenty-four people were injured, two of them seriously. That the death toll was not catastrophic is largely a testament to the structural engineering of the 747, which was built with safety margins that could absorb extraordinary loads. The aircraft did sustain structural damage — investigators later found evidence of significant stress on the airframe — but it held together through forces that were never supposed to occur in commercial service.

## Diversion to San Francisco

Having recovered from the dive at approximately 11,000 feet, the crew assessed the aircraft. Engine four had been restarted. The airframe appeared to be intact. The flight continued toward the California coast, but rather than proceeding to Los Angeles, the crew diverted to San Francisco International Airport, where emergency services had been alerted.

The flight landed without further incident. Post-flight inspection revealed that the aircraft had sustained damage consistent with the extreme forces it had experienced. The structural assessment required significant repair before the aircraft could return to service. The 24 injured passengers received treatment; none of the injuries were fatal.

## What the Investigation Found

The National Transportation Safety Board investigated the incident and identified the probable cause: the flight crew's failure to monitor the aircraft's flight instruments and their subsequent failure to recognize and recover from an unusual attitude in a timely manner. The crew had become focused on the engine restart procedure and had not maintained awareness of the aircraft's attitude and trajectory.

The NTSB specifically cited the crew's over-reliance on the autopilot — an assumption that the automated system would handle the aircraft while they addressed the engine — as a contributing factor. The case became a reference point in aviation training for crew resource management and for the dangers of automation complacency: the tendency of pilots to trust automated systems so completely that they fail to monitor what those systems are doing. Flight 006 survived because the 747's engineering margins were extraordinary. It came close enough to the edge that no such margin should have been needed.

## Flight Context

The incident occurred at approximately 32.544°N, 116.966°W over the North Pacific before the crew recovered and diverted to San Francisco. The aircraft was descending toward the Southern California coast when control was regained. San Diego's airspace lies below the North Pacific routes that carry transoceanic traffic from Asia; San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 20 miles west of the incident recovery location. The Pacific approach corridors used by Asia-Pacific flights pass through this region at cruise altitude.
