# Rancho Jamul

An 8,926-acre land grant given to Pío Pico in 1829, abandoned after a raid in 1837, and subject to decades of legal dispute after the Mexican-American War — today it is the Rancho Jamul Ecological Reserve.

**Location:** 32.6700, -116.8300

**Categories:** Ranchos of San Diego County, California, East County, San Diego County, 1831 establishments in Alta California

## Introduction

Pío Pico received his land grant for Rancho Jamul in 1829 and spent the next several decades watching others fight over it. His brother Andrés ran the ranch, then a raid by Native people in 1837 forced its abandonment. His brother-in-law claimed to sell it on his behalf in 1851 without clear authority. The American land commission rejected Pico's ownership claim in 1855 for lack of documentation; Pico said his papers had been lost in a raid on the rancho. By the time the dust settled, the rancho was in other hands entirely — but the land itself, more than a century and a half later, has reverted to something closer to its original character.

## The Grant and Its First Occupants

Governor José María de Echeandía granted Rancho Jamul to Pío Pico in 1829, covering 8,926 acres extending from present-day Jamul southeast to Dulzura in the hills east of Chula Vista. Governor Manuel Victoria reconfirmed the grant in 1831, and Pío's brother Andrés Pico managed the ranch from 1836 to 1838.

In 1837, the rancho was attacked by Native raiders. The attack was significant enough that the Pico family abandoned the property entirely. The attack later became central to the legal proceedings around the rancho's ownership: Pico's claim before the American land commission included the explanation that his papers had been lost in a raid on the rancho, and the family's 1837 abandonment of the property later counted against claims to it. The commission was not persuaded, rejecting the claim in 1855 on the grounds of insufficient documentation.

## Sold Without Authority

In 1851, John Forster — Pico's brother-in-law and the man to whom Governor Pico had granted the much larger Rancho de la Nación — claimed to be acting as Pico's agent and sold Rancho Jamul to Bonifacio Lopez, Philip Crosthwaite, Richard Rust, and William E. Rust. Nothing appears ever to have been done by anyone to carry out the contract. The sale existed on paper but not in practice.

This paper sale became the seed of decades of litigation. When American courts began processing California land claims after the Mexican-American War, the question of who actually owned Rancho Jamul was genuinely tangled: Pico claimed original title but lacked documentation; the Forster-facilitated sale created competing claims; and the American legal system had to sort through Spanish, Mexican, and American legal concepts to determine what any of it meant.

## María Ruiz de Burton and the Long Litigation

The figure who fought most tenaciously for the rancho was María Amparo Ruiz de Burton, who arrived at Rancho Jamul in 1854 as the wife of Captain Henry Stanton Burton of the First New York Volunteers. Her husband purchased various interests in the property during the 1850s, and after his death she continued to press the legal case for the family's title.

María Ruiz de Burton was not a passive figure in the litigation. She arranged, in 1870, for Pío Pico himself to declare that he had sold his interest to Burton — a declaration that added a documentary basis to the claim that the 1876 patent reflected. She is better known to history as the author of the novel The Squatter and the Don, a critique of Anglo-American treatment of California's Mexican landowners — and the Jamul litigation she lived through was the raw material from which that critique was built. Mortgages and claims against the estate kept the litigation going for years; the title changed hands repeatedly, and Henry Burton's estate was not finally settled until 1891.

## From Litigation to Ecological Reserve

The rancho passed through multiple owners after the Burton family's tenure, including John D. Spreckels, the San Diego sugar magnate, who sold it in 1915, and the Daley family of San Diego, who ranched and farmed it from 1929 until selling it to the state. Between 1998 and 2001, the California Department of Fish and Wildlife purchased what remained of the original grant lands.

The Rancho Jamul Ecological Reserve now encompasses roughly 5,600 acres of oak woodland, chaparral, coastal sage scrub and grassland, with further expansions added in recent years, in the hills east of Chula Vista — a landscape that the Pico family would recognize in its basic character, if not its legal status. The reserve protects habitat for California gnatcatchers, mountain lions, and a range of other species adapted to San Diego County's inland hills. The land that was attacked in 1837, litigated in court for decades, sold by figures who may or may not have had authority to sell it, is now managed for the wildlife that never participated in any of those transactions.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.67°N, 116.83°W in the hills east of Chula Vista, in San Diego County's backcountry. The Rancho Jamul Ecological Reserve occupies rolling oak woodland and chaparral terrain that is visible from altitude as undeveloped hills east of the South Bay urban area. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is about 21 miles west-northwest. The contrast between the developed South Bay coastal plain and the undeveloped eastern hills is clearly visible from cruising altitude.
