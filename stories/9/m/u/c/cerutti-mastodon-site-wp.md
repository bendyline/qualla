# Cerutti Mastodon site

Mastodon bones found during a 1992 freeway expansion in San Diego County were dated to 130,700 years ago — potentially the oldest evidence of hominins in the Americas, and one of the most controversial findings in American archaeology.

**Location:** 32.6636, -117.0617

**Categories:** Paleontology in California, Pleistocene California, Pre-Clovis archaeological sites in the Americas, Mastodons

## Introduction

A freeway expansion crew in 1992 was widening State Route 54 in San Diego County when the excavator blade hit something in the substrate nine feet down: bones. Richard Cerutti, a San Diego Natural History Museum paleontologist monitoring the work, recognized what they were. What he found would eventually be dated to 130,700 years ago — more than 100,000 years before the generally accepted timeline for human arrival in the Americas. If the subsequent interpretation is correct, something was here, doing something deliberate, at a time when mainstream science says no humans were in the Americas.

## What Was Found in the Ground

The excavation of the Cerutti Mastodon site between 1992 and 1993 recovered the fossil remains of a juvenile male Mammut americanum — an American mastodon. The specimen (SDNHM 49926) included two tusks, three molars, four vertebrae, sixteen ribs, and more than 300 additional bone fragments.

Alongside the mastodon bones lay five cobblestones — rounded rocks foreign to the fine-grain sandy sediment in which everything was buried. The cobblestones showed what researchers described as use-wear and impact marks: flaking, battering, and the kind of damage seen on stones used as tools. Also present were bones from dire wolves, horses, camels, mammoths, and ground sloths — a cross-section of the Pleistocene megafauna that once roamed Southern California.

Uranium-thorium dating of the mastodon bones gave a date of approximately 130,700 years ago, plus or minus 9,400 years. At that date, anatomically modern humans had not yet spread across Asia, let alone reached the Americas.

## The Interpretation That Upended Everything

The research team, led by Steven Holen and Thomas Deméré, concluded that the cobblestones had been used by hominins as hammerstones and anvils to break the mastodon bones — most likely to extract marrow or to obtain raw bone material for tool production. The spiral fracturing patterns in the bones, they argued, matched the patterns seen in experimentally broken fresh bone rather than the crushing or weathering damage that would come from natural causes.

If correct, this meant some form of hominin — not necessarily modern Homo sapiens, but some member of the human lineage — was present in the Americas 130,700 years ago. The current scientific consensus places the first human arrivals in the Americas at between 15,000 and 26,000 years ago, with some pre-Clovis sites pushing that estimate earlier, but none approaching 130,000 years. The Cerutti findings, if accepted, would require a complete reconstruction of human migration history.

## The Scientific Pushback

The response from the archaeological community was largely skeptical. Critics raised several specific concerns. No unequivocal stone tools — the shaped, worked flints and obsidians that definitively indicate intentional manufacture — were found at the site. No human bones were recovered. The taphonomic evidence typically required to establish that a site represents human activity rather than natural accumulation was absent or disputed.

Michael R. Waters, one of the leading researchers on early American archaeology, commented that without unequivocal stone artifacts, the site was 'likely just an interesting paleontological locality.' A former Caltrans land surveyor suggested an alternative explanation: that the heavy construction equipment used during the freeway expansion could have dragged cobbles from elsewhere and fragmented bones, producing damage that mimicked tool use.

Chris Stringer, while noting that the evidence deserved serious scrutiny rather than dismissal, framed the fundamental problem: 'Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.'

## An Open Question Under the Freeway

A 2020 follow-up study found bone micro-residues on two of the cobblestones, with residues concentrated on the impact surfaces rather than distributed uniformly — a pattern the authors argued was inconsistent with environmental contamination and consistent with intentional contact between stone and bone. The residues appeared to be covered by carbonate crust that formed over thousands of years, suggesting they dated to the site's formation rather than to modern disturbance. Skeptics remained unconvinced.

The Cerutti Mastodon site occupies a specific and peculiar position in American archaeology: a claim so large that it cannot be confirmed by the available evidence, but also one that has not been definitively refuted. The bones are dated. The cobblestones are real. The interpretation of what happened between them remains, nearly a decade after publication, genuinely unsettled. Below a Southern California freeway, the question sits in the sediment.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.664°N, 117.062°W in San Diego County, near the State Route 54 corridor in the South Bay area of the San Diego metro. The site itself is not visible from altitude — it was found nine feet underground during freeway construction. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 9 miles west-northwest. San Diego Bay is visible to the west on clear days from higher altitudes.
