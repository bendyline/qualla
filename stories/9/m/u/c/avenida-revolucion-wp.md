# Avenida Revolución

Tijuana's famous main drag has hosted American tourists since Prohibition, given the world the Caesar salad, and once claimed the largest bar on earth — a street that has always been many things to many people.

**Location:** 32.5300, -117.0400

**Categories:** Streets in Mexico, Tijuana, Tourism in Mexico

## Introduction

On July 4, 1924, an Italian immigrant named Caesar Cardini ran short of ingredients in his Tijuana kitchen and improvised a salad from what he had: romaine, eggs, olive oil, Worcestershire sauce, parmesan. He prepared it tableside — a theatrical flourish that became the dish's signature. The Caesar salad was born on Avenida Revolución, and it went on to become one of the most widely eaten restaurant dishes on earth. The street that produced it has always been better at casual invention than at receiving credit for what it creates.

## Prohibition and the Street That Prospered

Avenida Revolución owes its modern character to a political decision made in Washington, D.C. When Prohibition took effect in the United States in 1920, Southern Californians seeking alcohol crossed the border in numbers that transformed Tijuana's commercial district. The street — originally called Avenida Olvera before being renamed to honor the Mexican Revolution — became lined with bars, restaurants, and entertainment venues catering to American visitors who brought dollars and thirst.

The first paved road in Tijuana ran along this corridor, a practical advantage in a border city where rain turned unpaved streets to mud. By the mid-1920s, Avenida Revolución had established itself as the axis of Tijuana's tourist economy, a function it has maintained for a century despite enormous changes in what tourists seek and what the city offers them.

## La Ballena and the Art of Excess

For a period in the mid-twentieth century, Avenida Revolución was home to what was promoted as the largest bar in the world. La Ballena — The Whale — occupied an enormous ground-floor space and served customers at a bar that stretched what seemed like the length of a city block. Whether the superlative was technically accurate mattered less than the impression it created: this was a place that took its hospitality seriously, at a scale commensurate with the American appetite for Mexican excess.

La Ballena is gone, but the tradition of the large, loud, unapologetically theatrical bar persists on Avenida Revolución. The street has always catered to visitors who want an experience as much as a drink — who come to Tijuana for the performance of being somewhere different, somewhere slightly outside the rules that govern daily life in San Diego and Los Angeles.

## A Salad and Its Table

Caesar Cardini's restaurant occupied a corner on the street that became one of the most famous eating establishments in Mexico. His Caesar salad spread northward into California in the 1920s and 1930s, promoted by celebrities and socialites who encountered it at the source and then demanded it at home. By the time Julia Child wrote about her childhood memories of dining at Caesar's — tasting the salad prepared tableside by Cardini himself — the dish had already circled the globe.

The restaurant still operates, more than a century after its founding. Waiters still prepare the Caesar salad tableside, in the tradition established by the founder. The fact that this tradition has been maintained through multiple ownership changes, through the closure and reopening the 9/11 recession caused, and through a century of competition from every other restaurant on earth, says something about the staying power of a dish invented in a moment of improvisation.

## The Street Today

Avenida Revolución has changed character repeatedly across its century of tourist commerce. The farmacias that drew Americans seeking medications without prescriptions, the nightclubs aimed at college students on spring break, the souvenir shops offering painted pottery and papier-mâché donkeys — different eras have put different things on display, and the street has accommodated them all.

In recent years, the street has seen investment in higher-end restaurants, craft breweries, and cultural venues that cater to a more diverse visitor base, including Mexican nationals from other parts of the country for whom Tijuana represents not just a border city but a genuine destination. The transformation is ongoing and incomplete — the old tourist economy and the new creative economy coexist in the same blocks, sometimes in the same buildings. The street is still defining itself, as it always has been.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.53°N, 117.04°W in downtown Tijuana, Mexico. Avenida Revolución runs north-south through Tijuana's commercial center. The US-Mexico border crossing at San Ysidro — one of the busiest land border crossings in the world — is approximately 2 miles north. From altitude, the dense urban fabric of Tijuana is clearly visible south of the border. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 18 miles north.
