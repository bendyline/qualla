# International Boundary Wastewater Treatment Plant

Authorized in 1989 and completed in 1997, this U.S.-built plant was designed to treat 25 million gallons of Tijuana wastewater a day — a landmark in cross-border environmental cooperation that still can't fully keep up with demand.

**Location:** 32.5430, -117.0640

**Categories:** Wastewater treatment, US-Mexico border, Environmental infrastructure

## Introduction

For decades before the plant opened, untreated sewage from Tijuana flowed across the international boundary and into the Pacific Ocean, contaminating beaches in Imperial Beach and San Diego and periodically triggering public health emergencies that closed swimming areas for weeks at a time. The problem was well understood. The solution required both countries to agree on who would build it, who would pay for it, and who would operate it. The agreement took years to reach. When the International Boundary Wastewater Treatment Plant — officially the South Bay International Wastewater Treatment Plant — came online in spring 1997, it became the centerpiece of a binational effort to treat Tijuana's sewage on the U.S. side of the line.

## A Problem That Required Two Countries

The Tijuana River drains a watershed that spans the US-Mexico boundary, but its pollution load originates predominantly on the Mexican side. As Tijuana grew rapidly through the late twentieth century, its sewage infrastructure consistently struggled to keep pace. During rainfall events, stormwater overwhelmed treatment systems, and untreated sewage entered the river and its tributaries, crossing the international boundary and reaching the Pacific at Imperial Beach.

The affected communities — Imperial Beach, San Diego's southernmost neighborhoods, and the Tijuana Estuary — had no mechanism to independently address a problem that originated in another country. The solution required federal-level action: the United States Congress authorized the construction of a treatment facility in 1989, and a formal agreement between the US and Mexico was signed in 1990, establishing the framework for who would build, operate, and fund the plant.

## Building on 75 Acres at the Boundary

The plant was constructed on 75 acres of land west of San Ysidro in the Tijuana River Valley, just north of the border and a few miles upstream of the river mouth. The site selection reflected both geography — the river flows past this area on its way to the estuary and the ocean — and jurisdiction, placing the American-funded facility within US territory while processing wastewater from Mexico.

Construction proceeded through the mid-1990s and the plant was completed in spring 1997. The facility was built to treat up to 25 million gallons of Tijuana wastewater daily, drawing the flow from a collection system that captures sewage before it crosses the border uncontrolled. The United States paid the majority of the construction cost — a significant investment in infrastructure located to address a problem that, left unmanaged, would continue to impose costs on American beaches and coastal ecosystems.

## Capacity and Its Limits

The plant's 25-million-gallon daily capacity addressed the chronic baseline pollution that had made the Tijuana River a public health concern under normal conditions. But Tijuana's population has continued to grow, and the gap between the city's sewage production and its treatment infrastructure has not closed. During significant rainfall events, when stormwater combines with sewage flows to exceed the capacities of collection systems and treatment plants on both sides of the border, untreated material still reaches the river.

The plant itself also fell behind: by the mid-2020s it was described as only intermittently working, and in 2024 work began on a roughly $600 million rehabilitation and expansion. A fast-tracked first step raised its capacity from 25 to 35 million gallons a day by September 2025, with 50 million or more targeted for around 2027. The result is a pattern that persists: long beach closures at Imperial Beach — the shoreline nearest the river mouth was closed in December 2021 and was still closed in the fall of 2026 — public health advisories about water contact, and ongoing debates about what level of additional investment would be required to address the problem more completely. The IBWTP eased the chronic baseline problem, which was itself significant. The storm events, and the failures of aging pipes and pumps on both sides of the border, represent a harder and more expensive challenge that remains.

## An Ongoing Binational Partnership

The International Boundary and Water Commission, the binational agency whose roots go back to an 1889 boundary commission and which manages shared water resources between the United States and Mexico, owns the plant through its U.S. Section. The commission's role in managing shared waterways along the nearly 2,000-mile border gives it jurisdiction over a facility that sits at one of the most complex points in that boundary — where an international river crosses a line that both divides and connects two major metropolitan areas.

The IBWTP represents a model of what international environmental cooperation can accomplish and where it falls short. The political will and financial commitment that produced the plant in the 1990s addressed the most visible manifestation of Tijuana's sewage crisis. The underlying dynamic — a rapidly growing city, infrastructure investment that lags behind population growth, and a river that doesn't respect national boundaries — continues to require attention, funding, and ongoing collaboration between two governments with different priorities and resources.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.543°N, 117.064°W west of San Ysidro near the Tijuana River, close to the US-Mexico border. The plant's infrastructure occupies a 75-acre site in the Tijuana River Valley. The Tijuana Estuary and the Pacific lie about 3.5 miles west. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 15 miles north-northwest. From altitude, the treatment plant's tanks are identifiable among the open fields of the river valley.
