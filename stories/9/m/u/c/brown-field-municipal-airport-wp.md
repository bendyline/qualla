# Brown Field Municipal Airport

Opened as a military airfield in 1918, Brown Field is where a strange turboprop Navy fighter first successfully transitioned from vertical to horizontal flight in 1954 — and where a tragic crash took the lives of members of Reba McEntire's band in 1991.

**Location:** 32.5720, -116.9800

**Categories:** Airports in California, Aviation history of the United States, San Diego County, California

## Introduction

On November 2, 1954, a peculiar aircraft lifted off from Brown Field and rose straight into the air like a helicopter — then tilted forward and flew like a fixed-wing plane. The Convair XFY-1 Pogo was the Navy's attempt to create a fighter that could take off vertically from the deck of any ship, requiring no runway, no carrier, no special infrastructure. The Pogo's test pilot, James "Skeets" Coleman, completed that first successful transition from vertical to horizontal flight right here, at what was then Naval Auxiliary Air Station Brown Field, near the Mexican border. The aircraft went nowhere as a practical design, but for a few months in 1954, Brown Field hosted aviation history.

## From Army Field to Municipal Airport

Brown Field began as East Field in 1918, part of the United States Army's network of military airfields established during World War I. The location — on the flat mesa south of San Diego near the Mexican border — offered open ground and consistent weather. The Navy took over the field in 1943 and that year renamed it Brown Field, honoring Commander Melville S. Brown, a Navy aviator killed in a 1936 plane crash near Descanso, California.

After World War II, the Navy handed the field to San Diego County, then reopened it during the Korean War; the Pogo flew from Naval Auxiliary Air Station Brown Field. In 1962 the Navy transferred the airfield to the City of San Diego on condition that it remain a public airport. Today, Brown Field Municipal Airport operates with a 7,972-foot primary runway, designated KSDM by ICAO and SDM by the FAA and IATA — long enough to accommodate a range of aircraft types. The airport serves general aviation, charter operations, and training flights, and as a port of entry it sees a steady stream of aircraft arriving on international flights from Baja California.

## The Pogo's Impossible Geometry

The Convair XFY-1 Pogo was designed to solve a problem that seemed fundamental to naval air power: aircraft carriers were enormously expensive to build and maintain, and only a small number of ships could be fitted with flight decks. What if fighters could take off vertically, directly from the deck of any ship? The Pogo was one attempt at that answer.

The aircraft sat on its tail, nose pointing skyward, its contra-rotating propellers spinning to lift the plane vertically. Once airborne, the pilot tilted forward and transitioned to conventional wing-borne flight. The landing required the reverse — tilting back to vertical while descending onto the tail, essentially blind to the landing surface below. The engineering worked; the practicality did not. The transition maneuver was extremely demanding to fly, and faster jet fighters made the turboprop Pogo obsolete before it could enter service. But on one November day in 1954, at Brown Field, the whole maneuver worked.

## A March Night in 1991

On March 16, 1991, a chartered Hawker jet carrying members of Reba McEntire's band crashed into a mountain near Brown Field, killing all ten people aboard: seven band members, her tour manager, and the two pilots. The flight had departed from Brown Field in the early-morning darkness; the crash occurred during the climb out, as the aircraft struck Otay Mountain northeast of the airport.

The deaths devastated McEntire personally and professionally, and the crash became one of the most widely reported tragedies in country music history. The National Transportation Safety Board blamed improper flight planning, the failure to maintain enough altitude and clearance over mountainous terrain, and the co-pilot's failure to adequately monitor the flight. McEntire memorialized the eight members of her band and crew who died in her next album, For My Broken Heart.

## Training at the Border

Brown Field's location near the Mexican border has given it a particular role in aviation training. In the mid-to-late 1960s, PSA (Pacific Southwest Airlines) trained its own pilots at Brown Field and also held a contract to train Lufthansa pilots there, until Lufthansa's training moved to Phoenix in 1970. The arrangement reflected a broader pattern: border region airports often have useful attributes that don't exist at more congested urban facilities.

Today, flight training remains a significant portion of Brown Field's activity. Student pilots learning instrument procedures, cross-country navigation, and multi-engine techniques use the airport's runways and the airspace that surrounds it. The border proximity adds a layer of procedure — international flight planning, customs requirements for Mexico transits — that gives students experience unavailable at purely domestic airports.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.572°N, 116.980°W south of San Diego, approximately 1.5 miles north of the Mexican border, with Tijuana's international airport just across the line. Brown Field (KSDM) has a 7,972-foot primary runway and is used primarily for general aviation and training. The airport is clearly visible from altitude on the flat, built-up Otay Mesa. Otay Mountain, where the 1991 crash occurred, rises to the northeast of the field. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 16 miles northwest.
