# Brown Field Municipal Airport

A military airfield since 1918, Brown Field is where a strange Navy jet first successfully transitioned from vertical to horizontal flight in 1954 — and where a tragic crash took the lives of members of Reba McEntire's band in 1991.

**Location:** 32.5720, -116.9800

**Categories:** Airports in California, Aviation history of the United States, San Diego County, California

## Introduction

On November 2, 1954, a peculiar aircraft lifted off from Brown Field and rose straight into the air like a helicopter — then tilted forward and flew like a fixed-wing plane. The Convair XFY-1 Pogo was the Navy's attempt to create a fighter that could take off vertically from the deck of any ship, requiring no runway, no carrier, no special infrastructure. The Pogo's test pilot, James Coleman, completed that first successful transition from vertical to horizontal flight right here, at a regional airport near the Mexican border. The aircraft went nowhere as a practical design, but for a few months in 1954, Brown Field hosted aviation history.

## From Army Field to Municipal Airport

Brown Field began as East Field in 1918, part of the United States Army's network of military airfields established during World War I. The location — on flat terrain south of San Diego near the Mexican border — offered clear approaches and consistent weather. The field was renamed Brown Field in 1943, honoring Melville Brown, a San Diego pilot who was killed in World War I.

After World War II, the airport transitioned to civilian and general aviation use, though it retained ties to military aviation operations for years. Today, Brown Field Municipal Airport operates with a 7,972-foot primary runway, designated KSDM in the FAA system and SDM by IATA — long enough to accommodate a range of aircraft types. The airport serves general aviation, charter operations, and training flights, with a steady stream of aircraft crossing the border on legitimate international flights to and from Baja California.

## The Pogo's Impossible Geometry

The Convair XFY-1 Pogo was designed to solve a problem that seemed fundamental to naval air power: aircraft carriers were enormously expensive to build and maintain, and only a small number of ships could be fitted with flight decks. What if fighters could take off vertically, directly from the deck of any ship? The Pogo was one attempt at that answer.

The aircraft sat on its tail, engines pointing skyward, propellers spinning to lift the plane vertically. Once airborne, the pilot tilted forward and transitioned to conventional wing-borne flight. The landing required the reverse — tilting back to vertical while descending onto the tail, essentially blind to the landing surface below. The engineering worked; the practicality did not. The transition maneuver was extremely demanding to fly, and the development of jet aircraft made the piston-engined Pogo obsolete before it could enter service. But for one November day in 1954, at Brown Field, it flew.

## A March Night in 1991

On March 16, 1991, a charter aircraft carrying members of Reba McEntire's band crashed into a mountain near Brown Field, killing all seven band members and the tour manager aboard. The flight had departed from Brown Field; the crash occurred during the climb out, as the aircraft struck Otay Mountain in conditions of low visibility.

The deaths devastated McEntire personally and professionally, and the crash became one of the most widely reported tragedies in country music history. It prompted investigations into charter aviation safety standards and contributed to discussions about requirements for experienced flight crews on charter operations. A memorial for the eight people who died was established at Brown Field, where their final flight began.

## Training at the Border

Brown Field's location near the Mexican border has given it a particular role in aviation training. PSA (Pacific Southwest Airlines) used the airport to train Lufthansa pilots during a period when the German carrier needed a facility offering good weather, long runways, and relatively uncrowded airspace — all of which Brown Field provided. The arrangement reflected a broader pattern: border region airports often have useful attributes that don't exist at more congested urban facilities.

Today, flight training remains a significant portion of Brown Field's activity. Student pilots learning instrument procedures, cross-country navigation, and multi-engine techniques use the airport's runways and the airspace that surrounds it. The border proximity adds a layer of procedure — international flight planning, customs requirements for Mexico transits — that gives students experience unavailable at purely domestic airports.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.572°N, 116.980°W south of San Diego, approximately 3 miles north of the Mexican border. Brown Field (KSDM) has a 7,972-foot primary runway and is used primarily for general aviation and training. The airport is clearly visible from altitude as one of the few large paved surfaces in the otherwise hilly terrain southeast of San Diego. Otay Mountain, where the 1991 crash occurred, rises to the east of the field. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 15 miles northwest.
