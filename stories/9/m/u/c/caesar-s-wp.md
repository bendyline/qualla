# Caesar's

The Tijuana restaurant where Caesar Cardini invented the Caesar salad on July 4, 1924, still prepares the dish tableside in the tradition established by its Italian immigrant founder.

**Location:** 32.5321, -117.0371

**Categories:** Restaurants in Mexico, Organizations based in Tijuana, Caesar salad

## Introduction

Julia Child remembered the experience her whole life. As a child, she had eaten at Caesar's restaurant in Tijuana and watched Caesar Cardini himself prepare a Caesar salad at her table — the dressing built ingredient by ingredient in a large wooden bowl, the lettuce tossed with authority, the result transferred to her plate with the care of something that mattered. She wrote about it in 1975, fifty years later, still precise in her recollection. That a child's restaurant memory remained so vivid across half a century says something about what happened at the table.

## The Italians Who Founded It

Cesare Cardini and his brother Alessandro — known as Cesar and Alex — were Italian immigrants who opened their restaurant on Avenida Revolución at a moment when Tijuana was experiencing an economic boom. Prohibition had taken effect in the United States in 1920, and Southern Californians were crossing the border in large numbers seeking alcohol, restaurants, and entertainment unavailable at home.

The early establishment was called 'Caesar's Place' or 'Caesar's Franco-Italian Restaurant,' a name that positioned it somewhere between the French culinary tradition that dominated fine dining and the Italian heritage of its owners. By 1931, business had grown enough that the Cardinis moved to a larger building with hotel rooms, accommodating visitors from California who wanted to spend the night in Tijuana rather than making a day trip.

## The Fourth of July, 1924

According to the account that Tijuana historian Fernando Escobedo de la Torre and businessman Armando Avakian Gámez researched across eight years — drawing on testimonies and historical archives — the Caesar salad was created on July 4, 1924, when Caesar Cardini improvised a dish from the ingredients he had on hand during an unusually busy day. The ingredients were simple: romaine lettuce, eggs, olive oil, lemon or Worcestershire sauce, Parmesan cheese, croutons. The method was theatrical: the dressing was prepared at the table, in front of the diners.

The salad spread rapidly. Celebrities visiting Tijuana from Los Angeles encountered it at Caesar's and brought the recipe — or at least the idea — back home. By around 1930, it appeared on the restaurant's menu as 'Romaine Parmigian Dressing' for 50 cents, according to an original menu still displayed in the restaurant. Within a generation, the Caesar salad had become one of the most ubiquitous dishes on restaurant menus around the world.

## Ownership and Continuity

After Caesar Cardini sold the hotel and restaurant, ownership passed through several hands before the Avakian family took over in 1948 and maintained the operation for decades. They preserved the tableside preparation tradition and the restaurant's identity as the birthplace of the salad — a claim with some competition over the years, as various names were put forward as alternative inventors, but one that the historical research of the Avakian family and their collaborators has supported.

The restaurant closed in 2009 when the sharp decline in American visitors to Tijuana following September 11 attacks — a decline that lasted years and was compounded by safety concerns about border violence — reduced business to a level it could no longer sustain. Javier Plascencia's family took over operations, renovated the space, and reopened it in July 2010. The Avakian family continues to own the property.

## The Salad and the Room

Walk into Caesar's today and the waiters still prepare the salad tableside. It is a performance that has been repeated tens of thousands of times across a century, adapted and simplified and sometimes debated — whether Worcestershire belongs or anchovies should be included, whether the original used coddled eggs or raw — while the core ritual of the tableside preparation persists.

The restaurant sits inside a hotel building on Avenida Revolución, in the heart of Tijuana's tourist district. The dining room's atmosphere carries the weight of its history without being suffocated by it. The San Diego Reader called it the 'classiest joint in Tijuana' in 2017, a description that would have pleased the Italian immigrants who built it. The salad, a century old, remains on every menu.
