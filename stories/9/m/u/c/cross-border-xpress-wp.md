# Cross Border Xpress

A 120-meter pedestrian bridge opened in 2015 lets ticketed passengers walk from a terminal on the San Diego side across the US-Mexico border straight into Tijuana's international airport — an infrastructure solution conceptualized in 1989 that took 26 years to build.

**Location:** 32.5480, -116.9750

**Categories:** Transportation infrastructure, US-Mexico border, Airports in Mexico

## Introduction

The idea came to Ralph Nieders in 1989: build a bridge across the US-Mexico border directly to Tijuana's airport, allowing travelers to park in San Diego and walk across to their departure gate. It was a straightforward concept with complicated execution — crossing an international boundary requires the cooperation of two federal governments, two sets of customs and immigration agencies, real estate on both sides of the line, and a legal framework for who is responsible for what happens on a 120-meter bridge that spans two nations. It took 26 years. When Cross Border Xpress opened on December 9, 2015, 600,000 people used it in the first seven months.

## What the Bridge Solves

The Tijuana International Airport (known as General Abelardo L. Rodríguez International Airport) is located immediately south of the US-Mexico border, close enough that the runways are visible from San Diego's southernmost neighborhoods. Hundreds of thousands of people in San Diego's South Bay live closer to Tijuana's airport than to San Diego International Airport, and Tijuana offers flights — particularly to destinations throughout Mexico — that are unavailable or significantly more expensive from San Diego.

Before Cross Border Xpress opened, reaching Tijuana's airport from the American side required navigating the San Ysidro Port of Entry, one of the busiest land border crossings in the world, with wait times that could extend to hours during peak periods. The bridge eliminates that bottleneck for departing and arriving passengers: a boarding pass, a bridge fee, a passport check, and a walk across an enclosed bridge deliver travelers directly to the terminal.

## Building Across Two Countries

The conceptual simplicity of the bridge obscured its regulatory complexity. A structure spanning the US-Mexico boundary required authorization from both federal governments. The customs and immigration processes at each end had to be designed and staffed. Even construction needed a special arrangement: a temporary border waiver let U.S. cranes and U.S.-fabricated steel bridge sections cross the line to be set over Mexico's six-lane Federal Highway 2.

The project required agreements between US Customs and Border Protection, Mexico's Servicio de Administración Tributaria, and the two countries' immigration agencies, as well as state-level approvals on both sides and coordination with the Mexican company that operates the Tijuana airport. When the bridge finally opened in December 2015, its operators billed it as one of a kind: a privately built toll bridge crossing an international boundary to provide direct airport access.

## Nearly Six Thousand People in One Day

Demand was strong from the moment Cross Border Xpress opened. The 600,000 passengers who used the bridge in its first seven months — an average of roughly 3,000 per day — represented a pace that the bridge's operators had hoped for but couldn't guarantee. A single-day record of 5,800 passengers in those early months demonstrated the latent demand that had existed for years without an adequate crossing option.

By its tenth anniversary in December 2025, about 4 million people a year were using it. For the San Diego region, the bridge effectively expanded local airport options without adding a single runway or gate at San Diego International Airport, a single-runway airport hemmed in by the bay and the city.

## What It Looks Like to Use It

Passengers park at the terminal on the San Diego side, check in for their flight and pay the bridge fee, then walk an enclosed corridor that passes over the international boundary. There is no U.S. inspection on the way out; Mexican immigration and customs wait at the airport end. The bridge itself is only about 120 meters long, a walk of a few minutes when the checkpoints are clear. On the return, arriving passengers pay the fee in the Tijuana baggage claim, walk the bridge, and clear U.S. Customs and Border Protection in the San Diego terminal.

The experience collapses the conceptual distance between San Diego and Tijuana's airport to something closer to what the geography suggests: these are neighboring cities, separated by a political boundary that was drawn through terrain they share. The bridge doesn't dissolve the boundary — passports are checked, fees are paid, agencies are satisfied — but it demonstrates that the boundary can be managed in ways that serve the practical needs of people on both sides.
