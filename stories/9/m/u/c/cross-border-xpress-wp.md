# Cross Border Xpress

A 120-meter pedestrian bridge opened in 2015 lets passengers walk directly from a San Diego parking garage across the US-Mexico border to the departure gates of Tijuana's international airport — an infrastructure solution conceptualized in 1989 that took 26 years to build.

**Location:** 32.5480, -116.9750

**Categories:** Transportation infrastructure, US-Mexico border, Airports in Mexico

## Introduction

The idea came to Ralph Nieders in 1989: build a bridge across the US-Mexico border directly to Tijuana's airport, allowing travelers to park in San Diego and walk across to their departure gate. It was a straightforward concept with complicated execution — crossing an international boundary requires the cooperation of two federal governments, two sets of customs and immigration agencies, real estate on both sides of the line, and a legal framework for who is responsible for what happens on a 120-meter bridge that spans two nations. It took 26 years. When Cross Border Xpress opened on December 9, 2015, 600,000 people used it in the first seven months.

## What the Bridge Solves

The Tijuana International Airport (known as General Abelardo L. Rodríguez International Airport) is located immediately south of the US-Mexico border, close enough that the runways are visible from San Diego's southernmost neighborhoods. Millions of people in the San Diego metropolitan area live closer to Tijuana's airport than to San Diego International Airport, and Tijuana offers flights — particularly to destinations throughout Mexico — that are unavailable or significantly more expensive from San Diego.

Before Cross Border Xpress opened, reaching Tijuana's airport from the American side required navigating the San Ysidro Port of Entry, one of the busiest land border crossings in the world, with wait times that could extend to hours during peak periods. The bridge eliminates that bottleneck for departing and arriving passengers: a small fee, a passport check, and a walk across a climate-controlled enclosed bridge delivers travelers directly to the terminal.

## Building Across Two Countries

The conceptual simplicity of the bridge obscured its regulatory complexity. A structure spanning the US-Mexico boundary required authorization from both federal governments. The bridge crosses airspace controlled by two different countries' aviation authorities. The customs and immigration processes at each end had to be designed and staffed. The physical structure had to meet both American and Mexican engineering standards.

The project required agreements between US Customs and Border Protection, Mexico's Servicio de Administración Tributaria, and the two countries' immigration agencies, as well as state-level approvals on both sides and coordination with the Mexican company that operates the Tijuana airport. When the bridge finally opened in December 2015, it became the only structure of its type in the world: a private toll bridge crossing an international boundary to provide direct airport access.

## Five Thousand People in One Day

Demand exceeded projections from the moment Cross Border Xpress opened. The 600,000 passengers who used the bridge in its first seven months — an average of roughly 3,000 per day — represented a pace that the bridge's operators had hoped for but couldn't guarantee. The single-day record of 5,800 passengers demonstrated the latent demand that had existed for years without an adequate crossing option.

For the San Diego region, the bridge effectively expanded the local airport capacity without adding a single runway or gate at San Diego International Airport. Passengers who shifted from KSAN to the Tijuana airport via Cross Border Xpress reduced load on a facility that has long struggled with its geographic constraints — hemmed in by the bay, the city, and military flight corridors on three sides.

## What It Looks Like to Use It

Passengers park in a San Diego parking structure, pay the bridge fee, and walk through an enclosed, climate-controlled corridor that passes over the international boundary. US customs on the way out, Mexican immigration at the terminal end. The bridge takes about ten minutes to walk, assuming no congestion at the check points. On the return, the process reverses: Mexican exit procedures, the walk, US Customs and Border Protection waiting on the American side.

The experience collapses the conceptual distance between San Diego and Tijuana's airport to something closer to what the geography suggests: these are neighboring cities, separated by a political boundary that was drawn through terrain they share. The bridge doesn't dissolve the boundary — passports are checked, fees are paid, agencies are satisfied — but it demonstrates that the boundary can be managed in ways that serve the practical needs of people on both sides.
