# Rancho de la Nación

A 26,632-acre land grant given by Governor Pío Pico to his brother-in-law in 1845 became the foundation on which the South Bay cities of National City and Chula Vista were built.

**Location:** 32.6600, -117.0000

**Categories:** Ranchos of San Diego County, California, National City, California, 1845 establishments in Alta California

## Introduction

The same land that is now National City, Chula Vista, Bonita, and the western Sweetwater Valley was once cattle grazing ground for the Presidio of San Diego. The Spanish called it Rancho del Rey — Ranch of the King — and maintained it since 1795 as a source of beef and horses for their military garrison. When Mexico won independence, the name changed to Rancho de la Nación: Ranch of the Nation. When California became American territory, the ranch's 26,632 acres passed through the hands of an English immigrant, a San Francisco financier, and New Hampshire brothers before becoming the urban South Bay that exists today.

## The Governor's Gift to His Brother-in-Law

In 1845, Governor Pío Pico — the last governor of Mexican California — granted the rancho to John Forster, an English-born immigrant who had married Ysidora Pico, the governor's sister. The grant was six square leagues, roughly 26,632 acres, encompassing the broad coastal plain south of San Diego and the lower watershed of the Sweetwater River.

Forster, known in California as Don Juan, was already a significant figure in the territory. He had arrived in California in 1833, married into one of its most powerful families in 1837, and was accumulating landholdings across the region. The rancho grant was a further expression of the Pico family's ability to channel California's land wealth toward their own circle — a pattern that was common in the final years of Mexican rule. Forster would later own Rancho Santa Margarita y Las Flores in northern San Diego County, adding to an already substantial land portfolio.

## Between Two Sovereignty Claims

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, which ended the Mexican-American War in 1848, promised that Mexican land grants would be honored by the new American government. But honoring the grants required proving them — documenting their boundaries, their history, and their legitimate transfer — through an American legal system that was slow, expensive, and often skeptical of Mexican-era paperwork.

Forster filed his claim for Rancho de la Nación with the Public Land Commission in 1852. The process took fourteen years: the grant was finally patented to Forster in 1866. By that point, he had already sold it — in 1854, he transferred ownership to François Louis Alfred Pioche, a San Francisco financier who then sold it in 1868 to Frank A. Kimball of New Hampshire and his brothers Warren and Levi. The Kimballs would play a foundational role in transforming the rancho land into the South Bay's first organized communities.

## Ranch into City

Frank Kimball arrived in California from New Hampshire with a vision for the rancho land that looked nothing like its pastoral past. He surveyed and platted National City in 1868, creating one of the first planned communities in Southern California. The Kimball brothers donated land for public institutions, lobbied for the transcontinental railroad to terminate at National City rather than San Diego, and generally invested in the infrastructure that transformed cattle grazing land into urban property.

The effort to capture the railroad terminus ultimately failed — the transcontinental rail came to San Diego instead — but the platting and development of National City and the surrounding areas proceeded regardless. The flat, well-watered land south of San Diego proved suitable for agriculture, industry, and eventually the dense residential development that characterizes the South Bay today.

## A Rancho in the Landscape

Rancho de la Nación no longer exists as a distinct place — it has been entirely absorbed into the urban fabric of San Diego County's South Bay. The communities that occupy its former boundaries — National City, Chula Vista, Bonita — are densely developed, commercially active parts of a metropolitan area that extends continuously to the Mexican border.

But the rancho's geography still shapes these places. The broad coastal plain that made it suitable for cattle grazing made it equally suitable for urban development. The Sweetwater River that ran through its western valley is now dammed, its water managed for the communities that grew on the rancho's land. The presidial grazing ground that Spain maintained beginning in 1795, passed through Spanish, Mexican, English, and American hands, and eventually became the South Bay, contains within its urban grid the outline of decisions made by governors, grantees, and speculators across two centuries.
