# USS Bennington Monument

A 60-foot granite obelisk at Fort Rosecrans National Cemetery marks the graves of sailors killed in a boiler explosion aboard the USS Bennington in 1905 — then considered the worst peacetime disaster in US Navy history.

**Location:** 32.6900, -117.2440

**Categories:** Monuments in San Diego, Fort Rosecrans National Cemetery, United States Navy history

## Introduction

On July 21, 1905, the USS Bennington was at anchor in San Diego Bay when one of its boilers exploded. The blast killed 66 men in what was considered the worst peacetime disaster the United States Navy had experienced up to that point. Most of the dead were buried at Fort Rosecrans, on the high ground of Point Loma overlooking the harbor where they had died. Three years later, a 60-foot granite obelisk was dedicated over their graves. It stands there still, high above the bay — a marker of sudden catastrophe in a place that had been at peace.

## The Day the Boiler Exploded

The USS Bennington was a gunboat, a relatively small naval vessel, anchored in San Diego Bay in the summer of 1905. Peacetime routine governed the ship's operations; there was no enemy, no combat, no particular danger anticipated. When a boiler exploded at mid-morning, the violence was sudden and total. Steam, scalding water, and shrapnel tore through compartments where sailors worked. Some died instantly; others succumbed to burns and injuries in the hours that followed.

The death toll of 66 men represented a substantial fraction of the ship's crew. Nearly all of the 46 survivors were injured. San Diego received word quickly, and the community rallied; the casualties overwhelmed the city's medical facilities, and volunteers tended many burn victims in makeshift wards. Investigators traced the blast to oily feed water, an improperly closed steam valve, and a faulty steam gauge, and despite rumors of misconduct an official inquiry found the crew had not been negligent. The disaster drew increased attention to boiler safety and maintenance across the Navy.

## Burial on the Point

Most of the dead were buried two days after the explosion at Fort Rosecrans Military Reservation, on the high ground of Point Loma that commands the entrance to San Diego Bay. In 1905, Fort Rosecrans was an active military installation; the cemetery there would later be transferred to the jurisdiction of the National Cemetery Administration and designated Fort Rosecrans National Cemetery, one of the most dramatically situated national cemeteries in the country.

The graves of the Bennington's dead occupy a section of the cemetery with a view of the bay and the Pacific. On clear days, which are common on Point Loma, ships can be seen entering and leaving the harbor below. The men buried here died in that harbor.

## The Obelisk on the Hill

The USS Bennington Monument was dedicated on January 7, 1908, two and a half years after the disaster. The 60-foot granite obelisk rises over the graves on the Point Loma ridge, above the waters of the bay and the ocean approaches to the harbor entrance.

The monument was designed as a collective marker: it stands for all 66 men, though some were later disinterred and shipped home for burial by their families. It is one of several memorials at Fort Rosecrans to naval disasters and wartime dead, but the Bennington monument predates most of them, and its scale reflects the gravity with which the Navy and the San Diego community regarded the loss. At the time of its dedication, the Bennington explosion was considered the deadliest peacetime disaster the Navy had suffered.

## Remembered in Stone, Forgotten in History

The USS Bennington explosion is not widely remembered outside of San Diego and naval history circles, overshadowed by the wars and disasters that followed in the twentieth century. But in 1905, it was a significant event — newspaper coverage was extensive, eleven survivors received the Medal of Honor for their heroism that day, and the disaster drew lasting attention to boiler safety in the fleet.

Fort Rosecrans National Cemetery now holds the remains of veterans from wars spanning more than a century, and the Bennington monument stands among thousands of white grave markers, one landmark among many in a cemetery that has grown far beyond its original boundaries. The men it commemorates were young, most of them — enlisted sailors going about routine duties on a July morning in a harbor that seemed entirely safe. The obelisk that marks their graves stands as a reminder that peacetime carries its own casualties.
