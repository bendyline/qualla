# San Diego–Coronado Bridge

The arching blue span that connects San Diego to Coronado was engineered to curve not for aesthetics but to accommodate naval air traffic, and its eastern pillars became the canvas for one of California's most celebrated public art projects.

**Location:** 32.6865, -117.1583

**Categories:** Bridges in San Diego, Coronado, California, Transportation in San Diego County

## Introduction

The San Diego–Coronado Bridge curves through the air in a long, graceful arc, and people often assume the curve was designed for beauty. The truth is more interesting. The bridge bends because it had to: the Navy required that aircraft approaching North Island Naval Air Station not be endangered by a structure in their flight path. Engineers responded by curving the bridge, letting it sidestep the approach corridor. The result — a sweeping, elegant span painted a distinctive blue-gray — happens to be one of the most photographed bridges in California. But it was born of military necessity, not aesthetic ambition.

## Building Across the Bay

Construction on the bridge began in 1967 and proceeded at remarkable speed for a project of its scale. When it opened on August 3, 1969, the date carried symbolic weight: it was the 200th anniversary of the founding of San Diego. Architect Robert Mosher designed the bridge to meet the Navy's requirements while creating something that wouldn't be an eyesore on the bay. The solution — gentle curves, slender piers, a distinctive color — resulted in a structure that has come to define the San Diego skyline.

The numbers are substantial: 11,179 feet long, consuming 20,000 tons of steel. The bridge stands tall enough above the water that naval vessels can pass beneath. It replaced a ferry service that had connected Coronado to San Diego since 1886, and within a short time of opening, traffic volumes demonstrated that the peninsula had been effectively isolated by the absence of a fixed crossing. The ferry didn't survive the bridge's competition.

## The Murals Underneath

When the bridge was built, the eastern approach descended through Barrio Logan, a predominantly Mexican-American neighborhood that had been severely disrupted by the construction of Interstate 5 and then the bridge itself. Community members had been advocating for a park in the neighborhood for years when, in April 1973, a confrontation occurred: residents occupied land under the bridge that had been slated for a California Highway Patrol substation, demanding it be used for the park they had been promised.

The occupation held. Chicano Park was established under the bridge's eastern pillars, and community artists began painting the concrete columns — the largest collection of Chicano murals in the world. The murals depict Aztec mythology, Mexican-American history, political heroes, and the neighborhood's own story. They transformed the bridge's massive infrastructure from an imposition on the community into a canvas for that community's art. Chicano Park was designated a National Historic Landmark in 2017.

## The Weight It Carries

The bridge is San Diego's primary connection between the city and the Coronado peninsula, carrying tens of thousands of vehicles daily. It has also carried a tragic burden: by July 2017, more than 400 people had died by suicide from the bridge, making it one of the most frequently used sites for suicide in California. The toll led to sustained advocacy for barrier installation, a debate that played out over decades before nets were approved and installation began.

The bridge's design makes the issue particularly complex. Its height — sufficient to make the fall fatal — combined with its accessibility made intervention difficult to implement without structural changes. The outcome of the barrier effort represents a shift in how infrastructure designers and public agencies consider the unintended uses of elevated structures.

## An Urban Legend About the Center Span

A persistent local story holds that the bridge's center span is slightly misaligned with the rest of the structure — that the two halves, built simultaneously from opposite shores, didn't quite meet in the middle, requiring a gap-bridging solution. Engineers and bridge historians have disputed this story. The bridge's curvature means it looks different from every angle, and segments that appear misaligned from certain vantage points are in fact properly joined.

The legend persists because the bridge is unusual enough to inspire unusual explanations. The reality — that the curve was a calculated response to naval aviation requirements, that the meeting of the two halves was carefully engineered, that the apparent irregularities are optical artifacts — is if anything more impressive than the folklore.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.687°N, 117.158°W, arching across the northern section of San Diego Bay between San Diego and Coronado. At 3,000-5,000 feet, the bridge's distinctive curve is clearly visible, along with the Chicano Park murals on the eastern pillars. NAS North Island (KNZY) is immediately north of the Coronado end. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 3 miles northwest. The bridge's clearance allows naval vessels to pass beneath, and active Navy ship traffic is often visible in the bay below.
