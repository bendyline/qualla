# San Diego–Coronado Bridge

The arching blue span that connects San Diego to Coronado was engineered to curve not for aesthetics but to give Navy ships 200 feet of clearance on a gentle grade, and its eastern pillars became the canvas for one of California's most celebrated public art projects.

**Location:** 32.6865, -117.1583

**Categories:** Bridges in San Diego, Coronado, California, Transportation in San Diego County

## Introduction

The San Diego–Coronado Bridge curves through the air in a long, graceful arc, and people often assume the curve was designed for beauty. The truth is more interesting. The bridge bends because it had to: the Navy would accept a bridge only if ships from its San Diego base could pass at least 200 feet beneath it. Climbing that high on a reasonable grade took more length than a straight line across the bay allowed, so the engineers curved the bridge to lengthen it. The result — a sweeping, elegant span painted a distinctive blue-gray — happens to be one of the most photographed bridges in California. But it was born of military necessity, not aesthetic ambition.

## Building Across the Bay

Construction on the bridge began in 1967 and proceeded at remarkable speed for a project of its scale. When it opened on August 3, 1969, the date carried symbolic weight: it was the 200th anniversary of the founding of San Diego. Architect Robert Mosher designed the bridge to meet the Navy's requirements while creating something that wouldn't be an eyesore on the bay. The solution — gentle curves, slender piers, a distinctive color — resulted in a structure that has come to define the San Diego skyline.

The numbers are substantial: 11,179 feet long, consuming 20,000 tons of steel. The bridge stands about 200 feet above the water so that naval vessels can pass beneath. It replaced a ferry service that had connected Coronado to San Diego since 1886. The car ferry made its last run the night the bridge opened; passenger ferries did not return to the crossing until 1987.

## The Murals Underneath

When the bridge was built, the eastern approach descended through Barrio Logan, a predominantly Mexican-American neighborhood that had been severely disrupted by the construction of Interstate 5 and then the bridge itself. Community members had been advocating for a park in the neighborhood for years when, on April 22, 1970, a confrontation occurred: residents occupied land under the bridge that had been slated for a California Highway Patrol substation, demanding it be used for the park they had been promised.

The occupation held. Chicano Park was established under the bridge's eastern pillars, and from 1973 community artists began painting the concrete columns — widely described as the largest collection of Chicano murals in the world. The murals depict Aztec mythology, Mexican-American history, political heroes, and the neighborhood's own story. They transformed the bridge's massive infrastructure from an imposition on the community into a canvas for that community's art. Chicano Park's designation as a National Historic Landmark was announced in January 2017.

## The Weight It Carries

The bridge is San Diego's primary connection between the city and the Coronado peninsula, carrying tens of thousands of vehicles daily. It has also carried a tragic burden: by July 2017, more than 400 people had died by suicide from the bridge, making it one of the most frequently used sites for suicide in California. The toll led to sustained advocacy for barrier installation, a debate that played out over decades before the California Coastal Commission approved an eight-foot mesh barrier along the full length of the bridge in June 2026, with construction expected to begin late that year.

The bridge's design makes the issue particularly complex. Its height — sufficient to make the fall fatal — combined with its accessibility made intervention difficult to implement without structural changes. The outcome of the barrier effort represents a shift in how infrastructure designers and public agencies consider the unintended uses of elevated structures.

## An Urban Legend About the Center Span

A decades-old local story holds that the bridge's center span was engineered to float if the bridge ever collapsed, so that Navy ships could push the wreckage aside and keep the channel open. Caltrans and the bridge's principal architect, Robert Mosher, have both said the story is false. It probably grew from the hollow box design of the roughly 1,800-foot center span, and from construction, when the low barges carrying its sections made them look as if they were floating on their own.

The legend persists because the bridge is unusual enough to inspire unusual explanations. The reality — that the curve was a calculated response to the Navy's clearance requirement, and that the hollow center span is simply a strong, light box girder — is if anything more impressive than the folklore.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.687°N, 117.158°W, arching across the northern section of San Diego Bay between San Diego and Coronado. At 3,000-5,000 feet, the bridge's distinctive curve is clearly visible, along with the Chicano Park murals on the eastern pillars. NAS North Island (KNZY) lies about 3 miles west-northwest of the Coronado end. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 4 miles north-northwest. The bridge's clearance allows naval vessels to pass beneath, and active Navy ship traffic is often visible in the bay below.
