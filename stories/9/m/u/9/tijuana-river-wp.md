# Tijuana River

The Tijuana River travels 120 miles from the mountains of Baja California through the US-Mexico border to its outlet at the Pacific, carrying two nations' urban runoff into one of California's most ecologically significant estuaries.

**Location:** 32.5550, -117.1250

**Categories:** Rivers of California, Rivers of Baja California, US-Mexico border

## Introduction

The Tijuana River is one of California's more unusual waterways: it flows out of Mexico and into the United States, crossing the international boundary in its final miles before reaching the Pacific. For most of its 120-mile length, it runs intermittently — a dry channel for much of the year, transformed by winter rains into a surging stream that carries everything its urbanized watershed has accumulated: silt, sewage, debris, and the runoff of a city that has grown faster than its infrastructure.

## A River That Crosses a Border

The Tijuana River originates in the mountains of Baja California, draining a watershed that encompasses both sides of the US-Mexico boundary. The river flows generally northwestward through Tijuana before crossing into California near San Ysidro, then travels a final five miles through Imperial Beach and the Tijuana River Valley before entering the estuary and the Pacific.

This geography creates a jurisdictional complexity that has no clean solution. The watershed is binational; the river's pollution sources are predominantly in Mexico; but the downstream consequences fall on American beaches, wetlands, and coastal waters. The river is classified as intermittent throughout most of its length, meaning it carries significant flow only during and after rain events — but those events, precisely because they are intermittent, tend to carry concentrated loads of everything accumulated during the dry months.

## What the River Carries

Tijuana's rapid growth since the mid-twentieth century has repeatedly outpaced the city's ability to build and maintain sewage treatment infrastructure. During dry weather, treatment plants manage most of the flow. During rain events, stormwater overwhelms the system and untreated sewage enters the river directly. This is not a new problem — it has been documented for decades and has been the subject of international negotiations, binational agreements, and engineering projects.

The International Boundary and Wastewater Treatment Plant, built in the 1990s on the American side of the border, was designed to treat millions of gallons of Tijuana sewage daily. It has substantially reduced pollution, but the river's basin continues to generate pollution volumes that exceed treatment capacity during significant storms. The result: periodic sewage spills that close Imperial Beach for weeks or months at a time, creating public health crises that have become a defining feature of life in San Diego's southernmost communities.

## The Valley Between the River and the Border

The Tijuana River Valley — the broad floodplain through which the river travels in its final miles before the estuary — is an unusual landscape for Southern California: relatively undeveloped, open, dominated by the native willow and cottonwood riparian vegetation that once lined many California river valleys before urban development consumed them.

The valley's lack of development reflects both its location in a floodplain subject to periodic inundation and its proximity to the international boundary, which has complicated development proposals over the years. The result is an accidental open space — a greenbelt created not by conservation planning but by the inconvenience of developing a floodplain adjacent to a border fence. The valley is now managed in part as a regional park, offering horse trails, wildlife habitat, and a green buffer between the suburb of San Ysidro and the estuary.

## A River in Recovery and Decline

The ecological story of the Tijuana River is one of simultaneous recovery and ongoing damage. Restoration projects in the valley and estuary have improved native plant communities, reduced invasive species, and enhanced habitat for birds and other wildlife. Long-term monitoring shows the river system supporting remarkable biodiversity despite its pollution burden.

At the same time, climate change is altering the hydrology: droughts concentrate pollutants, intense storms deliver larger sediment pulses, and sea level rise threatens the estuary's delicate salinity balance. The river that reaches the Pacific carries the accumulated decisions of two countries — about urban development, sewage infrastructure, water management, and land use. Those decisions continue to be made in real time, upstream, while the river keeps flowing.

## Flight Context

Located where the river reaches the Pacific at approximately 32.555°N, 117.125°W in Imperial Beach. From altitude, the Tijuana River Valley is visible as an open, vegetated corridor running inland from the coast, contrasting with the surrounding urban development. The river channel itself is often dry but becomes visible after significant rainfall. The border fence is visible to the south. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 15 miles north.
