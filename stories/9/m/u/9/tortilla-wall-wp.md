# Tortilla Wall

The fourteen-mile barrier stretching from Otay Mesa to the Pacific was one of the first major physical enforcement structures on the US-Mexico border, built in the early 1990s from surplus military landing mats.

**Location:** 32.5340, -117.1220

**Categories:** US-Mexico border, Border security, Immigration history of the United States

## Introduction

Before the Tortilla Wall, the border between San Diego and Tijuana was more suggestion than barrier. The fourteen-mile stretch from Otay Mesa to the Pacific Ocean was patrolled but not fenced in any meaningful way, and crossings were common enough that the Border Patrol tracked its apprehensions in the hundreds of thousands annually. Figures later cited in congressional testimony put apprehensions in the fenced stretch at 202,000 in 1992 and just 9,000 in 1994, after the wall had been built. Whatever else the barrier accomplished or failed to accomplish, it moved people.

## Built from Landing Mats

The first significant physical barrier along this stretch of the border was constructed using surplus military landing mats — the perforated steel planking used by the military to create temporary runways in combat zones. The material was available, cheap, and already in government possession. Welded together and set in concrete, the mats became the initial barrier, rust-colored and utilitarian, very different from the sleek barriers that would follow in subsequent infrastructure upgrades.

The barrier came to be known informally as the Tortilla Wall. The name's origin is not well documented, but it echoes the "Tortilla Curtain," the label critics had pinned on federal plans for border fences at El Paso and San Diego in 1978. In the years that followed, sections of the original mat barrier were replaced with heavier, more sophisticated materials, and additional security layers — vehicle barriers, sensors, lighting, and increased patrol presence — were added. The wall evolved from improvised to engineered.

## What the Numbers Said

The drop in apprehensions — from 202,000 in 1992 to 9,000 in 1994, figures Representative Ed Royce cited in congressional testimony for the area with a fence — is the statistic most often cited by proponents of physical barriers as evidence of their effectiveness. It describes only the fenced stretch: across the whole San Diego Sector, the Border Patrol still made 447,540 apprehensions in fiscal 1994, and Operation Gatekeeper, launched that October, poured in agents and equipment as well. What the numbers don't directly answer is where the crossings went, because the people attempting unauthorized entry didn't simply stop.

Research on border crossing patterns after the San Diego wall was built showed displacement: crossings shifted east, into the Arizona desert and other remote areas where physical barriers were absent but conditions were far more dangerous. The Sonoran Desert in summer can kill unprepared travelers within days. The shift in crossing locations that followed San Diego's fortification correlated with a significant increase in border crossing deaths in the desert Southwest — a tragic consequence that took years to fully document.

## The Circus Cannon and the Tunnels

The wall, for all its physical presence, has drawn creative responses and circumvention alike. One of the more unusual crossings was a work of art: on August 27, 2005, as part of the inSite border art exhibition, the human cannonball David Smith Sr. held up his passport and was fired from a circus cannon on the beach in Tijuana, over the fence, into a net in Border Field State Park, where Border Patrol agents were waiting. The stunt made a point about any physical barrier: it is fixed, while human ingenuity is not.

Below the barrier, tunnels posed a more systematic challenge. Sophisticated underground passages, some equipped with electricity, ventilation, and rail systems, were discovered beneath the border in San Diego County over the years. These tunnels were engineering projects representing substantial investment — too expensive for most individuals, but worthwhile for organizations moving large volumes of contraband. The barrier's presence above ground intensified the economic incentive to invest in infrastructure below it.

## The Fence That Enters the Ocean

At the western end of the fourteen-mile barrier, where the wall meets the Pacific at Playas de Tijuana, the physical structure does something that seems almost impossible: it walks into the sea. Steel pillars extend beyond the tideline, their bases submerged at high water, making the barrier visible from boats offshore and photographed endlessly by visitors and journalists.

This endpoint — where enforcement infrastructure meets an ocean it cannot cross — has become one of the most symbolically charged images along the entire US-Mexico boundary. People have gathered at the fence here for decades, on the Mexican side, sometimes to protest and sometimes simply to be in proximity to family members on the other side. The fence that separates has also, paradoxically, been a place of connection — a meeting point defined entirely by the separation it imposes.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.534°N, 117.122°W, stretching from Otay Mesa (east) to the Pacific Ocean at Playas de Tijuana (west). From altitude, the border barrier is one of the most visible features along the US-Mexico boundary — a clear line visible in satellite imagery and from low-altitude flight. The point where the fence enters the Pacific is particularly distinctive. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 14 miles north-northwest. At 5,000-8,000 feet in clear weather, the barrier's full fourteen-mile extent from Otay Mesa to the coast is visible.
