# South Bay Salt Works

Operating continuously since before 1872, South Bay Salt Works is San Diego's second-oldest business and one of only two places in the Western United States where sea salt is still commercially extracted.

**Location:** 32.6000, -117.0900

**Categories:** Salt production, Industrial history of California, San Diego Bay

## Introduction

The La Punta Salt Company was already harvesting salt from San Diego Bay's southern tidal flats before 1872, making South Bay Salt Works one of the oldest continuously operating businesses in California. The operation is so old that records of its exact founding have been lost to time. What is known is that for well over 150 years, workers have been managing tidal flows into shallow ponds, letting the California sun do its work, and raking crystallized salt from evaporating basins — a process essentially unchanged from the one the earliest operators used.

## How Salt Is Made from Sea

The salt works operates on a principle as old as human civilization: seawater is introduced into shallow ponds, the sun evaporates the water, and the dissolved sodium chloride is left behind as crystals. At South Bay, water from San Diego Bay flows into a series of increasingly concentrated ponds, each stage saltier than the last as evaporation progresses. The process takes months, moving water through a sequence of ponds with different salinity levels before reaching the crystallization basins where the salt finally forms.

The ponds cover a substantial area of the southern bay's shoreline. When viewed from altitude, they appear as a patchwork of colors — varying hues of pink, orange, and brown produced by different communities of algae and brine shrimp that thrive at specific salinity levels. These colors are not incidental; they are ecological indicators of where in the process each pond sits. The brine shrimp that color the ponds are also food for the birds that gather in enormous numbers, making the salt works an unintentional wildlife refuge.

## The Narrow Gauge Crossing

For much of its history, the salt works used a narrow-gauge railway to move salt from the crystallization ponds to processing and storage facilities. This railway crossed a standard-gauge main line — and for decades, it was the only place in the United States where a narrow-gauge track crossed a standard-gauge track. Railroad enthusiasts and industrial historians noted it as a genuinely unusual feature of American transportation infrastructure. The crossing was eventually retired as the operation's logistics evolved, but during its operational period it represented a quirk of industrial geography found nowhere else in the country.

The railway's existence spoke to the scale of the operation. Seventy-five thousand tons of salt per year, as the operation produces, requires meaningful infrastructure to move from ponds to market. Salt is dense, corrosive, and heavy; moving it by hand over any significant distance is impractical. The narrow-gauge solution was practical engineering adapted to a specific landscape.

## Two Points of West Coast Salt

South Bay Salt Works is one of only two locations on the entire West Coast where sea salt is commercially extracted — the other being in San Francisco Bay. The scarcity of sea salt production in the Western United States reflects both the geology of the coastline and the economics of salt production: inland mineral sources, imported salt, and manufactured salt have largely displaced traditional sea-evaporation operations everywhere they competed.

That South Bay has survived as a commercial operation while so many others closed speaks to a combination of favorable geography, established infrastructure, and market position. The operation has produced more than 1.5 million tons of salt over its history — a genuinely staggering quantity for a single site. Much of that production has gone to industrial uses: water treatment, chemical manufacturing, and de-icing applications that require bulk quantities rather than the culinary-grade product most consumers associate with sea salt.

## Birds, Business, and the Bay's Future

The salt works' ponds, while industrial, function as crucial bird habitat. The concentrated brine supports brine shrimp and brine flies in enormous numbers, creating a food source that attracts shorebirds, wading birds, and waterfowl by the tens of thousands. The American avocet, various species of plover, and the endangered California least tern all use the ponds as part of their annual cycles. In a bay where more than 90 percent of natural wetland habitat has been destroyed, the salt works' ponds provide something the natural system no longer can: shallow water with abundant invertebrate food.

The future of the site is subject to ongoing discussion about conversion to restored wetlands — a transformation that would replace the industrial operation with managed habitat. The economics of such a conversion, and the question of what would be lost alongside what would be gained, make the salt works' future one of the more complex land-use questions along the southern California coast.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.60°N, 117.09°W on the southern shore of San Diego Bay, near the US-Mexico border. From altitude, the salt ponds are among the most distinctive features of San Diego Bay's southern end — a patchwork of colored ponds contrasting with the surrounding developed landscape. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 10 miles north. The salt works' distinctive pond colors are best visible in clear morning light at 5,000-10,000 feet.
