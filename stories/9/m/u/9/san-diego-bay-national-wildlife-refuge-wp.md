# San Diego Bay National Wildlife Refuge

A 2,620-acre urban refuge protecting the remnants of coastal wetlands that once lined San Diego Bay, home to the light-footed Ridgway's rail and a plant found naturally nowhere else in the United States.

**Location:** 32.6000, -117.1200

**Categories:** National Wildlife Refuges in California, San Diego Bay, Wetlands of California

## Introduction

Yerba reuma, also called Palmer's frankenia, grows naturally in only one place in the United States: the salt marshes of San Diego Bay. This low, scraggly shrub — unremarkable to the casual eye — is a botanical marker of something extraordinary: a pocket of coastal habitat so rare that more than 90 percent of what once existed here has been filled, drained or diked. The roughly 2,620 acres of the San Diego Bay National Wildlife Refuge - the 316-acre Sweetwater Marsh Unit and the 2,300-acre South San Diego Bay Unit - protect what remains of an ecosystem that stretched along the bay's margins before the Navy, the salt works, and suburban development transformed the shoreline.

## A Bay Remade

When the Kumeyaay people alone fished, hunted and gathered along the shores of San Diego Bay, coastal wetlands framed the water's edge for miles. Salt marshes, mudflats, and upland transition zones formed a complex ecological zone where fish spawned, birds nested, and marine and terrestrial worlds overlapped. Over the course of the twentieth century, this habitat was systematically eliminated — filled for military installations, dredged for shipping channels, converted to industrial uses, and paved for development.

By the time the refuge was established in 1988, with the South San Diego Bay Unit and its salt ponds added in 1999, more than 90 percent of the bay's historic wetlands were gone. What the refuge protects is not pristine wilderness; it is the last fragments of a much larger system, carefully managed to maintain their ecological function against the pressure of one of California's most intensely developed coastal landscapes.

## The Kelp Plant That Stood Here

Before the refuge, before the Navy's great buildup on the bay, before modern San Diego had taken shape, the shoreline at this location held something unusual: a 30-acre kelp processing plant operated by the Hercules Powder Company between 1916 and 1920. During World War I, kelp harvested from local offshore beds was processed here into potash and acetone — ingredients of smokeless powder, much of it for the British — which is why the spot is still called Gunpowder Point. The factory was a wartime expedient, and when the war ended and the economics shifted, it was dismantled.

The site's industrial history is a reminder that the bay's shoreline has been put to many uses over the generations, and that what looks like 'natural' refuge land today has its own complex past. The land the kelp plant occupied is now part of the refuge's Sweetwater Marsh Unit.

## Species That Cling to the Margin

The light-footed Ridgway's rail, long known as the light-footed clapper rail, is the refuge's signature inhabitant — a secretive, chickenlike bird of the salt marshes, pushed toward extinction as those marshes were filled. A captive breeding program run at Sweetwater Marsh with partners has released hundreds of rails into the wild. The rail's rasping call, a machine-gun staccato that carries across the marsh at dawn, is a sound that would have been commonplace along the California coast a century ago. Today it is heard in only a limited number of Southern California marshes.

The California least tern nests on the refuge's sandy areas, a species that requires open sandy beaches free from human disturbance — the exact habitat that modern coastal development has nearly eliminated. The threatened western snowy plover nests in both units, and the endangered Belding's savannah sparrow and salt marsh bird's beak hold on here too. Managing the refuge means constant vigilance: controlling invasive plants that crowd out native marsh species, managing predators that raid nests, and maintaining the tidal flow that keeps the marsh alive.

## Refuge at the Water's Edge

The San Diego Bay National Wildlife Refuge sits on one of the most heavily militarized and developed bays in the United States. Naval Air Station North Island, Naval Base San Diego, the submarine base at Point Loma, and industrial port facilities ring the bay. Against this backdrop, the refuge's marshes and mudflats occupy their small but critical acreage, maintained by federal wildlife managers who work to restore what development erased.

Restoration efforts have included removing invasive plants, replanting native marsh vegetation, and beginning to return former commercial salt ponds in the South Bay to tidal flow. The refuge's success in maintaining populations of endangered and threatened species within this context represents one of conservation's more improbable achievements — a functioning remnant of a lost ecosystem, surrounded on all sides by the infrastructure of the modern world.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.60°N, 117.12°W in south San Diego Bay; the Sweetwater Marsh Unit lies a few kilometers north at Chula Vista's Gunpowder Point, and the South San Diego Bay Unit, with its salt ponds, wraps the bay's southern tip. The refuge's salt marshes and mudflats are visible from low altitudes as a band of green-brown vegetation along the bay's margin, distinct from the surrounding developed and military areas. Naval Air Station North Island (KNZY) lies about 14 km northwest across the bay, and San Diego International Airport (KSAN) about 16 km (10 miles) north-northwest; Naval Outlying Field Imperial Beach (KNRS) is about 4 km south. At lower altitudes and in clear weather, the transition between marsh and developed land is clearly visible.
