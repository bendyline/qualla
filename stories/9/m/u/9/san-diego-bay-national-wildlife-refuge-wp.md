# San Diego Bay National Wildlife Refuge

A 316-acre refuge protecting the remnants of coastal wetlands that once lined San Diego Bay, home to the light-footed clapper rail and a plant found naturally nowhere else in the United States.

**Location:** 32.6000, -117.1200

**Categories:** National Wildlife Refuges in California, San Diego Bay, Wetlands of California

## Introduction

Yerba reuma grows in only one place in the United States: the tidal flats and salt marshes of San Diego Bay. This low, scraggly shrub — unremarkable to the casual eye — is a botanical marker of something extraordinary: a pocket of coastal habitat so rare that 90 percent of what once existed here has been destroyed. The 316 acres of the San Diego Bay National Wildlife Refuge are what remains of an ecosystem that stretched along the bay's margins before the Navy, the salt works, and suburban development transformed the shoreline.

## A Bay Remade

When the Kumeyaay people lived along the shores of San Diego Bay, coastal wetlands framed the water's edge for miles. Salt marshes, mudflats, and upland transition zones formed a complex ecological zone where fish spawned, birds nested, and marine and terrestrial worlds overlapped. Over the course of the twentieth century, this habitat was systematically eliminated — filled for military installations, dredged for shipping channels, converted to industrial uses, and paved for development.

By the time the refuge was established in 1988, more than 90 percent of the bay's historic wetlands were gone. What the refuge protects is not pristine wilderness; it is the last fragments of a much larger system, carefully managed to maintain their ecological function against the pressure of one of California's most intensely developed coastal landscapes.

## The Kelp Plant That Stood Here

Before the refuge, before the Navy, before modern San Diego had taken shape, the shoreline at this location held something unusual: a kelp processing plant operated by the Hercules Powder Company between 1916 and 1920. During World War I, kelp harvested from local offshore beds was processed here into potash and acetone — industrial chemicals needed for munitions production. The factory was a wartime expedient, and when the war ended and the economics shifted, it was dismantled.

The site's industrial history is a reminder that the bay's shoreline has been put to many uses over the generations, and that what looks like 'natural' refuge land today has its own complex past. The kelp plant left no permanent structures, but the land it occupied is now home to some of the refuge's most ecologically valuable marsh habitat.

## Species That Cling to the Margin

The light-footed clapper rail is the refuge's signature inhabitant — a secretive, chickenlike bird that lives in salt marshes and was once hunted to near extinction before hunting was banned and habitat protection began. The rail's rasping call, a machine-gun staccato that carries across the marsh at dawn, is a sound that would have been commonplace along the California coast a century ago. Today it is heard in only a handful of places.

The California least tern nests on the refuge's sandy areas, a species that requires open sandy beaches free from human disturbance — the exact habitat that modern coastal development has nearly eliminated. The snowy plover shares these nesting grounds. Managing the refuge means constant vigilance: controlling invasive cordgrass species that crowd out native marsh plants, managing predators that raid nests, and maintaining the tidal flow that keeps the marsh alive.

## Refuge at the Water's Edge

The San Diego Bay National Wildlife Refuge sits within one of the most heavily militarized and developed coastal zones in the Western Hemisphere. Naval Air Station North Island, the Coronado naval complex, naval submarine bases, and industrial port facilities ring the bay. Against this backdrop, the refuge's marshes and mudflats occupy their small but critical acreage, maintained by federal wildlife managers who work to restore what development erased.

Restoration efforts have included removing invasive plants, replanting native marsh vegetation, and improving tidal circulation. The refuge's success in maintaining populations of endangered and threatened species within this context represents one of conservation's more improbable achievements — a functioning remnant of a lost ecosystem, surrounded on all sides by the infrastructure of the modern world.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.60°N, 117.12°W along the southern reaches of San Diego Bay. The refuge's salt marshes and mudflats are visible from low altitudes as a band of green-brown vegetation along the bay's margin, distinct from the surrounding developed and military areas. Naval Air Station North Island (KNZY) is immediately to the north. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 8 miles north. At lower altitudes and in clear weather, the transition between marsh and developed land is clearly visible.
