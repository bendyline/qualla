# Tijuana Estuary

Straddling the US-Mexico border, the 2,500-acre Tijuana Estuary is the largest remaining coastal wetland between Santa Barbara and Mexico's San Quintín — and one of the most polluted natural areas in California.

**Location:** 32.5567, -117.1260

**Categories:** Estuaries of California, National Natural Landmarks in California, Tijuana River watershed

## Introduction

Three times the size of Central Park, the Tijuana Estuary occupies a stretch of coastline just north of the international border, its salt marshes and mudflats extending inland from the Pacific in a landscape that looks nothing like the surrounding development. More than 370 species of birds have been recorded here, drawn by the Pacific Flyway — the migratory corridor that channels millions of birds along the California coast each year. The estuary's ecological value is extraordinary. Its pollution problem is equally so.

## The Last Large Wetland

The Tijuana Estuary was designated a National Natural Landmark in 1973, recognition of what was already becoming clear: this was the last significant coastal wetland in a region where development had consumed nearly everything else. Between Santa Barbara and San Quintín in Baja California, coastal estuaries and salt marshes had been systematically filled, dredged, and developed through the twentieth century. The Tijuana Estuary survived largely because it sat at the border — a zone where jurisdiction complications and the difficulty of developing floodplain land near an international boundary created a kind of accidental protection.

The estuary's 2,500 acres encompass salt marshes, mudflats, upland scrub, and the lower reach of the Tijuana River as it nears its entry into the Pacific. The variety of habitats within this relatively compact area supports an ecological richness that far outstrips what its acreage alone might suggest. Rare plant communities grow in the salt marsh, and the endangered California least tern and light-footed clapper rail both nest within the estuary's boundaries.

## Under the Flyway

The Pacific Flyway is one of four major migratory routes in North America, channeling birds from their Arctic and subarctic breeding grounds to wintering areas in California, Mexico, and Central and South America. The Tijuana Estuary sits directly in this flyway's path, making it a critical stop for long-distance migrants that need food and rest.

The variety of birds recorded here reflects the estuary's position: shorebirds that breed in Alaska, waterfowl that winter in Mexico, resident herons and egrets that remain year-round. Species like the marbled godwit, the long-billed dowitcher, and the western sandpiper appear in large numbers during migration. For birders, the estuary is among the most productive sites in Southern California — a concentration of avian diversity in a small, accessible area.

## A River Carrying Two Countries' Problems

The estuary's central challenge is the Tijuana River. The river drains a watershed that spans the US-Mexico border, and by the time it reaches the estuary, it carries the accumulated pollution of a rapidly growing binational metropolitan area. Tijuana's sewage infrastructure has chronically struggled to keep pace with the city's growth, and during rain events — when stormwater overwhelms treatment capacity — raw sewage enters the river and flows into the estuary and the adjacent ocean.

The resulting pollution has periodically forced closure of beaches on both sides of the border, created public health hazards in the estuary itself, and degraded habitat that depends on water quality. The problem is structural: it requires infrastructure investment on the Mexican side of the border, coordination between two national governments, and ongoing maintenance of treatment facilities that must handle a growing population's waste. Progress has been made in fits and starts, but the estuary continues to receive pollution loads that would be unacceptable in almost any other protected natural area in the country.

## Restoration in Progress

Despite its pollution challenges, the Tijuana Estuary has been the site of sustained ecological restoration work. Scientists and land managers have removed invasive plants, replanted native marsh vegetation, and worked to improve the hydrology of areas where channels have been disrupted. The estuary's research reserve status brings ongoing scientific study — data on bird populations, water quality, sediment dynamics, and plant communities that documents both the estuary's health and the effects of restoration interventions.

The estuary also serves a role that no amount of pollution can entirely eliminate: it is a living demonstration that nature, given any foothold at all, continues to function. Birds appear by the thousands regardless of the river's condition. The salt marsh plants persist. The tidal cycle moves water in and out twice daily, refreshing the system with each flush. The estuary endures, imperfect and essential, at the edge of two countries.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.557°N, 117.126°W at the Pacific coast just north of the US-Mexico border. From altitude, the estuary is visible as a band of green-brown marsh and open water contrasting with the surrounding developed landscape. The border fence is visible to the south, and the Pacific Coast stretches north toward San Diego. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 15 miles north. At 3,000-6,000 feet in clear weather, the distinct habitats within the estuary are visible.
