# Pacific Reserve Fleet, San Diego

San Diego's mothball fleet stored up to 223 surplus warships after World War II, a silent armada held in reserve for a war on the scale of the last one that never came.

**Location:** 32.6791, -117.1250

**Categories:** 1946 establishments in California, Military in San Diego, United States Navy reserve fleets, Pacific Reserve Fleet, San Diego

## Introduction

At its peak, 223 ships rode at anchor in San Diego Bay, their machinery sealed against the damp, their guns covered, their engines cold. This was the Pacific Reserve Fleet — the Navy's mothball fleet — a city of idle warships preserved against the possibility of another catastrophic war. The name fit perfectly: vast, orderly, slightly melancholy.

## The Art of Putting a Fleet to Sleep

After World War II ended in 1945, the United States faced an unprecedented problem: it had built far more ships than it could use during peacetime. Rather than scrap them immediately — a decision that would have been irreversible — the Navy developed techniques to preserve entire fleets in a state of suspended animation. Ships were dehumidified, their machinery coated in rust-preventive compounds, their hulls sealed against corrosion. Openings were covered and hatches secured. The vessels weren't dead, exactly; they were resting, waiting for orders that might never come.

San Diego's reserve fleet occupied a stretch of bay near Naval Base San Diego, its rows of gray vessels becoming a fixture of the waterfront landscape through the 1950s and 1960s. The collection included everything from massive carriers to humble minesweepers — a cross-section of American naval power that had won the war and now sat in careful storage. Seven Commencement Bay-class escort carriers rode alongside four Casablanca-class carriers. Minesweepers with names like Chief, Zeal, and Competent floated silently in formation.

## Wars That Called the Ships Back

The mothball fleet was never purely static. When the Korean War broke out in 1950, the Navy reached into reserve stocks and reactivated ships, pulling them from their cocoons, restoring their systems, and sending them to sea again. The same happened during the Vietnam War era, when demand for certain vessel types outpaced what the active fleet could supply.

This was the fleet's essential purpose — not display, not nostalgia, but strategic reserve. The ships represented sunk costs that had already been paid, hulls that could be restored to service faster than new vessels could be built. Some of the ships stored at San Diego had even older histories: the reserve fleet had stored post-World War I vessels, some of which were reactivated for World War II.

## The Last Ship Leaves

By the 1970s, the logic of large reserve fleets had shifted, and hulls built for the last world war were aging out of usefulness. The San Diego fleet shrank steadily as ships were sold for scrap or designated as target vessels for naval exercises.

When the fleet finally closed in June 1975, only twelve ships remained. Most were sold to scrap yards; a few met their end on the bottom of the Pacific, serving one last purpose as targets for weapons testing. The final ship to leave was the USS Galveston, a Cleveland-class cruiser later rebuilt to fire Talos missiles, laid up here in 1970 and sold for scrap in 1975 - the distinction of being the last vessel to exit a fleet that had once housed 223 warships.

The closure ended nearly three decades of San Diego's association with the mothball fleet, a quiet chapter in naval history that had played out largely out of public sight, in the waters off a base that had been keeping ships since it was founded as a destroyer base in 1922.

## What the Fleet Represented

The mothball fleet was a hedge against catastrophe — a nation's insurance policy written in steel. The United States maintained reserve fleets on both coasts and in other locations, understanding that the cost of building a new navy from scratch in the middle of a major war would be staggering in both time and lives. The ships at San Diego represented a particular calculation: that a war of the World War II scale might happen again, and that if it did, every preserved hull would be worth more than the expense of keeping it.

That scale of war never came. The ships waited through Korea, through Vietnam, through the Cold War's tensest years, and were eventually scrapped as obsolete technology overtook the vessels' usefulness. The light carrier USS San Jacinto, laid up here in 1947 and sold for scrap in 1971, the carrier USS Philippine Sea, the repair ship USS Aristaeus — names that had once meant something in American naval power — quietly disappeared from the bay they had occupied for years. Only the historical record remains of the fleet that once crowded San Diego's waterfront.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.68°N, 117.12°W, near Naval Base San Diego along the eastern shore of San Diego Bay. At 5,000-8,000 feet, the bay's extensive naval installations are visible — destroyers, cruisers, amphibious ships and support vessels call Naval Base San Diego home, the Pacific Fleet's principal homeport, while aircraft carriers berth across the bay at North Island; today's ships are active rather than mothballed. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) sits on the north shore of the bay, about 9 km northwest. The Silver Strand separates the bay from the Pacific, running from Imperial Beach up to Coronado and North Island Naval Air Station (KNZY), about 9 km west-northwest.
