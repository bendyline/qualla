# San Diego Bay

California's third-largest bay is home to ten museum ships, the Navy's Pacific fleet, and a natural harbor so perfect that Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo recognized its value the moment he sailed through the entrance in 1542.

**Location:** 32.6480, -117.1910

**Categories:** Bays of California, San Diego Bay, San Diego geography

## Introduction

Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo was the first European to sail into San Diego Bay, in September 1542, and he called it San Miguel. He seems to have understood immediately what he had found: a protected harbor twelve miles long, sheltered from the open Pacific by a narrow peninsula, deep enough for ships, calm enough for anchorage. He was right about all of it. What he couldn't have known was that this bay would eventually become home to the largest concentration of naval power in the Western Hemisphere.

## The Shape of the Bay

San Diego Bay runs roughly north to south, twelve miles from tip to tip, shaped by the Silver Strand — a narrow sandbar separating the bay from the Pacific Ocean to the west. The bay is California's third largest, after San Francisco Bay and Humboldt Bay, though its relative compactness compared to San Francisco makes it feel more intimate. The entrance from the ocean is narrow enough that ships entering can be seen clearly from shore.

The harbor's geography made San Diego strategically irresistible to the United States Navy, which established a presence here in the nineteenth century and expanded dramatically during World War II. Today, Naval Base San Diego on the eastern shore, Naval Air Station North Island at the bay's northern end, and Naval Submarine Base Point Loma combine to make this bay one of the most militarized stretches of coastline in the world.

## A Fleet of Museum Ships

San Diego Bay is home to ten museum ships — a floating collection that spans more than a century of maritime history. The USS Midway, a Cold War-era aircraft carrier that served from 1945 to 1992, now sits at Navy Pier as the most-visited naval aviation museum in the country. The Star of India, an iron-hulled sailing ship launched in 1863, is the world's oldest active sailing ship still regularly taken to sea.

Between these bookends lies an extraordinary collection: a Soviet submarine, a steam ferry, a pilot boat, and several other vessels that together tell the story of American maritime history. The Maritime Museum of San Diego, which maintains most of these ships, has assembled one of the most concentrated collections of historic vessels anywhere on the Pacific Coast. Visitors can walk the decks of ships ranging from the age of sail to the jet age, all within sight of an active naval base.

## Commerce, Nature, and the Living Bay

Beyond its military and museum presence, San Diego Bay supports a complex ecology and a working commercial waterfront. The bay's waters host marine mammals, fish, and seabirds alongside container ships, fishing vessels, and the Navy's active fleet. The San Diego Bay National Wildlife Refuge protects remnant salt marshes along the southern shoreline, where endangered birds nest within sight of naval installations.

The Port of San Diego handles significant cargo traffic, and a commercial tuna fishing industry operated from the bay for much of the twentieth century — San Diego once styled itself the 'Tuna Capital of the World.' Recreational boating, kayaking, and sailing are year-round activities on waters that typically remain calm behind the Silver Strand's protection. The bay's geography creates a kind of shelter that makes it simultaneously a military asset and a recreational paradise.

## The View from the Bridge

Every Fourth of July, the Big Bay Boom sends fireworks skyward from multiple barges anchored across the bay, creating a synchronized display visible from both shores. It is the largest single-display Fourth of July fireworks show in the United States by some measures — fitting for a city whose relationship with the military has made patriotic spectacle a natural part of civic identity.

The San Diego-Coronado Bridge arches across the bay's northern section, its curving design engineered to accommodate naval ship traffic below. Standing on the bridge and looking south, the bay's full length is visible on clear days, from the busy naval piers in the middle distance to the thin line of the Silver Strand and the open Pacific beyond. This is the view that Cabrillo's pilot might have imagined: a bay that still rewards those who can see its full extent.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.648°N, 117.191°W. San Diego Bay is one of the most visually distinctive features of Southern California from the air — a twelve-mile-long protected harbor flanked by the Silver Strand to the west. The USS Midway's distinctive carrier deck is visible at Navy Pier. Naval Base San Diego's rows of warships line the eastern shore. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) sits at the bay's northern end. NAS North Island (KNZY) occupies the northern tip of Coronado. Best viewed at 5,000-10,000 feet in clear weather.
