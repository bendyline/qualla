# San Diego Bay

California's third-largest bay is home to ten museum ships, the Navy's Pacific fleet, and a natural harbor that Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo, sailing in through the entrance in 1542, called a very good enclosed port.

**Location:** 32.6480, -117.1910

## Introduction

Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo was the first European to sail into San Diego Bay, in September 1542, and he called it San Miguel. His expedition's account described a very good enclosed port: a protected harbor twelve miles long, sheltered from the open Pacific by a narrow peninsula, deep enough for ships, calm enough for anchorage. Sixty years later Sebastian Vizcaino renamed it San Diego. What Cabrillo couldn't have known was that this bay would eventually become one of the great concentrations of naval power in the world.

## The Shape of the Bay

San Diego Bay curves from northwest to southeast, twelve miles from tip to tip and one to three miles wide, shaped by the Silver Strand — a narrow sandbar separating the bay from the Pacific Ocean to the west. The bay is the third largest of California's three large natural bays, after San Francisco Bay and Humboldt Bay, though its relative compactness compared to San Francisco makes it feel more intimate. The entrance from the ocean is narrow enough that ships entering can be seen clearly from shore.

The harbor's geography made San Diego strategically irresistible to the United States Navy, which established a presence here around the turn of the twentieth century and expanded dramatically during World War II. Today, Naval Base San Diego on the eastern shore, Naval Air Station North Island on the north tip of Coronado, home port of several aircraft carriers, and the submarine piers of Naval Base Point Loma combine to make this bay one of the most militarized stretches of coastline in the world.

## A Fleet of Museum Ships

San Diego Bay is home to ten museum ships — a floating collection that spans more than a century of maritime history. The USS Midway, an aircraft carrier that served from 1945 to 1992, now sits at Navy Pier as a museum that bills itself as the most-visited naval ship museum in the world, with more than a million visitors a year. The Star of India, an iron-hulled sailing ship launched in 1863, is the world's oldest active sailing ship, still occasionally taken to sea.

Between these bookends lies an extraordinary collection: a Soviet submarine, a steam ferry, a pilot boat, and several other vessels that together tell the story of American maritime history. The Maritime Museum of San Diego, which maintains most of these ships, has assembled one of the most concentrated collections of historic vessels anywhere on the Pacific Coast. Visitors can walk the decks of ships ranging from the age of sail to the jet age, all within sight of an active naval base.

## Commerce, Nature, and the Living Bay

Beyond its military and museum presence, San Diego Bay supports a complex ecology and a working commercial waterfront. The bay's waters host marine mammals, fish, and seabirds alongside container ships, fishing vessels, and the Navy's active fleet. The San Diego Bay National Wildlife Refuge protects remnant salt marshes along the southern shoreline, where endangered birds nest within sight of naval installations.

The Port of San Diego handles significant cargo traffic, and a commercial tuna fishing industry operated from the bay for much of the twentieth century — San Diego once styled itself the 'Tuna Capital of the World.' Recreational boating, kayaking, and sailing are year-round activities on waters that typically remain calm behind the Silver Strand's protection. The bay's geography creates a kind of shelter that makes it simultaneously a military asset and a recreational paradise.

## The View from the Bridge

Every Fourth of July, the Port of San Diego's Big Bay Boom sends fireworks skyward from four barges anchored across the bay, creating a synchronized display visible from both shores and watched by about half a million people. It is one of the largest annual fireworks displays in the United States — fitting for a city whose relationship with the military has made patriotic spectacle a natural part of civic identity.

The San Diego-Coronado Bridge, opened in 1969, arches across the bay's northern section, rising 200 feet so that most Navy ships can pass beneath; the largest carriers berth north of it. Looking south from its crest - the bridge carries cars, not pedestrians, outside of special events - the bay's full length is visible on clear days, from the busy naval piers in the middle distance to the thin line of the Silver Strand and the open Pacific beyond. This is the view that Cabrillo's pilot might have imagined: a bay that still rewards those who can see its full extent.
