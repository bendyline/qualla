# Tijuana River National Estuarine Research Reserve

A federally protected research reserve at the US-Mexico border that protects 370 bird species and tracks the complex ecology of a wetland shaped by two nations and one of California's most polluted rivers.

**Location:** 32.5556, -117.1230

**Categories:** National Estuarine Research Reserves, Tijuana River, Wildlife refuges in California

## Introduction

The research station sits a few miles from downtown Imperial Beach, in the middle of one of the most complicated ecological situations in California: a national estuarine reserve receiving chronic pollution from a river that flows out of Mexico, managed by American federal agencies, in a wetland whose health depends on decisions made in two countries. The scientists who work here study not just the ecology of the estuary but the intersection of political geography and natural systems — because at the Tijuana River National Estuarine Research Reserve, the two cannot be separated.

## What a Research Reserve Does

National Estuarine Research Reserves are designated sites managed cooperatively by NOAA and state governments for long-term research, education, and stewardship. The Tijuana River reserve, established at the US-Mexico border, is unique in the national system for its international context: the estuary it protects is fed by a river originating in Mexico, and the ecological stresses it faces — particularly sewage pollution — arise from binational conditions that no single government fully controls.

The reserve encompasses the Tijuana Estuary and adjacent lands, protecting habitat that supports more than 370 species of birds, several federally listed endangered species, and one of the last intact coastal salt marshes in Southern California. Long-term monitoring data gathered here documents seasonal changes, pollution events, bird population trends, and the effects of restoration interventions — creating a scientific record that would otherwise not exist for this type of ecosystem.

## The Sediment Problem at Goat Canyon

One of the reserve's persistent management challenges comes from Goat Canyon, a drainage arroyo that channels stormwater and sediment from the urban areas south of the border into the estuary system. During significant rain events, Goat Canyon delivers enormous quantities of sediment — debris, sediment, and other materials carried by fast-moving stormwater — into the reserve's sensitive habitats.

In 2005, the reserve constructed sediment basins at Goat Canyon to capture some of this material before it reached the most sensitive areas. The basins require periodic cleaning as they fill with accumulated sediment. The project represented a practical response to a problem without a simple solution: the sediment comes from an urbanized watershed across an international boundary, and the only feasible intervention point is within the reserve itself, at the point where the drainage enters American jurisdiction.

## Cleanup at Scale

The reserve has received substantial federal investment for cleanup and restoration. A $1.6 million cleanup grant funded work to remove debris, restore habitat, and improve conditions for the endangered and sensitive species that depend on the estuary. The cleanup work targeted materials deposited by flood events and chronic pollution — tires, construction debris, and other solid waste mixed with the natural sediment accumulation.

The scale of cleanup required reflects decades of inadequate pollution control upstream. What flows into the reserve during major rain events is not simply dirty water; it contains solid materials that must be physically removed. The restoration work is ongoing, not a single project but a continuous effort to maintain the minimum habitat quality that protected species require.

## The Birds That Make It Worth the Effort

Despite everything — the sewage events, the sediment pulses, the chronic pollution — the Tijuana River National Estuarine Research Reserve remains one of the best birding sites in Southern California. The 370-plus species recorded here span shorebirds, waterfowl, raptors, songbirds, and species found almost nowhere else in the region. The light-footed clapper rail, one of the most endangered birds in California, maintains a population here. The California least tern nests on reserve property during the summer months.

For the scientists, the birds are simultaneously the point and the measure. The presence of these sensitive species in a reserve this polluted tells you something important about ecological resilience — about how much stress a natural system can absorb while still functioning. The reserve exists to understand that threshold, to document where the line is between a wetland that works and one that doesn't. The birds, by continuing to appear year after year, are providing the data.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.556°N, 117.123°W in Imperial Beach, California, near the US-Mexico border. The reserve is visible from altitude as wetland habitat adjacent to the coast, just north of the border. The Tijuana River channel enters the Pacific here, and the estuary's open water and marsh are distinct from the surrounding developed landscape. San Diego International Airport (KSAN) is approximately 15 miles north. At 3,000-6,000 feet in clear weather, the reserve's distinct habitat patches are visible.
