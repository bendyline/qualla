# San Jacinto Battlefield

A 567-foot obelisk rises from the coastal marsh east of Houston - taller than the Washington Monument, deliberately so - marking the spot where an eighteen-minute battle ended one revolution and started a republic.

**Location:** 29.7489, -95.0803

**Categories:** battles, texas-revolution, monuments, texas, military-history

## Introduction

The monument is the first thing you see, and Texans designed it that way. Rising 567 feet from the flat coastal prairie where Buffalo Bayou meets the San Jacinto River, the San Jacinto Monument is, by a few stubborn feet, taller than the Washington Monument - a fact that appears in every guidebook and on most of the bronze plaques at the base. Texas planners measured the rival obelisk in Washington and added enough height to win. The thirty-four-foot star on top, plus a few extra feet of pedestal, did the trick. The monument was dedicated in 1939 on a slow-sinking patch of bayou land where, on April 21, 1836, the Texas Revolution was decided in less time than it takes to walk across a parking lot.

## The Setting and the Stakes

In the spring of 1836, the new Republic of Texas was barely a republic at all. Its declaration of independence was six weeks old. Its army was a retreating coalition of farmers, frontiersmen, Tejano volunteers under Juan Seguin, and recent immigrants from the United States - perhaps nine hundred fighters in total, commanded by Sam Houston. They had been falling back for weeks across east Texas, trailed by the Mexican army of General Antonio Lopez de Santa Anna. Behind them stretched a procession of refugees - women, children, slaves, families on foot - in what Texans would later call the Runaway Scrape. Behind Santa Anna, the Alamo and Goliad had already happened. The garrison at the Alamo had been killed to a man. At Goliad, more than three hundred Texan prisoners had been marched out and shot. Vengeance was not an abstraction at San Jacinto. It was the air the Texan camp breathed.

## Eighteen Minutes

Santa Anna chose his ground badly. His camp on the plain near the San Jacinto River was hemmed in by water and woods, with only one road of retreat. On the morning of April 21, General Cos arrived with about 540 reinforcements, raising the Mexican force to roughly 1,400. The men had marched through the night. They were exhausted. With no Texan attack apparent by midday, Santa Anna let his army rest - many soldiers were asleep when, at about 4:30 in the afternoon, Houston ordered the advance. The Texan line moved through tall grass and into a single artillery volley from the Twin Sisters, two cannons donated by the city of Cincinnati. Then the line broke into a charge. Houston's men poured over the breastworks shouting 'Remember the Alamo! Remember Goliad!' The fighting lasted about eighteen minutes. The killing went on much longer.

## The Killing After

What followed the battle has always been the harder thing to write about. Mexican soldiers, many of them conscripts from Indian villages in central Mexico who had not chosen this war, threw down their weapons and ran for the marsh. Texan riflemen lined the banks of Peggy Lake and shot at them as they tried to swim. Soldiers crying 'Me no Alamo, me no Goliad' were killed where they stood. Houston and Secretary of War Thomas Rusk rode through the field trying to stop the slaughter and could not. By dusk, about 630 Mexican soldiers were dead and 730 had been captured. Texan losses were nine killed and about thirty wounded, Houston among them - shot in the ankle. Santa Anna was found the next day hiding in the tall grass, wearing the jacket of a common soldier. His own captured men gave him away by saluting when he was brought into camp.

## The Monument and the Marsh

The land where the battle happened belonged in 1836 to a widow named Margaret McCormick. When she asked Houston who would bury the hundreds of Mexican dead lying on her farm, he told her she should consider herself honored to host the battle that won Texas its independence. She did not. The bodies were never properly buried. A century later, in 1936, Texas began construction on the monument - the centennial year of the battle, a year of self-conscious nation-building inside a state. The 567-foot obelisk, faced in cream-colored Texas limestone and topped with a thirty-four-foot Lone Star, is the tallest masonry column in the world. An observation deck near the top, reachable by elevator, looks out over the Houston Ship Channel, the chemical plants and refineries that line it, and the battlefield's preserved acres of coastal prairie. The whole site sinks at about an inch a year. The ground here is younger than the monument.

## What Remains

The San Jacinto Battleground State Historic Site preserves about twelve hundred acres of prairie, marsh, and bayou. Walking trails follow the approximate routes of the Texan advance and the Mexican retreat. The Battleship Texas was moored just offshore from 1948 until 2022, when she was towed to Galveston for hull repairs. The monument's Museum of History tells the story in a long single hall at the base. What none of the displays say outright, but what the place still seems to insist on, is that this was not a clean victory. It was a brief, brutal battle followed by hours of pursuit and slaughter, and the result - a republic, then a state, then a war with Mexico a decade later, then statehood and slavery and the long argument about what Texas meant to be - is what the obelisk was built to commemorate. The wind through the prairie grass does most of the rest of the talking.
