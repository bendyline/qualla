# New Orleans: The City That Care Forgot

The city where jazz was invented, where the dead are buried above ground, where Mardi Gras never really ends, and where Katrina's floodwaters couldn't drown a 300-year-old culture.

**Location:** 29.9511, -90.0715

**Categories:** louisiana, new-orleans, city, jazz, mardi-gras

## Introduction

New Orleans sits mostly below sea level, protected by levees that failed catastrophically on August 29, 2005, when Hurricane Katrina drowned 80% of the city. More than 1,800 people died across the Gulf Coast; whole neighborhoods were wiped from existence. Yet New Orleans persists, just as it has persisted through yellow fever epidemics, fires, French rule, Spanish rule, and American neglect. The city operates on its own logic - a French colonial grid in the Quarter, a legal system based on Napoleonic Code, an attitude toward pleasure that other American cities find alarming or liberating. They call it the 'City That Care Forgot' and the 'Big Easy,' names that capture both its casual relationship with responsibility and its gift for finding joy in ordinary moments.

## The Birthplace of Jazz

Jazz emerged from New Orleans in the early 1900s, a fusion of African rhythms, European harmonies, brass band traditions, and blues feeling that became America's original art form. The musicians who created it - Buddy Bolden, Jelly Roll Morton, Louis Armstrong - grew up in neighborhoods where music accompanied every occasion: funerals, parades, dances, church services. Storyville, the legalized red-light district that operated from 1897 to 1917, provided steady work for musicians; when it closed, they dispersed to Chicago and New York, spreading jazz north. Today Preservation Hall hosts traditional jazz nightly in a shabby French Quarter room with no air conditioning and no microphones. The Treme neighborhood, America's oldest Black neighborhood, still produces musicians. Jazz invented here reverberates worldwide.

## Above-Ground Dead

New Orleans buries its dead above ground in elaborate tombs because the water table lies just inches below the surface - dig a grave and it fills with water, coffins floating up during floods. The result is cities of the dead: St. Louis Cemetery No. 1 (where the supposed tomb of Voodoo Queen Marie Laveau draws visitors), Lafayette Cemetery in the Garden District, Metairie Cemetery with its elaborate Victorian monuments. The tombs are designed to handle multiple burials - after a year and a day, remains are pushed to the back to make room for the next family member. Yellow fever epidemics in the 1800s filled these cemeteries quickly; one in four New Orleanians died in the 1853 outbreak. Walking the narrow cemetery paths, past crumbling tombs and weathered angels, captures something essential about a city that has always lived alongside death.

## Mardi Gras Year-Round

Mardi Gras - Fat Tuesday - falls 47 days before Easter, the final celebration before Catholic Lent. In New Orleans, Carnival season begins January 6 and builds for weeks: parades every weekend, masked balls, the krewes (private organizations) preparing floats and throws. The final weekend is chaos - multiple parades daily, a million visitors, beads flying, brass bands on every corner. But New Orleans celebrates beyond Mardi Gras. Jazz Fest in late April, French Quarter Festival, the Essence Festival, St. Patrick's Day, the Greek Festival, the second line parades that follow jazz funerals and social club events year-round. The city looks for excuses to parade, to dance in the street, to make music. The party never truly ends; it just varies in intensity.

## After the Flood

Hurricane Katrina made landfall east of New Orleans on August 29, 2005, but the city's destruction came from the levees - earthen barriers that broke in over 50 places, flooding neighborhoods that had been dry throughout the storm. The Lower Ninth Ward was devastated; the water marks on houses reached rooftops. For weeks, bodies floated in the streets. The population dropped from 450,000 to fewer than 100,000; years passed before many neighborhoods recovered. Some never did. But New Orleans rebuilt, stubbornly, imperfectly, with less population and different demographics but the same fundamental character. The food is still extraordinary. The music still pours from every venue. The attitude that earned the nickname 'Big Easy' survives the worst natural disaster in American history.

## Navigating the Crescent City

Louis Armstrong New Orleans International Airport (MSY) lies west of the city in Kenner. The French Quarter, the original colonial settlement, remains the tourist center - Jackson Square, Bourbon Street, St. Louis Cathedral, beignets at Cafe Du Monde. But New Orleans extends far beyond: the Garden District with its antebellum mansions, Magazine Street's shops, the Bywater's artists and bars, the Treme's musical heritage. Streetcars run on St. Charles Avenue and Canal Street, the oldest continuously operating streetcar system in the world. The Mississippi River curves through the city, earning it the nickname 'Crescent City'; the French Quarter sits on high ground that's actually above sea level. From altitude, New Orleans appears as development pressed between the river and Lake Pontchartrain - a precarious position for a city that refuses to relocate.

What appears from the air as a city in a precarious position between lake and river is the Big Easy - where jazz was born, where the dead rise above ground, and where the good times roll despite everything.
