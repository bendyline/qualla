# Fort Worth Stockyards

On April 1, 1981, a hundred-thousand-square-foot honky tonk opened in a converted cattle barn - and the Fort Worth Stockyards, ten years past their last auction, reinvented themselves without ever pretending the old life was gone.

**Location:** 32.7902, -97.3461

**Categories:** history, western-heritage, historic-district, texas, music-venues

## Introduction

The last cattle auction in Fort Worth's Stockyards happened in 1971. After eight decades of bawling steers, shouting buyers, and the constant percussion of hooves on wooden chutes, the pens went quiet. The packing houses had closed years earlier. Swift and Armour, the two giants who had pulled Fort Worth into the meatpacking century, were gone. The district north of downtown looked like what it was: an industrial graveyard with brick streets and a cattlemen's hotel that nobody seemed to need anymore. Then, on April 1, 1981, Billy Bob Barnett opened a honky tonk inside a former exhibit hall and cattle barn - a hundred thousand square feet of dance floor, bull-riding arena, and bar - and the Stockyards stopped being a memorial.

## How Cowtown Got Its Name

The Texas and Pacific Railway reached Fort Worth in 1876, and a frontier garrison town with maybe five hundred residents began turning into something else. Cattle drives that had pushed north along the Chisholm Trail now had a closer destination: the Fort Worth railhead, where Eastern markets could be reached by rail instead of by trail. Local boosters incorporated the Fort Worth Stockyards Company in 1893 and recruited the Swift and Armour packing companies to build slaughterhouses on site. By 1907 the yards were handling a million head of cattle a year. At its peak, Fort Worth processed more livestock than any city in the country except Chicago - a distinction the town wore with the same swagger as the boots its workers ordered from Leddy's down the street.

## The Long Decline

The end did not arrive all at once. Refrigerated trucks made it possible to slaughter cattle closer to the ranch, and regional auctions began siphoning business away in the 1950s. Armour shut its Fort Worth plant in 1962. Swift held on until 1971, the same year the central livestock auction ran its last sale. For a decade the Stockyards drifted - too historically dense to demolish, too economically dead to revive. The buildings were listed on the National Register in 1976, but a listing is not a livelihood. Restaurants came and went. The Livestock Exchange Building, once thick with brokers in three-piece suits, rented rooms by the month. The wooden pens, weathered silver-gray, stood empty against the Texas sky.

## Billy Bob's and the Reinvention

Billy Bob's Texas changed the math. Opened on April 1, 1981 in a building that had previously held a department store, a roller rink, and an exhibit hall for the Fort Worth Fat Stock Show, it billed itself as the world's largest honky tonk - and the claim has held up. There is an indoor bull-riding arena, forty bar stations, a dance floor wide enough to lose a small herd in, and a stage that has hosted Willie Nelson, Garth Brooks, and Merle Haggard. The crowds Billy Bob's pulled in brought the rest of the district back to life. Saloons, boot shops, leather goods, hat makers, and steakhouses filled the brick storefronts along Exchange Avenue. The Fort Worth Herd, the daily cattle drive that has become the district's signature, was introduced in 1999 as part of the city's sesquicentennial celebration. Twice a day, a small longhorn herd is walked down the avenue by drovers in period dress - tourism, yes, but tourism built on muscle memory.

## Honest Work and the Smell of It

The Stockyards never quite scrub up. The brick is stained where it should be stained. The pens still smell faintly of what once filled them - manure and dust and the metallic tang of fresh paint laid over old iron. M.L. Leddy's hand-stitches custom boots in the back of its shop, and a pair takes months to make because that is how long it takes when one bootmaker is working leather with awl and thread. The Stockyards Hotel rents rooms named after Bonnie Parker, who is said to have stayed there with Clyde Barrow, and Bonnie's room is the one tourists request. Cattle work was filthy, brutal, and lucrative for a few - the meatpacking floor was no place anyone romanticized while they worked it. What the Stockyards preserve is not a sanitized memory of that life but the buildings and the rhythms it left behind, and the people who came to keep both going.

## Where the West Begins, Still

Fort Worth advertises itself as 'Where the West Begins,' a slogan that earns its keep about a mile north of downtown. The longhorns walk Exchange Avenue at eleven-thirty and four. The Cowtown Coliseum, built in 1908, still hosts the Stockyards Championship Rodeo every Friday and Saturday night - the longest-running indoor rodeo in the world. Billy Bob's still opens its doors most nights. The district has its critics, who argue that the reinvention is closer to theme park than to history, and they have a point about some of the souvenir storefronts. But the bones of the place are real - the brick, the rail spurs, the Exchange Building's terra cotta. Walk Exchange Avenue at six in the morning, before the tourists arrive and the longhorns are still in their pens, and you can hear what the place used to sound like: nothing much, just the wind moving through wooden chutes and the city waking up around them.
