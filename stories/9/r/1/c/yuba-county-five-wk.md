# The Yuba County Five

Five men with intellectual disabilities or mental illness set off home from a basketball game in February 1978 and were never seen alive again - their car found about 70 miles from Chico on a snowy Sierra forest road, four bodies recovered months later, the fifth still missing, the families still waiting for answers.

**Location:** 39.6500, -121.2800

**Categories:** california, unsolved, 1970s, sierra-nevada, history

## Introduction

Jack Madruga, Bill Sterling, Ted Weiher, Jack Huett and Gary Mathias drove home from a college basketball game in Chico on a Friday night in February 1978. They never got home. Their families called them 'the boys,' though all five were grown men in their twenties and thirties, all of whom lived with mild intellectual disabilities or a treated mental illness, all of whom played together on a Special Olympics-style team called the Gateway Gators that had a tournament game the next day, with a free week in Los Angeles for the winners. The route from Chico to Yuba City is a flat hour south down Highway 99. Madruga's 1969 Mercury Montego was found four days later about seventy miles from Chico, stuck in snow on a mountain road at 4,400 feet in the Plumas National Forest. Forty-eight years later, nobody has been able to say why.

## The Gators

Jack Madruga was 30, the oldest after Weiher, the group's driver, a former Army soldier who his family said would never let anyone else drive his car. Bill Sterling, 29, was Madruga's closest friend. Ted Weiher, 32, lived with his family and had worked as a janitor. Jack Huett, 24, was the youngest. Gary Mathias, 25, was an Army veteran who had served in West Germany and whose schizophrenia was, by all accounts, well-controlled on medication; his doctors counted him a success. They were teammates, not the kind of friends who took spontaneous road trips. Their families described the same routine over and over to investigators: the men did not improvise. They told someone where they were going. They came home on time. The tournament the next day mattered to them. So did dinner.

## Friday Night

They watched UC Davis play Chico State at the Chico State gymnasium and left after the game. The clerk at Behr's Market in downtown Chico remembered them buying snacks, sodas and cartons of milk just before her 10 p.m. closing; the big group had held her up. After that the trail goes cold. The route south to Yuba City runs through farmland on flat highway. Somewhere they turned the other direction instead - east and then north and then up, climbing through the foothills of the Sierra Nevada and onto the Oroville-Quincy Highway, a narrow paved-then-gravel road that disappears into Plumas National Forest. On February 28, four days after the men had failed to arrive home, a Forest Service ranger who had noticed the car days earlier led deputies to Madruga's Montego on that road, well past where any of them had any reason to be, stuck in snow.

## What the Car Showed

The Montego was stuck, but not badly; police thought five young men could have pushed it out. The keys were gone. There was a quarter tank of gas, and when police hot-wired the car, it started at once. It was unlocked, with a window rolled down, which Madruga's family said he would never have done. A California road map lay inside, neatly folded. There was no sign of a struggle. The wrappers and empty cartons from the Chico store were still in the car. The temperature on the mountain that night dropped well below freezing. Whatever happened next, the men did not stay with the car. They set off on foot up the road, deeper into the mountains, through drifts four to six feet deep. None of them was dressed for it. None of them lived in country where you would expect that to make sense.

## What the Forest Showed

The snow began to retreat in June. On June 4, a group of motorcyclists came upon a Forest Service trailer about 19 miles up the road from where the car had been left. Ted Weiher's body was inside, wrapped in eight bedsheets; his own shoes were missing. His beard had grown out far beyond a few weeks; estimates from its growth put his survival at roughly eight to thirteen weeks. He had lost nearly half his body weight. He had died of starvation and hypothermia even though a locker nearby held enough dehydrated food to feed all five men for a year, unopened, the fuel tank for the heater went unused, and there were matches by a fireplace no one had lit. Someone had opened C-ration cans from a shed outside. Madruga and Sterling were found the next day on opposite sides of the road, 11.4 miles from the car, likely victims of hypothermia. Huett's remains were found in the woods about two miles from the trailer. Only Gary Mathias has never been found. His tennis shoes were in the trailer with Weiher's body, suggesting he had been there too and then left - investigators thought perhaps in Weiher's missing shoes - for somewhere none of the searchers have ever located.

## Why It Still Hurts

Theories are easy and most of them are unkind. Police came to believe the men took a wrong turn, possibly heading for a friend of Mathias's in Forbestown, then left the car and followed a Forest Service snowcat's tracks toward shelter. Some relatives were sure someone had forced them up the mountain; police found no evidence of a pursuit, but never ruled out foul play, and the case has never been solved. The men's families have spoken publicly for decades, and the men at the center of this story were sons and brothers and teammates, not a true-crime curiosity. Melba Madruga, Jack's mother, told reporters she knew 'good and well somebody made them do it.' The case is often called the 'American Dyatlov Pass,' after the 1959 Soviet incident in which nine experienced hikers fled their tent into a freezing night and died. What the comparison cannot do is bring anyone home.

## The Country

Plumas National Forest covers more than a million acres of pine and granite in the northern Sierra Nevada. The Oroville-Quincy Highway climbs out of the foothills above Lake Oroville and crests over 4,000 feet before dropping into the old gold-rush town of Quincy. The stretch where the car was found is often snowbound in winter and lonely in any season; the 2020 North Complex Fire later burned through this stretch of forest. Yuba City and Marysville, where the men lived, sit in the flat farmland of the Sacramento Valley about forty-five miles to the south-southwest. Chico, where they were last seen for certain, is about fifty miles north of Yuba City by road. Sacramento International Airport (KSMF) is the nearest major field, about 30 miles south of Yuba City.

## Flight Context

The Yuba County Five disappearance is centered on the Oroville-Quincy Highway in Plumas National Forest, northern California; Madruga's car was found in the Rogers Cow Camp area, around 39.77 N, 121.31 W. From the air, the terrain transitions sharply from the green rectangles of Sacramento Valley farmland into the brown-and-pine ridges of the Sierra Nevada foothills. Lake Oroville is the largest visible landmark, an irregular blue Y west of the route. The road itself winds out of the small town of Oroville and climbs steadily northeast through forested ridges, eventually cresting above 4,000 feet near the area where Madruga's car was found. The Forest Service trailer where Ted Weiher's body was discovered sat roughly 19 miles further up that road. Recommended viewing altitude is 8,500-10,500 feet AGL to clear the surrounding ridges with margin. Nearest tower-controlled fields are Chico Regional (KCIC, formerly Chico Municipal; tower part-time) about 25 nm west of the car site and Sacramento International (KSMF) about 65 nm south-southwest. Winter visibility is often limited by valley fog and mountain snow.
