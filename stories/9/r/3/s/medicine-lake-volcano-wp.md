# Medicine Lake Volcano

The largest volcano by volume in the Cascade Range hides in plain sight as a broad shield rather than a dramatic peak, its Glass Mountain obsidian flow erupting just 1,000 years ago.

**Location:** 41.6110, -121.5536

**Categories:** Cascade Volcanoes, Volcanoes of Siskiyou County, California, Lava Beds National Monument, Modoc National Forest, Modoc Plateau, Religious places of the Indigenous peoples of North America, Sacred mountains of the United States, Shield volcanoes of the United States, Subduction volcanoes, Calderas of California

## Introduction

In 1910, observers reported a light ashfall drifting from Glass Mountain. Was the volcano waking up? Scientists searched for evidence of an eruption but found nothing conclusive. The mystery endures because Medicine Lake Volcano operates differently from its famous neighbors. While Mount St. Helens and Mount Shasta built themselves into iconic cones through explosive eruptions, Medicine Lake spread outward, coating itself with flow after flow of basaltic lava over 500,000 years. The result is the largest volcano by volume in the entire Cascade Range, yet most people drive past without recognizing it as a volcano at all. In January 2025, President Biden designated much of this landscape as the Sattitla Highlands National Monument, protecting a place where glass still gleams from eruptions within human memory.

## The Hidden Giant

Newberry Volcano in Oregon claims second place. Medicine Lake holds first. The shield volcano rises from the Modoc Plateau in northeastern California, its lavas estimated to exceed all other Cascade volcanoes in total volume. The difference is shape: Medicine Lake spreads rather than towers. The thick shield extends east to west and north to south, covering an immense area. A caldera sits at the center, formed not by a single catastrophic eruption but by gradual collapse as lava repeatedly drained from beneath. The small lake within the caldera gave the volcano its name. Multiple small magma chambers, rather than one large reservoir, feed the system, making Medicine Lake unique among Cascade volcanoes.

## Glass Mountain's Black Mirror

The most recent eruption occurred around 1,000 years ago. Rhyolite and dacite pushed through vents near the caldera's eastern rim, creating Glass Mountain, a spectacular steep-sided flow of obsidian that remains nearly treeless today. The black volcanic glass flowed down the eastern flank, its surface too young and sharp for soil to accumulate. Ten additional small domes extend along a line to the north. Radiocarbon dating of an incense-cedar tree preserved at the flow's edge yielded an age of 885 years, give or take forty. The tree had lost its bark and outer rings, so the eruption may be somewhat younger. The obsidian still fractures into razor edges, prized by ancient peoples for toolmaking.

## Half a Million Years of Fire

Medicine Lake began growing about one million years ago, following massive eruptions of high-alumina basalt. Similar basalt has continued erupting ever since, building the lower flanks. Higher on the shield, andesite dominates, with rhyolite and dacite appearing near the summit. The volcano has proven episodic rather than steady. Eight eruptions produced basaltic lava about 10,500 years ago during a few hundred years of activity. Then silence for millennia. A small andesitic eruption broke the quiet around 4,300 years ago. Between 3,000 and 900 years ago, eight more eruptions laid down lavas ranging from basalt to rhyolite. Cinder cones dot the flanks, marking vents aligned along zones of crustal weakness trending northeast to northwest.

## Monument to Deep Time

Lava Beds National Monument sprawls across the volcano's northeast flank, protecting a landscape riddled with lava tubes, spatter cones, and ancient flows. The United States Forest Service manages most of the Medicine Lake Highlands as part of Klamath, Shasta-Trinity, and Modoc National Forests. The caldera may have formed through a process similar to Kilauea in Hawaii: repeated extrusions drained magma chambers, causing the surface to sag. Ring faults along the caldera rim channeled later eruptions. The volcano's location, in a zone of east-west crustal extension east of the main Cascade axis, explains its different character. It builds like a Hawaiian shield, even as it erupts like a Cascade volcano. In 2025, the new Sattitla Highlands National Monument added federal protection to this singular landscape.

## Flight Context

Medicine Lake Volcano centers at approximately 41.61°N, 121.55°W, northeast of Mount Shasta. The broad shield shape makes it less visually dramatic than typical stratovolcanoes. Look for the caldera depression and Medicine Lake at the summit area. Glass Mountain's dark obsidian flow is visible on the eastern flank. Lava Beds National Monument lies to the northeast. Nearest airports include Tulelake Municipal Airport (O81) and Montague-Yreka Rohrer Field (1O5). The volcano rises above the Modoc Plateau, with terrain varying from forested slopes to barren lava flows.
