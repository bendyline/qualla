# The Black Panther (newspaper)

Founded in Oakland in 1967 after a police killing went unreported by mainstream media, The Black Panther newspaper grew from a four-page newsletter into the most widely read Black newspaper in the United States, selling over 300,000 copies a week at its peak.

## Introduction

A twenty-two-year-old named Denzil Dowell was shot and killed by police in North Richmond, California, in April 1967. The mainstream press did not cover the story. For Huey P. Newton and Bobby Seale, two young organizers building a new political party in Oakland, the silence was the point. If no one else would tell the story, they would print it themselves. On April 25, 1967, they published a four-page newsletter called The Black Panther. It cost a quarter. Within a few years, it would become the most widely read Black newspaper in the United States.

## Ink as Infrastructure

The Black Panther Party is often remembered for its armed patrols and confrontations with law enforcement, but the newspaper was arguably its most effective tool. From the beginning, Newton and Seale understood that a movement needed a voice that could reach beyond the neighborhoods where organizers were physically present. The paper served as the connective tissue of the party's national network, distributing information about survival programs, free breakfast initiatives, and community health clinics alongside political analysis and calls to action. It was consciousness-raising in newsprint, and the party treated its production with the seriousness of any other operation. By 1970, the newspaper was generating a net income of $40,000 a month, making it the party's most stable revenue source. National headquarters received about 12.5 cents for each copy sold, a modest margin that added up fast when the press run reached six figures.

## Three Hundred Thousand Copies a Week

Between 1968 and 1971, The Black Panther achieved a weekly circulation of more than 300,000 copies, numbers that dwarfed most underground publications and rivaled established Black newspapers with decades of history behind them. The paper sold in cities across the United States and had an international readership. Its visual identity was as important as its prose. From 1967 to 1971, each issue averaged six images, many of them the bold, graphic illustrations of Revolutionary Artist Emory Douglas, whose work gave the paper a visual language as distinctive as its editorial voice. After a revision of the party's Ten Point Program in 1972, the number of images per issue dropped to an average of 2.4, and contributing artists thinned. The shift reflected broader changes within the party as it moved from its early militant phase toward electoral politics and community organizing in Oakland.

## The Women Who Built the Paper

By 1969, two-thirds of Black Panther Party members were women, and the newspaper reflected that reality. Women were heavily represented among the paper's staff and leadership, shaping its editorial direction and daily operations. Judy Juanita, an undergraduate at San Francisco State University, served as editor during the late 1960s, guiding the paper through its period of fastest growth. JoNina Abron was the final editor, steering the publication through its later years until the party dissolved in 1980. The contributions of these women and their colleagues challenge the popular image of the Black Panther Party as a predominantly male organization. The newspaper's production required not just political vision but sustained labor: writing, editing, layout, distribution, and the unglamorous work of keeping a weekly publication running on movement wages.

## What Remains on the Archive Shelf

The Black Panther ceased publication when the party dissolved in 1980, ending a thirteen-year run that tracked the arc of one of the most significant political movements in American history. The newspaper's archives are held at the African American Museum and Library at Oakland, and additional collections exist at California State University, Dominguez Hills, and Medgar Evers College in New York. Thirty issues are in the permanent collection of the Museum of Modern Art, recognized as much for their graphic design as their political content. In October 2016, the Oakland Museum of California mounted an exhibit called "All Power to the People: Black Panthers at 50," organized in cooperation with about 100 former party members. A commemorative 20-page issue of The Black Panther was published for the occasion, proof that the paper's voice still carried weight half a century after Denzil Dowell's name appeared on its first page.
