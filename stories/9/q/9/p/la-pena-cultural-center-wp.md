# La Peña Cultural Center

A Berkeley cultural center born from the ashes of Chile's democracy, where Latin American exiles turned art into resistance and built a community that outlasted the dictatorship.

## Introduction

On September 11, 1973, the Chilean military overthrew President Salvador Allende in a coup that would install Augusto Pinochet as dictator for seventeen years. Thousands of Chileans fled. Some ended up in Berkeley, California, where two years later they joined with American allies to open a storefront on Shattuck Avenue with a name borrowed from the informal gathering places of Latin American folk music: a peña. La Peña Cultural Center was not just a venue. It was an act of defiance in exile, a declaration that the music Pinochet was trying to silence would keep playing, eight thousand miles from Santiago.

## Eight Founders and a Borrowed Tradition

The founding group was deliberately binational. Five Americans - Eric Leenson, Kevin Duncan, Craig McCaleb, Janis Teruggi, and Kay Cole - partnered with three Chilean expatriates: Hugo Brenni, Patricia Brenni, and Juan Orson. Several had worked with Non-Intervention in Chile, an organization dedicated to supporting the Allende government and exposing Washington's role in undermining it. When they opened La Peña in 1975 at 3105 Shattuck Avenue in the Ashby neighborhood, they built it on a conviction that would define the center for decades: art and culture are not luxuries separate from political life. They are tools for resistance, solidarity, and community building. The peña tradition - informal spaces where musicians gather to play folk music - gave the center both its name and its philosophy.

## A Mural, a Menu, and a Movement

Walk past La Peña and the building announces itself before you step inside. Its façade is covered in a mural inspired by Nueva Canción, the "new song" movement that swept Latin America in the 1960s and 1970s, blending folk music with protest poetry. Inside, Café Valparaíso opened in 1976 and became part of Berkeley's famed Gourmet Ghetto. The menu leaned heavily on Chilean empanadas, and the café sold foodstuffs and novelties from Chile - small, tangible connections to a homeland many patrons could not safely visit. During the 1980s, as civil wars and political violence tore through Central America, La Peña became a gathering place for a new wave of refugees. Salvadorans, Guatemalans, and Nicaraguans found in Berkeley's peña what the Chileans had built: a place where displacement did not mean disappearance.

## Rhythms from Across the Americas

What began as a Chilean exile space grew into a hub for the musical traditions of an entire hemisphere. Puerto Rican bomba y plena, with its call-and-response drumming rooted in Afro-Caribbean communities, found a regular home at La Peña. So did Afro-Peruvian cajón and dance, Capoeira Angola from Brazil, Son Jarocho jarana and zapateo from Mexico, and Cuban charanga and salsa. These were not museum displays of frozen traditions. They were living classes and performances, taught by practitioners who carried the rhythms in their bodies. The center's Little Friends of La Peña concert series introduced bilingual children to these art forms, planting seeds in a generation that would grow up hearing the Americas as one interconnected musical conversation.

## Exile, Memory, and Return

Every year, the Chilean diaspora gathers at La Peña to mark the dates that define their community's calendar: the fiestas patrias celebrating Chilean independence, Christmas, and the anniversary of the 1973 coup. That last observance carries particular weight - a day of mourning for the democracy that was lost and for the thousands who were killed, tortured, or disappeared under Pinochet's regime. When the 2010 earthquake devastated Chile, La Peña hosted Mano a Mano, a benefit concert aimed at raising ten thousand dollars for relief. The center also served as a fundraising venue for KPFA, Berkeley's community radio station, and organized relief efforts for Puerto Rico following Hurricane Maria. As a registered 501(c)(3) nonprofit, La Peña channels its mission through concrete action: not just performing solidarity but funding it.

## Still Playing After Fifty Years

Half a century after its founding, La Peña endures on the same block of Shattuck Avenue where it started. Pinochet is dead. Chile returned to democracy in 1990. The urgent political cause that created the center has been won, in the narrowest sense. But La Peña's founders understood something that outlasted the dictatorship: communities forged in struggle do not dissolve when the struggle ends. They find new causes, new music, new arrivals who need what the center has always offered - a room where the door is open, the café is warm, and the art on the walls and the music from the stage insist that culture and justice are the same fight.
