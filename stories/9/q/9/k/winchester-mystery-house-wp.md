# Winchester Mystery House

A Victorian mansion built by the widow of the rifle fortune heir, famous for its architectural oddities and the debunked legends of ghost-driven construction.

**Location:** 37.3184, -121.9508

**Categories:** Historic Sites, Architecture, San Jose, California

## Introduction

The staircase climbs forty-four steps but rises only ten feet. The door opens onto a sheer drop where a balcony once stood. The window looks into an interior wall. These peculiarities have fed a century of ghost stories about the Winchester Mystery House, but the truth behind Sarah Winchester's sprawling San Jose mansion has more to do with earthquakes, arthritis, and architectural ambition than supernatural guilt over rifle deaths.

## A Widow Heads West

Sarah Winchester arrived in California in 1885, a 46-year-old widow escaping grief and New England winters. Her infant daughter Annie had died in 1866. Between fall 1880 and spring 1881, she lost her mother, father-in-law, and husband William Wirt Winchester, heir to the rifle company fortune. Her doctor recommended a warmer climate for her worsening rheumatoid arthritis. She remembered traveling to San Francisco with William in the 1870s and persuaded her three remaining sisters to follow her west. In 1886, she purchased a 45-acre ranch near San Jose with an eight-room farmhouse, naming it Llanada Villa after a region in the Spanish Basque country.

## The Architect Was the Owner

Sarah hired architects but dismissed them, preferring to design rooms herself and consult directly with carpenters. This was unusual for a woman in the 1880s, leading historian Colin Dickey to call her an architectural pioneer. She drew inspiration from world's fairs and rebuilt sections that failed to meet her standards. The San Jose News reported in 1897 that a seven-story tower was torn down and rebuilt sixteen times. The result was a maze of Victorian, Gothic, and Romanesque elements, with teak and mahogany floors, Shakespearean quotes etched into fireplace windows, chandeliers from Germany, and art glass from Austria. At its largest, the house contained approximately 500 rooms.

## What the Earthquake Left Behind

The 1906 San Francisco earthquake devastated Llanada Villa. The seven-story tower collapsed along with most chimneys and an entire wing. Sarah had the rubble cleared but did little to rebuild. This is the source of the house's famous oddities: doors that open to nothing, pipes protruding from former window boxes, staircases ending abruptly where upper floors once continued. After 1910, her failing health confined construction to maintenance work and an elevator added in 1916. When she died in 1922 at age 83, the house had 160 rooms, 2,000 doors, 10,000 windows, 47 stairways, 47 fireplaces, 13 bathrooms, and 6 kitchens.

## The Legend Factory

Nine months after Sarah's death, investors leased the house to John and Mayme Brown, who opened it as a tourist attraction. Mayme became the first tour guide, and the ghost stories began. According to biographer Mary Jo Ignoffo, neighbors and former employees were distressed by the falsehoods. They described Sarah as clearheaded and financially shrewd. The famous tale of a Boston medium named Adam Coons commanding her to build endlessly first appeared in a 1967 book. Scholars have searched spiritualist records and found no evidence anyone named Adam Coons existed. Her companion Henrietta Severs stated Sarah had no superstitious beliefs. Her staff never witnessed seances.

## The House That Marketing Built

Harry Houdini visited briefly in 1924 and reportedly suggested the name Winchester Mystery House. When Keith Kittle became general manager in 1973, he renovated the property, added a rifle museum, and launched billboard campaigns implying ghost encounters. Attendance soared. Today tour guides follow scripts that biographer Ignoffo calls fabrications. One guide told her: "I feel so torn because I have to tell people untruths. Every time I go through the house and have to talk about 13s and other kooky things, my heart breaks a little for Sarah." The shallow stairs exist because of her arthritis and four-foot-ten-inch height. The trap doors drained an indoor greenhouse. The bell tower called workers and served as a fire alarm.
