# Psycho Donuts

A Campbell, California donut shop that opened in 2009 with a psychiatric-institution theme became a flashpoint in the debate over mental health stigma -- and ended up changing its own menu in response.

**Location:** 37.2867, -121.9495

**Categories:** food-and-drink, local-business, controversy, mental-health

## Introduction

Two weeks. That is how long Psycho Donuts operated in Campbell, California before the protests began. Jordan Zweigoron and Kipp Berdianski opened the shop in March 2009 with a concept that was hard to ignore: donuts named after mental health conditions, staff in lab coats, a straitjacket on the wall, and a padded cell where customers could sit. The eating area was called 'Group Therapy.' Within a month, the shop had become the most controversial donut store in America -- a distinction that, until then, no one had thought possible.

## Donuts on Medication

The concept was maximalist from the start. Psycho Donuts described itself as a shop that had 'taken the neighborhood donut and put it on medication, and given it shock treatment.' The donuts themselves were creative -- topped with pretzels, Froot Loops, and other unexpected ingredients -- but it was the names that drew attention. A donut called 'Bipolar.' Another called 'Massive Head Trauma.' The decor matched: a psychiatric-institution aesthetic complete with a straitjacket, a padded room, and staff dressed as nurses and doctors. Zweigoron described the atmosphere as a 'fun mental institution.' Whether the fun extended to everyone who walked in was about to become a public question.

## The Pushback

Mental health advocates responded quickly and forcefully. The National Alliance on Mental Illness pointed out that few physical illnesses are mocked and merchandised the way mental illnesses were at Psycho Donuts. Author Pete Earley compared the shop's gimmick to the now-unacceptable practice of making marginalized groups the butt of public jokes. A Campbell city council meeting on April 7, 2009, debated whether the shop should be forced to change its theme. Another protest march followed on August 2. The controversy raised a real question: where does irreverent humor end and stigmatization begin? For advocates who had spent years fighting the casual trivialization of conditions like bipolar disorder and schizophrenia, the answer was not complicated.

## A Change of Menu

By late August 2009, Zweigoron -- now the sole owner after Berdianski's departure -- made concessions. The straitjacket and padded cell came down. 'Bipolar' became 'Mood Swing.' 'Massive Head Trauma' was renamed 'Headbanger.' The shop also announced plans to donate part of its profits to the National Alliance for Research on Schizophrenia and Depression. It was not a full reinvention, but it acknowledged something the protests had made clear: the people most affected by mental illness were not laughing. The core business survived the controversy. In 2013, head chef Ron Levi won first place on Food Network Canada's Donut Showdown, proving the kitchen could generate attention on its own merits.

## Stirring the Pot Again

Psycho Donuts was not finished generating headlines. In June 2013, the shop created the 'Foie Bomb' -- a foie gras donut offered free on National Donut Day. The giveaway was a deliberate provocation: California law forbade the sale of foie gras on animal cruelty grounds, but the shop argued that giving it away was legal. The stunt drew its own wave of outrage, including death threats serious enough to require a police presence at the store. Ownership changed hands in 2014 when Web Granger took over. A second location opened in downtown San Jose and a third in Santa Clara in 2016, though both eventually closed. By 2021, a pop-up location appeared in Valley Fair mall. The original Campbell shop endures -- calmer now, still eccentric, still selling donuts that resist easy categorization.
