# SanDisk

Born from a refugee scientist's breakthrough in semiconductor memory, SanDisk grew from a Milpitas startup into the company that put flash storage in every pocket on the planet.

**Location:** 37.4188, -121.9239

**Categories:** technology, silicon-valley, corporate-history, innovation

## Introduction

In 1988, Eli Harari had a problem only he could solve. The Israeli-American engineer had already proven that data could be stored reliably on silicon chips using the floating gate EEPROM he helped develop -- but nobody had figured out how to make that technology cheap enough to matter. Working out of an office in Santa Clara County with two co-founders, Sanjay Mehrotra and Jack Yuan, Harari launched a company called SunDisk. The name was a portmanteau of Sanjay and disk, and the mission was audacious: replace the spinning platters and magnetic tape that the entire computing world depended on with silent, shockproof chips of silicon. From this corner of the South Bay, a storage revolution would spread to every phone, camera, and laptop on Earth.

## Twenty Megabytes for a Thousand Dollars

The first product told you everything about where the industry stood. In 1991, SunDisk delivered a flash-based solid-state drive to IBM -- twenty megabytes crammed into a 2.5-inch hard drive form factor, priced at roughly a thousand dollars. By any modern standard, the specs were laughable. By the standards of 1991, they were a glimpse of the future. No moving parts. No seek time. No vulnerability to the bumps and drops that killed spinning hard drives in laptops. A year later, the company introduced FlashDisk, a PCMCIA memory card that could slot into the expansion bays of early laptops and handheld PCs. Unlike competitors, FlashDisk needed no battery to retain data -- the bits stayed put when the power went off. SanDisk kept making these cards until 2002, by which point the highest-capacity model held eight gigabytes, a figure that would have seemed like science fiction a decade earlier.

## A Name Change and a Music War

On November 8, 1995, SunDisk became SanDisk. The reason was prosaic: too many people confused them with Sun Microsystems, the server giant down the road. The rebrand coincided with the company's IPO, and SanDisk began its life as a publicly traded entity under the ticker SNDK on the Nasdaq. A decade of quiet growth in memory cards followed -- CompactFlash, SD cards, microSD -- the little rectangles of plastic and silicon that made digital cameras and early smartphones possible. Then in 2005, SanDisk did something unexpected: it launched an MP3 player. The Sansa e100 was a direct challenge to Apple's iPod dominance. By 2006, SanDisk had climbed to second place in the American digital audio player market, behind only Apple. It was a brief, fierce run that proved the company could compete beyond components.

## The Acquisition Machine

Between 2005 and 2014, SanDisk went on a buying spree that reshaped the enterprise storage landscape. Matrix Semiconductor in 2005. M-Systems in 2006. Pliant Technology for $327 million in 2011. SMART Storage Systems for $307 million in 2013. The crown jewel was Fusion-io, acquired in 2014 for $1.1 billion, which brought flash memory technology purpose-built for the massive data centers that companies like Facebook and Amazon were constructing at breakneck speed. In the middle of all this dealmaking, in 2014, co-founder Eli Harari received the National Medal of Technology and Innovation from President Barack Obama -- recognition that the floating gate technology he had championed decades earlier had fundamentally changed how the world stores information.

## Swallowed Whole, Then Reborn

In October 2015, Western Digital announced it would acquire SanDisk for $19 billion. The deal closed in May 2016 at a reduced valuation of $16 billion after Chinese conglomerate Unisplendour withdrew from a related stake purchase. For eight years, the SanDisk brand lived inside Western Digital, its identity gradually blurred with WD's own flash products. Then the pendulum swung back. In October 2023, Western Digital announced it would spin off its entire flash storage business as a standalone company under the SanDisk name. The separation unfolded across 2024, with WD-branded flash products migrating to the SanDisk website and a new pixel-inspired logo replacing the familiar wordmark that had endured in various forms since 1995. On February 24, 2025, Sandisk Corporation returned to the Nasdaq under its original ticker, SNDK -- the same symbol it had carried from its 1995 IPO until the 2016 acquisition. The company had come full circle.

## Silicon Valley's Quiet Giant

SanDisk's headquarters in Milpitas sits in the industrial sprawl between San Jose and the salt flats of the southern Bay. There are no dramatic campus quads or whimsical cafeterias -- just the unassuming offices of a company whose products live inside other things. The USB drives in desk drawers, the microSD cards in phones and dash cams, the SSDs humming inside servers that handle millions of transactions per second -- SanDisk's fingerprints are everywhere, even if the brand itself rarely makes headlines. That quiet ubiquity is perhaps Eli Harari's greatest achievement. He did not just prove that silicon could store data reliably. He and his co-founders built the company that made flash storage so affordable and so small that an entire generation takes it for granted. Every photograph saved to a phone, every game loaded from an SSD, every document carried on a thumb drive traces a lineage back to that first twenty-megabyte drive delivered to IBM in 1991.
