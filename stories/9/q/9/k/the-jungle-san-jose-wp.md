# The Jungle (San Jose)

For fifteen years, one of the largest homeless encampments in the United States existed in the shadow of Silicon Valley's wealth, along Coyote Creek in San Jose -- until authorities demolished it in December 2014.

**Location:** 37.3275, -121.8625

**Categories:** History, Social Issues, Homelessness, Silicon Valley, San Jose

## Introduction

The irony was geographic. In the same stretch of the Santa Clara Valley where Apple, Google, and Facebook were minting billionaires, roughly 300 people lived in makeshift dwellings along Coyote Creek, in a thicket of willows and cottonwoods that residents and city officials alike called the Jungle. It was, by most accounts, one of the largest homeless encampments in the United States. From the air, you would not have seen it. The dense riparian canopy hid the shacks, treehouses, and tents that spread across several acres of floodplain off Story Road, on the east side of San Jose. On the ground, walking through it was something else entirely.

## Coyote Meadow's Hidden City

The encampment took shape gradually, beginning around 1999 in an area known as Coyote Meadow. At first it was a handful of tents pitched in the creek-side brush -- a pattern familiar to any California city where mild weather and undeveloped land meet housing scarcity. Over the years, the settlement grew into something more permanent. Residents built structures from scavenged lumber and tarps, some surprisingly elaborate: multi-room shacks with doors and windows, treehouses perched in the branches above the floodplain. Pathways connected the dwellings. A community, of sorts, formed. One former resident told reporters in 2014 that she had lived there, off and on, for fifteen years. For people priced out of the most expensive housing market in the country, the Jungle offered something the shelters could not: space, privacy, and a place to keep belongings without fear of them being discarded.

## The Valley of Extremes

What made the Jungle a national story was not its size alone but its location. San Jose is the largest city in Silicon Valley, the capital of an industry that was generating unprecedented wealth during the same years the encampment was growing. Business Insider visited in 2013 and published photographs under the headline "Welcome to the Jungle: The Largest Homeless Camp in Mainland USA Is Right in the Heart of Silicon Valley." The New York Times, The Guardian, and KQED all covered the camp as a symbol of the widening gap between the tech economy's winners and the people it left behind. Housing costs in San Jose had climbed relentlessly through the 2000s and early 2010s, driven by demand from well-paid tech workers. For those earning minimum wage -- or nothing at all -- the math was impossible. Affordable units were scarce, waitlists for subsidized housing stretched for years, and shelter beds filled up nightly.

## Dismantling Day

On December 4, 2014, San Jose city workers and police officers arrived at the Jungle and began evicting its residents. The city cited unsanitary conditions and a rise in violent incidents as justification, along with environmental damage to the creek corridor. Over the following days, crews demolished the structures, loaded the debris into garbage trucks, and fenced the site off. Some residents accepted offers of transitional housing or shelter beds. Many others scattered to smaller camps along other creeks and underpasses, beginning what one KQED reporter described as a "game of whack-a-mole" -- people displaced from one encampment reappearing at another. The city had removed the Jungle. It had not removed the conditions that created it.

## What the Creek Took Back

Within months of the demolition, Coyote Meadow began reverting to wildland. By July 2015, the Mercury News reported that vegetation had reclaimed the site, covering the scars left by bulldozers and garbage trucks. The willows filled back in. The creek ran cleaner. From above, the parcel once again looked like any other stretch of riparian corridor along the South Bay's urban waterways -- unremarkable, anonymous. But the people who had lived there did not vanish as easily as their shelters. A 2016 KQED follow-up found that homelessness in San Jose had grown more complex since the closure, with former Jungle residents dispersed across dozens of smaller, harder-to-reach encampments. The underlying crisis -- a housing shortage in a region where median rents outpaced median incomes -- remained.

## A Landscape of Contradiction

The Jungle no longer exists as a place. The land along Coyote Creek is fenced, revegetated, quiet. But the story it told -- of a wealthy society unable or unwilling to house hundreds of its own people -- did not end with the demolition. San Jose's homeless population continued to grow in the years after the closure, and encampments remained a visible feature of the city's creeks, parks, and freeway margins. The Jungle became a reference point, invoked by journalists, advocates, and policymakers whenever the conversation turned to what Silicon Valley owed the people living in its shadow. It was never just a camp. It was a mirror, held up at an uncomfortable angle, reflecting the distance between the valley's aspirations and its reality.
