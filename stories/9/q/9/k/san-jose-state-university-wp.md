# San Jose State University

The oldest public university in the western United States, born as a modest evening teaching school in 1857 San Francisco and now a sprawling 154-acre campus in downtown San Jose that produced Olympic sprinters, civil rights icons, and Silicon Valley's workforce.

## Introduction

Tommie Smith and John Carlos raised their black-gloved fists on the medal podium in Mexico City in 1968, and the image became one of the most recognized photographs of the twentieth century. Both men were students at San Jose State. That connection is not incidental. The university has been quietly shaping history since 1857, when it opened as the Minns Evening Normal School in San Francisco with classes held once a week and a graduating class composed entirely of women. It was California's first public institution of higher education, and it remains the founding campus of the California State University system. Today San Jose State sprawls across 154 acres of downtown San Jose, its Tower Hall clock visible for blocks in every direction, training the engineers, nurses, and business graduates who keep Silicon Valley running.

## Six Moves and a Fire

The school's early decades read like a real estate saga. Founded in San Francisco in 1857, the California State Normal School moved locations six times within the city, driven out by noise complaints, sanitary concerns, and a running feud with the San Francisco Board of Education, which poached its students and denied it proper facilities. When word spread that the school was looking for a permanent home, several cities bid for it. San Jose won, largely because the San Jose Railroad Company paid to transport the entire school south. The campus took root in 1871. Wooden buildings rose around a central quadrangle, and for nine years the arrangement held. Then in 1880, fire destroyed the campus. The replacement buildings, constructed in stone and masonry, survived the 1906 earthquake so well that demolition crews were tearing them down as a precaution when an aftershock struck and caused no damage. Officials called off the demolition. The surviving structures became Tower Hall, Morris Dailey Auditorium, and Washington Square Hall, landmarks that still anchor the campus.

## Speed City

In the 1960s, track coach Lloyd "Bud" Winter turned San Jose State into a sprinting powerhouse so dominant that the campus earned the nickname Speed City. Winter's program produced Tommie Smith, John Carlos, and Lee Evans, athletes who were not only fast but fiercely principled. Smith and Carlos, after finishing first and third in the 200 meters at the 1968 Mexico City Olympics, stood on the podium with raised fists and bowed heads during the national anthem. The gesture cost them dearly; both were expelled from the Olympic Village. But the image endured, becoming a symbol of athletic protest that resonates to this day. In 2005, a statue commemorating the salute was erected on the campus's Tower Lawn, designed by artist Rigo 23 and titled "Victory Salute." A blank space on the second-place podium invites passersby to step up and recreate the moment. Altogether, San Jose State alumni have won twenty Olympic medals, including seven gold, in disciplines ranging from track and field to judo, swimming, and water polo.

## Silicon Valley's Training Ground

San Jose State occupies a peculiar position in higher education. It is not Stanford, and it is not Berkeley, yet it may be more consequential to the daily functioning of Silicon Valley than either. The university enrolls roughly 37,000 students across nine colleges, offering more than 250 degree programs. Its engineering school, ranked among the top twelve nationally for programs without doctoral offerings, feeds graduates directly into the technology companies that surround the campus. Adobe, Cisco, eBay, and dozens of other firms recruit heavily from its programs. The Lucas College and Graduate School of Business, the second-largest college on campus, holds AACSB accreditation at both the graduate and undergraduate levels. As a federally designated Hispanic-Serving Institution and Asian American and Native American Pacific Islander-Serving Institution, San Jose State reflects the demographic reality of the region it serves, educating a student body as diverse as the valley itself.

## The Library That Bridges Two Worlds

Perhaps nothing captures San Jose State's identity quite like its main library. The Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. Library, a joint facility shared between the university and the San Jose Public Library system, houses over 1.6 million volumes and serves both students and the general public. It is one of the few libraries in the country where a college freshman and a retired neighbor can check out books from the same circulation desk. This blurring of boundaries between campus and city defines San Jose State. The university sits in the middle of downtown, not walled off from it. Students walk to internships at tech companies a few blocks away. The Spartan Daily, the student newspaper founded in 1934, covers city politics alongside campus events. KSJS, the student-run radio station broadcasting at 90.5 FM since 1963, reaches well beyond the campus perimeter. Town and gown are not separate here. They never really were.

## Flight Context

San Jose State University is located at 37.34°N, 121.88°W in downtown San Jose. The 154-acre main campus is bordered by San Fernando Street to the north, San Salvador Street to the south, and is identifiable by Tower Hall's clock tower and the distinctive Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. Library building. Norman Y. Mineta San Jose International Airport (KSJC) is approximately 2 miles northwest. A separate south campus with athletics facilities sits about 1.5 miles south along South 7th Street. The three Adobe towers and San Jose City Hall are nearby visual references.
