# SETI Institute

Headquartered in Mountain View, the SETI Institute has spent four decades listening for signals from extraterrestrial civilizations -- a search funded largely not by government but by private philanthropists.

**Location:** 37.3879, -122.0508

**Categories:** science, space, research, mountain-view

## Introduction

The question is simple enough: are we alone? The SETI Institute, incorporated in 1984 and headquartered in Mountain View, California, has dedicated four decades to finding the answer. SETI -- the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence -- uses radio and optical telescopes to scan the sky for deliberate signals from technologically advanced civilizations. The institute employs over 100 researchers and operates three centers: the Carl Sagan Center for the study of life in the universe, the Center for Education, and the Center for Public Outreach. Its financial supporters have included Carl Sagan, Frank Drake, David Packard, William Hewlett, Gordon Moore, and Paul Allen -- a roster that reads like a who's who of Bay Area science and technology.

## Listening in the Valley

Jill Tarter and Thomas Pierson co-founded the SETI Institute as a 501(c)(3) nonprofit, and Tarter became its most recognized figure -- the scientist who inspired Jodie Foster's character in the film Contact. The institute's SETI searches depend almost entirely on private philanthropy -- federal money for them has been limited to occasional research grants -- and in 2023 the estate of engineer Franklin Antonio gave the institute $200 million. NASA funds the institute's other astrobiology research, which covers everything from exoplanet detection to Mars rover science to meteor tracking. Two Nobel laureates have been associated with the institute: Charles Townes, who helped invent the laser, and Baruch Blumberg, who developed the Hepatitis B vaccine.

## Instruments and Ambitions

The institute's researchers use an impressive array of instruments, including the Allen Telescope Array, ground-based optical telescopes at Lick Observatory and the Keck telescopes in Hawaii, and space-based observatories including Hubble, Spitzer, Kepler, and TESS. SETI scientists contribute to major space missions including New Horizons, the Cassini mission to Saturn, and the Mars rovers Opportunity and Curiosity. The breadth of the institute's work extends well beyond listening for alien radio signals -- it encompasses planetary exploration, astrobiology, climate science, and computational astrophysics.

## The Big Question from a Small Office

In February 2015, scientists including Seth Shostak and David Brin debated at an American Association for the Advancement of Science meeting whether humanity should actively transmit messages into space -- a question that sounds like science fiction but carries real implications. A statement signed by many in the SETI community cautioned that a worldwide discussion must occur before any message is sent. That this debate took place in Silicon Valley -- at the meeting in San Jose, then at a follow-up workshop at the institute in Mountain View -- surrounded by companies building algorithms to predict what you want for dinner, captures something about the institute's position: asking the biggest question in science from the heart of a region obsessed with optimizing the smallest ones.

## Flight Context
