# Sespe Creek

Sespe Creek — the Chumash called it S'eqp'e, or 'Kneecap' — is one of Southern California's last undammed wild rivers, flowing 61 miles through a wilderness sanctuary that shelters California condors, steelhead trout, and the memory of vanished beavers.

**Location:** 34.3800, -118.9550

**Categories:** Rivers, Conservation, Wildlife, Chumash, California

## Introduction

The Chumash name for this creek translates as 'Kneecap' — S'eqp'e — a name recorded for a Chumash village here in 1791. Sespe Creek runs 61 miles through Ventura County without a single dam. In Southern California, that is almost unheard of. The creek rises above 5,000 feet in the Sierra Madre Mountains and descends through canyon walls and rugged slopes before contributing 40 percent of the water in the Santa Clara River.

## A Free-Flowing Stream

A dam was once proposed for the Sespe, at a site called Topa Topa near Sespe Hot Springs. It was never built. The creek remains free from major habitat modifications. It flows intermittently through much of its length but maintains a series of permanent deep pools. More than 30 creeks and springs feed into it. This combination of wild character and consistent water supports species that have disappeared from most of Southern California's waterways. The Sespe is one of the region's last free-flowing streams capable of supporting southern steelhead trout, a fish that once ran through rivers across the Southern California coast.

## A Sanctuary for the Sky

The Sespe Creek watershed contains the 53,000-acre Sespe Condor Sanctuary, established in 1947 — one of the earliest protected areas created specifically to shelter the California condor, which was then in severe decline. The sanctuary preceded the wilderness area that now surrounds it: the Sespe Wilderness, established in 1992, covers approximately 219,700 acres, and the same 1992 law added 31.5 miles of Sespe Creek to the National Wild and Scenic Rivers System — 27.5 miles classed wild and 4 miles scenic. The condor population that the sanctuary helped protect is now slowly recovering, and birds are sometimes visible from the creek canyon, riding thermals above the ridgelines.

## The Beaver Evidence

A male California golden beaver — Castor canadensis subauratus — was collected from 'along the Sespe River in Ventura County' in May 1906, just before California instituted statewide beaver protection. The skull is housed at the Museum of Vertebrate Zoology in Berkeley. Oral history collected from Ventureño Chumash elders by ethnolinguist John Peabody Harrington in the early twentieth century corroborates this: 'There were beavers at Ventura and also at Saticoy.' A Chumash pictograph of a beaver exists at Painted Rock in the Cuyama River watershed, about 35 miles from the Sespe headwaters. The Hearst Museum in Berkeley holds a Ventureño Chumash shaman's rain-making kit made from a beaver tail skin, produced in the 1870s.

## What Lives Here

Where Sespe Creek meets the Santa Clara River, four federally listed species find critical habitat: the southwestern willow flycatcher, the least Bell's vireo, the arroyo toad, and the California red-legged frog. The Sespe population is the largest known arroyo toad habitat within the species' current range. This confluence is a biological crossroads — a corridor connecting upland wilderness to coastal lowlands that most species in this region have lost access to. The creek is also designated a wild trout stream from the Lion Camp area down to the Los Padres National Forest boundary north of Fillmore. People still come here to fish. They also come to raft, to hike, and to simply be in a place where Southern California looks as it might have looked before so much of it was changed.

## Flight Context

Sespe Creek runs at approximately 34.38°N, 118.955°W through the Santa Clara River Valley and Los Padres National Forest. The creek and its canyon are visible from altitude as a distinct drainage cutting through rugged terrain north of the valley floor. Nearest airports: Santa Paula Airport (SZP) about 6 miles west-southwest and Camarillo Airport (CMA) about 14 miles southwest. Terrain in the wilderness area is mountainous — maintain altitude and watch for mountain wave turbulence.
