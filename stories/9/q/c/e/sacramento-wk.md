# Sacramento: Where the Gold Rush Ended and California Began

California's capital where the Gold Rush created a city overnight, where the transcontinental railroad began its westward push, where the Sacramento and American rivers converge, and where Old Sacramento preserves the Wild West waterfront that supplied the Sierra mining camps.

**Location:** 38.5816, -121.4944

## Introduction

In January 1848, James Marshall discovered gold at Sutter's Mill on the American River, forty miles east of what would become Sacramento. By 1849, the Gold Rush had transformed John Sutter's agricultural colony into a boomtown, a jumping-off point where argonauts disembarked from San Francisco riverboats to outfit themselves for the Sierra diggings. Sacramento became a city of merchants who realized the real money was in selling supplies to miners - picks and pans and dreams at inflated prices. The Central Pacific Railroad chose Sacramento as its western terminus in 1863; the golden spike at Promontory Summit in 1869 connected California to the nation. The state capital moved here in 1854, and Sacramento settled into its role as government town - less glamorous than San Francisco, less sprawling than Los Angeles, but the place where California's decisions are made in a domed Capitol surrounded by a park full of trees from every climate zone on Earth.

## Old Sacramento

The wooden boardwalks and false-front buildings of Old Sacramento preserve the waterfront that served Gold Rush commerce. The district stretches along the Sacramento River where steamboats once unloaded passengers and freight bound for the mines. The California State Railroad Museum anchors the district, housing 21 restored locomotives including the massive cab-forward steam engines that pulled Southern Pacific trains over Donner Pass. The Sacramento History Museum occupies the reconstructed 1854 city hall. Horse-drawn carriages roll past saloons and candy shops; the Delta King, a 1927 paddlewheel riverboat, is permanently moored as a hotel. Old Sacramento can feel touristy - it is touristy - but it preserves the scale and feeling of a 19th-century river town, the place where California's modern history began.

## Capitol Park

The California State Capitol sits within a 40-acre park that serves as an arboretum, planted with trees from around the world - coast redwoods and giant sequoias, cedars from Lebanon and deodars from the Himalayas, cork oaks and magnolias. The Capitol itself, completed in 1874 and renovated extensively since, houses the legislature and governor's office beneath a dome modeled on the U.S. Capitol. The Capitol Museum preserves historic offices, including reconstructed chambers showing the building's 19th-century grandeur. Memorials dot the park: Vietnam, California peace officers, firefighters. Squirrels are everywhere - allegedly descendants of ones released in the 1890s from the estate of Governor Stanford, founder of the university that bears his son's name.

## River City

Sacramento exists because of water - the Sacramento and American rivers converge here, providing transportation before railroads and irrigation after. The American River Parkway preserves 23 miles of riparian habitat for hiking and cycling; rafters float the lower American through summer heat, emerging at Discovery Park where the rivers meet. The Sacramento River runs through downtown, its levees lined with trails; the Tower Bridge, an Art Deco lift bridge from 1935, has become the city's symbol. The Delta - a thousand miles of waterways spreading west toward San Francisco Bay - begins at Sacramento's doorstep. The city flooded catastrophically multiple times in its early decades; the solution was to raise the downtown streets a full story, burying the original ground floors (some visible on tours) beneath elevated sidewalks.

## Midtown and Beyond

Midtown Sacramento, the grid of lettered and numbered streets between downtown and East Sacramento, has become the city's dining and nightlife district. Victorian houses converted to restaurants, breweries in former warehouses, the art deco Tower Theatre showing independent films. Sutter's Fort, the adobe trading post that preceded the city, still stands amid the urban grid - a reconstruction of the 1840s compound where John Sutter's agricultural empire collapsed when gold was discovered on his property and squatters overran his land. Land Park, south of downtown, holds the Sacramento Zoo and the Funderland amusement park. The city sprawls into suburbs - Elk Grove, Folsom, Roseville - but the central core maintains walkable urban character that surprises visitors expecting only government buildings.

## Valley Position

Sacramento International Airport (SMF) offers connections to major hubs, a convenient alternative to the Bay Area's crowded airports. I-80 runs east toward Reno and the Sierra passes; I-5 runs the length of California north and south; Highway 50 follows the Gold Rush route to Lake Tahoe. Sacramento anchors the Central Valley, California's agricultural heartland stretching 400 miles to Bakersfield. From altitude, Sacramento appears at the confluence of rivers, the Capitol dome visible in its park, the grid of streets spreading from the rivers toward the foothills. What appears from the air as California's somewhat overlooked capital is where the Gold Rush created a city, where the transcontinental railroad began, and where California's government still makes decisions that shape the nation's most populous state.
