# First Synagogue West of the Mississippi

Born from Gold Rush-era High Holy Day gatherings in 1849, Congregation B'nai Israel became the first synagogue west of the Mississippi -- and survived floods, schisms, arson, and a century and a half of transformation.

**Location:** 38.5470, -121.5082

**Categories:** Religious sites, Jewish history, California Gold Rush, Sacramento

## Introduction

In the autumn of 1849, while other arrivals in Sacramento were panning streambeds and staking claims, a group of Jewish settlers gathered to observe the High Holy Days. There was no synagogue, no permanent building, no rabbi -- just a community of faith assembling in a boomtown that barely qualified as a town. Three years later, on September 2, 1852, the congregation purchased a building at 7th and L Streets, making Congregation B'nai Israel the first synagogue west of the Mississippi River. The Gold Rush brought prospectors looking for fortune in the Sierra foothills. It also brought people looking for something that no amount of gold could replace: a place to pray, to mourn, to celebrate, and to belong.

## From Orthodoxy to Reform

B'nai Israel began as an Orthodox congregation, its rituals and liturgy rooted in the traditions the founders had carried from the East Coast and from Europe. But Sacramento in the mid-nineteenth century was a place of constant reinvention, and the congregation reflected the city around it. In 1879, B'nai Israel turned from Orthodoxy to Reform Judaism, a shift that would reshape its identity for the next century and a half. The congregation formalized the transition in 1885 by affiliating with the Union of American Hebrew Congregations. Not everyone followed. The more traditionally observant members departed to form Sacramento's Mosaic Law Synagogue, carrying the Orthodox practice forward in a separate institution. It was the first of several splits that would mark B'nai Israel's history -- each one a reminder that a congregation old enough to have deep roots will inevitably produce strong disagreements.

## Floods, Splits, and Reunions

Between 1858 and 1861, a splinter group called B'nai Ha'Shalom broke away over differences of opinion that the historical record does not fully specify. The separation might have lasted indefinitely, but Sacramento's geography intervened. Floods in 1861 damaged the Bet Shalom cemetery badly enough that the breakaway group could not maintain it alone. The two congregations reunited, their theological disagreements rendered secondary by the practical realities of rising water. More than a century later, in 1970, another departure occurred when members left to form Temple Beth Shalom, this time in protest over the firing of the congregation's cantor. B'nai Israel's history reads like a pattern: unity, friction, separation, and -- sometimes -- return. The congregation endured because it was large enough and old enough to absorb these departures without losing its center.

## A Night of Fire

On June 18, 1999, arsonists set fire to three Sacramento-area synagogues in what appeared to be a coordinated attack. B'nai Israel sustained more than $800,000 in damage. The sanctuary was badly burned. The library was destroyed entirely -- shelves of books, documents, and congregational records consumed in a few hours of flame. The attack could have broken the community. Instead, that same evening, members gathered at the Sacramento community center for Shabbat services. The sanctuary was gone, but the congregation was not. Days later, a rally drew more than five thousand people from the broader Sacramento community, a show of solidarity that crossed every religious and ethnic line in the city. The arsonists were later connected to the murders of Gary Matson and Winfield Mowder, a gay couple killed in Redding, California -- crimes driven by the same hatred that had targeted the synagogues.

## The Weight of Being First

A plaque near the old site at 7th and Capitol marks B'nai Israel's founding location, a reminder of the congregation's claim as Sacramento's oldest Jewish community. The congregation helped establish the Home of Peace Cemetery in 1924, succeeding an earlier burial society called Chevra Kaddisha. Today, B'nai Israel worships at 3600 Riverside Boulevard, a location that reflects the congregation's migration through the city over its long history. Being the oldest carries obligations beyond memory. It means absorbing every wave of change -- demographic, theological, cultural -- while maintaining enough continuity that the community recognizes itself across the generations. B'nai Israel has done this for more than 170 years, from the Gold Rush tents to a modern Reform synagogue on the banks of the Sacramento River.
