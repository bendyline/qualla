# A Wooden Stick in Rohnert Park

The 1997 fatal shooting of Kuanchung Kao by a Rohnert Park police officer over a wooden stick became a rallying point for Asian American civil rights and exposed troubling patterns in Sonoma County policing.

**Location:** 38.3302, -122.6797

**Categories:** civil-rights, california-history, law-enforcement, asian-american-history

## Introduction

Kuanchung Kao had been celebrating. On the night of April 28, 1997, he went out to the Cotati Yacht Club with friends to mark a new job. At the bar, another patron mistook him for Japanese. When Kao corrected him -- he was Chinese, born in Taiwan -- the man replied, "You all look alike to me." The bartender separated them. Hours later, Kao was dead in the street outside his own home in Rohnert Park, California, shot by a police officer who had been on the scene for less than a minute. He was holding a wooden stick the size of a closet rod. He left behind a wife, Ayling Wu, a registered nurse, and their three young children.

## Ninety Seconds on a Suburban Street

What happened between 2:11 a.m. and 2:16 a.m. on April 29 unfolded with the terrible compression of most police shootings. Neighbors called 911 to report Kao waving a six-foot wooden stick in the street outside his driveway -- a drunken disturbance, nothing more. Officer Mike Lynch arrived first and pulled his patrol car close to Kao in what witnesses described as an attempt to intimidate him. Kao struck the vehicle with the stick. Officer Jack Shields, a 25-year veteran of the Rohnert Park Department of Public Safety, arrived moments later. Within seconds, Shields fired his weapon. Kao died in front of the home he shared with his family. The weapon that prompted his killing was a piece of wood comparable to a closet rod, retrieved from a motorhome parked in his own driveway.

## Three Investigations, One Answer

The Sonoma County Sheriff's Department compiled a 600-page report and delivered it to the District Attorney's office in May 1997. On June 19, District Attorney Michael Mullins declined to file charges, ruling that Shields had acted in self-defense. In August, Rohnert Park's own Department of Public Safety cleared Shields, finding he had followed departmental policies for the use of deadly force. The U.S. Attorney's office in San Francisco opened a federal investigation at the same time, and Senator Barbara Boxer met with Ayling Wu and community advocate Nancy Wang in October to hear their concerns. But in January 1998, U.S. Attorney Michael Yamaguchi declined to prosecute, citing insufficient evidence that Kao's civil rights had been violated. Three separate investigations examined the same shooting. All three reached the same conclusion: the system had worked as designed.

## The Hundred-Day Vigil

The Asian American community refused to accept that conclusion. Within weeks of the shooting, civil rights attorney John Burris and the Asian Law Caucus filed a wrongful death claim on behalf of Wu and her children, followed in February 1998 by a federal lawsuit against Rohnert Park, its police department, and the two officers involved. The case was part of a broader pattern that activists had been tracking for years -- a string of fatal police shootings in Sonoma County that drew growing scrutiny. On the 100th day after Kao's death, mourners held a candlelight vigil in San Francisco demanding a new investigation. That activist pressure, combined with the county's troubling record, prompted the United States Commission on Civil Rights to hold a public hearing in Sonoma County on February 20, 1998. Police officers and their supporters packed the room, forcing community members with opposing viewpoints to stand outside. The civil suit was eventually settled in 2001 for a million dollars, with no admission of liability.

## What Remained

The settlement, meant to provide for a widow and three children, was largely consumed by legal fees and moving expenses. Ayling Wu relocated her family to Orange County by 1998, far from the house where her husband had been killed. All of Kao and Wu's extended family lived in Taiwan, and contact gradually faded after his death. Alone with three young children and no support network, Wu spiraled into severe depression and suicidal thoughts. She eventually stabilized and built a career as a Director of Nursing in medical rehabilitation facilities. Her children grew up. One of the twins became a physical therapist; the other became a wildland firefighter specializing as a smokejumper. Officer Jack Shields, who had previously been demoted from fire commander to patrol officer after falsifying employee time cards, retired with a CalPERS pension. Officer Mike Lynch left the department within a year and was last reported working in a local pet shop.
