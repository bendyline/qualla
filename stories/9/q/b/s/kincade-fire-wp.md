# The Kincade Fire: When Wine Country Burned

A failed transmission line ignited the largest wildfire of the 2019 California season in Sonoma County's wine country, burning nearly 78,000 acres and forcing the largest evacuation in the county's history.

**Location:** 38.7925, -122.7801

**Categories:** California wildfires, Sonoma County California, Natural disasters, Infrastructure

## Introduction

The power was about to be shut off. That is the bitter irony at the heart of the Kincade Fire: PG&E was preparing to de-energize its lines in the Geysers area on the evening of October 23, 2019, a preemptive measure against the extreme winds that forecasters had been warning about for days. But at 9:24 p.m., minutes before the shutoff would have reached the area, a 230,000-volt transmission line failed near John Kincade Road and Burned Mountain Road. Sparks met tinder-dry brush. Within hours, fire was racing southwest through Sonoma County's wine country, driven by gusts that overwhelmed every containment effort. By the time firefighters fully contained the blaze on November 6, it had consumed 77,758 acres, threatened more than 90,000 structures, and triggered the largest evacuation in Sonoma County history.

## A County Already Scarred

Sonoma County understood wildfire. Just two years earlier, the Tubbs Fire had torn through Santa Rosa, killing 22 people and destroying thousands of homes. The trauma of October 2017 remained fresh when the Kincade Fire ignited in almost the same region. Residents had practiced evacuation routes. They had go-bags packed. Many had already experienced the surreal routine of loading pets and photo albums into cars at midnight, headlights cutting through smoke. When Sonoma County officials issued evacuation orders for Geyserville on October 24, people moved quickly -- but the scale of what followed dwarfed anything the community had rehearsed. By October 27, evacuation orders and warnings covered the majority of Sonoma County and parts of Lake County, including Geyserville, Healdsburg, Windsor, and sections of Santa Rosa. Nearly 190,000 people were told to leave their homes.

## The Utility in the Crosshairs

PG&E had already filed for bankruptcy in January 2019, crushed by billions in liabilities from the 2017 and 2018 fire seasons. The Kincade Fire landed like a verdict on the utility's safety record. Cal Fire's investigation confirmed what PG&E itself had admitted within days of the blaze: the company's equipment started the fire. A compulsory report documented the transmission line failure near the point of origin. Editorials in the San Jose Mercury News and San Francisco Chronicle called for regulatory action. The timing compounded the outrage -- PG&E had simultaneously been conducting its largest-ever Public Safety Power Shutoff, cutting electricity to an estimated three million customers across Northern California to prevent exactly the kind of fire its own equipment had just caused. Lawsuits followed the bankruptcy exit. The contradiction was stark: the utility responsible for preventing fires was also starting them.

## Fourteen Days of Fire

The Kincade Fire burned for two weeks. In its first days, driven by what the National Weather Service called a "historic wind event," the fire grew explosively, jumping containment lines and forcing firefighters into a defensive posture. By October 28, nearly 5,000 firefighters were deployed. Containment crept upward: 15 percent by October 29, 60 percent by October 31, 72 percent by November 3. The fire destroyed 374 structures, including 174 homes. Four civilians and six firefighters were injured. Remarkably, no one died -- a testament to early evacuations and a county that had learned, at terrible cost, to take wildfire warnings seriously. Full containment came on November 6, 2019, after the fire had burned an area larger than the city of San Francisco. It was the largest wildfire in Sonoma County's recorded history at that time, a record that would stand for less than a year before the LNU Lightning Complex fires surpassed it in 2020.

## The Darkness Beyond the Flames

Beyond the fire itself, the blackouts reshaped daily life across Northern California. PG&E's Public Safety Power Shutoffs left millions without electricity for days. Hospitals ran on generators. Traffic signals went dark. Businesses lost inventory. The elderly and medically vulnerable faced dangerous conditions. SMART train service in Sonoma and Marin counties was suspended. Schools closed. The shutoffs exposed a paradox of modern California: a state that leads the world in renewable energy technology could not keep its lights on during fire season. Public anger at PG&E reached levels that editorial boards described as unprecedented. The Mercury News called for the utility to be restructured; the Chronicle urged the California Public Utilities Commission to act. For residents who had already endured fire, the darkness felt like a second disaster layered on top of the first.

## Landscape of Recovery

The land the Kincade Fire burned stretches across some of Northern California's most recognizable terrain -- the rolling hills and vineyard-covered slopes northeast of Healdsburg, the rugged canyons of The Geysers geothermal field, the oak woodlands above the Alexander Valley. From the air, the fire's footprint is still legible in the patchwork of regrowth and bare ridgelines. Sonoma County has continued to build its wildfire resilience, investing in evacuation infrastructure, vegetation management, and early-warning systems. But the Kincade Fire remains a case study in the entanglement of infrastructure, climate, and risk. The transmission line that failed was built decades ago, for a climate that no longer exists. The winds that drove the fire were consistent with patterns that scientists say are intensifying. For a county that grows some of America's finest wine, fire has become as much a part of the terroir as fog and volcanic soil.
