# Yosemite National Park

Nearly 1,200 square miles of granite, glaciers, and giant sequoias in the Sierra Nevada, where Abraham Lincoln signed the first land preservation act in history and where the very idea of a national park was born.

**Location:** 37.7425, -119.5375

**Categories:** national-park, geology, indigenous-history, preservation, world-heritage-site

## Introduction

On June 30, 1864, in the middle of the Civil War, Abraham Lincoln put his signature on a piece of legislation that had nothing to do with the conflict consuming his presidency. The Yosemite Grant set aside a valley and a grove of giant trees in the Sierra Nevada "for public use, resort and recreation," to be "inalienable for all time." It was the first time any government on Earth had preserved scenic land for the public. Lincoln never visited Yosemite. He signed the bill because people who had seen the place - Galen Clark, Senator John Conness, landscape architect Frederick Law Olmsted - could not stop talking about it. That urgency has never faded. Today Yosemite National Park covers nearly 1,200 square miles, receives over four million visitors annually, and remains the place where America's relationship with wild land was fundamentally redefined.

## Dwellers in Ahwahnee

The Ahwahneechee, whose name means "dwellers" in their homeland of Ahwahnee, were the only tribe to live within what are now the park's boundaries. They were part of the larger Southern Sierra Miwok cultural group, with kinship and trade ties to the Northern Paiute and Mono tribes east of the Sierra crest. Central Sierra Miwoks and Yokuts from the San Joaquin Valley visited to trade and intermarry, creating a cultural blend that helped sustain indigenous presence even as European American settlement intensified. Before the Gold Rush, California's indigenous population was estimated at 300,000. Within a decade of the rush, it had plummeted to roughly 50,000. In December 1850, California funded a state militia to suppress indigenous resistance, and the Mariposa Battalion entered the valley in 1851 as a punitive force. Dr. Lafayette Bunnell, attached to the unit, was so awestruck by the landscape that he wrote an entire book about it. He named the valley "Yosemite" based on interviews with Chief Tenaya - mistakenly believing the Miwok word meant "grizzly bear" when it actually meant "those who kill." The last full-blooded Ahwahneechee, Totuya, granddaughter of Chief Tenaya, died in 1931.

## The Grant That Changed Everything

What Lincoln signed in 1864 was not yet a national park - that concept would not exist for another eight years. The Yosemite Grant ceded the valley and the Mariposa Grove to California as a state park, with a board of commissioners and Galen Clark as its first guardian. But Clark lacked the authority to evict homesteaders, including the promoter James Hutchings, and an eleven-year legal struggle followed that twice reached the Supreme Court. Meanwhile, John Muir arrived. The Scottish-born naturalist became the valley's most effective advocate, writing articles that popularized the science of glacial geology and challenged the established theory that Yosemite was formed by a catastrophic collapse. Muir's lobbying, particularly his alliance with Century Magazine editor Robert Underwood Johnson, led Congress to create Yosemite National Park on October 1, 1890 - a much larger territory surrounding the valley. California did not cede the valley and grove to the federal government until 1906. The U.S. Army administered the park until 1914. Two years later, the newly formed National Park Service took over.

## A Geography of Extremes

The park contains thousands of lakes and ponds, 1,600 miles of streams, 800 miles of hiking trails, and 350 miles of roads. Two Wild and Scenic Rivers - the Merced and the Tuolumne - originate within its borders. Almost all of its landforms are cut from the granitic rock of the Sierra Nevada Batholith, shaped by erosion acting on joint and fracture systems rather than faults. Mount Lyell, the park's highest point at 13,120 feet, once anchored the largest glacier in Yosemite. That glacier and the nearby Maclure Glacier are the park's last two, and both are expected to disappear due to climate change. Three groves of ancient giant sequoias survive: the Mariposa Grove with 200 trees, the Tuolumne Grove with 25, and the Merced Grove with 20. The Wawona Tree, a sequoia 234 feet tall and 90 feet in circumference, became one of America's most photographed attractions after a tunnel was carved through its trunk in 1881. The tunnel permanently weakened the tree. It fell under a heavy load of snow in 1969, estimated to have been 2,100 years old.

## Firefall to Wilderness

For decades, Yosemite was managed more as an amusement park than a wilderness. The Curry Company staged the Firefall - burning embers pushed off Glacier Point at night to cascade like liquid flame - as a nightly spectacle that drew enormous crowds. Automobiles flooded in after all-weather highways opened, and by 1968 summer traffic congestion had become a genuine management crisis. The Park Service began dismantling artificial attractions, eliminating the Firefall and shifting focus toward preservation. In 1984, Congress designated 677,600 acres - about 89 percent of the park - as the Yosemite Wilderness, restricting human use to temporary visitation. Clare Marie Hodges, hired in 1918, had been the park's first female ranger. Enid Michael followed in 1921. Their legacy persisted as the park evolved from spectacle toward stewardship. In 2016, the Trust for Public Land purchased 400-acre Ackerson Meadow on the park's western edge, the largest addition to Yosemite since 1949, and the Park Service began restoring it from decades of cattle ranching damage.

## Four Million Reasons

Yosemite received over four million visitors in 2024, most of them concentrated in the seven square miles of Yosemite Valley. The tension between access and preservation - the same tension Lincoln's 1864 act tried to resolve - remains unresolved. Reservation systems have been implemented, lifted, and reimplemented since 2020. The park's Mediterranean climate means most precipitation falls as winter snow, leaving summers hot, dry, and vulnerable to wildfire. Lightning-caused fires are a regular occurrence above 8,000 feet. The Southern Sierra Miwok, whose ancestors shaped this landscape through millennia of deliberate burning, have seen some recognition of their connection to the land: the National Park Service created policies to protect sacred sites and allow Native peoples to return to their homelands and use park resources. In 2025, laid-off Park Service workers draped a giant upside-down American flag on El Capitan as a distress signal. The gesture captured something essential about Yosemite: it has always been a place where Americans argue about what matters most.

## Flight Context

Located at 37.7425°N, 119.5375°W in the central Sierra Nevada. The park covers nearly 1,200 square miles. Yosemite Valley is the primary visual landmark, running east-west with El Capitan and Half Dome as unmistakable features. Tuolumne Meadows lies at 8,600 ft in the high country to the northeast. The Mariposa Grove of giant sequoias is near the park's southern entrance. Nearest airports: Fresno Yosemite International (KFAT), 65 miles south; Columbia Airport (O22), 40 miles northwest. The Sierra Crest and Pacific Crest Trail traverse the park's eastern boundary. Best viewed at 8,000-12,000 ft AGL for a full overview of the park's 1,200 square mile expanse.
