# Shell Building

A 28-story Art Deco tower adorned with carved seashell motifs, the Shell Building has anchored the corner of Bush and Battery Streets in San Francisco's Financial District since 1929.

**Location:** 37.7915, -122.4000

**Categories:** Skyscraper office buildings in San Francisco, Art Deco architecture in California, Financial District, San Francisco

## Introduction

Look closely at the upper stories of 100 Bush Street and you will notice something peculiar for an oil company headquarters: seashells. Carved shell motifs decorate the cornice, shell patterns are set into the lobby floor, and a decorative shell grill graces the entrance. The Shell Building, completed in 1929, is one of the few skyscrapers in San Francisco whose ornamental program is a visual pun on its original tenant's name. Designed by George W. Kelham in a style that blends Gothic Revival with Art Deco -- sometimes called Gothic Moderne -- it rises 28 stories and 378 feet above the Financial District, a terracotta-clad monument to the era when oil companies built their offices to look like cathedrals.

## Kelham's Cathedral of Commerce

George W. Kelham was one of San Francisco's most prolific architects during the rebuilding years after the 1906 earthquake. By the time he designed the Shell Building, he had already shaped much of the city's civic and commercial architecture. The tower at 100 Bush Street was completed in 1929, the same year the stock market crashed. Its design draws on the same vertical Gothic impulse that Eliel Saarinen had made fashionable with his celebrated second-place entry for the Chicago Tribune Tower competition in 1922. Kelham translated that impulse into San Francisco's particular idiom: terracotta cladding that catches the afternoon light, setbacks that step the tower up to a square-topped crown, and ornamentation that rewards close inspection without overwhelming the overall composition.

## From Oil to Icons

The Shell Oil Company occupied the building until the 1960s, when the company relocated. The departure could have meant decline, but the building's location at Bush and Battery, at the edge of the Financial District where it meets the old commercial waterfront, kept it relevant. A renovation by Heller Manus Architects in the 1990s earned a San Francisco Architectural Heritage Award in 1994, recognizing the careful restoration of the building's original details. The shell motifs that once served as corporate branding became architectural heritage, appreciated for their craftsmanship rather than their commercial intent. Film fans may recognize the building from the rooftop scene at the end of David Fincher's 1997 thriller The Game, where it appears in the background of the climactic sequence.

## Art Deco in the Fog

The Shell Building belongs to a remarkable cluster of Art Deco and Gothic Revival towers built in San Francisco's Financial District during the late 1920s and early 1930s, a period when the city's skyline took on the stepped, ornamental silhouette that still defines its character from certain angles. Unlike the sleek glass curtain walls that would dominate postwar construction, these buildings were designed to be read as compositions -- with bases, shafts, and crowns, each element receiving its own decorative treatment. The Shell Building's terracotta facade, with its warm tones and intricate detailing, represents a kind of architecture that assumes pedestrians will stop and look up. In a city increasingly defined by tech-campus minimalism, these 1920s towers feel almost rebelliously ornate.

## Flight Context

The Shell Building stands at 37.79N, -122.40W in San Francisco's Financial District, at the corner of Bush and Battery Streets. At 28 stories (378 feet), it is visible among the cluster of historic towers in the eastern part of downtown.
