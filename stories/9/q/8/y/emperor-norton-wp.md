# Emperor Norton

The self-proclaimed Emperor of the United States who ruled San Francisco by decree for twenty years, abolishing Congress, ordering a bridge across the bay, and dying beloved by the entire city.

**Location:** 37.6794, -122.4686

**Categories:** History of San Francisco, American folklore, Self-proclaimed monarchs

## Introduction

On September 17, 1859, a letter arrived at the offices of the San Francisco Bulletin. Its author, Joshua Abraham Norton, a failed businessman who had lost his fortune speculating on Peruvian rice, declared himself "Norton I, Emperor of the United States." The paper printed the letter that evening for humorous effect, and thus began the most extraordinary civic performance in American history: a twenty-year reign by a self-appointed emperor who issued decrees, inspected public works, dined for free at the city's finest restaurants, and was treated by San Franciscans not as a madman but as a municipal treasure.

## Decrees from the Emperor

Norton governed by proclamation, and his decrees ranged from the whimsical to the prescient. On October 12, 1859, he formally abolished the United States Congress. He ordered the construction of a bridge connecting San Francisco to Oakland -- decades before the Bay Bridge was built. He issued currency, which some San Francisco merchants accepted. He decreed that anyone calling the city "Frisco" would be subject to a fine. The newspapers printed his proclamations regularly, and the public followed them with a mixture of amusement and genuine affection. Norton attended sessions of the California legislature, where he was given a seat of honor. He inspected the streets for potholes and reported on the condition of public infrastructure.

## The Emperor's Daily Life

By 1865, and for the remainder of his life, Norton lived in a small room on the top floor of the Eureka Lodgings, a three-story rooming house at 624 Commercial Street between Montgomery and Kearny. He walked the streets of San Francisco daily in a military uniform with epaulettes, often accompanied by two dogs, Bummer and Lazarus, who became celebrities in their own right. Restaurants seated him without charge. The San Francisco Board of Supervisors provided him with an annual grant for new uniforms. He attended church services of every denomination, apparently believing that an emperor should show no favoritism. The city embraced him not as a joke but as an institution.

## Death of an Emperor

Norton collapsed on the evening of January 8, 1880, at the corner of California and Dupont Streets, on his way to a lecture at the California Academy of Sciences. He died before reaching the hospital. His funeral drew an estimated 10,000 to 30,000 mourners -- a staggering turnout for a man who held no office and possessed no wealth. The San Francisco Chronicle's headline read: "Le Roi Est Mort" -- the king is dead. His burial was paid for by a club of local businessmen. In 1934, his remains were moved to Woodlawn Cemetery in Colma, where his grave marker reads simply: "Norton I, Emperor of the United States and Protector of Mexico."

## The Emperor's Afterlife

Norton's legacy grew after his death into something approaching civic sainthood. In February 2023, San Francisco Board of Supervisors president Aaron Peskin introduced a resolution to add "Emperor Norton Place" as a commemorative name for the 600 block of Commercial Street, near Norton's former lodgings. The emperor has inspired novels, plays, operas, and comic book characters -- Neil Gaiman featured him in The Sandman. His story endures because it speaks to something specific about San Francisco: a city willing to play along with a magnificent delusion, to treat kindness and imagination as credentials sufficient for office. Norton had no power. He had something better: a city that chose to believe in him.
