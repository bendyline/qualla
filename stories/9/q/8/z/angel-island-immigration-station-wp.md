# Angel Island Immigration Station

The Ellis Island of the West, where 18% of arrivals were rejected and detainees carved poems of anguish into wooden walls.

**Location:** 37.8697, -122.4260

**Categories:** Chinese-American history, Buildings and structures in Marin County, California, National Historic Landmarks in the San Francisco Bay Area, Museums in Marin County, California, History museums in California

## Introduction

The words are still there, carved into the wooden walls of the detention barracks: America has power, but not justice. In prison, we were victimized as if we were guilty. Given no opportunity to explain, it was really brutal. I bow my head in reflection but there is nothing I can do. Between 1910 and 1940, hundreds of thousands of immigrants passed through Angel Island Immigration Station in San Francisco Bay. While Ellis Island welcomed newcomers to America with an acceptance rate above 97%, Angel Island rejected roughly 18% of all arrivals. The difference was intentional. This was the gateway designed to keep people out.

## The Island of Exclusion

Angel Island, the second-largest island in San Francisco Bay, had been Coastal Miwok fishing grounds, then a haven for Spanish explorer Juan Manuel de Ayala, then a cattle ranch, and then a Civil War military post. In 1904, officials recommended building a new immigration station on this isolated spot, far from the San Francisco waterfront where previous facilities had proven inadequate and unsanitary. The War Department transferred 20 acres on the island's north shore, deliberately facing away from the city, to the Department of Labor and Commerce. Architect Walter J. Mathews designed an enclosed detention center with guard tower, administration building, hospital, and wharf. The location was chosen specifically because isolation made it easy to control immigrants, contain disease outbreaks, and enforce the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882.

## Paper Sons and Paper Daughters

The Chinese Exclusion Act allowed only merchants, clergy, diplomats, teachers, and students to enter, barring laborers entirely. In response, Chinese immigrants, mostly men, claimed to be sons of Chinese Americans, exploiting the fact that children of citizens are also citizens regardless of birthplace. These immigrants falsely claiming familial ties became known as paper sons and paper daughters. Some Chinese American citizens participated in the scheme to help others of their ethnicity or for money. Immigration officials responded with grueling interrogations designed to catch inconsistencies. Chinese detainees faced questions about their villages, their families, the layout of their homes, anything that might expose a fraudulent claim. Japanese immigrants, who often carried documentation from government officials, typically passed through quickly. The Chinese had no choice but to endure.

## Voices Carved in Wood

Confined for weeks, months, sometimes years while appeals crawled through the system, detainees turned to poetry. Using pencil, ink, and brush, they wrote on the wooden walls and floors, then carved the characters deep into the wood. Some poems burned with anger: I thoroughly hate the barbarians because they do not respect justice. They continually promulgate harsh laws to show off their prowess. They oppress the overseas Chinese and also violate treaties. Others held hope: Twice I have passed through the blue ocean, experienced the wind and dust of journey. Confinement in the wooden building has pained me doubly. With a weak country, we must all join together in urgent effort. It depends on all of us together to roll back the wild wave. These poems survived decades of neglect, painted over and forgotten until a state park ranger discovered them in 1970.

## Legacy by the Golden Gate

The station closed on November 5, 1940, after fire destroyed the administration building. An estimated 100,000 Chinese immigrants and 85,000 Japanese immigrants had passed through, along with arrivals from 84 countries including India, Mexico, the Philippines, Australia, and New Zealand. In 1978, the Angel Island Chinese Monument was completed, its inscription reading: Leaving their homes and villages, they crossed the ocean only to endure confinement in these barracks. Conquering frontiers and barriers, they pioneered a new life by the Golden Gate. Among those who passed through was nine-year-old Tyrus Wong, who would grow up to become the chief artist for Disney's Bambi. The restored detention barracks now stand as a California Historical Landmark and National Historic Landmark, the carved poems preserved as testimony to the human cost of exclusion.
