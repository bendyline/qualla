# American Airlines Flight 28

On October 23, 1942, an Army B-34 struck an American Airlines DC-3 in Chino Canyon near Palm Springs, killing all 12 aboard the airliner — including Academy Award-winning composer Ralph Rainger, who had written Bob Hope's signature song.

**Location:** 33.8667, -116.5670

**Categories:** Aviation history, World War II, Palm Springs California, Mid-air collisions

## Introduction

The composer who wrote 'Thanks for the Memory' — the song Bob Hope made his own, the one audiences associated with the comedian for the rest of his career — died not in the noise of Hollywood but in the mountains above Palm Springs. Ralph Rainger was 41 years old. He had won an Academy Award, written hit after hit, and was traveling on American Airlines Flight 28 when a United States Army Air Forces B-34 bomber struck the DC-3 in Chino Canyon on October 23, 1942. All twelve people aboard the airliner died. The B-34 survived, with minor damage, and landed safely at the Army Airport of the Sixth Ferrying Command in Palm Springs. The Army pilot was court-martialed on manslaughter charges. He was found not guilty.

## The Aircraft and Their Crews

American Airlines Flight 28 was operated by a Douglas DC-3, registration NC16017, powered by two Wright Cyclone engines rated at 1,102 horsepower each. The aircraft had been approved and certified by the Civil Aeronautics Board for up to 21 passengers and four crew. Captain Charles Fred Pedley, 42, had logged more than 17,000 hours of flight time across twelve years with American Airlines. His co-pilot, First Officer Louis Frederick Reppert Jr., was 26 and had 800 hours of experience and six months with the airline. Stewardess Estelle Frances Regan, 27, completed the crew. The B-34 was piloted by Lieutenant William Norman Wilson, 25, attached to the Air Transport Command and stationed at Long Beach. His co-pilot was Staff Sergeant Robert Reed Leicht, also 25, of the Sixth Ferrying Command.

## What Was Lost

Ralph Rainger had been born Ralph Reichenthal in New York City and had studied piano at New York's Institute of Musical Art and earned a law degree from Brown University before finding his calling in popular songwriting. His catalog included 'Blue Hawaii,' which Elvis Presley would later record; 'Love in Bloom,' which became Jack Benny's signature tune; 'June in January'; and 'I Wished on the Moon.' But it was 'Thanks for the Memory,' from the 1938 film The Big Broadcast of 1938, that won him and lyricist Leo Robin an Academy Award and embedded him permanently in the soundtrack of American popular culture. He died at 41, before the wartime song boom had fully played out, before the recordings of 'Blue Hawaii' that would make the song iconic in the 1960s. The eight other passengers and three crew members who died with him were flying through a landscape that was, in 1942, filled with military aircraft and the particular hazards of shared airspace.

## The Mountain and the Investigation

Chino Canyon rises steeply from the floor of the Coachella Valley on the northeast face of the San Jacinto Mountains. In 1942, before the Palm Springs Aerial Tramway was built and before the canyon was developed for recreation, it was rugged terrain with limited access. Three separate inquiries followed: a coroner's inquest, an Army investigation that led to Lieutenant Wilson's court-martial, and an investigation by the Civil Aeronautics Board. Investigators learned that Wilson and the airliner's first officer, Louis Reppert, had trained together and had met the night before. The Board found the probable cause to be the 'reckless and irresponsible conduct' of Lieutenant Wilson in deliberately maneuvering his bomber in dangerous proximity to the airliner in an attempt to attract the attention of its first officer. The court-martial nonetheless acquitted him of manslaughter.

## The Canyon Today

The Palm Springs Aerial Tramway opened in Chino Canyon in 1963, carrying passengers from the valley floor at 2,643 feet to the Mountain Station at 8,516 feet in a rotating gondola. The canyon that in 1942 was accessible only by difficult trail is now a tourism destination, the dramatic rock walls a scenic backdrop for riders ascending toward the summit of Mount San Jacinto. Tram riders pass above the canyon where, on an October evening in 1942, a wartime B-34 crossed paths with an airliner carrying an Academy Award-winning composer and eleven other people who had no idea they would not arrive.

## Flight Context

Located at 33.87°N, 116.57°W, in Chino Canyon on the northeast face of the San Jacinto Mountains near Palm Springs. From the air, the dramatic face of the San Jacinto range is unmistakable, rising steeply from the Coachella Valley floor. The Palm Springs Aerial Tramway cables and towers are visible on the canyon wall. Palm Springs International Airport (PSP) is approximately 4 miles to the southeast.
