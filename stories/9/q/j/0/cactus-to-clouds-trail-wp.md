# Cactus to Clouds Trail

The Cactus to Clouds Trail climbs 10,300 feet from the desert floor near the Palm Springs Art Museum to the summit of San Jacinto Peak — one of the greatest single-day elevation gains achievable on foot anywhere in the United States.

**Location:** 33.8253, -116.5510

**Categories:** Hiking trails, Palm Springs California, San Jacinto Mountains, California outdoor recreation

## Introduction

The idea came from two hiking-club leaders who had been marking a faint trail up the mountain and wanted to connect the desert floor to its summit. Ray Wilson and Sue Birnbaum, Coachella Valley Hiking Club leaders, conceived the Cactus to Clouds hike in 1991, after marking the upper miles of the Skyline Trail with yellow metal tags so others could follow it to its end at Long Valley near the Palm Springs Aerial Tramway. The following year they walked it themselves — all the way from the trail's beginning near the valley floor to the summit of San Jacinto Peak, a gain of 10,300 feet. They offered the first official Cactus to Clouds Challenge to club members in 1993. It has become one of the most ambitious day hikes in California, a route that begins where temperatures can hit 120 degrees Fahrenheit and ends where freezing is not unusual.

## The Numbers

The route begins at roughly 520 feet of elevation near the Palm Springs Art Museum. San Jacinto Peak stands at 10,834 feet. The net gain — approximately 10,300 feet — happens over 16 miles of one-way trail, making it among the steepest routes of its length in the world. The first 11 miles, called the Skyline Trail, climb 7,900 feet through the desert and mountain terrain on the eastern face of the San Jacinto range. This section is known informally as Cactus to Crowds, reflecting both the crowds at the top near the tram station and the desert scrub at the bottom. After reaching Long Valley at 8,400 feet and the Palm Springs Aerial Tramway's Mountain Station, the trail continues another 5 miles and 2,400 feet to the summit. Most hikers descend by taking the tram.

## What the Trail Crosses

The Skyline Trail begins on the Museum Trail, merges briefly with the North Lykken Trail, then becomes an informal path — not maintained by government agencies — maintained by hikers and volunteers who place rescue boxes at two points along the route. The trail ascends the east side of Mount San Jacinto along a ridge between Chino Canyon and Tahquitz Creek, moving roughly westward along the ridgeline. At 7,500 feet, there is a section known as The Traverse — a short northward turn that becomes genuinely hazardous in winter conditions. At Long Valley, at 8,400 feet, the official state park trail system begins, leading through a ranger station (where potable water is sometimes available), past Wellman Divide, and to the summit of San Jacinto Peak.

## The Extremes

The primary hazard of the Cactus to Clouds Trail is the same thing that makes it compelling: the extraordinary range of environments it passes through in a single day. Lower sections can reach 120 degrees Fahrenheit in summer. The summit of San Jacinto Peak can be genuinely cold at any time of year, and in winter temperatures of -12 degrees Fahrenheit are not uncommon there. For these reasons, the hike typically begins in Palm Springs very early — sometimes at midnight — and is generally attempted in fall or spring. May and October are considered the best months. Hikers who start too late on a summer morning may find themselves in dangerous heat on the unshaded lower slopes while still hours from the summit.

## Seen From Above

From the air, the Cactus to Clouds route is essentially the entire eastern face of the San Jacinto Mountains — the great escarpment that rises from the Coachella Valley floor to the mountain's summit in one of the steepest topographic transitions in the continental United States. The Palm Springs Aerial Tramway, which follows a roughly parallel line up the mountain, offers a sense of what the terrain looks like from the outside: the cables and towers traversing rock walls that drop away thousands of feet below. The route that Wilson and Birnbaum marked with yellow tags in 1991 still climbs that same wall, a thread of trail connecting the desert to the sky.

## Flight Context

Located at 33.83°N, 116.55°W, starting near the Palm Springs Art Museum and climbing the dramatic face of the San Jacinto Mountains. From the air, the full sweep of the trail is visible — from the flat desert grid of Palm Springs to the rocky summit of San Jacinto Peak. The Aerial Tramway cables and towers are visible parallel to the trail's upper section. Palm Springs International Airport (PSP) is approximately 2.5 miles to the east of the trailhead.
