# Carl Eytel

Carl Eytel came from Germany to the American Southwest and became the spiritual figurehead of an early Palm Springs circle of desert writers and artists — living in poverty until his death in 1925, leaving behind detailed illustrations and landscapes that documented a landscape few others were looking at.

**Location:** 33.8224, -116.5340

**Categories:** Palm Springs history, Desert art, American impressionism, California artists

## Introduction

The man who became the spiritual figurehead of an early Palm Springs circle of desert writers and artists arrived in America from Germany in 1885 and spent years working his way toward the desert he would eventually make his subject. Carl Eytel settled in Palm Springs in poverty and remained there in poverty, painting the Coachella Valley, the surrounding mountains, the missions, the mining camps, the desert wildlife. When he developed a hacking and persistent cough in his later years and could not afford art supplies, a fellow artist — Jimmy Swinnerton — bought them for him. Eytel became increasingly reclusive. He died of tuberculosis in a Banning sanatorium in 1925 and, as a friend of the Cahuilla people, was buried in their cemetery in Palm Springs. A desert shrub was named in his honor. A short street in Palm Springs carries his name. His painting Desert near Palm Springs (1914) hangs in the California History Room of the California State Library.

## The Smoketree School

Journalist Ann Japenga has placed Eytel in what she calls the Smoketree School of desert artists — a loose grouping of painters who worked in the Coachella Valley in the early 20th century, influenced by French Impressionism but oriented toward the particular light and landscape of the American desert Southwest. The school had origins with Alson S. Clark and Jack Frost, who had been influenced by Claude Monet. Other Smoketree painters included Maynard Dixon, Agnes Pelton, and Hanson Puthuff. Eytel was not the most commercially successful member of this community — far from it — but his role as the 'spiritual figurehead' of Palm Springs' circle of desert writers — as historian Peter Wild later described it — suggests he provided something that money and recognition could not: a model of absolute dedication to the subject, regardless of reward.

## What He Drew

Eytel's work was wide-ranging within its geographic limits. He illustrated George Wharton James's Wonders of the Colorado Desert with detailed drawings that included Desert Bighorn Sheep, desert reptiles, and cattle. His Mirage in the Desert (1905), painted for that book, depicts cattle and cowboys in a landscape of heat and distance. He also illustrated Cone-bearing Trees of the California Mountains. His scenes from early Palm Springs — which in the 1890s and 1900s was a very small settlement — documented the stagecoach station and included portraits of local figures like William Pester, known as the Hermit of Palm Springs. He drew missions, prospectors working mines in Arizona, ranches near Encinitas and Fort Grant, the Yuma Territorial Prison. His range was the entire desert Southwest, but Palm Springs was his home.

## The Community He Belonged To

Eytel's closest companions included Edmund C. Jaeger, J. Smeaton Chase, and Charles Francis Saunders — naturalists and writers who were also learning the desert's grammar in these years. After Jaeger left to complete his studies and Chase married Isabel White in 1917, the group stayed connected through letters. Swinnerton, already a well-known newspaper cartoonist, was the one who bought Eytel art supplies when he could no longer afford them. The desert community of the early 20th century was small, intimate, and marked by poverty for most of those who chose to take it seriously.

## What Remained

In his honor, the desert shrub Amphipappus fremontii was given the common name 'eytelia.' Via Eytel, a short street in Palm Springs, carries his name, as does Eytel Road in nearby Cathedral City. The Autry Museum of the American West holds the original drawings Eytel made for Wonders of the Colorado Desert — 394 images in the Braun Research Library collection, available to researchers. His Desert near Palm Springs remains in the California State Library. He documented the desert Southwest before the automobile made it accessible, before the resorts came, before Palm Springs became a destination for people who could afford to be somewhere warmer in winter. What he saw is what he drew.
