# Agua Caliente Cultural Museum

The Agua Caliente Cultural Museum in downtown Palm Springs tells the story of the Cahuilla people who have called the Coachella Valley home for centuries — and as the first Native American institution accepted into the Smithsonian Affiliations Program, it occupies a nationally significant position.

**Location:** 33.8210, -116.5470

**Categories:** Native American history, Palm Springs California, Cahuilla people, California museums

## Introduction

The city of Palm Springs exists on land that the Agua Caliente Band of Cahuilla Indians has occupied for more than a millennium. The resort hotels, the golf courses, the mid-century modern houses that draw architecture tourists from around the world — all of it sits on what is, in legal and cultural terms, Cahuilla land. The museum at 140 North Indian Canyon Drive in downtown Palm Springs was established in 1991 to make that history visible and to tell the story of a people whose presence in the Coachella Valley predates the arrival of Spanish missionaries, American settlers, and the entire infrastructure of Southern California resort culture. That it became the first Native American museum accepted into the Smithsonian Affiliations Program is a measure of the seriousness with which it pursues that work.

## Who the Cahuilla Are

The Agua Caliente Band of Cahuilla Indians takes its name — agua caliente, warm water — from the hot springs that the Cahuilla people had long used in the area now occupied by downtown Palm Springs. Those springs, sacred and practical at once, gave the band's homeland a distinctive character. The Cahuilla were not nomadic but rather had deep roots in specific territories of the Coachella Valley and surrounding mountains, with complex social organization, sophisticated knowledge of desert ecology, and rich ceremonial traditions. The checkerboard land ownership pattern that characterizes Palm Springs today — where alternating square-mile sections of land belong to the federal government or to the Agua Caliente Band — is a legacy of how railroad land grants and reservation boundaries were drawn in the 19th century, a pattern that shaped the development of the entire city and remains a defining feature of the urban landscape.

## The Museum and Its Collections

The museum's core exhibitions cover Cahuilla culture and history, anchored in part by the Florence Patencio Collection, which documents a significant cultural leader of the Agua Caliente community. Patencio was central to the transmission of traditional knowledge and cultural practices in the early 20th century, a period when federal Indian policy actively suppressed Indigenous languages and ceremonies. The museum's off-site exhibitions extend its reach: into the Spa Resort Casino Hotel lobby, where an installation addresses the spiritual significance of the Agua Caliente hot spring; to California State University San Bernardino's Palm Desert campus, where Native American athletes are featured; and to Palm Springs City Hall, which displays milestones in Cahuilla history. A 1,200-volume reference library supports the museum's research function.

## The Smithsonian Affiliation

The Smithsonian Institution Affiliations Program connects museums and educational organizations across the United States to the resources, expertise, and collections of the Smithsonian. When the Agua Caliente Cultural Museum became the first Native American institution accepted into the program, it signaled both the museum's quality and the Smithsonian's recognition of the importance of Indigenous-led institutions in telling American history. The museum also participates in the American Alliance of Museums' Assessment Program and has received recognition for its publications — the 2009-2010 museum brochure and program announcement cards won a first-place award in the AAM Publications Design Competition.

## Growth and the Future

The current museum footprint does not fully represent the ambitions of its founders and community. Plans have been underway for a major expansion into a 100,000 square-foot facility designed by architects Jones and Jones — a scale that would give the institution room to display more of its collections, expand its educational programs, and become more fully a center for Cahuilla cultural life rather than simply a display space. The fundraising for that expansion reflects the Agua Caliente Band's investment in ensuring that the story of the Coachella Valley's first people is told on a scale commensurate with that history's depth.

## Flight Context

Located at 33.82°N, 116.55°W, at 140 North Indian Canyon Drive in downtown Palm Springs, between Andreas Road and Tahquitz Canyon Way. Palm Springs International Airport (PSP) is approximately 1.5 miles to the southeast.
