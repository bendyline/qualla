# Cornelia White House

Built in 1893 from the recycled railroad ties of a failed desert railway, the Cornelia White House is one of the oldest surviving structures in Palm Springs — a building that exists because a horse-drawn railroad was abandoned for lack of water.

**Location:** 33.8208, -116.5470

**Categories:** Palm Springs history, Historic houses, California heritage sites, Coachella Valley

## Introduction

The house at the Village Green Heritage Center in downtown Palm Springs is made of wood that was meant to be underfoot, not overhead. The Palmdale Railroad was a horse-drawn, narrow gauge short-line that ran along what is now Farrell Drive toward the proposed town of Palmdale at the foot of Mount San Jacinto. The railroad was short-lived. It had been abandoned by 1893, defeated by the same problem that defeated so many desert enterprises: lack of water. When the tracks came up, someone recognized that good railroad ties were good building material. The house built from those recycled ties in 1893, on the grounds of Welwood Murray's Palm Springs Hotel, later became the home of Cornelia B. White, a pioneer who arrived in Palm Springs in 1913 and bought the property; it is now one of the oldest surviving structures in Palm Springs.

## A Failed Railroad's Second Life

The Palmdale Railroad had been intended to connect Palm Springs to a proposed new settlement. It failed for lack of water, the problem that undid so many early desert settlement schemes. By 1893, the tracks had been pulled and the venture was done. What the railroad left behind was a quantity of wooden railroad ties — cut to size, functional and durable — that had never been used for very long. Those ties became the walls and structure of a house. It was a practical decision that left behind a surprisingly durable artifact.

## The Neighbor Next Door

The Cornelia White House now stands beside a building nine years its senior: the McCallum Adobe of 1884, built for John Guthrie McCallum with sun-baked bricks made by Cahuilla people; McCallum is described as the first European American settler in the area. The Adobe was also notable as the first successful adobe structure in the upper Coachella Valley. Neither stands where it was built: the adobe was moved to the Village Green in the early 1950s, and the house followed in 1979. Together, these two buildings anchor what became the Village Green Heritage Center — a small cluster of historic structures at 221 South Palm Canyon Drive that represents the oldest surviving residential architecture in Palm Springs. The juxtaposition is telling: one house made of earth that had been available for millennia, the other made of processed wood from a failed industrial venture.

## The Historical Society

Since 1961, the Cornelia White House and the McCallum Adobe have been directed by and are the property of the Palm Springs Historical Society. Together they operate as the McCallum Adobe-Cornelia White House Museum, open to the public. Inside are items of local history that include the earliest telephones used in Palm Springs — technology that arrived long after the railroad ties were repurposed into walls. The museum is a modest place by the standards of the resort city that has grown up around it, but it contains the physical evidence of Palm Springs before the resorts, before the celebrities, before the mid-century modern architecture that draws tourists from around the world. The railroad ties that became walls are still there.

## What the Village Green Represents

Palm Springs in the 1880s and 1890s was a small desert settlement, not the resort destination it would become after the automobile and the Depression-era popularity of desert health tourism transformed it. The McCallum Adobe and the Cornelia White House survived that transformation without being demolished — though both had to be moved to do so — which is not the fate of most 19th-century buildings in California resort towns. Their survival is partly circumstantial and partly the result of the Historical Society's stewardship since 1961. They stand today on Palm Canyon Drive — surrounded by mid-century modern hotels, boutique shops, and the apparatus of a tourist economy — as a reminder that someone was here before all of that, making do with railroad ties.
