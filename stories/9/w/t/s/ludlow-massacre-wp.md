# Ludlow Massacre

On April 20, 1914, Colorado National Guard troops and company mine guards attacked a tent colony of striking coal miners and their families near Ludlow, Colorado, killing 21 people - including two women and eleven children who suffocated in a pit beneath a burning tent.

## Introduction

They called it the "Death Special" - a steel-plated touring car mounted with a machine gun, built at the Colorado Fuel and Iron Company plant in Pueblo from the chassis of a large sedan. The Baldwin-Felts Detective Agency used it to patrol the perimeters of the tent colonies where evicted miners and their families lived after walking off the job. At night, agents shone searchlights into the canvas tents and fired bullets at random. The miners responded by digging pits beneath their tent floors - shelters from gunfire that would, on one terrible day in April 1914, become death traps. The Ludlow Massacre remains one of the deadliest events in American labor history, a confrontation between striking coal miners and the armed forces of industry and state that killed 21 people and ignited a ten-day guerrilla war across southern Colorado.

## Company Towns and Coal Dust

Colorado's southern coalfields in 1913 operated under a feudal system. Miners lived in company-owned towns, bought goods at company stores, and were paid by the ton for coal extracted - but "dead work" like shoring up unstable mine roofs went uncompensated. In 1913 alone, 110 miners died in workplace accidents. Nationally, unionized mines had 40 percent fewer fatalities than non-union operations. When the United Mine Workers of America called a strike in September 1913, miners were evicted from company housing and moved into tent colonies erected on land the union had leased near canyon mouths to block strikebreaker traffic. The tents sat on wooden platforms and were heated by cast-iron stoves. They were homes, and they would become a battlefield.

## The Day the Tents Burned

By spring 1914, the Colorado National Guard had been deployed for months but was running out of state funding. Governor Elias Ammons recalled most troops, leaving behind a company called "Troop A" - composed largely of mine guards given National Guard uniforms. On April 20, fighting erupted between the militia and the Ludlow tent colony. The battle raged all day. Machine guns on "Water Tank Hill" fired into the camp. At dusk, a freight train stopped on the tracks in front of the Guard's gun placements, and many families escaped toward hills to the east called the Black Hills. By 7 p.m., the camp was in flames. Louis Tikas, the Greek-born strike leader who had remained in the camp throughout the fighting, was captured by militiamen. Lieutenant Karl Linderfelt broke a rifle butt over Tikas's head. Tikas and two other captured miners were later found shot dead - Tikas with a bullet in his back. Their bodies lay along the Colorado and Southern Railway tracks for three days.

## The Pit Beneath the Tent

During the battle, four women and eleven children took shelter in a pit dug beneath one of the tents - the same kind of pit miners had excavated to protect their families from the nightly gunfire. When the tent above them was set ablaze, they were trapped. Two of the women and all eleven children suffocated. It was the discovery of these deaths that gave the event its name: the United Mine Workers called it the Ludlow Massacre. The image of women and children suffocating underground while soldiers burned their homes above became a national rallying cry. Protests erupted outside the Rockefeller building in New York City. Anarchists Alexander Berkman and Carlo Tresca led demonstrators who followed John D. Rockefeller Jr. to his family estate near Tarrytown.

## Ten Days of War

News of the dead women and children spread fast. Union leaders issued a call to arms, urging members to acquire "all the arms and ammunition legally available." In Trinidad, the UMWA openly distributed weapons at union headquarters. Over the next ten days, 700 to 1,000 strikers attacked mine after mine across Colorado's southern coalfields, driving off or killing guards and burning buildings. At least 50 people died in the fighting. The Colorado Coalfield War produced a total death toll of approximately 75. Hundreds of state militia reinforcements eventually restored order by disarming both sides. The strike was called off on December 10, 1914, when the union ran out of money. Of 408 arrested strikers, 332 were indicted for murder. Only one person was convicted - strike leader John R. Lawson - and the Colorado Supreme Court later overturned his conviction. No militia member or mine guard was ever held accountable.

## A Monument in Granite

The massacre forced the Rockefeller family into damage control that reshaped American labor relations. John D. Rockefeller Jr. hired labor expert W. L. Mackenzie King - the future Canadian Prime Minister - to develop mine reforms including paved roads, recreational facilities, and worker representation committees. He also brought in pioneer public relations expert Ivy Lee, who advised Rockefeller to go to Colorado, meet the miners personally, and listen to their grievances. In 1916, the United Mine Workers bought the Ludlow tent colony site. Two years later, they erected a granite monument - fabricated by the Jones Brothers Company of Barre, Vermont - to honor those who died. The site was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1985. George McGovern wrote his doctoral dissertation on the Ludlow events, publishing it in 1972 as The Great Coalfield War. The tent colony site has been the subject of archaeological excavations since 1996, uncovering artifacts that continue to tell the story of the families who lived and died there.

## Flight Context

Located at 37.34N, 104.58W in Las Animas County, southern Colorado, along Interstate 25 between Trinidad and Walsenburg. The site sits on the high plains east of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains. The Ludlow Monument is visible as a small clearing near the highway. The nearest airports are Perry Stokes Airport in Trinidad (KTAD), approximately 18 miles south, and Spanish Peaks Airport (KHHF) in Walsenburg. The surrounding terrain is rolling grassland transitioning to mesa country - the same landscape where tent colonies once dotted canyon mouths. Recommended viewing altitude: 2,000-4,000 feet AGL.
