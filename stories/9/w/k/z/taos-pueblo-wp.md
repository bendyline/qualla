# Taos Pueblo

The oldest continuously inhabited community in the United States - a UNESCO World Heritage Site where multi-story adobe buildings have stood against the Sangre de Cristo Mountains for a thousand years, and where the Tiwa-speaking people fought empires to keep their sacred Blue Lake.

**Location:** 36.4392, -105.5456

**Categories:** pueblo, native-american, world-heritage-site, adobe, new-mexico, historical

## Introduction

The word itself tells you something. "Taos" comes from the Tiwa word meaning "village" - borrowed into Spanish, given a plural ending, and handed back as the name of a place that has never stopped being exactly what that word describes. For somewhere between six hundred and a thousand years, the reddish-brown adobe buildings of Taos Pueblo have risen on either side of Red Willow Creek, backed by the snowcapped peaks of the Sangre de Cristo Range. When Hernando de Alvarado first arrived with the Spanish in the sixteenth century, he described adobe houses stacked five or six stories high, each level narrower than the one below, accessed not through doors but through square holes in the roof reached by wooden ladders. That description still applies. Taos Pueblo is the largest multi-storied Pueblo structure still standing, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and the oldest continuously inhabited community in the United States.

## Built Without Doors

The architecture of Taos Pueblo was designed for defense. Adobe walls several feet thick absorbed heat in winter and repelled it in summer, but they also stopped arrows and musket balls. The original buildings had no standard doorways and few windows. Residents climbed wooden ladders to reach square openings in the roof, then descended into their rooms - and when enemies approached, they pulled the ladders up. Engelmann Spruce logs called vigas supported roofs layered with branches, grass, mud, and plaster. The buildings rose in stepped tiers, each level providing the terrace and floor for the one above. The Rio Pueblo de Taos - also called Red Willow Creek - flows between the two main building complexes, its headwaters originating at Blue Lake high in the Sangre de Cristo Mountains. About 4,500 people live on the tribal land attached to the pueblo today, though the ancient core buildings no longer have electricity or running water - a deliberate choice to preserve traditional ways of life.

## The Fight for Blue Lake

Ask the Taos Pueblo community what the most important event in their history is, and the answer is not the founding of the pueblo or its designation as a World Heritage Site. It is the return of Blue Lake. In the early twentieth century, President Theodore Roosevelt took the Pueblo's mountain land and designated it as Carson National Forest. Blue Lake - Ba Whyea in the Tiwa language - was included in that seizure. The Pueblo people believe they originated from this lake, that their ancestors live there, and that they ascend the mountain only for ceremonial purposes. For decades, they fought to get it back, making the unusual decision to involve non-native allies in lobbying the federal government. Their argument was direct: unrestricted access to Blue Lake was necessary to ensure their religious freedom. In 1970, President Richard Nixon signed Public Law 91-550, returning the sacred land. Additional acreage south of the ridge between Simpson Peak and Old Mike Peak was transferred back in 1996. Blue Lake also serves as the community's primary water supply, sustaining corn cultivation, fruit growing, bean production, and livestock ranching.

## Revolt and Resilience

The history of Taos Pueblo is a history of resistance. Throughout the 1600s, tensions grew between Pueblo peoples and Spanish colonial authorities who imposed Catholicism by force. Reports indicate the people of Taos resisted the building of the church from the start. By 1660, they killed the resident priest and destroyed it. The Spanish retaliated brutally. After it was rebuilt, the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 swept the region. Then came the Americans. On January 19, 1847, Hispanos and Taos Pueblo people launched a rebellion against the new U.S. territorial government. Tomas Romero led a group of Taos Puebloans to the home of Governor Charles Bent in the town of Taos, where Bent was shot with arrows, scalped, and killed. Colonel Sterling Price marched from Santa Fe, defeated the insurgents at the Battle of Canada on January 24, and followed the rebels back to Taos Pueblo. On February 3, Price bombarded the pueblo and the church where defenders sheltered. The next day, soldiers broke a hole in the church wall and fired shells and grapeshot at those inside. More than 150 people were killed. Romero was executed. Other leaders were hanged.

## Shimmering Clay

Taos Pueblo is renowned for its craftspeople and traditional art forms - silver and turquoise jewelry, heishi bead necklaces carved from seashells and soft stones, and a distinctive pottery tradition unlike any other in the Southwest. The pueblo's potters use micaceous clay, a local material flecked with mica that gives finished pieces a shimmering, orange-gold surface when fired. These ceramics are valued for both beauty and function: they are sturdy enough to cook over open flames. The artistic legacy runs deep. The pueblo has produced generations of painters, from Albert Looking Elk in the early twentieth century to Eva Mirabal, one of the first Native American female comic artists, to fashion designer Patricia Michaels. Musician Robert Mirabal carries the tradition forward on the Native American flute. Since Spanish colonization, the Taos people have actively resisted cultural assimilation, and many ethnographers observe a profound interconnectedness between the community and its surrounding land - a relationship that defines both their art and their identity.

## Preserving Adobe

Adobe is strong but not permanent. Rain erodes it, frost cracks it, and gravity slowly pulls it apart. Taos Pueblo's preservation challenge is unique: this is not a museum or an archaeological site but a living community where people still maintain homes using techniques passed down for centuries. In 2011, the Taos Pueblo Preservation Program received an $800,000 grant from the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development to train workers in traditional adobe construction and restore 120 to 150 houses. The World Monuments Fund has listed the pueblo on its watchlist due to its culturally and structurally endangered nature, financing a second conservation phase that included laser scanning of the structures. The approach is deliberately community-based: local people are trained to manage their own property, and partnerships with outside organizations are structured to sustain cultural traditions rather than replace them. Today, more young people and women are taking part in tribal leadership and community activities - a shift from older traditions that once limited these roles to men over thirty.

## Flight Context

Located at 36.44°N, 105.55°W in northern New Mexico, approximately one mile north of the modern town of Taos. The pueblo is visible from altitude as a cluster of reddish-brown adobe structures set against the western face of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains. Red Willow Creek (Rio Pueblo de Taos) flows through the center of the complex. Blue Lake lies high in the mountains to the northeast. Nearest airport: Taos Regional Airport (KSKX) approximately 12 miles west-northwest. Albuquerque International Sunport (KABQ) is approximately 130 miles south-southwest. Elevation approximately 7,000 feet. The Rio Grande gorge is visible to the west.
