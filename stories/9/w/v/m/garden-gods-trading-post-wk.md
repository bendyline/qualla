# The Garden of the Gods Trading Post

A ten-year-old carved gypsum souvenirs for tourists in 1900, then built the adobe trading post he'd been dreaming of for twenty-five years - and nearly a century later, it is still selling the work of Pueblo and Navajo artists at the gates of Colorado's red rocks.

## Introduction

Charles Strausenback was ten years old when he started selling tourists figures he had carved himself from gypsum quarried near the red rocks. The year was 1900. By 1917 he had a real business; by 1929, after a quarter century of saving and planning, he had something else - a thick-walled, cupola-topped, Pueblo Revival adobe at the western edge of Colorado Springs, the trading post he had been imagining since he was a boy with a pocket knife. It opened on June 9, 1929, four months before the stock market crash. It is still open. The Garden of the Gods Trading Post has outlived Strausenback, two World Wars, the family that founded it, and the era of the American roadside curio shop. The original fireplace remains. The hand-hewn roof beams remain. The thunderbird-and-rattlesnake logo, drawn for Strausenback by the San Ildefonso painter Awa Tsireh in 1926, still appears above the door.

## The Boy with the Knife

Strausenback's first inventory cost him nothing. Gypsum was plentiful in the foothills around the Garden of the Gods, soft enough for a child to carve and white enough to look like alabaster after a polish. He sold the figures to visitors arriving by carriage and, later, by Model T. By his late twenties he had a stand; by his late thirties he had a plan. The 1929 trading post was the result. The main sales floor measured forty by sixty feet, with living quarters above for Strausenback and his family. Construction cost roughly ten thousand dollars, an enormous sum for a curio business, but he wanted the building itself to be part of the draw - not a shed at the side of the road but a true Pueblo Revival composition, cupolas and viga beams and all.

## The Artists in Residence

Strausenback did something unusual for a tourist trading post: he hired Pueblo and Navajo artists to live and work onsite. Awa Tsireh, born Alfonso Roybal of San Ildefonso Pueblo, was already a celebrated watercolorist when he came to Colorado Springs - his paintings now hang in the Smithsonian. Severo Tafoya of Santa Clara Pueblo, known by the name Ca-Ping, worked at the post too. So did the Navajo silversmith William Goodluck. Visitors could watch them paint, throw pots, hammer silver. The logo Awa Tsireh drew - a stylized thunderbird seizing a rattlesnake - became the trading post's identity. Strausenback copyrighted it in 1926 and it still marks the building. The arrangement was commerce, but it was also, for its era, something more honest than most: the artists were named, paid, and present.

## Two Families, One Building

Strausenback ran the trading post until his death. The family kept it for decades after. In 1979, T.A.T. Enterprises purchased the business and has operated it ever since - making the Garden of the Gods Trading Post one of the longest continuously running curio stores in the Southwest, passed through only two ownerships in nearly a century. The new owners kept what mattered: the fireplace where Strausenback warmed his hands, the original beams, the cupolas, the logo. They added a restaurant that serves buffalo burgers and green chile, an ice cream window, a patio that looks out toward Pikes Peak. Tourists eat lunch in the shadow of three-hundred-million-year-old sandstone. The business model is unchanged in its essentials. The merchandise has expanded - turquoise and pottery and kachinas and, yes, refrigerator magnets - but the architecture and the intention are Strausenback's.

## What the Building Holds

Trading posts as a category have largely vanished from the American West. The roadside ones that survive are mostly gas stations with a rack of dreamcatchers. This one is different because Strausenback meant it to be different. The thick adobe walls keep the interior cool in summer and warm in winter without much help from machinery. The viga beams cast shadows across the sales floor that move with the sun. There is no parking-lot architecture, no fluorescent ceiling, no big-box gloss. The thunderbird above the door is the same thunderbird Awa Tsireh drew. A child carving gypsum in 1900 would recognize the place. Most of the Pueblo Revival buildings of the same era have been demolished or remodeled past recognition. Strausenback's has not.

## Finding the Trading Post

The Garden of the Gods Trading Post stands at 324 Beckers Lane, at the southwestern entrance to Garden of the Gods Park in Colorado Springs. The park itself - 1,341 acres of tilted red sandstone donated to the city in 1909 with the stipulation that admission be free forever - is the larger attraction, but the trading post predates the visitor center by decades and feels more like the place itself. The cafe is open daily; the gift shop runs long summer hours. The patio is the best seat in the building, with Pikes Peak rising in the distance and Balanced Rock about a mile north. Colorado Springs Airport (COS) is fifteen miles southeast. Denver International (DEN) is ninety minutes north. Come early or late - the red rock burns brightest then, and Strausenback's adobe glows with it.
