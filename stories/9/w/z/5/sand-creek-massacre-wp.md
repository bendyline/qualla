# Sand Creek Massacre

On November 29, 1864, U.S. soldiers attacked a peaceful Cheyenne and Arapaho encampment flying an American flag and a white flag of truce, killing over 150 people - mostly women and children - in one of the most notorious atrocities in American history.

**Location:** 38.5494, -102.5114

**Categories:** history, massacre, native-american, national-historic-site, colorado

## Introduction

Black Kettle had done everything right. The Southern Cheyenne chief had attended the peace parley in Denver in September 1864. He had moved his people - around 750 Cheyenne and Arapaho, mostly women, children, and elderly - to Big Sandy Creek, less than 40 miles northwest of Fort Lyon, Colorado, exactly where U.S. Army officers told him to camp. He flew an American flag above his lodge with a white flag of truce tied beneath it. The Dog Soldiers, the militant warrior society responsible for raids on white settlers, were not part of his encampment. On the morning of November 29, 1864, none of that mattered. Colonel John Chivington and roughly 675 troops of the Colorado Cavalry descended on the sleeping camp and opened fire.

## A Betrayal at Dawn

Chivington arrived at Fort Lyon on November 28 with 425 men of the 3rd Colorado Cavalry. He took command of an additional 250 men from the 1st Colorado Cavalry and rode through the night toward Black Kettle's camp. Several officers protested before the attack began. Captain Silas Soule, Lieutenant Joseph Cramer, and Lieutenant James Connor argued that attacking a peaceful camp would violate the Army's pledge of safety and dishonor the uniform. Chivington was unmoved. His troops charged at dawn, ignoring both the American flag and the white flag hastily raised after the assault began. The camp held only about 75 men, most too old or too young to fight. Cheyenne warrior Morning Star later testified that most of the dead were killed by cannon fire, cut down as they fled up the creek bed. Some survivors, including the half-Cheyenne trader George Bent, dug holes beneath the sand banks and hid as troops fired into the streambed above them.

## The Toll of the Innocent

The death count remains debated. Chivington claimed 500 to 600 warriors killed - a grotesque fabrication. Historian Alan Brinkley documented 133 dead, 105 of them women and children. Eyewitness John S. Smith reported 70 to 80 killed, including only 20 to 30 warriors. Peace chief Ochinee, who had tried to broker peace for the Cheyenne, was among the dead. Eight members of the Council of Forty-Four - the traditional governing body of the Cheyenne - were killed, including White Antelope, Yellow Wolf, Big Man, and One Eye. These were the very leaders who had advocated peace with white settlers. After the shooting stopped, soldiers scalped the dead regardless of age or gender, mutilated bodies, and collected body parts as trophies. Chivington's men publicly displayed scalps and human remains at Denver's Apollo Theater and in area saloons.

## Survivors on the Prairie

Those who escaped hid all day in holes dug beneath the frozen banks of Sand Creek. Many were wounded. As darkness fell, they crawled out onto the open prairie and spent the night without shelter in the November cold. By morning, they began walking toward a Cheyenne camp on the headwaters of the Smoky Hill River, where they finally received help. The massacre devastated Cheyenne society. The Wutapiu, Black Kettle's own band, suffered the greatest losses. About half the Hevhaitaniu and Oivimana bands were destroyed. Of roughly 50 to 60 Arapaho under Chief Left Hand, only a handful survived. The massacre shattered the traditional power structure and silenced the peace faction - because most peace chiefs were now dead.

## War Pipes and Broken Treaties

The massacre produced exactly the opposite of its intended effect. The war pipe was smoked and passed between Sioux, Cheyenne, and Arapaho camps. In January 1865, a thousand warriors attacked Camp Rankin at Julesburg, Colorado, followed by raids across the South Platte valley. Black Kettle, remarkably, still advocated peace. He refused to join the retaliatory raids and returned to the Arkansas River with 80 lodges seeking reconciliation. Congressional investigations condemned Chivington, but he had already resigned his commission and faced no punishment. The Treaty of the Little Arkansas, signed in 1865, promised reparations and land rights to survivors. Washington abrogated it within two years. The Medicine Lodge Treaty that replaced it reduced reservation lands by 90 percent, relocating the Cheyenne and Arapaho to far less desirable land in Oklahoma.

## Sacred Ground Reclaimed

The site on Big Sandy Creek in Kiowa County, Colorado was preserved as the Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site, dedicated on April 28, 2007 - nearly 143 years after the massacre. The American Battlefield Trust has added thousands of acres to the protected area. In October 2022, an additional 3,478 acres were announced for preservation. A monument on the Colorado State Capitol grounds originally listed Sand Creek among Civil War "battles and engagements"; in 2002, a corrective plaque was added noting that the original inscription had "mischaracterized" the event. On December 3, 2014, Governor John Hickenlooper formally apologized to descendants gathered in Denver for the 150th anniversary: "We should not be afraid to criticize and condemn that which is inexcusable. On behalf of the State of Colorado, I want to apologize. We will not run from this history."

## Flight Context

Located at 38.55N, 102.51W on the high plains of eastern Colorado, in Kiowa County. The site sits along Big Sandy Creek - from altitude, a thin line of vegetation in otherwise flat, open grassland. The nearest airport is Lamar Municipal Airport (KLAA), approximately 40 miles southeast. Approach from any direction reveals the emptiness of this landscape - the same emptiness that survivors crossed on foot after the massacre. The National Historic Site is visible as a preserved area along the creek bed. Recommended viewing altitude: 3,000-5,000 feet AGL for context of the terrain.
