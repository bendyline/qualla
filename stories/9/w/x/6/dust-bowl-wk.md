# The Dust Bowl

Children died of dust pneumonia. Families walked to California with everything they owned tied to a Model T. The Oklahoma Panhandle was once called No Man's Land - and for a decade in the 1930s, it earned the name again.

**Location:** 37.0000, -102.0000

## Introduction

On Palm Sunday afternoon, April 14, 1935, a homesteader named Caroline Henderson sat in her Oklahoma farmhouse and watched the sky go black. In Pampa, Texas, a twenty-three-year-old guitar player named Woody Guthrie was inside with his young wife when the dust wall hit; he thought he was going to die and wrote a song about saying goodbye - 'So Long, It's Been Good to Know You.' Outside Boise City, the Coble family huddled in their farmhouse while their fence posts disappeared under drifts of soil that had been farmland a month before. The storm that day moved at sixty to a hundred miles an hour, hundreds of miles wide, carrying an estimated 300,000 tons of topsoil out of the Oklahoma and Texas panhandles and into the eastern sky. The Associated Press reporter Robert Geiger watched it sweep through Guymon, Oklahoma, and filed his dispatch the next morning. He used the phrase 'Dust Bowl.' The name stuck for the decade, the region, and the catastrophe - but the catastrophe had a more local name first. The Oklahoma Panhandle was already called No Man's Land.

## How the Soil Came Loose

The southern Plains had always been semi-arid grassland, sustained by deep-rooted buffalo grass and gramma that had evolved to hold thin soil against constant wind. In the 1910s and 1920s, wheat prices climbed and tractor technology spread, and millions of acres of that grass were plowed under in a few short seasons. The wheat boom of the First World War accelerated everything. By the end of the 1920s, the southern Plains had been broken open the way a chest is broken open in surgery, and the topsoil sat exposed. When the rains failed in 1931 and the drought settled in for almost a decade, there was nothing left to hold the dirt down. Wind did the rest. The storms began in 1932 and worsened each year until Black Sunday. The most affected area - Cimarron County, Oklahoma; Dallam County, Texas; Baca County, Colorado; Morton County, Kansas; Union County, New Mexico - was where the Bowl had its deepest center.

## Dust Pneumonia

Children died first. The fine particles that the wind carried were small enough to enter the lungs and lodge there, and a generation of doctors learned to recognize a new disease they called dust pneumonia: a wet, suffocating cough that filled the chest with mud. Babies died in their cribs. Older children died in their schoolhouses. Mothers hung wet sheets over the windows to filter the air; the sheets turned brown within an hour. Hazel Lucas, who had grown up near Boise City riding her horse Pecos across the grasslands, lost her infant daughter Ruth Nell to dust pneumonia in 1935. She was not alone in her county that year. The Red Cross set up emergency hospitals in school gymnasiums. The dust got into food, into wells, into the lungs of livestock that had to be shot when their stomachs filled with dirt. Caroline Henderson, writing for the Atlantic Monthly from her homestead, described scraping dust from her dishes before serving every meal.

## The Road West

By the mid-1930s, the bank had taken many of the farms. Tenant farmers were turned off the land first - they had no equity to defend. About 2.5 million people left the Plains during the decade, more than 300,000 of them landing in California. They drove or walked Route 66 with their possessions roped to running boards: mattresses, sewing machines, photographs, children. Californians called them Okies whether they came from Oklahoma or not. The label combined poverty, dust, and contempt. Border patrols at the California line sometimes turned them back. The growers who did hire them paid them less than the going rate and housed them in ditch camps. Dorothea Lange, working for the Farm Security Administration, photographed a thirty-two-year-old mother named Florence Owens Thompson at a frozen pea pickers' camp in Nipomo in 1936; the photograph became 'Migrant Mother,' the most famous image of the decade. Thompson was Cherokee, not Okie. She had not eaten that day. Her children had been surviving on field scavenge and frozen vegetables.

## What the Songs Carried

Woody Guthrie left Pampa in 1936 and went west himself, following the migrants he had grown up alongside. He recorded the 'Dust Bowl Ballads' in 1940 in New York City - fourteen songs that documented what he had seen. 'I've lived in these dust storms just about all my life,' he wrote in the liner notes. 'I met millions of good folks trying to hang on and to stay alive with the dust cutting down every hope.' The Grapes of Wrath, John Steinbeck's novel about a fictional Oklahoma tenant family driven west, was published the year before. It won the Pulitzer Prize and was burned in Kern County, California, by growers who objected to how they were portrayed. Steinbeck had not invented the Joads. He had interviewed them in the federal camps and shadowed Tom Collins, a camp manager, for months. The songs and the novel together fixed the Dust Bowl in American memory more durably than any government report could have.

## What Remains

The rains returned in late 1939 and the worst of the dust ended. Caroline Henderson stayed on her Oklahoma homestead until her death. The Coble children grew up. Hazel Lucas left and came back. The Soil Conservation Service, created in 1935, planted shelterbelt trees and taught contour plowing across the Plains, and the region eventually grew wheat again - this time irrigated from the Ogallala Aquifer, which is now being drawn down faster than it recharges. The No Man's Land Museum in Goodwell, Oklahoma, holds photographs of the Coble family and Caroline Henderson's letters. The Cimarron Heritage Center in Boise City has a Black Sunday exhibit. Most visitors to the region today drive through it on the way somewhere else, past wheat fields where there were once dust dunes, past towns whose populations are a third of what they were in 1930. The wind still blows. The soil is still thin. The lesson, the survivors said, was that the land had limits and the people had not respected them.

## Flight Context

The Dust Bowl's most devastated zone centered on the four-state intersection of the Oklahoma Panhandle, Texas Panhandle, southeastern Colorado, and southwestern Kansas, with Boise City, Oklahoma (36.73N, 102.51W) near its heart. From altitude, the modern landscape appears as a patchwork of circular center-pivot irrigation fields - green where the Ogallala Aquifer still reaches, brown beyond. The terrain is nearly flat with shallow river valleys (Cimarron, Beaver, North Canadian). Useful airports: Amarillo (KAMA) 90 nm south, Liberal Mid-America (KLBL) 50 nm east, Pueblo Memorial (KPUB) 130 nm west. Strong surface winds are routine; turbulence and reduced visibility from blowing dust still occur on dry, windy days, especially March through May.
