# Bent's Old Fort

The adobe castle on the Santa Fe Trail - where the Bent brothers built an empire trading with Cheyenne and Arapaho in the 1830s, until smallpox, war, and the end of the fur trade led William Bent to blow it up himself.

**Location:** 38.0394, -103.4267

**Categories:** colorado, santa-fe-trail, trading-post, fur-trade, reconstruction

## Introduction

Bent's Old Fort was the only major permanent settlement on the Santa Fe Trail between Missouri and the Mexican settlements, an adobe castle in the wilderness where trappers, traders, Native Americans, and Mexican merchants met to exchange goods, information, and cultures. Charles and William Bent built it in 1833, creating an outpost that controlled trade across the southern Plains. For fifteen years, it was the center of a commercial empire. Kit Carson worked here. The Cheyenne and Arapaho were business partners and friends. Then came the Mexican-American War, smallpox epidemics, and the destruction of the buffalo-based economy. In 1849, William Bent - the only surviving partner - mysteriously destroyed the fort, perhaps by explosion, and moved downstream. The ruins moldered until the National Park Service reconstructed the fort in 1976, recreating what was once the most important trading post in the American West.

## The Building

Bent's Fort was massive - an adobe structure 180 by 137 feet, with walls 15 feet high and 4 feet thick. Two round towers provided defensive positions. Inside were living quarters, storage rooms, a blacksmith shop, and a press for packing buffalo robes. A billiard table and ice house brought civilization to the wilderness. The fort was built with adobe bricks made from local clay - the same technique used in Santa Fe. The Bent brothers hired Mexican laborers who knew adobe construction. The resulting structure was fortress, warehouse, and cultural meeting place.

## The Trade

The Bents built relationships, not just buildings. William Bent married Owl Woman, daughter of a Cheyenne chief, cementing an alliance that made the Cheyenne and Arapaho his trading partners rather than his enemies. The tribes brought buffalo robes - up to 15,000 annually - which the fort pressed into bales for transport to St. Louis. In return, the Bents traded manufactured goods: knives, cloth, beads, weapons. Mountain men like Kit Carson and Jim Bridger came to sell beaver pelts and resupply. Mexican merchants traveled from Santa Fe. The fort was the hub of a trading network spanning from Missouri to Mexico.

## The Collapse

The 1840s brought disaster. Charles Bent became governor of New Mexico and was killed in the Taos Revolt of 1847. The Mexican-American War turned the trading post into a military supply depot, disrupting the careful diplomatic balance. Cholera and smallpox swept through the Plains tribes, killing thousands of the Bents' trading partners. The buffalo began their long decline. By 1849, the trade was dying. William Bent tried to sell the fort to the Army; negotiations failed. In August 1849, Bent destroyed the fort. Some accounts say he exploded the powder magazine; others say he simply set it ablaze. He never explained why.

## The Reconstruction

Bent built a new, smaller post downstream (Bent's New Fort, now in ruins) and continued trading until his death in 1869. The old fort's adobe walls melted back into the prairie. For over a century, only foundations remained. Then, in the 1970s, the National Park Service undertook an ambitious reconstruction, rebuilding the fort based on archaeological evidence, historical descriptions, and paintings. The reconstructed fort, completed in 1976, is the only one of its kind - a full-scale recreation of an 1840s trading post, staffed by living history interpreters in period clothing.

## Visiting Bent's Old Fort

Bent's Old Fort National Historic Site is located 8 miles northeast of La Junta, Colorado, along the Arkansas River. The reconstructed fort is open year-round, with living history programs in summer. Interpreters in 1840s clothing demonstrate blacksmithing, cooking, and trading. The adobe architecture is impressive - the walls are cool inside even on hot days. The setting, along the cottonwood-lined river with the Rocky Mountains visible to the west, helps convey the fort's isolation. Special events include a fall encampment with mountain men and traders. Colorado Springs Airport (COS) is 120 miles west; La Junta has limited services. The site is remote; combine with visits to the Comanche National Grassland or Great Sand Dunes.

## Flight Context

Located at 38.04°N, 103.43°W along the Arkansas River in southeastern Colorado, 8 miles northeast of La Junta. From altitude, the reconstructed adobe fort is visible as a rectangular compound near the river, surrounded by cottonwood trees in otherwise open prairie. The Arkansas River, which the Santa Fe Trail followed, flows past. The Rocky Mountains are visible to the west. The isolation that made the fort necessary - the vast empty space between Missouri and Santa Fe - is evident from the air.
