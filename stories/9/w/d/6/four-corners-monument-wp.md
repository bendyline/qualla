# Four Corners Monument

The only point in the United States where four states meet, a granite-and-bronze marker on the Colorado Plateau where Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, and Utah converge.

**Location:** 36.9990, -109.0452

**Categories:** monuments, historical-sites, border-landmarks, indigenous-culture, colorado-plateau

## Introduction

Circling the brass disk embedded in the ground, two words appear in each quadrant: "Here meet in freedom under God four states." This is the Four Corners Monument, the sole point in the United States where four states share a single corner -- Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, and Utah. Visitors drive long distances across the Colorado Plateau to reach this remote spot, then drop into Twister-like poses, straddle state lines, or kiss directly over the marker while cameras click. It is a monument to something invisible: a line drawn on paper by 19th-century politicians and surveyors that became, through legal precedent and sheer stubbornness, as real and permanent as the sandstone beneath it.

## Lines Drawn in Washington

The story of Four Corners begins with the Compromise of 1850, which created the New Mexico and Utah Territories and set the border between them along the 37th parallel north. In 1861, Congress carved the Colorado Territory from Utah's eastern portion, keeping that same parallel as Colorado's southern boundary while establishing the 32nd meridian west from Washington as the dividing line between Colorado and Utah. Then came the Civil War. In 1860, discontented residents of southern New Mexico Territory declared secession and announced their intent to join the Confederacy as Arizona. The Confederate plan split New Mexico along an east-west line, the 34th parallel, creating a corridor from Texas to the Pacific. The Union responded in 1863 by splitting New Mexico along the existing north-south line instead -- the 32nd meridian west from Washington -- extending the Colorado-Utah border straight south. That decision created the quadripoint.

## Surveyors in the Desert

After the war, the job of turning congressional language into physical markers fell to a succession of surveyors working in harsh, remote terrain. In 1868, Ehud N. Darling surveyed and marked the Colorado-New Mexico border along the 37th parallel, creating what became known as the Darling Line. In 1875, Chandler Robbins worked his way north from the Mexican border, marking the Arizona-New Mexico boundary along the 32nd meridian west from Washington. Where his line intersected the Darling Line, Robbins erected a sandstone shaft -- the first physical marker at what is now Four Corners. Rollin J. Reeves arrived in 1878 to survey the Colorado-Utah border northward from that same shaft. Howard B. Carpenter completed the last segment, the Arizona-Utah line, in 1901. The sandstone shaft broke over the years, and in 1931 surveyor Everett Kimmell replaced it with a brass disc set in concrete.

## The Monument That Moved (But Didn't)

The primitive surveying tools of the 19th century introduced small errors, and the actual markers did not always land precisely on the intended meridians and parallels. This raised a question that lingered for decades: were the legal borders the theoretical geographic lines, or the physical markers on the ground? New Mexico sued Colorado in 1919, and in 1925 the Supreme Court ruled definitively that the markers placed by the original surveyors were the legal boundaries, even where they deviated from the intended coordinates. The issue flared again in 2009 when media reports claimed the monument sat miles from its correct location. The confusion stemmed from a simple error: reporters assumed the surveys had used the Prime Meridian, when in fact the 19th-century surveyors had referenced the Washington meridian. The U.S. National Geodetic Survey confirmed the monument is roughly where it should be and, regardless, the 1875 survey was accepted by all four states as legally binding.

## Where Four States Meet

Today the monument sits on Navajo Nation land, maintained by the Navajo Parks and Recreation Department as a tribal park. The current marker, installed in 1992 and set in granite, is a disc-shaped aluminum-bronze plate surrounded by the seals and flags of the four states and the tribal nations of the area. Local Navajo and Ute artisans sell jewelry, pottery, and fry bread from stalls surrounding the monument. The site also marks the boundary between two semi-autonomous Native American governments: the Navajo Nation and the Ute Mountain Ute Tribe Reservation. Despite its isolation on the high desert of the Colorado Plateau -- accessed via State Road 597 southwest of Cortez, Colorado -- the monument draws steady crowds, a testament to the peculiar power of an abstract idea made tangible: the place where four invisible lines cross, and where a single step can carry you from one state to another.

## Flight Context

Four Corners Monument is located at 36.999N, 109.045W on the Colorado Plateau. From the air, look for the straight-line state borders visible as fenceline contrasts and road patterns converging at a single point. The monument pad is small but the parking area and vendor stalls are identifiable at lower altitudes. Best viewed at 2,000-4,000 ft AGL. Nearest airports: Cortez Municipal Airport (KCEZ), approximately 40 nm to the northeast, and Farmington Four Corners Regional Airport (KFMN), approximately 50 nm to the southeast. The terrain is high desert at roughly 5,000 ft MSL with generally clear visibility.
