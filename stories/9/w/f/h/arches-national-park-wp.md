# Arches National Park

More than 2,000 natural sandstone arches crowd into a single stretch of Utah desert, formed over 300 million years as an ancient salt bed buckled, cracked, and dissolved the stone above it into impossible shapes.

**Location:** 38.7281, -109.5400

**Categories:** national-park, geology, desert, utah, sandstone, natural-arch

## Introduction

Wall Arch collapsed sometime during the night of August 4, 2008. Park rangers arrived at dawn to find a pile of rubble where a stone span had stood along the popular Devils Garden Trail. No one was hurt - no one was even watching. That quiet demolition captures something essential about Arches National Park: this landscape is still under construction, and always has been. The same forces of water, wind, frost, and gravity that carved more than 2,000 natural sandstone arches into the desert north of Moab, Utah, are also steadily dismantling them. New arches are forming in the fins of Entrada Sandstone right now, invisibly slow. The park is a snapshot of a 300-million-year geological process, caught at a particularly photogenic moment.

## Three Hundred Million Years of Salt

The story begins with a sea. Around 300 million years ago, an inland ocean flowed into what is now the Paradox Basin of the Colorado Plateau, then evaporated, leaving behind a salt bed thousands of feet thick. Over the eons, debris from the Uncompahgre Uplift to the northeast buried the salt under layers of sediment. During the Jurassic period, about 200 million years ago, desert conditions deposited the Navajo Sandstone. Then came the Entrada Sandstone, roughly 140 million years ago - the salmon-colored rock that forms most of the park's arches today.

The weight of all that overburden caused the salt to behave like a liquid, pushing upward into domes and long ridges called anticlines. As the salt shifted, the rock above it cracked into parallel vertical slabs - the fins visible throughout the park. Rain, slightly acidic from dissolved carbon dioxide, seeped into the fins and dissolved the calcium carbonate cement holding the sandstone grains together. Weaker layers gave way first, opening holes that gravity and frost gradually widened into arches. The process is elegant and relentless: the same chemistry that builds the arches will eventually destroy them.

## The Prospector's Discovery

Alexander Ringhoffer, a Hungarian-born prospector living in Salt Valley, wrote to the Denver and Rio Grande Western Railroad in 1922 to alert them to the tourist potential of an area he had explored with his sons and son-in-law. He called it the Devils Garden. In September 1923, railroad passenger traffic manager Frank A. Wadleigh and photographer George L. Beam visited at Ringhoffer's invitation and were stunned by what they saw.

Wadleigh urged National Park Service director Stephen T. Mather to designate the area a national monument. A succession of government investigators examined the site, but confusion over the precise location led to a geographic mix-up: the name Devils Garden was accidentally transposed to a different area on the opposite side of Salt Valley, which includes Landscape Arch. Ringhoffer's original discovery was left out entirely. In April 1929, President Herbert Hoover signed the proclamation creating Arches National Monument. President Franklin D. Roosevelt enlarged it in 1938, and President Richard Nixon signed the legislation redesignating it a national park in 1971. Arches was formally dedicated in May 1972.

## A Catalog of Stone

The park holds the highest density of natural arches anywhere on Earth - more than 2,000 documented spans. Delicate Arch, a freestanding crescent of salmon-colored sandstone framing the La Sal Mountains, has become the symbol of Utah itself, appearing on the state's license plates and a U.S. quarter minted in 2014 with a production run exceeding 465 million coins.

Landscape Arch, in the Devils Garden, stretches more than 300 feet - the longest in the park and among the longest in the world. It is now so thin that chunks of rock have fallen from its underside in recent decades, and the trail beneath it has been permanently closed. Balanced Rock stands like a boulder balanced on a pedestal, roughly the size of three school buses. The Fiery Furnace is a maze of narrow sandstone passages and towering columns that requires a ranger-led permit to enter. Double Arch, the Three Gossips, the Tower of Babel, Dark Angel - the names alone suggest how deeply these formations have stirred human imagination.

## Walking on Living Ground

The dark, knobby crust underfoot in Arches is not dirt. It is a biological soil crust - a living community of cyanobacteria, lichens, mosses, green algae, and microfungi that binds the desert soil together. This crust absorbs and stores water in a landscape that receives less than 10 inches of rain per year, enabling the growth of plants like Utah juniper, pinyon pine, blackbrush, and Mormon tea. A single footstep can destroy decades of biological growth.

The park's sensitivity to compressional damage is well documented: the semiarid climate, unpredictable rainfall, lack of deep freezing, and absence of leaf litter all contribute to soils with low resistance to disturbance and extremely slow recovery. Desert bighorn sheep, mule deer, peregrine falcons, kangaroo rats, and midget faded rattlesnakes share the landscape with visitors - 1.8 million of them in 2021. Since 2022, timed entry reservations have been required during peak hours from April through October to manage the crowds.

## The Solitary Ranger

Edward Abbey worked as a park ranger at Arches National Monument in 1956 and 1957, before it became a national park, before the paved roads, before the parking lots filled at dawn. He kept journals during those seasons of solitude that became Desert Solitaire, a book that helped launch the modern environmental movement and drew generations of hikers, mountain bikers, and canyoneers to the red rock desert around Moab.

Abbey would barely recognize the park today. Timed entry tickets, commercial tour buses, and the Hayduke Trail - a long-distance backpacking route named after one of his fictional characters - now define the visitor experience. Yet the arches themselves remain indifferent to human attention. They were forming before humans existed, and they will continue forming long after the parking lots crack and return to sand. In 1980, vandals used kitchen cleanser to deface ancient Ute petroglyphs along the Delicate Arch trail; physicist John F. Asmus repaired the damage with laser pulses. The rock endured. It always does - until, like Wall Arch, it decides not to.

## Flight Context

Arches National Park is located at 38.7281N, 109.5400W in eastern Utah, on the Colorado Plateau north of Moab. The park's high desert terrain ranges from about 4,085 feet at the visitor center to 5,653 feet at Elephant Butte. The distinctive red sandstone formations - fins, arches, and balanced rocks - are visible from altitude, with the Windows section and Devils Garden being the most prominent clusters. The Colorado River runs along the park's southern boundary. Nearest airport: Canyonlands Field (KCNY) approximately 16 nm north of Moab. Grand Junction Regional (KGJT) in Colorado is about 75 nm to the northeast. The La Sal Mountains (12,780 feet) rise to the southeast and serve as a dramatic backdrop. Expect clear skies with excellent visibility most of the year; afternoon thunderstorms are common in summer months. Density altitude can be significant in summer heat.
