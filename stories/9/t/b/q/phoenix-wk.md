# Phoenix: The Desert City That Shouldn't Exist

The city that rose where ancient Hohokam canals once irrigated the desert, now America's fifth-largest metropolis where summer highs regularly exceed 110°F and air conditioning is not optional.

**Location:** 33.4484, -112.0740

**Categories:** arizona, phoenix, city, desert, climate

## Introduction

Phoenix exists because of a system of canals built by the Hohokam people between 300 BCE and 1450 CE - over 500 miles of irrigation channels that allowed agriculture in the Sonoran Desert. When those canals were rediscovered and rebuilt in the 1860s, a new city arose from the same foundations: Phoenix, named for the mythical bird that rises from its own ashes. The metaphor was apt. What the Hohokam created and abandoned, Americans rebuilt and expanded into America's fifth-largest city. Whether the modern Phoenix is sustainable remains an open question. Summer temperatures exceed 115°F (46°C); the city consumes vast quantities of Colorado River water in an era of permanent drought. Phoenix is an experiment in desert urbanization, and the results are not yet in.

## The Hohokam

The Hohokam people occupied the Salt River Valley for over 1,700 years, developing one of the most sophisticated irrigation systems in the pre-Columbian Americas. Their canals, some 75 feet wide and 50 feet deep, diverted river water to fields growing corn, beans, and squash. By 1300 CE, the Hohokam population may have reached 80,000 - a city-sized society in the desert. Then they vanished. By 1450, the canals were abandoned, the villages empty. Drought, soil salinization, flooding, or social collapse - historians debate the cause. The O'odham people who remained call their predecessors 'Huhugam' - 'those who are gone.' Modern Phoenix literally built on Hohokam foundations; many existing canals follow their ancient courses.

## Air Conditioning Changed Everything

Before air conditioning, Phoenix was a small desert outpost: 11,000 people in 1920, 65,000 in 1940. Willis Carrier's invention existed, but it was expensive and commercial. After World War II, affordable residential air conditioning transformed the Sun Belt. Phoenix exploded: 439,000 by 1960, over 1.6 million today. The city couldn't function without mechanical cooling - summer temperatures routinely exceed 110°F, sometimes reaching 120°F. Power consumption peaks on the hottest days as every building runs AC continuously. Climate change is making Phoenix hotter; the urban heat island effect adds degrees atop regional warming. The city that air conditioning built now faces the question of whether it can survive in an era of rising temperatures and stressed power grids.

## Water in the Desert

Phoenix receives about 8 inches of rain annually - barely more than Death Valley. The city survives on imported water: the Salt River Project's system of dams and reservoirs, the Central Arizona Project canal bringing Colorado River water 336 miles from Lake Havasu. But the Colorado River is over-allocated; Lake Mead, the nation's largest reservoir, has dropped to crisis levels. Arizona has mandatory water cutbacks; Phoenix conserves more aggressively than most desert cities. The green lawns that once characterized Phoenix are disappearing, replaced by xeriscaping - desert landscaping that requires minimal irrigation. Whether the water supply can sustain continued growth remains the existential question for a city of 4.9 million in the Sonoran Desert.

## Urban Heat Island

Phoenix is the hottest large city in America, and it keeps getting hotter. The urban heat island effect - pavement and buildings absorbing and radiating heat - adds up to 12°F to nighttime temperatures. A night that might cool to 80°F in the surrounding desert stays above 95°F downtown. Heat kills: Phoenix recorded over 400 heat-related deaths in 2022. The city has responded with cooling centers, shade tree programs, and cool-surface coatings for pavement. But the fundamental problem persists: Phoenix built a sprawling, low-density metropolis in a desert, covering natural cooling surfaces with asphalt and concrete. Adapting to a hotter future requires rethinking how the city itself is designed.

## Valley of the Sun

Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport (PHX) lies minutes from downtown, one of America's busiest airports. The Valley of the Sun sprawls across 517 square miles, connected by freeways and light rail. Scottsdale offers resorts and Old West atmosphere; Tempe contains Arizona State University; Mesa is the state's third-largest city. Camelback Mountain rises in the city's heart, a popular hiking destination. The Heard Museum showcases Native American art and culture. The Desert Botanical Garden displays Sonoran Desert plant life. From altitude, Phoenix appears as endless urban development spreading across a desert basin, the mountains rising abruptly from the valley floor - the city that air conditioning built, that water sustains, and that heat defines.
