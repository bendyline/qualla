# Battle of Hembrillo Basin: Victorio's Last Stand in the San Andres Mountains

In April 1880, Apache war chief Victorio held off 400 American soldiers and tribal scouts with brilliant defensive tactics, protected his people, and escaped -- the largest battle of Victorio's War fought in a basin that became sacred ground.

**Location:** 32.9240, -106.6450

**Categories:** battlefield, apache-wars, 19th-century-history, new-mexico, native-american-history

## Introduction

The Basin held water. In the Chihuahuan Desert, water meant survival, and for centuries the springs of Hembrillo Basin had drawn Native Americans to the western slopes of the San Andres Mountains. Rock art on the canyon walls testified to generations who had camped there, prayed there, survived there. In April 1880, Apache war chief Victorio brought his followers to this ancient sanctuary with the U.S. Army closing in from every direction. What followed was the largest battle of Victorio's War -- four days of fighting across ridgelines and around springs, with Buffalo Soldiers, Apache scouts, and Mescalero warriors locked in a deadly chess match. Victorio held, his people escaped, and months later he would die in Mexico. The battlefield now lies within White Sands Missile Range, visited only by archaeologists and military officers studying small-unit tactics.

## A Chief on the Run

Victorio was a Mimbres Chiricahua Apache chief, a veteran warrior who had fought Americans and Mexicans for decades. In August 1879, facing forced relocation to the hated San Carlos reservation in Arizona, he fled the Mescalero Reservation with roughly 80 warriors and their families. Other Apaches joined him -- Mescaleros, Chiricahuas, those who refused to surrender their homeland. The U.S. Army pursued with thousands of troops across southern New Mexico, into Texas, and across the Mexican border. Skirmishes and battles proved indecisive; Victorio always slipped away. In March 1880, Pueblo scouts reported that Victorio had taken refuge in Hembrillo Basin. Colonel Edward Hatch planned to surround him with four separate forces attacking from north, south, east, and west. The assault was set for April 8. Victorio would have nowhere to run.

## The Plan Falls Apart

Military plans rarely survive contact with the desert. Major Morrow's northern force ran out of water and never reached the battlefield. Unaware of this failure, Captain Carroll and his Buffalo Soldiers of the 9th Cavalry advanced up the Hembrillo Valley from the west on April 5 -- three days early. Lieutenant John Conline led 29 soldiers ahead as scouts and stumbled into 35 to 50 Apaches advancing on his position. A firefight erupted at 5:30 in the afternoon. Two soldiers fell wounded. One of Conline's scouts recognized Victorio personally leading the attack. When darkness came, Conline pulled back. Carroll, worried the Apaches would escape, pushed into the basin the next day with 71 men. He captured a small ridge, found himself surrounded, and spent a waterless night cut off from the only spring while Apaches held the high ground in every direction.

## Buffalo Soldiers and Apache Scouts

Reinforcements saved Carroll. On the morning of April 7, Captain McClellan arrived with 85 soldiers, 40 tribal scouts, and 100 White Mountain Apache -- enemies of the Chiricahua who had joined the Army for their own reasons. Now facing nearly 400 soldiers and scouts, Victorio began his masterstroke. He pulled his warriors to a high ridge south of the battlefield and held the Americans at bay while Apache women and children fled to safety. Lieutenant Charles Gatewood led the Apache scouts in a flank attack on the encampment, but they found only abandoned shelters. Victorio withdrew his men in stages, leap-frogging from ridgetop to ridgetop, a rear guard watching until the last moment. On April 8, after a brief final skirmish, they vanished into the desert. The great majority of American forces engaged that day were either African-American Buffalo Soldiers or tribal scouts -- a fact that white officers' accounts later downplayed.

## Forensic Archaeology

In 1988, archaeologists surveyed the Hembrillo Basin battlefield for the U.S. military. Over a decade of work, they recovered more than 800 spent cartridges, tagged each one with GPS, and conducted forensic analysis. The cartridges came from 147 unique rifles and carbines, plus 39 separate pistols. But the patterns revealed something remarkable: Victorio had exercised precise tactical control over his warriors. Men armed with short-range Winchester repeating rifles held the springs to deny water to the Army. Warriors with longer-range weapons occupied ridgetops and kept attackers at distances where their firepower advantage meant nothing. 'Victorio's Apache were doing what they did best,' the archaeologists concluded, 'fighting a defensive battle with a mountain at their back.' Today, officers from White Sands Missile Range and Fort Bliss study the battlefield to learn small-unit tactics against overwhelming odds. Cartridge cases still surface in the 21st century.

## The Aftermath

Victorio escaped west and was not seen again until April 28, when his warriors killed 41 civilians in the Alma Massacre. In May he attacked Fort Tularosa. Meanwhile, Colonel Hatch ordered 1,000 soldiers to converge on the Mescalero Reservation near Fort Stanton, convinced that reservation residents were supplying Victorio with men and material. The 400 Mescaleros fled in fear of forced removal. Fourteen men were killed in the roundup. The captured were initially held in a horse stable with manure six inches deep on the floor. Some escaped to Mexico or joined Victorio. The chief himself would die that October at the hands of Mexican forces at Tres Castillos, Chihuahua -- betrayed, some say, by Tarahumara scouts. Hembrillo Basin became military property, folded into White Sands Missile Range, preserved by accident as a battlefield where Victorio won his last great victory.

## Flight Context

Located at 32.92N, 106.64W in the San Andres Mountains of southcentral New Mexico. The battlefield lies within White Sands Missile Range restricted airspace -- check NOTAMs and do not enter without authorization. From authorized altitudes outside the restricted zone, the San Andres Mountains appear as a rugged north-south desert range rising from the Tularosa Basin. Hembrillo Basin is a natural amphitheater on the western slopes, not readily visible from altitude. The white gypsum dunes of White Sands National Park are visible to the east. Alamogordo-White Sands Regional Airport (KALM) lies roughly 30 miles east. The basin's springs, which made it strategically vital in 1880, would not be visible from flight altitude.
