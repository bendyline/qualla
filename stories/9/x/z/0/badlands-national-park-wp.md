# Badlands National Park

Sacred Lakota land, World War II bombing range, and one of the world's richest fossil beds share the same eroded moonscape in South Dakota.

**Location:** 43.7500, -102.5000

**Categories:** National Parks, Paleontology, Native American History, Military History

## Introduction

In 1980, the Supreme Court awarded the Sioux $105 million for the illegal seizure of the Black Hills. The Sioux refused the money. They wanted the land back, including the striped badlands where their ancestors hunted for eleven thousand years and where, in the desperate winter of 1890, ghost dancers gathered on Stronghold Table believing they could bring back the buffalo and their dead. The money sits in a trust, accruing interest, unclaimed.

## The Bones Remember

The Lakota found massive fossilized bones in these eroding hills long before European science arrived. They concluded correctly that the bones belonged to creatures no longer alive, that this harsh terrain had once been underwater. When trappers began carrying fossils back from their routes between Fort Pierre and Fort Laramie in the 1840s, a scientific gold rush followed. A jaw fragment reached a St. Louis physician named Hiram Prout, who published his findings in 1846. By 1854, scientists had identified 84 distinct fossil species in North America, and 77 of them came from the White River Badlands. Yale's O.C. Marsh refined extraction techniques here in 1870. The South Dakota School of Mines has sent expeditions almost every year since 1899. These hills contain the richest Oligocene mammal deposits known, a window into life 33 million years ago.

## The Ghost Dance Winter

By the fall of 1890, thousands of Native Americans had become followers of the prophet Wovoka, who taught that dancing the Ghost Dance while wearing sacred shirts impervious to bullets would cause the white man to vanish and restore the hunting grounds. On Stronghold Table in what is now the park's South Unit, one of the last Ghost Dances took place. As winter closed in, the dancers returned to Pine Ridge Agency. A band of Minneconjou Sioux, led by Chief Spotted Elk, crossed through the Badlands Wall heading south, pursued by the Army. They were overtaken near Wounded Knee Creek. When soldiers attempted to disarm them, shooting erupted. Nearly three hundred Lakota and thirty soldiers died. It was the last major armed conflict between Plains tribes and the U.S. military until the 1973 standoff at the same location.

## Bombs Over Sacred Ground

During World War II, the Army Air Force claimed over 341,000 acres of Pine Ridge Reservation for a gunnery range, including portions of what was then Badlands National Monument. Old car bodies and yellow-painted 55-gallon drums served as targets. Bulls-eyes a quarter-mile across were plowed into the prairie for bombardiers. From 1942 to 1945, pilots from Ellsworth Air Force Base practiced air-to-air and air-to-ground combat overhead. Among the 125 families forcibly relocated was Dewey Beard, a survivor of the Wounded Knee Massacre. Those who remained nearby recall diving under tractors to escape stray bombs. In Interior, shells crashed through the roofs of a church and the post office. At least a dozen flight crew died in crashes. Unexploded ordnance still litters the Stronghold District.

## The Park Takes Shape

President Calvin Coolidge authorized Badlands National Monument on his final day in office, March 4, 1929. A decade of land acquisition and highway construction followed before President Franklin Roosevelt signed the proclamation establishing the park on January 24, 1939. The timing was deliberate: the same ten-year period saw Mount Rushmore blasted and sculpted, part of a federal drive to build tourism in western South Dakota. Badlands became a national park in 1978. Today, more than 1,200 bison graze an expanding habitat. The black-footed ferret population has recovered to approximately 100 individuals after near-extinction. The Ben Reifel Visitor Center, built under Mission 66 in 1957-58, welcomes visitors to a landscape that looks like another world but holds very earthly stories of broken promises, sacred ceremonies, and creatures that lived and died here thirty million years before humans arrived.

## Flight Context

Badlands National Park lies at 43.75N, 102.50W, roughly 50nm southeast of Rapid City Regional Airport (KRAP). The jagged badlands wall is unmistakable from the air, pale striped sediments cutting across green prairie like a geological scar. The North Unit contains most visitor facilities; the South Unit (Stronghold District) on Pine Ridge Reservation is largely undeveloped. Red Shirt Table marks the park's highest point. Interstate 90 runs along the north edge, with exits at Wall and Interior. Minuteman Missile National Historic Site, also administered by this park, is nearby. Best viewed at 2,000-5,000 feet AGL.
