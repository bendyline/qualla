# Grand Teton National Park

Home to some of the oldest rocks in any American national park and the youngest mountain range in the Rockies, where 2.7-billion-year-old granite towers above a valley still settling into its fault.

**Location:** 43.8200, -110.7000

**Categories:** National Parks, Rocky Mountains, Wyoming, Geology, Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem

## Introduction

The rocks beneath Grand Teton are nearly 2.7 billion years old, among the most ancient found in any American national park. Yet the mountains themselves are the youngest in the Rocky Mountain chain, having begun forming only six to nine million years ago. This paradox defines Grand Teton National Park: ancient materials thrust skyward by recent forces, creating a landscape where deep time meets geological youth. The Teton Range rises from the floor of Jackson Hole without foothills, a wall of peaks climbing more than 7,000 feet above the valley along an active fault that has produced magnitude 7.5 earthquakes since it formed.

## A Fault-Block Wall

The Teton Range runs roughly north to south for about forty miles along a fault line. The valley of Jackson Hole is a graben, a block of earth that has dropped between parallel faults, while the mountain block has risen. The vertical displacement between the two exceeds 30,000 feet, though erosion and sediment have filled the valley so the visible relief measures closer to 7,000 feet. Grand Teton itself reaches 13,775 feet, the highest point in the range. Eight more peaks exceed 12,000 feet, clustered in the Cathedral Group between Avalanche and Cascade Canyons. Mount Moran rises 12,605 feet to the north, its flat summit still capped by Flathead Formation sandstone, a remnant of sedimentary layers that once covered the entire range.

## Deep Time Made Visible

By the close of the Precambrian era, this region was intermittently submerged under shallow seas, and for 500 million years various sedimentary rocks accumulated. Most have since eroded from the central peaks, but they remain visible on the northern, southern, and western flanks. During the late Cretaceous period, volcanic ash from an arc to the west deposited fine-grained material that became bentonite. Conglomerate rocks containing quartzite, mudstone, and sandstones testify to a now-vanished mountain range that once stood northwest of the current Tetons. These deposits hold trace quantities of gold and mercury, though no mining efforts ever proved economically viable.

## Glaciers in Recession

A dozen small glaciers persist at higher elevations, though all are in recession. The largest is Teton Glacier, which sits below the northeast face of Grand Teton, measuring about 3,500 feet long and 1,100 feet wide. This heavy glaciation carved the U-shaped valleys that slice through the range: Webb, Moran, Paintbrush, Cascade, Death, and Granite Canyons. The glaciers also scooped out the lakes at the mountains' base. Jackson Lake stretches fifteen miles long, six and a half miles wide, and 438 feet deep at its maximum. Jenny Lake and Leigh Lake fill similar glacial basins. Lake Solitude sits at 9,035 feet in a cirque at the head of Cascade Canyon's north fork. Some alpine lakes, like Icefloe Lake, remain ice-clogged for much of the year.

## Creatures of the Greater Yellowstone

More than 1,000 species of vascular plants grow here, along with dozens of mammal species, over 300 bird species, more than a dozen fish species, and several reptiles and amphibians. The reestablishment of wolves has ensured every indigenous mammal species now exists within the park. Grizzly bears and the more common black bears roam the backcountry. Elk migrate between the National Elk Refuge and Yellowstone, passing through the park in spring and fall. Pronghorn, the fastest land mammal in the Western Hemisphere, graze the valley floor. The Snake River fine-spotted cutthroat trout is found only in these waters and tributaries below Jackson Lake Dam, making Grand Teton one of the few places to catch this subspecies.

## Where Human History Meets Wilderness

Human presence here dates back at least 11,000 years. Paleo-Indians followed migratory patterns similar to those of modern elk, spending summers in Jackson Hole and wintering in valleys west of the Tetons. The Shoshone people had a close spiritual relationship with the range; stone enclosures on several peaks, including one near the summit of Grand Teton known as The Enclosure, are thought to have been used for vision quests. French-Canadian trappers named the peaks in the early nineteenth century. Today the park draws an average of 2.75 million visitors annually, with 4,000 climbers per year attempting Grand Teton's summit. The park permits elk hunting to manage populations, a compromise written into the 1950 legislation that combined Jackson Hole National Monument with the original park.

## Flight Context

Grand Teton National Park is located at approximately 43.82N, 110.70W in northwestern Wyoming. The park covers about 310,000 acres and is bordered by Yellowstone National Park to the north via the John D. Rockefeller Jr. Memorial Parkway. Jackson Hole Airport (KJAC) is the only commercial airport within a U.S. national park, located at 6,451 feet MSL with strict noise abatement procedures. The Teton Range rises without foothills from the Jackson Hole valley floor, making the peaks dramatically visible from the east. Best viewed at 10,000-14,000 feet MSL. The Snake River winds through the valley, and glacial lakes including Jackson Lake are prominent visual landmarks. Salt Lake City (KSLC) is 280 miles south, Denver (KDEN) is 475 miles southeast.
