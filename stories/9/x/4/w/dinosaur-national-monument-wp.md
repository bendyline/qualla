# Dinosaur National Monument

Where the Morrison Formation meets the sky at a 67-degree angle, thousands of Jurassic fossils remain embedded exactly where ancient rivers deposited them 150 million years ago.

**Location:** 40.5333, -108.9833

**Categories:** national-monument, paleontology, geology, dark-sky-park, river-canyon

## Introduction

On August 17, 1909, Earl Douglass of the Carnegie Museum spotted eight vertebrae of an Apatosaurus protruding from a sandstone ridge in northeastern Utah. He had come looking for mammal fossils but found something far more spectacular: one of the richest dinosaur quarries ever discovered. The bones lay in the Morrison Formation, a river deposit from the Jurassic Period, now tilted to a steep 67 degrees by the same forces that built the Uinta Mountains. Douglass spent the rest of his career here, though he died preferring his beloved mammal fossils to the dinosaurs that made him famous.

## A 150-Million-Year Graveyard

The Morrison Formation tells the story of an ancient river system that carried dinosaur carcasses to their final resting place during the Jurassic Period, roughly 150 million years ago. Bodies accumulated on sandbars, bones piled upon bones, and sediment slowly buried them. Millennia compressed these remains into rock. When the Uinta Mountains rose, they tilted the formation to its current steep angle, and erosion has been revealing specimens ever since. The diversity is remarkable: predators like Allosaurus and Ceratosaurus, towering sauropods including Apatosaurus, Diplodocus, and Camarasaurus, plated Stegosaurus, and smaller ornithopods like Camptosaurus and Dryosaurus. Several species were first described from specimens found here.

## Building the Cathedral of Bones

The quarry's story is one of persistence across decades. Douglass and his Carnegie Museum crews excavated from 1909 until work ceased in 1922, shipping thousands of fossils to Pittsburgh. President Woodrow Wilson proclaimed the site a national monument in 1915, initially protecting just the quarry area. The Depression brought new workers through the Civil Works Administration and Works Progress Administration, who built roads and removed overburden. World War II interrupted, but the 1950s brought the Mission 66 initiative and an architecturally daring quarry building that opened in 1958. Its spiral ramp and glass walls let visitors watch technicians reveal fossils in the rock. The design proved controversial, and the building's placement on unstable clay eventually forced closure in 2006.

## Canyon Country Preserved

The monument expanded dramatically in 1938, growing from a small area around the quarry to encompass the river canyons of the Green and Yampa across both Utah and Colorado. This expansion came just in time. The 1950s brought proposals for Echo Park Dam, which would have flooded these wild canyons. Conservationists mounted a fierce campaign, and the Colorado River Storage Project Act of 1956 settled the matter: no dam or reservoir constructed under the act would be built within any National Park or Monument. The victory helped define modern wilderness preservation and saved canyons that the Fremont people had called home from 200 to 1300 AD.

## Dark Skies and Deep Time

In 2019, Dinosaur National Monument earned designation as an International Dark Sky Park, recognizing what the high desert has always offered: an unobstructed view of the universe. The same remoteness that makes the clay roads impassable when wet keeps artificial light at bay. The Fremont left their petroglyphs on these canyon walls centuries ago, looking up at the same stars. The dinosaurs that died in Jurassic rivers never saw stars at all, yet their bones now rest beneath some of the darkest skies in the American West. The contrast captures something essential about this place: deep time made visible in rock, recent time recorded in art, and cosmic time stretching overhead.

## The Wall Restored

When the quarry building closed in 2006, its structural problems seemed intractable. Built on unstable clay, the foundation had shifted since opening day in 1958. The Obama administration's stimulus plan provided $13.1 million in 2009 for a solution. Engineers drove 70-foot steel micropile columns through the clay to reach bedrock, creating a stable foundation that the original builders never achieved. The Dinosaur Quarry reopened in fall 2011 with its Mission 66-era exhibit hall preserved. Visitors can once again stand before the Wall of Bones, watching technicians carefully expose fossils that have waited 150 million years. The bones remain tilted at 67 degrees, exactly as the Uinta Mountains left them.

## Flight Context

Located at 40.53N, 108.98W where the Green and Yampa Rivers converge on the Utah-Colorado border. The monument spans from Moffat County, Colorado to Uintah County, Utah. Look for the Uinta Mountains to the north and the sinuous river canyons cutting through desert terrain. The quarry area is near Jensen, Utah. Nearest airports: Vernal Regional Airport (VEL) approximately 20 miles southwest; Dinosaur, Colorado has a small airstrip. Best viewing: 8,000-10,000 feet AGL. Watch for afternoon thunderstorms in summer and mountain weather from the Uintas.
