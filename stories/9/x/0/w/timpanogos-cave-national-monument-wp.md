# Timpanogos Cave National Monument

A 340-million-year-old limestone cave system whose formations began growing only 750,000 years ago, shaped by faults radiating from the Wasatch.

**Location:** 40.4406, -111.7094

**Categories:** national-monument, cave, geology, utah, wasatch-range

## Introduction

The cave was already ancient when the first formations began to grow. The Deseret Limestone that cradles Timpanogos Cave formed 340 million years ago, during the Mississippian age when shallow seas covered Utah. But the cavities themselves are younger, carved by faults branching off the great Wasatch Fault that defines Utah's mountain front. Hydrothermal waters, superheated and mineral-rich, hollowed out the passages. Then, within the last 750,000 years, as the Wasatch Range slowly rose above the unnamed stream that flows through American Fork Canyon, the speleothems began their patient work of decoration.

## The Duke Onyx Company's Damage

When Martin Hansen discovered the first cave in October 1887, he had been tracking cougar footprints high up the canyon wall. The formations he found would not remain intact for long. The Duke Onyx Company arrived, along with the general public, and began removing pieces of the cave. Stalactites were broken off. Flowstone was chipped away. By the time the cave gained federal protection, Hansen Cave had been stripped of much of its original beauty. The lesson would not be lost on those who discovered the remaining chambers. When Vearl James Manwill rediscovered the second cave on August 14, 1921, he wrote in his journal that same night about finding ways to preserve its beauty for posterity instead of allowing it to be vandalized as Hansen's Cave had been.

## Three Caves Become One

The Works Progress Administration connected the separated chambers in the 1930s, blasting tunnels through solid rock to link Hansen Cave, Middle Cave, and Timpanogos Cave into a single tour route. Today visitors enter through a man-made portal near Hansen's original discovery, pass through the WPA tunnels into Middle Cave, continue to Timpanogos Cave and its famous Great Heart formation, then exit through another constructed passage. The journey takes visitors past cave bacon, flowstone, cave popcorn, drapery formations, and the twisted helictites that defy gravity through capillary action. The temperature holds steady at 46 degrees Fahrenheit throughout, regardless of the season raging outside.

## A Visitor Center Rises from Ashes

The monument's original visitor center burned in 1991. For nearly three decades afterward, staff worked from a double-wide trailer perched in a dangerous rockfall zone. The temporary became permanent until September 2018, when construction finally began on a new facility. The monument closed for the season, workers broke ground, and by June 2019 visitors returned to a proper trailhead and visitor center. The new construction reflected changing priorities: the National Park Service now limits tour group sizes, controls lighting to prevent invasive organism growth, and constantly monitors the delicate balance between public access and cave preservation.

## Written in Iron

Scientists determined the age of Timpanogos Cave's formations through an unexpected method: paleomagnetism. Iron deposits within the speleothems recorded the orientation of Earth's magnetic field at the time they formed. By matching these magnetic signatures to the known record of polar reversals, researchers established that the cave decorations began growing within the last 750,000 years. This makes the formations surprisingly young compared to the 340-million-year-old rock that contains them. The cave system continues to rise, lifted by the ongoing uplift of the Wasatch Range, gradually separating from the waters that once shaped its passages.

## Flight Context

Located at 40.44N, 111.71W in American Fork Canyon, Wasatch Range, Utah. The cave system sits in the Deseret Limestone formation on the canyon's north wall. Elevation at the visitor center is approximately 5,600 feet; cave entrance at 6,730 feet. Best viewed from 8,000-10,000 feet AGL. Approximately 40 miles south of Salt Lake City International (KSLC). The 1.5-mile trail climbing to the cave entrance is visible as a lighter line against the dark canyon wall. Highland, Utah lies to the west at the canyon mouth.
