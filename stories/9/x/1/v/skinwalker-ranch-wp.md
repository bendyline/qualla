# Skinwalker Ranch

A 512-acre Utah ranch bordering Ute tribal lands has drawn UFO researchers, billionaire investors, and Defense Department funding despite decades of investigation yielding no conclusive evidence.

## Introduction

The name comes from Navajo legend: the skin-walker, a malevolent witch capable of transforming into animals. What brought this designation to a 512-acre cattle ranch in the remote Uintah Basin of northeastern Utah is a question that has consumed investigators, skeptics, and the curious for decades. Reports of strange lights, mutilated cattle, and inexplicable phenomena on this property date back to the 1970s, drawing everyone from tabloid journalists to Defense Intelligence Agency officials. The ranch has changed hands for prices ranging from $200,000 to $500,000, attracted $22 million in federal research funding, and spawned books, documentaries, and a History Channel series - all without producing a single piece of evidence that scientists can verify.

## The Sherman Family's Strange Years

Terry and Gwen Sherman purchased the ranch in 1994, attracted by its isolation and potential for cattle ranching. What they claimed to experience over the next two years would transform a quiet property into America's most famous paranormal hotspot. According to their accounts, cattle vanished without explanation or turned up mutilated in ways that defied conventional predators. They reported seeing large animals with glowing red eyes that appeared impervious to bullets. Strange lights moved through the night sky. Unusual impressions appeared in the grass and soil. By 1996, the Shermans had had enough. They sold the property for $200,000 to a buyer who was specifically interested in their strange stories.

## A Billionaire's Scientific Quest

That buyer was Robert Bigelow, a Las Vegas real estate mogul who had founded the National Institute for Discovery Science to investigate paranormal claims using rigorous methodology. Bigelow deployed researchers and surveillance equipment across the property, hoping to capture hard evidence of the phenomena the Shermans described. Over the years, investigators documented close to 100 incidents - UFO sightings, orbs of light, invisible objects allegedly emitting magnetic fields. Retired Army Colonel John B. Alexander, who participated in the research, described it as an attempt to gather data using 'a standard scientific approach.' Yet the team acknowledged a persistent problem: they had 'difficulty obtaining evidence consistent with scientific publication.'

## From Ranch to Pentagon Line Item

The ranch's influence extended beyond paranormal circles in an unexpected way. In 2005, journalist George Knapp and researcher Colm Kelleher published 'Hunt for the Skinwalker,' documenting Bigelow's investigations. That book caught the attention of James Lacatski, a Defense Intelligence Agency official who arranged to visit the ranch and reportedly had his own unusual experience there. Lacatski conveyed his impressions to Bigelow, who shared them with his friend, Nevada Senator Harry Reid. The result was remarkable: Reid and Alaska Senator Ted Stevens, himself a UFO experiencer, inserted language into the Defense Department budget appropriating $22 million to study unidentified aerial phenomena. A remote Utah ranch had become federal policy.

## The Skeptics' Counter-Narrative

Not everyone accepts the ranch's reputation. Skeptical author Robert Sheaffer points out that despite years of monitoring by Bigelow's team, no verifiable proof ever emerged. More tellingly, the Myers family - Kenneth and Edith Myers who owned the property for sixty years before the Shermans - reported that nothing supernatural had ever occurred there. Sheaffer and others suggest the simplest explanation: the Shermans invented their stories before selling to a buyer they knew would pay a premium for a haunted property. James Randi awarded Bigelow a satirical Pigasus Award in 1996 for funding 'the most useless study of a supernatural claim.' In 2023, ufologist Barry Greenwood characterized the ranch as 'always in the business of selling belief and hope.'

## The Modern Era of Surveillance

Bigelow sold Skinwalker Ranch in 2016 for approximately $500,000 to Adamantium Real Estate LLC. The new ownership immediately transformed the property into a fortress: blocked roads, surveillance cameras, barbed wire perimeters, and warning signs deterring curious visitors. In 2020, Utah real estate developer Brandon Fugal revealed himself as the owner, bringing the ranch into a new era of publicity including a History Channel television series. The property is now a registered trademark, with merchandising rights extending to everything from entertainment content to coffee mugs. Whatever secrets the ranch may or may not hold, its commercial value has proven quite real. The investigations continue, the evidence remains elusive, and the legend grows.

## Flight Context

Located at 40.26N, 109.89W in Uintah County, Utah, southeast of the small community of Ballard. The ranch sits in the Uintah Basin, bordered by the Uintah and Ouray Indian Reservation. From the air, the property appears as a typical high-desert ranch with sparse vegetation. Note: the property is private, heavily monitored, and closed to visitors. Nearest public airport is Vernal Regional Airport (VEL) approximately 20 miles to the east. The surrounding basin offers wide views of the Uinta Mountains to the north. Best observed from 5,000 feet AGL or higher to maintain appropriate distance from the restricted private property.
