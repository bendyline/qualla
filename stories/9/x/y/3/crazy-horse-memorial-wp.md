# Crazy Horse Memorial

A monument seven decades in the making, carved into sacred Lakota land by three generations of one family, honoring a warrior who refused to be photographed in life.

**Location:** 43.8354, -103.6213

**Categories:** monument, sculpture, Native American history, Black Hills

## Introduction

"My fellow chiefs and I would like the white man to know that the red man has great heroes, too." With those words, written in 1939, Lakota elder Henry Standing Bear set in motion what would become the longest-running sculptural project in human history. Nearly nine decades later, the face of Crazy Horse stares out from Thunderhead Mountain, his arm forever pointing toward the horizon where his people once roamed free. The monument remains unfinished, a work in progress that has consumed the lives of one sculptor, his widow, his children, and now his grandchildren.

## A Snub That Launched a Mountain

The story begins with rejection. In 1931, Luther Standing Bear wrote to Gutzon Borglum, then carving Mount Rushmore just seventeen miles away, suggesting that Crazy Horse deserved a place alongside Washington and Lincoln. Borglum never replied. Luther's younger brother Henry refused to let the idea die. For eight years he campaigned for recognition, at one point writing to a friend: "I am struggling hopelessly with this because I am without funds, no employment and no assistance from any Indian or White." Finally, in 1939, he contacted Korczak Ziolkowski, a Polish-American sculptor who had worked under Borglum at Rushmore. Ziolkowski traveled to Pine Ridge and spent three weeks learning about Crazy Horse and the Lakota way of life. Standing Bear's anger about the broken Treaty of Fort Laramie left a lasting impression. The project would accept no government funding, relying instead on admission fees and private donations.

## Carving Against Time

The first blast on Thunderhead Mountain came in 1948. Ziolkowski estimated thirty years to completion. He was wildly optimistic. When he died in 1982 at age 74, the mountain still showed only rough outlines. His widow Ruth took over, making a strategic pivot: rather than work on the horse first as her husband planned, she focused on Crazy Horse's face. Her reasoning was practical. A recognizable face would draw tourists and generate the revenue needed to continue. The 87-foot-high face was completed in 1998, fifty years after work began. Ruth and seven of their ten children carried on the work. Daughter Monique, herself a sculptor, modified her father's engineering plans. Three grandsons now work on the mountain, including Caleb, who holds the title of "chief mountain officer." By 2024, the left hand was complete, and a new $5.2 million tower crane arrived on seventeen truck beds to accelerate the work on the horse's mane.

## Sacred Ground, Contested Legacy

The memorial sits on land the Lakota consider sacred, and not all Native Americans celebrate its existence. Crazy Horse was a warrior who resisted imprisonment and reportedly refused to be photographed, believing cameras stole the soul. Some Lakota argue that carving his likeness into a mountain he considered holy violates everything he stood for. Activist Russell Means compared it to carving up Mount Zion. Others point out that the Standing Bear family acted without the consensus that Lakota culture requires for such decisions. Former South Dakota Senator Jim Bradford, himself Oglala Sioux, criticized the project bluntly: "one non-Indian family has become millionaires off our people." The foundation has accumulated $128 million in assets and earns $14.5 million annually. A 2019 New Yorker article quoted Lakota residents saying the memorial honors the Ziolkowski family more than Crazy Horse himself.

## The Unfinished Vision

When complete, the sculpture will stand 563 feet high and 641 feet long, dwarfing Mount Rushmore. The outstretched arm alone will measure 263 feet. But completion remains a moving target. As of 2022, there was no official timeline, though estimates suggested the hand, arm, shoulder, hairline, and horse's head might be finished by 2037. The project now includes more than the mountain carving: an Indian Museum of North America, a Native American Cultural Center, and the Indian University of North America, which has offered summer programs for Native American university students since 2011. The geology itself presents challenges. The monument is carved from Harney Peak leucogranite, the same Proterozoic rock as Rushmore, famous for its pegmatite formations. A geologic fault separates the sculpture from the museum and university buildings.

## The View from Above

From the air, the scale becomes comprehensible. The face alone could hold all four Rushmore presidents. The pointing arm will eventually stretch longer than a football field. The mountain sits between Custer and Hill City in the Black Hills, seventeen miles from Rushmore. Pilots approaching from the east can see both monuments in a single panorama: the four presidents facing northeast, Crazy Horse facing his tribal lands to the southeast. The contrast tells a story that words cannot capture. One monument was carved in fourteen years by four hundred workers and declared complete in 1941. The other has consumed three generations and may consume three more. Both emerged from the same ancient granite, shaped by human hands to embody very different dreams of what it means to be American.
