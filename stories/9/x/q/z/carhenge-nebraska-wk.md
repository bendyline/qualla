# Carhenge

Thirty-five Reinders family members spent the summer of 1987 burying Detroit's finest in a Nebraska wheatfield - thirty-nine vintage cars, painted gray, arranged exactly like Stonehenge, all because Jim Reinders wanted to remember his father.

**Location:** 42.1419, -102.8578

**Categories:** nebraska, roadside-attraction, folk-art, memorial, quirky

## Introduction

Jim Reinders missed his father. That is the part most visitors miss, standing in the Nebraska wind taking selfies in front of an upended Plymouth. The Reinders family farm sits two and a half miles north of Alliance, on the kind of road that runs straight to the horizon, and somewhere on this ground his father had lived and worked and eventually been buried. When the elder Reinders died, Jim - who happened to be an engineer who had recently been living in England and had spent his off hours studying the original Stonehenge - decided that a normal headstone was not going to cut it. He summoned roughly thirty-five family members to Nebraska for the summer of 1987. They brought thirty-nine cars. The result was either the most affecting folk memorial in the American Midwest or the most committed roadside-attraction prank ever pulled, depending on which Reinders cousin you asked.

## The Plan

Reinders had time on his hands in England and an engineer's instincts. He studied Stonehenge - its proportions, its solstice geometry, the spacing of the outer sarsens and the inner horseshoe of trilithons. When he returned to Nebraska, he had a working blueprint. The Stonehenge ring is about 97 feet across; Carhenge would be a hair under 96. The original has a heel stone outside the main circle aligned with the summer solstice sunrise; Carhenge would have one too. The whole thing would be in gray, to mimic weathered limestone, which meant a generous application of spray paint over the chrome and pastels of Detroit's golden years. Friends and family raised eyebrows. Reinders raised eyebrows back and started gathering automobiles.

## Building With Bel Airs

What the Reinders family clan accomplished in a single Nebraska summer would have impressed a small construction company. Holes had to be dug five feet deep - that is a lot of caliche soil to move with shovels and a couple of borrowed backhoes. Each upright car was lowered trunk-first into the ground until its hood pointed at the sky. Then more cars were welded across the tops to form the trilithon lintels. The roster of vehicles reads like a junkyard inventory of postwar America: an AMC Gremlin, a Plymouth Valiant, several Cadillacs, a string of Ford sedans, Chevrolet Impalas, Oldsmobile Delta 88s, even a 1951 Willys Jeep pickup. The heel stone is a 1962 Cadillac. By the time the last car was painted, the family had used spray cans by the carton, and Jim Reinders had dedicated the finished monument on the summer solstice, June 1987, exactly as planned.

## The City Tried to Tear It Down

Alliance was not amused. To the city's planning officials, Carhenge was a junkyard with delusions of grandeur, sitting in plain view of Highway 87 and damaging the dignity of a town that mostly minded its business. They issued orders. Reinders refused. The dispute went to the zoning board. And then a peculiar thing happened: tourists started showing up. Reporters started writing about it. The Nebraska Department of Roads put up a brown sign. Letters to the editor came in defending the monument. By the early 1990s the city had given up trying to remove Carhenge and joined the rest of the state in claiming it - the Nebraska Tourism Commission now lists it among the top attractions on the high plains. Reinders had won by being patient and right at the same time, which is the best way to win anything in a small town.

## What Came After

Reinders eventually deeded the ten-acre site to a nonprofit called Friends of Carhenge, who took on maintenance and built a small visitor center in 2006 selling postcards, T-shirts, and a surprisingly good guidebook. In 2013, Friends of Carhenge transferred ownership to the Citizens of Alliance - the same town that had once tried to bulldoze it. A surrounding sculpture garden, the Car Art Reserve, has accumulated additional pieces over the years: a salmon assembled from car parts, a dinosaur welded from fenders, a few abstract works whose meanings are negotiable. The 2017 total solar eclipse passed directly over Alliance, and Carhenge - aligned, of course, to the solstice - became one of the most photographed viewing sites in the country. Roughly 10,000 people showed up, which is more than the population of Alliance itself. The cars were unimpressed.

## Visiting

Carhenge is open every day of the year, all day, and admission is free. It sits 2.5 miles north of Alliance on U.S. Highway 87, in country flat enough that you can see the monument from a mile off. The visitor center keeps shorter hours and seasonal staffing; the grounds themselves never close. Dawn and dusk are the best times for photographs - the gray cars take warm light beautifully, and the summer solstice sunrise hits the heel stone almost on cue. Alliance has motels, fuel, and a small handful of restaurants; Scottsbluff is 60 miles west for a wider selection. The nearest commercial airport with regular service is Denver International (KDEN), 275 miles south, though Alliance Municipal (KAIA) handles general aviation. The Nebraska Sandhills lie to the east. Bring a windbreaker. The high plains do not negotiate.

## Flight Context

Located at 42.14 degrees north, 102.86 degrees west on the high plains of the Nebraska panhandle, 2.5 miles north of Alliance along U.S. Highway 87. From altitude the monument reads clearly: a circular arrangement of small gray rectangles set in a square of mown grass, with a heel-stone car offset to the northeast, the Stonehenge silhouette unmistakable even from cruise. Surrounding terrain is flat agricultural country - winter wheat, fallow ground, scattered cattle operations, the grid of section roads. The Nebraska Sandhills rise to the east in a long rolling expanse of grass-covered dunes. Scotts Bluff is 40 miles west; the wider Wyoming plains begin shortly beyond. Alliance Municipal Airport (KAIA) is two miles south of the monument and handles general aviation; nearest commercial service is Western Nebraska Regional in Scottsbluff (KBFF), 55 miles west. Density altitude can be a factor in summer afternoons; otherwise the panhandle offers excellent VFR conditions most of the year.
