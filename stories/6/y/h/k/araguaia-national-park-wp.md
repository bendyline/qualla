# Araguaia National Park

Bananal Island floats at the junction of two biomes, and the park that covers its northern 5,577 square kilometers is where rainforest turns to savanna.

**Location:** -10.5000, -50.1670

**Categories:** National parks of Brazil, Protected areas of Tocantins, Amazon rainforest, Cerrado

## Introduction

There is a river that swallows itself. About halfway down its course north toward the Tocantins, the Araguaia River splits around a landmass so large that early cartographers drew it as its own geography: Bananal Island, roughly 350 kilometers long, one of the largest inland river islands in the world. At the northern tip of Bananal, Araguaia National Park covers 5,577 square kilometers of terrain that sits exactly on the line where the Amazon biome gives way to the cerrado. Rainforest on the humid north side. Grassland and stunted woodland on the drier south. The river rises and falls around all of it, flooding the park for months at a stretch and exposing it again when the rains retreat.

## Decrees and Borders

The Brazilian government established Araguaia National Park on December 31, 1959, a late New Year's Eve act by federal decree number 45,570 - one of the earlier protected areas in the Amazon basin. The park takes the northern end of Bananal and has its formal area of 5,577 square kilometers. On December 19, 1973, Article 28 of Indian Statute Law 6001 expanded protection to the entire island, declaring the centre and south - some 13,584 square kilometers - a cultural preserve for the indigenous peoples who live there. The two zones share an island but serve different purposes: the northern third for ecological protection, the southern two-thirds for the four indigenous nations who preserve their own governance. The park's southern boundary is also the preserve's northern boundary. You do not cross it without permission.

## Where Biomes Meet

The park sits on an ecotone - a narrow zone where two biomes grade into each other. The Amazon rainforest presses in from the north and west, with its dense canopy, high rainfall, and year-round humidity. The cerrado - Brazil's vast tropical savanna - approaches from the south and east, characterized by scattered twisted trees, grass understorey, and seasonal drought. At their meeting place, you get species from both worlds sharing ground. A jaguar hunting in the rainforest edge might take down a marsh deer grazing at the boundary between forest and flood. The altitude is modest, 200 meters above sea level on average - the park is flat country. Its drama is horizontal rather than vertical: the expanding and contracting footprint of water across a landscape that reads like a tide zone measured in kilometers.

## Palms in the Flood

In the areas that flood regularly, two palms dominate. The piassava palm, Leopoldinia piassaba, produces the tough fibers once used in rope and brooms. The buriti palm, Mauritia flexuosa, is the keystone species of the flooded savanna - its fruits are a staple for wildlife and humans alike. Where the forest is drier, you find the cow tree, Cabralea, Tabebuia with its flaming yellow and pink flowers, Qualea parviflora, black sweetwood, and genipap, whose fruit indigenous peoples have used for centuries as a skin dye. In the drier cerrado part of the park, scattered trees like the souari nut and the pau-d'alho stand above continuous grass. The vegetation tells you instantly where you are: under palms you are in the wet zone; under small twisted trees, the dry savanna; under continuous canopy, the proper rainforest.

## What Lives Below

A mammal list for the park reads long. Marsh deer push through the reeds. Peccary travel in noisy groups. Capybara - the world's largest rodent, a kind of oversized water-loving guinea pig - graze the wet margins in family troops. Giant anteaters walk the savannas, their long tongues designed for termites. The maned wolf, Chrysocyon brachyurus, the tallest wild canid in South America, hunts the grassland at dawn. Jaguars and giant otters share the forested river edges. Armadillos work the leaf litter. In the water, you find two species of dolphin - Amazon river dolphins, Inia geoffrensis, pink and bulky, and the smaller gray tucuxi, Sotalia fluviatilis. Black caiman and spectacled caiman patrol the same water. Anacondas in the flooded swamps grow thick as a person's thigh. The South American river turtle lays its eggs on sandbars that appear only in the driest months.

## Birds You Hear Before You See

The bird list is the real reason specialists come. Common toucans with their oversized bills rattle through the canopy. The greater rhea - tall, flightless, faintly ostrich-like - strides the savanna. Quail and partridge hide in the grass. Ospreys plunge into the river with feet thrown forward, lifting out fish that seem too large for them. Orinoco geese patrol the shorelines. Anhingas, sometimes called snake-birds for the way their neck sticks out of the water when they swim, dry themselves on low branches between dives. Herons and egrets stalk the shallows with the patience of things that have nowhere better to be. The musician wren builds its reputation on a call so melodic it sounds composed rather than instinctive. And, rarely, the Spix's macaw - declared extinct in the wild in 2019 and the subject of a captive-breeding reintroduction program that began releasing birds in 2022 - once passed through these forests. The species' near-disappearance shapes the park's conservation planning as much as any living species.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 10.50 S, 50.17 W, situated between 09 degrees 51 minutes and 11 degrees 11 minutes south, 49 degrees 57 minutes to 50 degrees 27 minutes west. The northern third of Bananal Island, the world's largest inland river island. Recommended viewing altitude FL060-FL080. The park lies at the ecotone between Amazon rainforest and cerrado - from altitude, look for the transition from continuous dark canopy to mottled scrubland with scattered trees. Flood season (January-April) dramatically enlarges the wetted footprint. Nearest airports: Palmas (SBPJ) to the east, Sao Felix do Araguaia (SWFX) to the west, with smaller airstrips on the mainland bordering the river.
