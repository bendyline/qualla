# Maranhão

A northeastern Brazilian state of seven million, the world's tallest mangroves, and a capital liberated in 1823 by a Scottish admiral who became 1st Marques of Maranhão.

**Location:** -6.1833, -45.6000

**Categories:** Maranhão, Federative units of Brazil, Northeast Region, Brazil, Former Portuguese colonies

## Introduction

The Portuguese called their new settlers from a village in the Alentejo, and a Peruvian river once shared the name, but today Maranhão belongs to a 332,000-square-kilometer state in northeastern Brazil where the world's tallest mangrove forests meet the only dune field in the Americas that fills with lagoons. Seven million people live here, divided among 217 municipalities, governed from a Portuguese-feeling capital on an island. The state sits clockwise from north between the Atlantic and the states of Piauí, Tocantins, and Pará. Its 2,243-kilometer coastline is the second longest in Brazil, and the accent of its people is unmistakable even to fellow northeasterners.

## A Coastline Invented by Water

The north of Maranhão is a forested plain cut by rivers that all run toward the Atlantic. Clockwise from the west, the Gurupí forms the state's border with Pará. The Tocantins River forms part of the southwestern boundary. The Parnaíba defines the eastern edge with Piauí, joined within Maranhão by the Balsas, its main entirely-interior tributary. The Mearim, Pindaré, and Grajaú empty into the Baía de São Marcos; the Itapecuru and Munim discharge into the Baía de São José. Between these two contiguous bays sits the large island of São Luís. The Mearim, like the Amazon, produces a pororoca - a tidal bore that surges upriver and badly disrupts navigation. The western coast is fringed by the Maranhão mangroves, the tallest mangrove forests in the world, some trees exceeding 40 meters. East of the Baía de São José, the coast abruptly changes character, becoming the stark white dune fields of Lençóis Maranhenses National Park.

## French Colony, Portuguese City

Vicente Yáñez Pinzón first sighted this coast in 1500, but the Portuguese crown only granted it as a hereditary captaincy to João de Barros in 1534. Europeans barely touched the land for sixty years. Then a French trading expedition under Jacques Riffault of Dieppe arrived in 1594, losing two of his three vessels near São Luís Island and leaving some of his men behind. France pressed the advantage. In 1612, Daniel de La Touche founded a formal colony called Equinoctial France. The Portuguese expelled the French in 1615. The Dutch held São Luís briefly from 1641 to 1644. Jesuits then established remarkably successful missions among indigenous peoples, until they were temporarily expelled in 1684 after opposing the enslavement of those same peoples. The State of Maranhão remained administratively separate from the rest of colonial Brazil until 1774.

## Cotton, Cochrane, and Independence

In the late 18th century, enslaved Africans were forced into Maranhão in large numbers to work cotton fields. Historian Sven Beckert records that cotton exports doubled between 1770 and 1780, nearly doubled again by 1790, and nearly tripled once more by 1800. The state's wealth rested on this trade, and the architecture of São Luís still reflects it. When Brazil declared independence in 1822, Maranhão did not initially join. The next year the Portuguese loyalists were driven out by Admiral Thomas Cochrane, a Scottish sailor and liberator who also helped free Chile and Greece. For his service, Cochrane became 1st Marques of Maranhão and Governor of Maranhão Province. São Luís, which today has a population of over a million, remains the Brazilian state capital that most closely resembles a Portuguese city. UNESCO inscribed its historic center as a World Heritage Site in 1997.

## Babassu, Soybeans, and Ports

Maranhão is called the land of the palm trees, and the most economically important of them is the babassu. Its oil ends up in food and beauty products, and the women who harvest the nuts are known as quebradeiras de coco babaçu. Agriculture ranks fifth in Brazilian rice production and highest in the Northeast, with strong positions in soybeans, cotton, and cassava. But the headline numbers come from the ports. Itaqui is the second-deepest port in the world, and in 2017 alone it handled more than 17 million tons of cargo. Ponta da Madeira, operated by Vale, ships iron ore from the Carajás mountains in Pará down the Carajás Railway and out to Asian and European buyers. Maranhão is nonetheless one of Brazil's poorest states, accounting for 3.4% of the population but only 1.3% of GDP.

## Flag, Film, and a State at the Edge

The flag of Maranhão was designed by the poet Joaquim de Souza Andrade and adopted on December 21, 1889. Its red, white, and black stripes stand for the mixing of ethnic groups. The white star in the upper corner represents Maranhão itself and is said to be Beta Scorpii, part of the same constellation depicted on Brazil's national flag. The state's landscapes have drawn filmmakers from abroad. Andrucha Waddington's The House of Sand (2005) was shot in the Lençóis Maranhenses. The Tamil blockbuster Endhiran (2010) filmed the song Kadhal Anukkal, featuring Aishwarya Rai and Rajnikanth, on the same dunes. São Luís provided the colonial setting for Carla Camurati's Carlota Joaquina, Princess of Brazil (1995). Seen from above at altitude, Maranhão reveals itself as a transitional state - Amazon in the west, cerrado in the south, dunes in the east, mangroves in the north. Few places on Earth pack so many biomes into one coastline.

## Flight Context

State centroid at approximately 6.18°S, 45.60°W. Covers 332,000 km² with 2,243 km of Atlantic coastline. Primary airports: Marechal Cunha Machado International (SBSL) in São Luís, 13 km from city center; Renato Moreira (SBIZ) in Imperatriz. Visible from altitude: the Lençóis Maranhenses dune fields east of Barreirinhas (stark white, unmistakable); the Parnaíba delta at the Piauí border; the Maranhão mangroves along the western coast; the Itaqui Port complex near São Luís. Chapada das Mangabeiras, Serra do Tiracambu, and Serra das Alpercatas mark plateau edges in the interior. Recommended viewing altitude 25,000-37,000 feet for state-scale context.
