# Cazumbá-Iracema Extractive Reserve

A 750,000-hectare swath of Amazon rainforest in Acre, Brazil, where descendants of WWII rubber soldiers fought to keep their forest standing instead of becoming pasture.

**Location:** -9.0961, -68.8436

**Categories:** Amazon rainforest, Conservation, Extractive reserves, Brazil, Acre

## Introduction

On December 14, 2001, a rubber tapper named Aldeci Cerqueira Maia, known to his neighbors as Nenzinho, sat down to write a letter to the president of Brazil. He called the Amazon the lungs of the Earth. He explained that he and his neighbors were the real conservationists, the ones who still lived where they had always lived, preserving the forest around them. Nine months later, by presidential decree, the Cazumbá-Iracema Extractive Reserve became real. It would protect 750,795 hectares of Acre rainforest from being cleared for cattle. The families who won that fight had been living quietly along the upper Caeté River for generations, many of them descendants of men brought to the Amazon during World War II to bleed rubber for an army that was running out.

## How They Got Here

Acre's rubber economy began in the 1870s, when contractors started shipping workers from Brazil's parched northeast to tap the wild Hevea brasiliensis trees that grew throughout the forest. The boom did not last. In the 1870s, seeds smuggled to Kew Gardens in London made their way to British Malaya, where plantations could undercut Brazilian wild-harvested rubber. By 1912, Malaya was outproducing Brazil, and Acre's rubber economy collapsed. Then came the war. In 1942, when Japan occupied Malaya and cut off the Allies from their rubber supply, the United States poured money into reviving Brazilian extraction. The Brazilian government recruited thousands of men from the dry northeast, calling them rubber soldiers and promising a new life in the Amazon. The conditions were brutal, the way out was nonexistent, and the men who survived had to learn the forest and how to live from it. Many of the families who still live in the reserve today are their descendants.

## A Stand-off in the Forest

When global rubber prices collapsed again in the early 1990s, Brazil's National Institute for Colonization and Agrarian Reform decided the Iracema seringal should be cleared and converted to cattle ranches. More than 200 families faced eviction from settlements scattered across over 60 kilometers of the upper Caeté River. Nenzinho organized the Cazumbá Rubber Tappers Association in 1993. With the help of Father Paolino Baldassari, the local Catholic priest, the association fought the evictions and won. In 1995, Nenzinho persuaded a number of families to consolidate at Nucleo Cazumbá on land his grandfather had owned, where they could share resources and prevent speculators from buying up the land piecemeal. They sold rubber together. When prices dropped further, they began harvesting Brazil nuts. Sometimes they stopped loggers with human chains made of women and children.

## The Forest Underneath

What the rubber tappers were defending is one of the most biodiverse pieces of forest left in the western Amazon. Open palm rainforest dominates, threaded with bamboo and stretches of dense canopy along the Caeté River. Researchers have catalogued 179 bird species, 44 mammals, 18 fish, and 8 reptiles in preliminary surveys, and those are almost certainly fractions of the actual numbers. Among the threatened residents are giant anteaters, giant armadillos, jaguars, and bush dogs. The Brazil nut trees that the families harvest are themselves giants that can live for 500 years. The reserve borders Chandless State Park to the west and the Macauã National Forest to the southeast, forming part of a green corridor that gives wide-ranging species like jaguars enough room to actually be jaguars. In 2013, when Brazil audited 247 of its conservation units, only ten earned high implementation marks. Cazumbá-Iracema was one of them.

## What Comes Next

The pressures have not gone away. Most families in Nucleo Cazumbá now own televisions. They watch the outside world. Young people want phones, even where there is no signal, even just for the games. Rubber pays poorly even with government subsidies, and in the nearby Chico Mendes reserve a community leader observed in 2013 that the son of a tapper born today wants to raise cattle. In 2008, several thousand head of cattle were discovered grazing on a large tract of illegally cleared forest inside the reserve. The reserve's açaí pulp operation took third prize among 120 entrants in a national entrepreneurship award in 2015, when it employed 50 people. By 2016, three biodiversity monitoring stations were tracking what is happening in the canopy. Whether the next generation will choose forest or pasture remains the open question, the same question Nenzinho was already asking when he wrote his letter to the president.

## Flight Context

Cazumbá-Iracema sits in the Brazilian state of Acre at 9.10°S, 68.84°W, south of the BR-364 highway in the Purus River basin. Nearest major airport is Rio Branco (SBRB), about 135 km east-southeast. From cruising altitude the reserve appears as an unbroken dark green carpet bisected by the meandering Caeté River, with oxbow lakes glinting silver in the alluvial terraces. Best viewed at 8,000-10,000 ft for the river meanders. Visibility often hazy in the wet season (October-May).
