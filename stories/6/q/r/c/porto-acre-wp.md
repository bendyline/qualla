# Porto Acre

The town where a Spanish-born dreamer declared an Independent State of Acre, printed his own money, and was arrested inside of a year - and where rubber tappers later won the war.

**Location:** -9.5878, -67.5328

**Categories:** brazil, acre, amazon, rubber-boom, acre-war

## Introduction

On a July day in 1899, a man named Luis Gálvez Rodríguez de Arias expelled the Bolivian authorities from a small river settlement and announced the birth of an Independent State of Acre. He renamed the town from Puerto Alonso - which honored a Bolivian president - to Cidade do Acre and made it his capital. He minted currency. He drafted a constitution. He named ministers. He raised an army. Within a few months he had been deposed, arrested, and sent home. The Bolivians came back. Three years later, a different man with a different army would finish what Gálvez started. The place is now called Porto Acre, a quiet Brazilian municipality of about 16,700 people on the west bank of the Acre River. The history it sits on is anything but quiet.

## Puerto Alonso

On January 3, 1899, when the rubber boom was making the western Amazon one of the most economically valuable stretches of forest on Earth, a settlement was founded on the left bank of the Acre River. It was named Puerto Alonso, after Severo Fernández Alonso Caballero, then president of Bolivia - because at that time, the Acre territory was Bolivian. The border drawn decades earlier by the Treaty of Ayacucho had handed this patch of latex-rich rainforest to La Paz. But the men tapping the rubber trees were mostly Brazilian nordestinos who had migrated into the forest during the drought years, and they had no interest in paying taxes to a distant Andean capital. The rubber was theirs. The story of Porto Acre over the next several years is the story of what happened when the people on the ground refused to accept lines drawn far from the trees.

## Gálvez's Republic

Luis Gálvez Rodríguez de Arias was a Spaniard - a journalist, an adventurer, and, as it turned out, a capable rabble-rouser. In 1899 he arrived in Puerto Alonso, threw out the Bolivian authorities, and proclaimed the Independent State of Acre. He made himself head of state. He designed a flag. He issued currency. He wrote a constitution modeled on the republican ideas then fashionable in South America. He appointed a cabinet. He even raised an army to defend the new country against whoever came for it. It was a near-perfect miniature of a nation-state, built in a rubber town on a tributary of the Amazon, by a man who had arrived with only a small expedition quietly backed by the governor of Amazonas and no formal standing. And it lasted, depending on how you count it, less than a year. Brazilian authorities - technically uninvolved but suspicious of a destabilizing element on the border - eventually moved against Gálvez. He was deposed, arrested, and the Bolivians resumed control of Puerto Alonso.

## Plácido de Castro Takes Xapuri

The second try went differently. In early 1902 a Brazilian soldier and surveyor named José Plácido de Castro accepted an invitation to lead a rubber-tapper revolt against Bolivian rule. He took a boat up the Acre River, pretending to conduct land surveys in the south while actually moving weapons and ammunition. He stopped briefly at Puerto Alonso to meet the Bolivian governor Lino Romero and size up the man he would soon be fighting. In the south he organized the seringueiros - the rubber tappers - and other frontier residents into a fighting force. Then he slipped back to Brazil, bypassing Puerto Alonso so as not to tip his hand. On August 6, 1902, Plácido captured Xapuri. The next day he proclaimed Acre's independence from Bolivia. On October 14, his forces took outer defenses of Puerto Alonso along with the armed Bolivian launch Rio Afua, which they promptly renamed Independencia. On January 8, 1903, they surrounded the town. On January 15 they attacked. The assault stalled. They started digging zigzag trenches toward the Bolivian lines, foot by foot. On January 24, 1903, out of ammunition and exhausted, Romero surrendered.

## What a Tributary Town Became

The Treaty of Petrópolis in November 1903 transferred Acre to Brazil in exchange for money, an unbuilt railway, and some territorial adjustments. The Independent State of Acre formally ceased to exist. Puerto Alonso became Porto Acre. The rubber boom peaked and collapsed, undercut by Asian plantations that produced latex cheaper than the wild-tapped trees of the Amazon ever could. Today Porto Acre is a municipality of about 16,700 people, reached from Rio Branco by 78 kilometers of the AC-010 highway or by boat up the Acre River. Its economy is modest - rubber extraction still, Brazil nuts, small-scale livestock, horticulture, and the movement of goods up and down the river. During the dry season farmers grow watermelon, banana, cassava, and vegetables on the fertile banks. Nine settlement projects cover 44 percent of the municipal area. The town gained formal autonomy from Rio Branco only on April 28, 1992, with its first mayor and councilors taking office on January 1, 1993. A century after Plácido de Castro's zigzag trenches, the independent state he helped win survived as one word and one municipality - Acre, Porto Acre - and as the memory of a time when rubber tappers with hunting rifles changed a border.

## Flight Context

Located at 9.59 degrees S, 67.53 degrees W in the northeast of the Brazilian state of Acre, on the west bank of the Acre River. About 51 km north-northeast of Rio Branco as the crow flies, 78 km by the AC-010 highway. Aerial access via Rio Branco's Plácido de Castro International (SBRB), which bears the name of the rebel leader who took the town in 1903. Cruise 3,000-5,000 feet for a river-following view; the Acre and Purus rivers provide natural VFR navigation. Amazonian convection builds afternoons; morning operations recommended year-round, especially during the November-March rainy season.
