# Antimary State Forest

Acre's first state forest, where rubber tappers became foresters and the Brazilian Amazon ran one of the world's most-watched experiments in sustainable logging.

**Location:** -9.4298, -68.0671

**Categories:** 1997 establishments in Brazil, State forests of Brazil, Protected areas of Acre (state), Protected areas established in 1997

## Introduction

In 1907, a guerrilla leader named Jose Placido de Castro wrote a letter describing a tributary of the Acre River, mentioning the shacks he saw on its banks. The shacks belonged to seringueiros - rubber tappers - working out a living that would still be defining this forest a century later. Acre was barely Brazilian then. It had been Bolivian until rubber tappers, led by Castro, fought a war to make it briefly independent, then Brazilian. The Antimary they tapped became, in 1997, the first state forest Acre ever created. It has been argued over and studied harder than almost any forest in the world.

## The Tappers' Country

Antimary sits in flat country at the western edge of Brazil, north of the BR-364 highway, straddling two municipalities - Bujari and Sena Madureira - in the eastern part of Acre state. The numbers tell some of the story: 47,064 hectares now, after years of being redrawn; about 100 to 109 families living inside, mostly descended from rubber tappers who came down from the Brazilian northeast in the late 1800s; an average of 114 trees per hectare, more than 600 plant species identified in the forest. What the numbers do not tell is the texture - bamboo-dominated patches alternating with palms and emergent giants, yellow latosol soils thick with clay, the dry season from June to September that makes everything dusty and the wet season that turns the Antimary River into the only road. In the rains, families move Brazil nuts and cassava flour by canoe.

## The Experiment

Acre's history with the forest is unusual. While most of the Brazilian Amazon was being cleared by ranchers and soybean farmers, Acre's tappers - led most famously by Chico Mendes, who was murdered in 1988 - fought to keep their forest standing because they made their living inside it. The Antimary State Forest Project began that same year, partnered with the International Tropical Timber Organization. The idea was radical: keep the forest, but let people log it carefully, taking out maybe two trees per hectare and letting the rest regrow. Not preservation. Not clearcutting. Something in between, tested in real time on a working forest with real residents. By 2002 ITTO was calling Antimary one of its three best examples. By 2004 it had Forest Stewardship Council certification. The experiment was watched - and copied - around the tropics.

## The Tappers Watch

Not everyone among the tappers welcomed the change. Many had spent decades fighting loggers and ranchers from outside Acre, and now timber was being extracted - certified, sustainable timber, but timber. Some accepted it as a way to earn more from the forest without destroying it. Others remembered too well what the loggers used to do. The forest's traditional economy still ran on Brazil nuts and rubber - 100 tonnes of nuts, 12 tonnes of rubber in 2003 - alongside copaiba oil, forest seeds, cassava, bananas. Each family got the right to use about 300 to 400 hectares of forest, the rough area a family could tap for rubber. They could not sell the land. They could only live on it.

## The Lidar Sees Everything

By 2008, forestry made up nineteen percent of Acre's economy and half its exports by value. The proportion of processed wood coming from managed forests had jumped from 5.7 percent in 2002 to 84 percent six years later - a structural shift in how an Amazonian state was earning its money. Antimary remained the only certified public forest in Brazil. Researchers used it for everything. A 2012 study flew lidar scanners over the canopy to estimate above-ground biomass and detect where logging had happened, even when the canopy had closed back over. The lidar found the harvest tracks, the skid trails, the landings, the missing tonnes of wood - all from the air, all confirmed by ground crews. The technology and the forest were teaching each other what sustainable logging actually looked like.

## Life in the Reserva

What the forest now offers its residents is something unfamiliar to their grandparents. Health centers - four of them, with vaccination rates of 90 percent and three years without a malaria case as of the 2003 survey. Three schools, for adults and children, where illiteracy dropped from 90 percent to 15 percent. A telephone line at the project headquarters. Ten graduate and undergraduate students working on forest management questions in any given year, supported by the state. The trees still go down, two per hectare or so, and the rubber still flows on cuts in the bark, and the Brazil nuts still fall in their hard pods, and the river still rises and falls. What changed is who was watching, and who was getting paid, and who decided. In a region where so much forest has been lost so fast, that change matters.

## Flight Context

Located at 9.43S, 68.07W in eastern Acre, Brazil. The forest sits north of the BR-364 highway, with tributaries of the Acre River cutting across flat country at about 200-300m elevation. Visual landmarks: BR-364, the Antimary River, the boundary with Amazonas state to the northeast. Nearest airports: Rio Branco (SBRB) about 70km southeast, Sena Madureira's small airfield about 75km to the northwest. Persistent cloud cover during November-March wet season.
