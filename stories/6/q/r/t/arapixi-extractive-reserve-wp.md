# Arapixi Extractive Reserve

A 133,000-hectare reserve in Brazil's Amazonas state where rubber tappers, Brazil nut gatherers, and the giant otter share the meanders of the Purus River.

**Location:** -8.8530, -67.8640

**Categories:** 2006 establishments in Brazil, Protected areas of Amazonas (Brazilian state), Extractive reserves of Brazil

## Introduction

The fight for Arapixi began in 2000, when local communities in the municipality of Boca do Acre realized soybean farmers and cattle ranchers were heading their way. The Union of Rural Workers wrote letters. The National Council of Rubber Tappers backed them. Six years of meetings, petitions, and pressure later, the federal decree finally came down on 21 June 2006. About 300 families had won the right to keep doing what their parents and grandparents had done in this stretch of the Amazon basin: tap rubber, gather Brazil nuts, fish the Purus, plant a little cassava and a few bananas, and not be paved over.

## Where the Purus Meanders

The Purus River wanders through Arapixi from west to east, a brown, sediment-loaded ribbon doing what Amazon tributaries do - looping back on itself, cutting new channels, abandoning old ones, leaving oxbow lakes behind. The reserve covers 133,637 hectares of basically flat country, bounded by the Igarape Capana Indigenous Reserve to the west, the Mapia-Inauini National Forest to the north, and the Camicua Indigenous Territory to the east. The vegetation is layered Amazon: open alluvial rainforest along the riverbanks, open and dense lowland forest farther in, with scattered patches that have been cleared for cattle or crops and other patches in various stages of grow-back. It rains hard. It is hot. The river is the road, the larder, and the limit.

## The Animals the Survey Found

A rough survey turned up 25 fish species, seven reptiles, 123 birds, 33 mammals - a fraction of what is probably actually there. The big-name endangered animals showed up: jaguars, giant armadillos, giant anteaters, giant otters. The fish included some of the Amazon's most pressured commercial species - tambaqui, the giant arapaima, two species of large catfish, all overfished or threatened by overfishing for markets downstream. The bird list is dense with species of restricted range: speckled chachalaca, pale-winged trumpeter, dusky-headed parakeet, festive amazon, Purus jacamar, semicollared puffbird, lemon-throated barbet. Several are confined to the western Amazon, and the Purus jacamar takes its name from the river that runs through the reserve.

## Why an Extractive Reserve

Brazil invented the extractive reserve as a category of protected area in the late 1980s, born of the rubber tapper movement and the murder of Chico Mendes. The idea was to recognize that the people who were already in the forest - tapping rubber, gathering nuts, fishing - were keeping it alive, and that pushing them out to create empty parks was both cruel and counterproductive. Arapixi falls in IUCN Category VI, which permits sustainable use of natural resources. The reserve's stated purpose is to protect the livelihoods and culture of the traditional extractive population while ensuring sustainable use. In practice that means rubber, Brazil nuts, brown sugar from sugarcane, tobacco, manioc flour, bananas, watermelons, beans - the same mix Amazonian river-dwellers have made for generations, now with a federal decree backing their right to keep doing it.

## Paperwork and Place

After the creation decree came the bureaucracy. In December 2006, administrative responsibility shifted to the Acre office of IBAMA - just easier to manage from there than from Manaus. The same month, INCRA, Brazil's land reform institute, recognized the reserve as serving 150 families of small rural producers, qualifying them for federal family-farming credit. By 2009 the recognized number was 200 families. A deliberative council formed that August. The management plan was approved in August 2010. Today the reserve is administered by the Chico Mendes Institute for Biodiversity Conservation - the agency named for the rubber tapper whose murder helped birth this entire category of protected land.

## The River Is the Reserve

Arapixi from the air looks like green carpet veined with the brown loop of the Purus. From inside, it is louder than that. There are macaws screaming over the canopy, monkeys complaining at intruders, the slap of fish on the river surface, the chuck of a machete in a Brazil nut grove. Brazil nuts come from one of the tallest trees in the Amazon, and they fall hard - hard enough to kill a tapper unlucky enough to be underneath. So the work happens after the fall, gathering the cannonball-sized pods from the forest floor, splitting them open, drying the nuts on tarps, hauling them to the river to send downstream. The arrangement is fragile. Soybeans and cattle keep pushing west. The reserve's boundaries are lines on a map, defended by the families who live inside them and by the federal decree they fought to win.

## Flight Context

Located at 8.85S, 67.86W in southwestern Amazonas state, Brazil. The reserve straddles the Purus River in basin lowlands at about 100-150m elevation. Visual landmarks: the meandering Purus River with its oxbow lakes, the boundary with Acre state to the south. Nearest airports: Boca do Acre's Novo Campo (SWNK) about 60km east, Rio Branco (SBRB) about 115km south. Heavy cloud cover during November-April wet season.
