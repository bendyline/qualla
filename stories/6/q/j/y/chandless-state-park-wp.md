# Chandless State Park

A 695,000-hectare bamboo forest on the Brazil-Peru border so dense with thorns that researchers cannot easily walk through it, used in part by uncontacted indigenous people.

**Location:** -10.0223, -70.3603

**Categories:** Amazon rainforest, State parks, Brazil, Acre, Conservation, Bamboo forest

## Introduction

The forest is hard to penetrate because the bamboo has thorns. That is the first thing the management plan tells anyone who tries to enter Chandless State Park, a 695,303-hectare expanse of southwestern Brazilian Amazon stretching from the Peruvian border to the Cazumbá-Iracema Extractive Reserve. The park lies in the basin of the Purus River. The Chandless River enters from Peru and runs through it diagonally before joining the Purus to the north. The park headquarters can be reached by boat from Manoel Urbano or by chartered float plane from Rio Branco. There is no road. Some of what lives inside the bamboo is endemic and undescribed. Some of who lives inside the bamboo are members of the Mashco-Piro people, who have not been contacted by the outside world and who appear to want it to stay that way.

## A Park Inside a Corridor

Chandless State Park sits at the heart of what Brazilian conservation planners call the western Amazon green corridor. To the west across the international border lie Peru's Alto Purús National Park and Purús Communal Reserve. To the east is the Cazumbá-Iracema Extractive Reserve, where rubber tappers fought in the 1990s to keep their forest standing. To the north is the Alto Rio Purus Indigenous Territory. To the south is the Mamoadate Indigenous Territory. The park is not isolated. It is the connective tissue holding a continental-scale piece of intact forest together, allowing wide-ranging animals like jaguars, harpy eagles, and white-lipped peccaries to move across territories that human politics has divided. The park lies mostly in the municipality of Manoel Urbano, with portions in Santa Rosa do Purus and Sena Madureira. Created by Acre state decree on September 2, 2004, the park was set up explicitly to enable scientific research, environmental education, and ecotourism alongside strict ecological protection.

## Bamboo Country

The park sits at the center of the southwestern Amazon's bamboo forest region. Three endemic species of the Guadua bamboo genus grow here, and the bamboo dominates the vegetation classification: forest with palms, forest with bamboo, alluvial forest with bamboo, and dense forest. A 2010 Rapid Ecological Assessment for the park's management plan identified 264 plant species including 24 species of palm trees alone, 407 bird species, 47 species of large mammals, 63 amphibians, 40 reptiles, and 71 fish. Researchers estimate the actual numbers are far higher: at least 800 bird species including 13 endemic to bamboo habitats, 100 species of moths and butterflies, 200 mammals, 120 amphibians, 80 reptiles, and 200 fish. The flora and fauna remain poorly understood because so little of the park has been surveyed. Average annual rainfall runs between about 1,770 and 2,090 millimeters. The white-water rivers run high from October through May and lowest from June through September.

## The People Who Are Not There

Part of the park is used by nomadic Mashco-Piro people, who have refused contact with the outside world. Their presence shapes how the park is managed. There is no settlement push from the surrounding municipalities, which still have more than half of their settlement capacity unused, and the park is genuinely difficult to reach during the dry months. When the park was formed in 2004, twelve families lived inside the boundaries, about 60 people total, doing work that had little impact on the forest. They supported the park's creation and asked to help monitor it. The consultative council was formed on September 8, 2009, and the management plan approved on May 16, 2011. The park is administered by the Instituto de Meio Ambiente do Acre with support from the Amazon Region Protected Areas Program. Compared to many Amazon protected areas, Chandless faces unusually low settlement pressure, helped by how remote it is.

## Rapids, Ravines, and Beaches

The park sits on the Amazon plain at low elevation, but the topography is more varied than the satellite view suggests. Dry ravines drop fifteen meters in places, exposing layered sediment. Rocky outcrops break through the bamboo here and there. Small rapids interrupt the otherwise placid rivers, and where the Chandless and its tributaries curve, sandy beaches form during the dry season, then disappear under floodwater the rest of the year. Oxbow lakes mark places where the rivers have changed course over centuries, leaving crescent-shaped pools. The Cuchichá stream forms part of the northern border before joining the main Chandless. The park exists in part to keep all of this connected. Without the corridor, the protected pieces upstream in Peru and the indigenous territories north and south would be islands. With it, they form something closer to an ecosystem at the right scale.

## Flight Context

Chandless State Park sits at 10.02°S, 70.36°W in western Acre, Brazil, on the Peruvian border. Nearest airport is Rio Branco (SBRB), about 270 km east, which is where the park's float plane access originates. The park itself has no improved airfields. From altitude the park appears as unbroken green forest with the meandering Chandless River and its tributaries visible as dark sinuous lines, oxbow lakes glinting along the floodplain. Alto Purús National Park lies just across the international border to the west. Weather is wet tropical: low cloud common, afternoon thunderstorms during October-May. Best visibility June-September.
