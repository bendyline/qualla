# Aspero

Five thousand years ago, on the Pacific coast of what is now Peru, people built temple mounds and made the first ceremonial offerings of one of the world's oldest urban civilizations.

**Location:** -10.8156, -77.7410

**Categories:** Andean preceramic, Norte Chico civilization, Archaeological sites in the Department of Lima

## Introduction

Five thousand years ago, when the pyramids of Giza were still centuries in the future and Mesopotamian cities were just learning to write things down, the people of Aspero were already building monuments. They did it on a stretch of coast where the Supe River meets the Pacific, in a landscape of sandy hills and wetlands that today looks like nowhere special. The mounds they raised - layered up in tiers ten meters tall, full of clay figurines, feathers, and the remains of human sacrifice - belong to the Norte Chico culture, also called Caral-Supe. It is one of the oldest civilizations on Earth and the only one to grow up entirely without pottery.

## What They Built

Aspero covers about 14 hectares of low Pacific coastline, two kilometers south of the modern port of Supe Puerto and only 500 meters from the ocean. The site features more than a dozen mounds, the largest of them temple platforms the local people called huacas. The most prominent are the Huaca Alta, the Huaca de los Idolos, and the Huaca de los Sacrificios. Each was built up in stages, two or three terraces rising about ten meters above the surrounding ground - small by Egyptian or Maya standards, enormous in their own time and place. Inside, archaeologists have found ceremonial spaces, plazas, and large middens where the daily debris of a coastal people piled up over centuries. The Huaca de los Sacrificios got its name honestly. The remains found there of a sacrificed individual date to about 4,500 years ago. Aspero is real, and its temples are old.

## Fish Bones and a Theory

The middens of Aspero are crowded with what the residents ate, and the menu is overwhelmingly maritime. Fish hooks, nets, and the bones of anchovies and shellfish appear everywhere in the trash heaps. This led the archaeologist Michael Moseley in the 1970s to propose what he called the Maritime Foundations of Andean Civilization theory: that complex society in Peru first arose not from agriculture but from fishing. Most early civilizations needed grain surpluses to feed cities. The Norte Chico, Moseley argued, fed itself on sardines and anchovies pulled from the cold, productive Humboldt Current and on cotton grown for nets. The theory was controversial when proposed and remains so. Other scholars pointed to inland sites with irrigation agriculture and argued the cities grew up around farms, not fisheries. The argument has produced more research than most archaeological debates.

## Caral Up the Valley

Twenty-five kilometers up the Supe River from Aspero sits the city of Caral, the better-known centerpiece of the Norte Chico civilization. Carbon dating in the 1990s and 2000s placed both sites firmly in the Late Archaic period, with Aspero's dates running from roughly 3700 to 2500 BCE. They were trading partners. The current view among most archaeologists is that Aspero served as Caral's coastal port, providing dried fish and shellfish in exchange for cotton, beans, and other crops grown in the irrigated valley. This is not the maritime-only origin Moseley proposed - it is something more interesting, an early Andean economy already linking ocean to mountain to valley, already specializing, already trading. Aspero was not isolated. It was a node in a system that stretched up several river valleys at once.

## The Things They Left

The caches found inside Aspero's temple mounds say things words cannot. Clay figurines - some of them small human forms, some of them stylized into something stranger. Wooden bowls. Feathers. Cotton. String objects, cane objects, the small carefully-made things people offer when they want a god to listen. Aspero had no pottery. The Norte Chico civilization rose and fell without ever making fired ceramics, which makes them unusual among early civilizations. Cotton was their wealth. They wove it into nets that hauled in the anchovies, into clothing, into ritual offerings. The 4,500-year-old body in the Huaca de los Sacrificios was wrapped in textiles when it was placed there. Whoever wrapped it had reasons we will probably never recover. They left their dead and their offerings inside the mounds because the mounds mattered, because the act of building mattered, because something at Aspero needed to be sustained, and the sustaining required these gestures.

## What Remains, What Returns

Aspero today lies about two kilometers southwest of the Plazuela Grau in Supe Puerto, at the edge of the dunes. The mounds rise out of the sand like the natural hills they were once mistaken for. For almost thirty years the site served as the municipal dump for Supe Puerto and Supe, piling up more than 8,400 tons of waste. Since 2005, Ruth Shady's Caral team has cleared and excavated it, and visitors can now walk past the Huaca Alta, the Huaca de los Idolos and the Huaca de los Sacrificios, with a viewpoint and an interpretation center. The site is small, the temples are eroded, and the layers of human history are thin and easily lost. But the dates are real. The mounds were built before the Egyptians finished the Great Pyramid. The people who built them watched the same Pacific that visitors watch today, and the bones they left behind are the foundations of one of the oldest urban traditions in the world.

## Flight Context

Located at 10.82S, 77.74W on the central coast of Peru, in Lima Department's Barranca Province. The site sits about 500m from the Pacific between sandy hills and wetlands. Visual landmarks: the Supe River mouth, Supe Puerto immediately north, Pacific coastline. Nearest airports: Lima Jorge Chavez (SPJC) about 150km south-southeast; a small general-aviation strip near Huacho is closer. Coastal fog (garua) is common in the morning, especially May-October.
