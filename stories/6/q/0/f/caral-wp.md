# Caral

The 5,000-year-old Sacred City of Caral-Supe, often called the oldest city in the Americas, built without weapons or warfare in a Peruvian desert valley while pyramids rose in Egypt and ziggurats in Mesopotamia.

**Location:** -10.8936, -77.5203

**Categories:** archaeology, ancient-civilizations, unesco, pyramids, andean, peru

## Introduction

No weapons. No defensive walls. No mutilated bodies. In a city that grew over six centuries and housed three thousand people, archaeologists found 32 flutes made from condor and pelican bones, 37 cornetts of deer and llama bone, and almost nothing that suggests organized violence. This is the puzzle of Caral. Often called the oldest city in the Americas, contemporary with the Egyptian pyramids and the great cities of Mesopotamia, appears to have been built and sustained by a society organized around commerce, music, and ceremony rather than warfare. The site sits in a desert valley 182 kilometers north of Lima, just 23 kilometers from the Pacific coast, and it has been quietly forcing scholars to reconsider what early civilization had to look like.

## A City Built Before the Pottery

The Sacred City of Caral-Supe occupies more than 60 hectares in the arid Supe Valley of central Peru. It was inhabited from roughly 2600 BCE to 2000 BCE, during what archaeologists call the Late Archaic and Lower Formative periods. The Caral civilization developed about 1,500 years earlier than the Olmec, who founded the first complex society of Mesoamerica. The radiocarbon date of 2627 BCE for the city is based on reed and woven carrying bags found on site, which were used to haul stones for the temples. Max Uhle came across the site in 1905 and archaeologist Paul Kosok studied it in 1948, but it received little attention because it lacked the pottery that excavators of that era expected to find. It was Ruth Shady, a Peruvian archaeologist, whose later work proved Caral was both immensely older than anyone had assumed and entirely without ceramics.

## The Pyramids of the Supe Valley

Seven major pyramids and dozens of smaller structures spread across the central area of Caral. The Main Temple complex measures 150 meters long, 110 meters wide, and 28 meters tall, a substantial monument by any era's standards. Caral was flanked by 19 other temple complexes scattered across roughly 90 square kilometers of the Supe Valley, suggesting a total population of around 20,000 people sharing the same culture. Closely related sites include El Aspero, an early fishing city on the coast at the mouth of the Supe River, where excavators have found human remains including the burial of an elite woman from around 2500 BCE and, separately, evidence of human sacrifices involving two children and a newborn. The relationship between coastal fishing communities and inland agricultural settlements appears to have been the economic foundation of the entire civilization.

## Music, Trade, and No Sign of War

What makes Caral distinct among the world's earliest civilizations is what is missing from it. Ruth Shady's findings indicate a society built on commerce and what she has called pleasure rather than coercion. No battlements have been found. No weapons. No depictions of warfare in the iconography. This stands in clear contrast to the older site of Sechin Bajo in the Casma Valley to the north, where a clay relief frieze depicts weapons. At Caral, archaeologists have instead recovered musical instruments by the dozen, including the 32 flutes and 37 cornetts already mentioned. They found a knotted textile that the excavators identified as a quipu, a precursor to the much later Inca record-keeping system, suggesting that a kind of information storage was already in use 4,500 years ago. They found burial of a baby wrapped with a stone-bead necklace. They found, in short, a city.

## The Upper and Lower Halves

Caral was divided. The Upper Half contained six monumental complexes, each combining a pyramid, an open plaza, and residential buildings. The Lower Half housed laborers in less elaborate quarters, with a poorer diet documented in the bones recovered there. The river served as the divider between the two zones. Archaeologists now believe the city was intentionally planned this way, with the monumental architecture of the Upper Half designed both to house the elite and to make their political power physically visible. This arrangement is one of the earliest known examples of structured social inequality in the Americas, perpetuating distinctions that would become characteristic of later Andean civilizations. The city is, in this respect, both peaceful and unequal, a combination that complicates simple stories about how complexity emerges.

## Modern Threats and Continuing Discovery

UNESCO declared Caral a World Heritage Site in 2009. In early 2021, in the middle of the COVID pandemic, tensions broke out over squatters who, since the 2020 lockdown, had been claiming land and building houses on parts of the protected zone. Lead archaeologist Ruth Shady received death threats. Reports surfaced of looting and of a burial site containing mummies being partly destroyed. The Peruvian government responded, but the episode revealed how vulnerable the site remains. The work of discovery continues nonetheless: in 2025, the related citadel of Penico, part of the wider Caral civilization, opened to the public for the first time. Scholars believe research at Caral may eventually answer fundamental questions about how Andean civilizations originated and how the first cities of South America developed. The pyramids in the desert are not done teaching.
