# Caral

Visiting what is often called the oldest city in the Americas, a 5,000-year-old UNESCO site of seven monumental pyramids in Peru's Supe Valley, where you walk past avocado farms to reach a civilization contemporary with Egypt and Mesopotamia.

**Location:** -10.8936, -77.5203

**Categories:** archaeology, unesco, ancient-civilizations, pyramids, peru

## Introduction

Cross a pedestrian bridge over the Rio Supe, walk another 1.7 kilometers along a dry desert path, and you arrive at what is often called the oldest city in the Americas. Caral was built about 5,000 years ago, while the Egyptian pyramids were rising on the Nile and Mesopotamian cities were taking shape in the Fertile Crescent. The site sits 182 kilometers north of Lima in the Supe Valley, a green ribbon of irrigated farmland cutting through the brown desert. Most visitors never make it here. The journey takes most of a day, the signage is partly in Spanish only, and the surrounding landscape looks like nothing in particular until you reach the pyramids and realize what you are looking at.

## Getting to the Sacred City

There is no easy direct route. From Lima, the trip runs five to six hours one-way by public transport. The most reliable approach goes through Barranca, the nearest sizeable town with proper amenities. From Barranca, take a minibus to the small town of Supe (cost 2.50 soles), then catch a combi at the corner of Centro Supe and Jiron La Mar to the village of Caral itself, 23 kilometers inland. From the village, walk 1.2 kilometers to a pedestrian bridge over the Rio Supe, then another 1.7 kilometers to the site museum. Tours from Lima leave only on weekends and run about three hours each way. By car, two main routes work: from the south, turn off the Panamericana Norte at Tiroles onto Desvio Caral; from the north, take road 102 east of Supe.

## What You See When You Arrive

The site covers 66 hectares and contains roughly 18 ruins, of which seven large pyramids dominate. The biggest, the Main Temple, is 150 meters long, 110 meters wide, and 28 meters tall. There is an interpretation center near the entrance, though its texts run in Spanish only. Most signboards on the site itself are bilingual. Caral was built by people who had not yet developed pottery, and the absence of ceramic finds initially led archaeologists to overlook the place. What they did leave behind is extraordinary: monumental architecture, plazas, residential complexes, sunken circular courts, and along the Supe Valley nearby, more than a dozen contemporary settlements including Allcapoto, Cerro Colorado, Pueblo Nuevo, Pando, Era de Pando, Piedra Parada, Chupacigarro, Rurihuasi, Lurihuasi, Miraya, and the coastal site of El Aspero with its own museum.

## The Site Under Pressure

During the 2020 COVID pandemic, reduced security at Caral led to a serious threat: land developers and squatters began encroaching on the protected zone, destroying parts of the site to plant avocado and fruit trees. There were reports of looting, and of a burial site containing mummies being partly destroyed. The conflict turned dangerous; the lead archaeologist, Ruth Shady, received death threats. The Peruvian government has since pushed back, but the situation revealed how fragile a 5,000-year-old site can be when it sits in the middle of a productive agricultural valley with rising real estate value. Visitors are now welcomed back, and the site continues to expand its reach: in 2025, the related citadel of Penico opened to the public for the first time, adding another piece of the Caral civilization to the visitor circuit.

## Practical Realities

Caral is in a desert, and the desert is honest about that. There is little shelter on the site once you leave the reception area. Bring water, sunscreen, and a hat. Food options are limited; small stalls operate near the reception and the bridge, with more on weekends than weekdays. Caral village has a few places to eat. Supe has a small market. Most experienced visitors stay overnight in Barranca, which has hotels and restaurants. There is no formal luggage storage anywhere on the route; you can ask at the bus counter or the site entrance if you need to leave bags. The dry season runs roughly May through November, when it is sometimes possible to drive across the Rio Supe via road 102, avoiding the pedestrian bridge altogether.

## Why It's Worth the Effort

Caral is not a casual day trip. Getting there is genuinely involved. But the place rewards the effort in a way that more famous sites sometimes don't. You can walk freely among the pyramids. You are usually one of very few visitors. The civilization that built this complex did so without writing, without metal tools, without pottery. They raised some of the earliest monumental architecture in the Americas. The Caral people lived in this valley for more than a thousand years, then left, leaving stone-and-earth structures that the dry desert has preserved with unusual fidelity. UNESCO inscribed the Sacred City of Caral-Supe on the World Heritage list in 2009. From Caral, you can leave only by going back through Supe or Barranca, which means you also have time, on the return trip, to think about what you just saw.

## Flight Context

Located at 10.89 degrees south, 77.52 degrees west, in the Supe Valley about 182 kilometers north of Lima and 23 kilometers inland from the Pacific coast. Elevation is 350 meters. The site is visible from cruising altitude as a complex of pyramidal mounds along the green ribbon of the irrigated Supe Valley cutting through the surrounding tan desert. Lima Jorge Chavez International Airport (LIM) is the nearest major airport.
