# Bandurria

An archaeological site on Peru's Pacific coast that may be older than the famous Caral - a 5,200-year-old urban settlement with a sunken plaza and stone temples, half-destroyed by a 1973 irrigation project.

**Location:** -11.1871, -77.5884

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in the Department of Lima, Andean preceramic

## Introduction

In 1973, an irrigation project north of Lima cut a channel through what looked like a low rocky hill outside the small town of Huacho. The channel began infiltrating water into a site nobody knew was there. By the time the archaeologists arrived, much of it was gone - a domestic settlement covering most of the 54 hectares had already been ruined. What remained turned out to be older than Caral, the celebrated Norte Chico city up the coast. Bandurria, named for a wading bird called the buff-necked ibis, may be the oldest urban center in the Americas. Most of it is now under water or under squatters' shacks.

## Discovery by Disaster

The site sits about three kilometers south of Huacho, near a Pacific beach called Playa Chica at kilometer 141 of the Panamericana Norte highway. Domingo Torero Fernandez de Cordova found it in 1973 - by accident, because the Santa Rosa irrigation project had just started destroying it. Archaeologist Rosa Fung began investigations the same year and continued in 1977. Her work concluded that Bandurria belonged to the Late Archaic period, somewhere from 4000 to 2000 BCE. Some of her radiocarbon dates fell between 2,500 and 2,300 BCE. Then the site sat largely unstudied for almost three decades. Squatters moved in. Shacks went up on the surviving mounds. Salt water from the irrigation system kept seeping through the lower levels.

## What the Stone Hills Were

When Alejandro Chu's team restarted excavations in August 2005, they began with what looked like four low rocky hills surrounded by smaller bumps. A close survey of the surfaces showed the hills were not natural at all. They were monumental architecture - stone-and-mortar mounds raised by human hands over generations. The site has at least four major mounds in the monumental sector, with smaller mounds clustered around them. The architecture turned out to share key features with Caral and other Supe Valley sites: a sunken circular plaza, ceremonial stairways, structures built in symmetrical patterns. It was, in other words, recognizably a Norte Chico ceremonial center. But the carbon dates Chu got back in 2007 placed Bandurria at 3200 BCE - older than Caral itself, which had previously been called the oldest city in the New World.

## The Coast Was First

Bandurria fits into a growing list of very old coastal sites in Peru that keep pushing the dates back. Sechin Bajo, on the central coast, has yielded dates as early as 3600 BCE. Huaricanga in the Fortaleza Valley dates to about 3500 BCE. Bandurria itself sits at 3200 BCE in current readings, possibly earlier. Caral, 33 kilometers northeast, is younger than all of them. What this means is still being argued. Coastal sites had access to one of the most productive marine ecosystems on Earth - the Humboldt Current, with its anchovies and shellfish - and the early monumental architecture along this coast may have grown up faster than inland farming sites because the food was easier. Bandurria represents an early coastal fishing settlement that built ceremonial stone architecture before anyone, anywhere in the Americas, did it differently.

## The Bird and the Wetland

The site got its name from the buff-necked ibis - bandurria in Spanish - that still wades in the wetlands nearby. The ibis is a tall, gray-brown bird with a long downcurved beak and a bare buff-colored throat patch, making the rolling honking call that gave it its onomatopoeic name. The wetlands and lagoons along this stretch of coast support thousands of shorebirds and waterfowl. Five thousand years ago, those same wetlands would have been a food source for the people building the mounds - a year-round supply of birds, fish, frogs, and reeds, in addition to the abundant marine life from the nearby Pacific. The early urbanism here was made possible by an ecological accident: the cold Humboldt Current upwelling against a desert coast, the wetlands trapping freshwater between sea and sand, the river mouths producing fish in industrial quantities. The Norte Chico took advantage of all of it.

## What Can Still Be Saved

The Directorate General of Archaeological Heritage of the Peruvian National Institute of Culture has tried, with limited resources, to protect what is left of Bandurria. The regional government has helped. Students from the Faculty of Management and Tourism at Jose Faustino Sanchez Carrion National University in Huacho have contributed labor and visitor management. The site is open to visitors who know to look for it. The signage is sparse, the surviving mound visible from the road but easy to miss, and most travelers who visit Caral never realize there is something older - and arguably more important - just down the highway. Bandurria is what early urbanism in the Americas looks like before it became spectacular. It is a few low stone mounds with a sunken plaza, surrounded by wetlands and the sea, mostly destroyed by accident, partly preserved by stubbornness.

## Flight Context

Located at 11.19S, 77.59W on the central coast of Peru, in Lima Department's Huaura Province. The site sits near the Pacific shore at kilometer 141 of the Panamericana Norte highway. Visual landmarks: Huacho immediately north, Playa Chica beach, Pacific coastline, coastal wetlands. Nearest airports: Lima Jorge Chavez (SPIM) about 130km south, Huacho (SPHO) just north. Coastal fog (garua) common in the morning, especially May-October.
