# Cordillera Huayhuash

A thirty-kilometer cluster of impossible peaks south of the Cordillera Blanca where Joe Simpson cut the rope on his climbing partner and lived to write about it.

**Location:** -10.3000, -76.9000

**Categories:** Mountain ranges of Peru, Andes, Mountaineering, Reserved zones of Peru

## Introduction

In June 1985, two British climbers named Joe Simpson and Simon Yates summited a 6,344-meter peak in the Cordillera Huayhuash called Siula Grande. On the descent, Simpson broke his leg badly. Yates, in a snowstorm and unable to lower his partner safely, eventually faced an impossible choice: cut the rope and probably let Simpson die, or be pulled off the mountain himself and certainly die. He cut the rope. Simpson fell into a crevasse, survived, and over the next three days dragged himself back to base camp on one functioning leg. He later wrote a book about it called Touching the Void, which became one of the defining mountaineering accounts of the twentieth century. The mountain that nearly killed him is the second-highest peak in this small, ferocious range.

## Thirty Kilometers of Vertical

The Huayhuash range runs only thirty kilometers north to south, making it one of the most compact serious mountain ranges in the world. Inside that narrow ribbon are seven peaks above 6,000 meters, including Yerupajá at 6,617 meters - the second-highest mountain in Peru after Huascarán. Compared to the neighboring Cordillera Blanca, which sprawls across 200 kilometers, Huayhuash is denser, narrower, with higher passes and fewer escape routes. The Quechua name possibly comes from waywash, meaning weasel, or waywashi, meaning squirrel. The mountains earned their reputation through difficulty: many of these peaks were not climbed until the 1950s and 1960s, and several routes are still considered technical objectives at the upper end of what high-altitude alpinism permits.

## Geology of an Old Sea

The bones of these mountains were laid down underwater. The range is built primarily of limestone, with sandstone and slate mixed in - sedimentary rocks that began as ocean floor deposits. Tectonic plate convergence pushed them upward and folded them into the shapes you see today. Hike along the trails and you can occasionally find marine fossils embedded in the rock: bivalves, ammonites, the extinct ancestors of squid that swam these mountains when they were still seabed. The crags above the trail at Carhuacocha or Mitucocha are made of fossilized coastline. The glaciers above scrape down through layers of stone that were ocean before they were summit, which is true of many mountain ranges but rarely stated quite so directly by the rock you walk on.

## The Circuit

Most visitors come to walk around the range rather than up it. The classic Huayhuash trek loops the entire massif, takes 8 to 12 days, and crosses passes above 4,800 meters. The Alpine Circuit, the harder version, gets closer to the peaks and involves rock climbing and glacier travel over 8 to 10 days. Most campsites sit above the 4,000-meter treeline, so the views are unbroken. Glacial lakes appear at every turn: Solteracocha, Siula, Jahuacocha, Carhuacocha, Sarapococha, each one a different shade of cold blue. Hot springs at the Viconga campsite offer a moment of warmth in the middle of the high cold. Condors patrol the cliffs. Llamas, alpacas, and viscachas - the rabbit-like rodents that look like cartoons of themselves - watch hikers pass with mild interest.

## The Shining Path Years

Throughout the 1980s, the Cordillera Huayhuash was used as a remote base by the Maoist guerrilla group known as the Shining Path. The remoteness that made it appealing to climbers also made it useful to insurgents. On July 28, 1988, a group of Canadian and Peruvian climbers were held hostage for twelve hours after a failed assassination attempt on military police; nobody on either side of the climbers and police was hurt, though one Senderista died. In the late 1980s, European trekkers were robbed and told to carry a message back to Huaraz: future intruders would be killed. The Shining Path was effectively defeated in 1992 with the capture of its leader Abimael Guzmán. The range emptied of guerrillas, but new dangers emerged: in August 2002, two foreign trekkers were murdered near Cajatambo in what appears to have been a robbery, and in 2004 four hikers who resisted armed robbery were shot, with one bleeding to death before rescue arrived. The local communities subsequently began charging passage fees in exchange for protection. Since then, the range is generally considered safe.

## What the Mountains Hold

Near the north shore of Lake Viconga, the remains of a Shining Path base camp can still be visited - a shooting range, barracks, and training field, slowly being reclaimed by Andean grass. The ecosystem above the treeline is dominated by tussock grasses and patches of Polylepis, the high-altitude red-barked tree that has adapted to growing where almost nothing else will. Andean alder, taruca deer, peregrine falcons, the endangered Andean mountain cat, the giant conebill bird so distinctive it was once thought to be its own family - all these are residents. In 2002, the Peruvian Ministry of Agriculture declared the entire range a Reserved Zone, prohibiting future mining. The Huayhuash now exists as one of the more strictly protected mountain ranges in Peru, partly through good policy, partly because its difficulty has done much of the protecting itself.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 10.30°S, 76.90°W, in the central Peruvian Andes south of the Cordillera Blanca. Maximum elevation 6,617 meters at Yerupajá. Recommended viewing altitude: 8,000-9,000 meters AGL minimum to clear the highest peaks safely. The range is compact - about 30 km north-south - and densely packed with serious terrain. Mountain weather changes rapidly; visibility best in the dry winter months of May through September. Nearest airport is Anta - Comandante FAP German Arias Graziani Airport (SPHZ) at Huaraz, about 100 km northwest. Lima Region, Ancash Region, and Huánuco Region all converge in this range.
