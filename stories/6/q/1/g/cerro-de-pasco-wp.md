# Cerro de Pasco

One of the highest cities on Earth, where 58,000 people live around an open-pit mine that has been eating their hometown for four centuries.

**Location:** -10.6864, -76.2625

**Categories:** High-altitude cities, Mining, Peru, Andes, Industrial history

## Introduction

The mine arrived first, and the city grew up around it. Since 1630, when Spanish miners discovered silver in the Andean tundra at 4,330 meters, Cerro de Pasco has been digging itself out of existence. Today the open pit cuts a wound nearly two kilometers long in the heart of town, swallowing buildings, streets, and neighborhoods as it expands. The 58,899 people who still live here breathe air laced with lead and cadmium and look out their windows at one of the largest urban excavations on Earth. A 2008 Peruvian law proposed evacuating the entire city and rebuilding it elsewhere. Nearly two decades later, no one has moved. Cerro de Pasco is one of the highest cities in the world with more than 50,000 inhabitants, and it is being demolished by the same industry that built it.

## Living Above the Tree Line

At 4,330 meters above sea level, Cerro de Pasco sits in a climate zone where trees cannot grow. The Köppen system classifies it as alpine tundra, the same category as Arctic landscapes near sea level, and the surrounding countryside has the bare, scoured look that altitude imposes. The average annual temperature is 5.5 degrees Celsius. Snow falls in any month, usually around dawn. The warmest months never average above 10 degrees Celsius, the threshold for tree growth, which is why the hills outside town look like they belong on the moon. The city is connected to Lima by 300 kilometers of road and by the Ferrocarril Central Andino, the railway that climbs up out of the Pacific desert and over the Andes, gaining nearly five kilometers of elevation. The football stadium downtown, named for local hero Daniel Alcides Carrión, is one of the highest in the world. Visitors who arrive without acclimatizing often find themselves struggling to climb a flight of stairs.

## Four Centuries of Digging

The Spanish began mining the silver-bearing oxide deposits in 1630, and they kept at it for nearly two hundred years until the Peruvian War of Independence brought everything to a halt between 1820 and 1825. In 1816, a Swiss-born mining entrepreneur named Francisco Uville arranged for steam engines designed by the Cornish engineer Richard Trevithick to be hauled up to Cerro de Pasco to pump water out of flooding shafts, which was an extraordinary engineering feat for the time. The geology underneath the city is the wreckage of an ancient caldera. Late-stage volcanism created hydrothermal veins running through Jurassic Pucara limestone, depositing galena, sphalerite, and pyrite alongside the silver. Today the operation extracts copper, zinc, and lead in addition to silver. The Atacocha and Milpo mines opened nearby in the twentieth century. By the 1930s, Cerro de Pasco had become Peru's most important mining center, and it remains active today, with the open pit creeping outward year after year.

## The American Colony

In 1902, at a dinner party in New York, the financier James Ben Ali Haggin raised ten million dollars in a single evening to begin extracting copper from Cerro de Pasco. He put up three million himself and kept thirty-four percent. By 1913, the syndicate that bought the mines included J. P. Morgan, Henry Clay Frick, Phoebe Hearst, and members of the Vanderbilt family. The Cerro de Pasco Mining Company built an American enclave at 4,300 meters, complete with stone cottages, a hospital, baseball diamonds, tennis courts, and a Masonic lodge they called the Roof of the World. The labor system, known as enganche or hooking, advanced cash loans to indigenous Andean workers who could only repay through labor in the mines. It was, as one scholar put it plainly, a debt bondage system. By 1929 the company employed thousands of Andean miners. Then the Great Depression hit, the company laid off two-thirds of its indigenous workforce, and labor unrest exploded. The American Popular Revolutionary Alliance gained recruits in the city. In 1930 a teaching sister in the city wrote that "all women have had to come to Lima for safety."

## What the Mine Eats

Cerro de Pasco's open pit is not on the edge of town. It is in the middle of town. National Geographic, in a 2015 piece titled "High in the Andes, A Mine Eats a 400-Year-Old City," documented the pit advancing block by block, with houses, schools, and a hospital demolished in its path. Heavy metal contamination from centuries of mining has poisoned the water and soil. Studies of children in the city have found dangerously elevated blood lead levels, with measurable cognitive and developmental impacts. The 2008 relocation law, intended to move the entire population to a new site nearby, has not been implemented. Many residents oppose leaving anyway, because their homes, jobs, and family histories are here. The miners who descend into the shafts every day are the descendants of the men the company called refractory a century ago. They live in a city named for the silver that built it and being demolished for the copper that succeeded it, at an altitude where every act of resistance, including breathing, requires effort.

## Flight Context

Cerro de Pasco sits at 10.69°S, 76.26°W, in central Peru at 4,330 m elevation. Nearest airport is Huánuco (SPNC) about 90 km north or Jauja (SPJJ) about 150 km southeast. Lima (SPJC) lies about 175 km southwest (300 km by road or the Ferrocarril Central Andino). From cruising altitude the city is unmistakable: a dense urban grid with a vast open-pit mine cut directly into its center, the pit visible as a stepped grey-brown void perhaps 1.5-2 km across. Mountain weather is volatile; expect afternoon convection and frequent low cloud.
