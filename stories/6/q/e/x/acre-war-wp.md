# Acre War

Between 1899 and 1903, Brazilian rubber tappers fought three small wars and proclaimed three short-lived republics to wrest a piece of Amazonia the size of five Belgiums from Bolivia.

**Location:** -7.1142, -73.8011

**Categories:** history, war, amazon, brazil, bolivia, rubber-history

## Introduction

At five in the morning on August 6, 1902, a 26-year-old gaúcho named José Plácido de Castro led 33 men in canoes up the Acre River and quietly took the Bolivian quartermaster depot in the town of Xapuri. The locals, watching the men move through the streets, mistook them for part of the Bolivian Independence Day celebrations. By the time anyone realized what was actually happening, Castro had locked up the Bolivian soldiers and started the second phase of a war that would, over the next eighteen months, hand Brazil a piece of Amazonia roughly five times the size of Belgium.

## Why a Forest Became Worth a War

Synthetic rubber was not invented until around the Second World War. Until then, the only commercially viable source was Hevea brasiliensis, the rubber tree of the Amazon. By the late 19th century, the global automotive industry was inhaling rubber for tires, hoses, belts, and seals, and prices climbed accordingly. About 18,000 settlers and adventurers, most of them Brazilians from drought-stricken northeastern states, pushed into the territory of Acre to tap rubber. There were almost no roads. River steamers, canoes, and rafts were the only way in or out. The Treaty of Ayacucho in 1867 had said this land belonged to Bolivia, but Bolivia had little ability to enforce that claim across a forest more than 1,500 kilometers from La Paz.

## Three Republics in Five Years

The first crisis came on January 2, 1899, when Bolivia opened a customs office at Puerto Alonso to start taxing the rubber. The Brazilian settlers were furious. A lawyer named Dr. José de Carvalho led a revolt that drove out the Bolivians on April 30 of that year. To stop them returning, the governor of Amazonas sent in a Spaniard named Luis Gálvez Rodríguez de Arias, who had served as Bolivian consul in Belém. On July 14, 1899, Gálvez proclaimed the First Republic of Acre. It lasted until March 15, 1900, when a Brazilian war flotilla, embarrassed by the awkwardness of supporting an illegal land grab, arrested Gálvez and dissolved the republic. A second uprising in November 1900, the Expedition of the Poets, declared the Second Republic. It was crushed by Bolivian forces around Christmas of that year.

## The Bolivian Syndicate

Frustrated by its inability to govern Acre and unwilling to lose the rubber revenue, on June 11, 1901, Bolivia signed an extraordinary lease with what was called the Bolivian Trading Company, more often known as the Bolivian Syndicate of New York City. The shareholders included the king of Belgium and relatives of the sitting U.S. President William McKinley. Under the agreement, U.S. and British capitalists would assume near-total control of Acre for 30 years, occupying it and exploiting it on Bolivia's behalf. Brazil and Peru were furious. Brazil withdrew its consul, closed Amazon tributaries to Bolivian trade, and made it clear that no foreign syndicate was going to administer Amazonian forest from Jersey City. The international powers protested the river closures, and Brazil eventually relented on most of them, but the syndicate scheme had poisoned the diplomatic well.

## Plácido de Castro's War

In 1902 the rubber tappers of Acre offered José Plácido de Castro, a young veteran of the Federalist Revolution in Rio Grande do Sul, the chance to train and command an army of 2,000 seringueiros against the Bolivians. After the August 6 surprise at Xapuri, Castro's force was ambushed on September 18 by a 180-man Bolivian battalion under Colonel Rosendo Rojas. Outgunned and reduced by tropical disease, Castro lost twenty men. He recruited again. By October 14, his force had captured the river vessel Río Afua, renaming it Independence and turning its guns against Puerto Alonso. On January 24, 1903, the Bolivians at Puerto Alonso surrendered. Three days later Castro proclaimed the Third Republic of Acre, this time with the open backing of President Rodrigues Alves and his celebrated Foreign Minister, the Baron of Rio Branco.

## The Treaty That Closed the Map

Diplomacy now took over from rifles. On March 21, 1903, Brazil and Bolivia signed a preliminary treaty. On November 11, 1903, they ratified the Treaty of Petrópolis. Bolivia ceded approximately 191,000 square kilometers, the bulk of which became the Brazilian state of Acre. In exchange, Bolivia received a small piece of Mato Grosso, two million pounds sterling, and Brazil's promise to build the Madeira-Mamoré Railway, which would in theory let Bolivia move its own goods down to the Atlantic. The Bolivian Syndicate was paid 110,000 pounds to walk away. Castro became the first president of the Acre Territory. He died on August 11, 1908. Three towns now bear the names of the men who made Acre Brazilian: Rio Branco for the foreign minister, Plácido de Castro for the gaúcho commander, and Assis Brazil for the diplomat who signed the treaty. The forest that started it all is still standing, mostly.

## Flight Context

Located at 7.11°S, 73.80°W in the western Amazon basin, in present-day Brazilian Acre near the Peruvian border. The Acre and Purús river basins were the central theater of the war. From the air, the green river-laced expanse of the Amazon dominates the view, with the Andean foothills visible far to the west. Nearest airports are Cruzeiro do Sul International (SBCZ) and Rio Branco International (SBRB). Best viewing 10,000 to 18,000 feet AGL; cloud cover is heavy year-round, with clearer skies in the dry season June through September.
