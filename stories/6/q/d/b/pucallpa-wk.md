# Pucallpa

Peru's second-busiest Amazon port, a mototaxi city on the Ucayali where the jungle still shapes everything - including where you sleep and how you get there.

**Location:** -8.3833, -74.5500

**Categories:** peru, amazon, peruvian-amazon, ucayali, river-port

## Introduction

You hear Pucallpa before you see it. The buzz of a hundred thousand two-stroke engines, because mototaxis - motorcycle tricycles with rickety passenger seats bolted on the back - are how anybody gets anywhere here. Two soles for a ride downtown. Three from the outskirts. Four across the river to Yarinacocha. The buzz is continuous, mixing with the shouts of vendors in Mercado Numero 3 and the low chug of boats along the Ucayali, which is the real street of this city. Pucallpa sits on the left bank of a major Amazon tributary that flows eventually into the main stem near Iquitos. It is the second most important river port in the Peruvian Amazon after Iquitos itself, and like Iquitos it is a place where the jungle decides what happens.

## A Port of the Jungle

Pucallpa was a Shipibo settlement long before it was a Peruvian city, and the Shipibo-Conibo people remain the most visible indigenous community in the region - their geometric kené textile patterns sold in markets alongside hammocks and carvings from other Amazonian groups. The city proper grew up around river commerce, connecting the interior Ucayali valley to the outside world through a combination of boat, plane, and eventually road. The Federico Basadre highway is the main land corridor, linking the northwestern side of town out to Captain Rolden International Airport. Boats run upstream to Atalaya or downstream to Iquitos all year round - a river journey of days, not hours, measured in sunsets and how much your hammock sways when the wake of a bigger vessel passes. There is no tourist veneer here. Pucallpa is a working city that happens to sit on one of the world's most remarkable rivers.

## How You Get Here

There are three ways in, none of them fast. You can fly from Lima, which is the obvious choice. You can come by boat from Iquitos, a downstream journey of several days, or upstream from Atalaya, somewhat less. Or you can attempt the bus from Lima - about 18 hours on a good run with Transmar or Movil Tours for roughly 100 soles, crossing the Andes and descending through cloud forest and finally into the selva. The road past Huánuco turns foggy with no warning. Past Tingo María, sections have been destroyed by landslides. You can get through, but slowly, through sections of mud. And the road between Tingo María and Pucallpa carries a long and ugly reputation for highway robbery - men who board buses as passengers, then stop the vehicle at a set location while accomplices arrive to strip everyone of valuables. It happens less now that the road is mostly paved, but local advice stays consistent: never travel it after dark, and avoid December when the thieves know everyone is carrying Christmas money.

## Yarinacocha

The lake is the reason most travelers stay longer than they planned. Yarinacocha is an oxbow lake of the Ucayali, a crescent of tea-dark water a few kilometers outside downtown Pucallpa, reached by mototaxi for about four soles. At its center is La Isla de Amor, an island of forest and small beaches that is a pleasant day trip. The banks of Yarinacocha are lined with Shipibo communities where you can buy textiles directly from the women who make them. The tourist infrastructure is light - a few simple restaurants, places to rent canoes - but the quiet after Pucallpa's mototaxi buzz is its own pleasure. Parque Natural, closer to the city, offers eco-walks and introductions to Amazonian flora and fauna. Cultural tourism here means, specifically, the visits travelers make to curanderos and shamans offering ayahuasca ceremonies - a practice the Shipibo developed over generations and which has become, for better or worse, one of Pucallpa's main draws for international visitors.

## What to Buy, Where to Eat

The markets are the city's circulation system. Mercado Numero 3, nearest the Ucayali River, is the best place for hammocks - essential equipment if you plan to take a riverboat anywhere. Booths around it sell artifacts and crafts from indigenous tribes across the region. Three grocery stores stock imports of varying origin: Los Andes carries European and North American products, Fisa has some Asian items no one else stocks, and Valdivia near the Plaza de Armas is the best bet for liquor and wine. El Huecito is where you go for inexpensive custom-built furniture. Sleep is cheap around the Plaza de Armas - hostels from 10 soles per bed - and the food is Amazonian: fish from the Ucayali, tacacho with fried bananas, juane served on bijao leaves at festival time. Pucallpa does not pretend to be anything other than what it is. That honesty is half its charm, and every traveler who spends a few days here leaves with a mototaxi buzz in their ears that takes a while to fade.

## Flight Context

Located at 8.38 degrees S, 74.55 degrees W on the Ucayali River in Peru's Ucayali Region. Main airport is Pucallpa's Cap. FAP David Abensur Rengifo International (SPCL) at 154 meters elevation. Boat access upstream from Atalaya or downstream from Iquitos. Road access via the Federico Basadre highway; buses from Lima take 18+ hours. Cruise altitudes over the selva should account for afternoon convective buildup common in Amazonian weather patterns. Morning operations advised. Dry season June through October gives best visibility; rainy season November through April can produce strong thunderstorms especially in late afternoon.
