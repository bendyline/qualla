# Chavín culture

The pre-Columbian Andean civilization that built drainage canals to make their temple roar like a jaguar and carved a four-and-a-half-meter granite shaft you could only see in fragments through narrow openings.

**Location:** -9.5938, -77.1774

**Categories:** Pre-Columbian, Andean civilizations, Archaeology, Peru, Religion

## Introduction

When the rains came down on the central Andes, water would rush through the canals built beneath the temple at Chavín de Huántar and the temple would roar. The Chavín priests had designed it that way. The drainage system that prevented seasonal flooding doubled as an acoustic instrument, and the sound it produced is said to have resembled a jaguar, the most sacred animal in the Chavín cosmology. The temple did not just stand on the landscape. It made noise during the storm. The Chavín culture flourished in northern Andean Peru from around 900 BCE to 250 BCE, named for the site at Chavín de Huántar where they built granite temples, carved a fanged stone deity that you could only see in pieces, and exerted cultural influence across an enormous portion of what would later become Peru. We do not know what they called themselves. Their language is lost.

## Engineering at 3,150 Meters

The Chavín built their best-known temple at 3,150 meters above sea level in the Mosna Valley of the present-day Ancash Region, where the Mosna and Huachecsa rivers join. They had a problem common to highland Peru: the rainy season would have washed their architecture away. Their solution was a network of drainage canals beneath the temple, sized and angled to channel torrential rainwater safely through the structure. As a side effect, the rushing water created a roaring sound. The temple's stone was white granite and black limestone, neither of which is found near the site, which means leaders organized labor to bring building materials from far away or arranged trade with other peoples. The Old Temple was constructed between roughly 900 and 500 BCE. The New Temple, built onto the older structure, went up between roughly 500 and 200 BCE. Around the temples, the Chavín domesticated llamas as pack animals and for fiber and meat, produced llama jerky called ch'arki for trade, and grew potatoes, quinoa, and some maize using irrigation.

## The Lanzón

The most extraordinary artifact at Chavín de Huántar is the Lanzón, a 4.53-meter carved granite shaft that extends through an entire floor of the Old Temple and pierces the ceiling above. It is carved with the image of a fanged deity, fingers ending in claws, hair turning into serpents, mouth open in something between a roar and a snarl. The Lanzón sits in a small interior gallery with four narrow openings cut into the surrounding chambers, which means no one ever saw the whole sculpture at once. Each opening gave a partial, framed view of one section. The figure could only be assembled in the imagination of whoever had been admitted to walk past those openings in sequence. Modern rollout drawings make the carving legible to scholars, but that is not how the Chavín meant it to be encountered. The Lanzón is believed to be a founding ancestor with oracle powers. It remains in its original location inside the Old Temple. The deliberate design that hides the deity in fragments is probably the single best summary of how Chavín religion worked.

## How Big a World

The Chavín influence radiated outward across surprising distances. At Pacopampa, about a three-week trek north of Chavín de Huántar, renovations on the main temple show characteristic Chavín style. At Caballo Muerto in the coastal Moche Valley, an adobe structure added during a temple renovation displays Chavín influence. At Garagay in the Lima region, sculptors carved a head with mucus pouring from the nostrils, an iconographic motif that turns up at Chavín itself. Chavín ceramics show up at the site of Cerro Blanco in the Nepena valley. The Chavín sat at a trade crossing point between the Pacific deserts and the Amazon jungle, and they used that position. What is striking is the apparent absence of warfare. The archaeology shows almost no defensive structures at Chavín centers, and warriors are not depicted in Chavín art, in sharp contrast to the violent imagery at the older site of Cerro Sechín. Influence was spread by religious authority rather than conquest. Sites that resisted Chavín influence sometimes show evidence of warfare, almost as if those communities had to defend themselves from a culture they could not stop with violence.

## The Cactus and the Trumpet

Chavín ritual relied heavily on overwhelming the senses. Strombus shell trumpets stored underground would have been carried by ritual practitioners playing them in procession through the dim galleries of the temple, the sound bouncing off stone walls. Mescaline-bearing cacti, particularly the San Pedro cactus, appear in Chavín art held in the hands of figures in ceremonial procession. Stone mortars and other paraphernalia for grinding and ingesting psychoactive snuff have been recovered from the temples. Whether the masses or only the shamanic elite consumed these substances is debated. Either way, the architecture amplified whatever the experience was: the U-shaped temples with circular plazas could hold thousands in the open spaces and only fifteen or so in the inner gallery with the Lanzón, which means the religious experience was deliberately tiered. Pilgrims gathered in the plazas. Selected initiates passed inside. The plain water of the drainage canals became the roar of jaguars. The fragments of the carved deity assembled in the mind. The Chavín did not need armies. They had architecture, sound, light, and a fanged god you could only see one piece at a time.

## Flight Context

The Chavín cultural homeland centers on the Mosna Valley of Ancash Region, Peru, at 9.59°S, 77.18°W, at about 3,150 m elevation. Nearest airport is Anta/Huaraz (SPHZ) about 100 km west; Chimbote (SPEO) lies on the coast about 170 km northwest. From altitude the area appears as a deep Andean valley between snow-capped peaks of the Cordillera Blanca to the west and the Cordillera Oriental to the east. Mountain weather: expect strong updrafts, afternoon convection, and rapid clouding, especially during the rainy season (December-March). Best aerial visibility April-November (Andean dry season).
