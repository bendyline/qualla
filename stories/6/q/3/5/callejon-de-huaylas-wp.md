# Callejón de Huaylas

The 150-kilometer alley between Peru's white and black cordilleras, where 10,000 years of human history meet some of the most concentrated alpine drama in the Americas.

**Location:** -9.2667, -77.1167

**Categories:** valleys, andes, archaeology, earthquake, national-parks, peru

## Introduction

Two mountain ranges run parallel for 150 kilometers in the Peruvian highlands, leaving an inter-Andean valley between them so distinctive it has its own name. To the east rises the Cordillera Blanca, white because its peaks hold snow above 5,000 meters year-round. To the west stands the Cordillera Negra, black because warm coastal winds keep it bare. In the alley between them, the Santa River runs north toward the Pacific, the towns sit at altitudes that take getting used to, and the human story stretches back at least 10,000 years to a cave on the eastern slopes of the dark range.

## Geography in Stark Contrast

The valley starts in the south at 4,000 meters above sea level, where Lake Conococha gives birth to the Santa River near the head of the corridor. From there, the river runs north-northwest, descending through the Callejon to about 2,000 meters at its northern outlet past the city of Caraz. To the east, the snow-covered Cordillera Blanca holds Peru's highest peak, Nevado Huascaran at 6,746 meters, looming above the town of Yungay. To the west, the Cordillera Negra reaches over 4,000 meters but sees almost no snow because warm coastal winds keep its western flank dry. The naming is geographic shorthand for a sharp climatic difference: the same valley is bordered by two completely different mountain ecologies, and the contrast holds for the entire length of the corridor.

## Ten Thousand Years in a Cave

Guitarrero cave sits on the eastern slopes of the Cordillera Negra, and its contents have rewritten the timeline of human habitation in the high Andes. Excavations led by archaeologist Thomas F. Lynch have uncovered preserved plant remains including domesticated maize and material from coastal species, all radiocarbon dated to at least 10,000 years ago. People were living in this corridor before pottery, before agriculture as we know it, and they were already trading or moving between the highlands and the Pacific coast. Nearby, the preceramic site of Quebrada Perron contains evidence of complex stone-working that continued over thousands of years as a workshop. The Callejon de Huaylas is one of those rare places where you can stand in a single landscape and look at a cave whose human story is older than nearly anything in the Americas.

## From Chavin to Wari to Empty

The Chavin culture took shape on a pass between the Conchucos Valley and the Callejon, at the great ceremonial complex of Chavin de Huantar. Between roughly 900 BCE and 200 BCE, this site became a religious and pilgrimage center whose influence reached hundreds of kilometers, with Chavin-style pottery, textiles, and temple designs appearing as far as the Jequetepeque and Lambayeque valleys to the north. After Chavin faded, the corridor entered what archaeologists describe as a dark age. Settlements moved to defensible hilltops. The Recuay culture rose around 250 CE, with sites like Pashash and Yayno positioned to dominate the Santa Valley, producing distinctive kaolin-clay pottery and underground tombs. By 600 CE, the Wari empire from the south had absorbed the region. By the eleventh century, the Wari were gone too, and the Callejon largely fades from the archaeological record before the Inca arrived.

## The Day the Mountain Came Down

On May 31, 1970, an earthquake offshore in the Pacific Ocean shook loose a massive piece of Nevado Huascaran's western face. The resulting landslide of rock, ice, and mud raced down the mountain at hundreds of kilometers an hour and buried the town of Yungay in seconds. About 25,000 people died. Only 92 survived from Yungay itself, most of them in the cemetery and the stadium when the slide arrived, the cemetery elevated and the stadium positioned just outside the path. The regional capital, Huaraz, and the towns of Caraz and Aija were partially destroyed by the broader earthquake. The Yungay site is now a memorial, the buried town deliberately left undisturbed under the mud. Survivors and their descendants still gather there every May 31.

## The Glaciers and Their Slow Retreat

In 1977, UNESCO designated Huascaran National Park a World Biosphere Reserve, recognizing both the alpine ecology and the archaeological wealth of the corridor. The park covers vast stretches of the Cordillera Blanca, including peaks like Alpamayo, often called one of the most beautiful mountains in the world, and Artesonraju, the peak whose silhouette appears on the Paramount Pictures logo. Climbing season runs roughly from May to September, when the dry months coincide with the northern hemisphere summer and the snow line sits above 5,000 meters. But the glaciers are receding fast. The popular Pastoruri glacier, once a destination for casual day visitors, has shrunk dramatically and may disappear within decades. Towns downstream depend on glacial meltwater. The corridor between the white and black ranges is, slowly, becoming a corridor between only one of them.

## Flight Context

Located at 9.27 degrees south, 77.12 degrees west, in Peru's Ancash Region. The 150-kilometer valley runs roughly north-northwest from Lake Conococha to the Canon del Pato near Caraz. Anta Airport (ATA) near Huaraz serves the region. The Cordillera Blanca's peaks above 6,000 meters are spectacular landmarks visible from any cruising altitude in clear weather; Nevado Huascaran is the dominant peak at 6,746 meters.
