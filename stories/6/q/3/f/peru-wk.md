# Peru

A country of impossible extremes - from Amazon jungle to coastal desert to Andean ice - where the Inca Empire rose and fell and where ceviche, condors, and Quechua are still daily life.

**Location:** -10.0000, -76.0000

**Categories:** peru, south-america, countries, inca-empire, andes, amazon

## Introduction

There is a reason every traveler you meet on the Gringo Trail has a story that starts "In Peru." A country that squeezes Amazon rainforest, Pacific desert, and Andean glaciers into borders you can cross in a day of flying. Home to an empire - Tahuantinsuyo, the Four Quarters - that in its last century stretched from southern Colombia to central Chile without a wheel or a written alphabet. The slogan Peru's tourism board once used, "Pack Your Six Senses," was aimed at something real: it is difficult to travel here and remain unmoved. The coastal fog that hangs over Lima in winter. The altitude that flattens you in Cuzco until it doesn't. The moment Machu Picchu appears through cloud and the crowd around you goes quiet.

## Before the Empire

People have lived in this territory for perhaps twenty thousand years, their stone tools scattered across caves at Piquimachay, Lauricocha, and the Paiján sites on the northern coast. By 6000 BCE the first communities were organizing at Chilca and Paracas on the coast, and some three thousand years later settled villages such as Kotosh and Huaca Prieta were growing maize and cotton. By 3000 BCE the Caral-Supe civilization was building stone pyramids in the Supe Valley without pottery, without writing, and at roughly the same moment as the Egyptians were building theirs. The Chavín religious complex rose around 900 BCE and propagated a distinctive art style across the northern Andes. Then came the Paracas with their embroidered mantles, the Nazca with their desert geoglyphs, the Moche with their portrait vessels and irrigation engineering, the Wari with their planned cities, and the Chimú with Chan Chan - the largest adobe city in the Americas. Only after all of that did the Incas appear.

## Tahuantinsuyo

The Inca expansion began slowly in the thirteenth century and accelerated under Pachacutec in the 1430s. Within a hundred years they built the largest empire in the pre-Columbian Americas and ran it without money, without a market economy, and without writing as most scholars recognize it. The administrative system worked through reciprocity - you gave labor to the state, the state gave you security and access to stored surplus - and through a network of stone roads that ran tens of thousands of kilometers across vertical terrain the Spanish could not believe. Then came 1532. Francisco Pizarro landed with fewer than two hundred men. Atahualpa, who had just won a civil war against his brother Huáscar, let the Spanish into Cajamarca to talk. They took him hostage, extracted a ransom of gold and silver that filled a room, then strangled him anyway in 1533. The empire unraveled. The last Inca resistance at Vilcabamba held out until 1572, when the Spanish captured and beheaded Tupac Amaru.

## The Country Peru Became

Three centuries of viceroyalty. Silver mines at Potosí that bankrolled Spain and destroyed indigenous labor. San Martín landing at Paracas in 1820 and declaring independence in Lima on July 28, 1821 - the date every Peruvian schoolchild memorizes. Bolívar and Sucre finishing the job at Junín and Ayacucho in 1824. A nineteenth century of caudillos and guano wealth and then the disaster of the War of the Pacific, when Chile took Tarapacá and occupied Lima too. The twentieth century brought APRA and Haya de la Torre, the Velasco military reform that broke up the haciendas, the Shining Path nightmare of the 1980s, and Fujimori's decade of stabilization purchased at terrible cost - a sterilization campaign that reached some 280,000 mostly poor and Indigenous women, many of whom say they never consented, among them. In the 2000s the economy grew. Poverty fell. Then Congress and the pandemic revealed how fragile the institutions still were.

## What a Traveler Finds

Machu Picchu through mist. Cuzco's stone walls where Inca and Spanish masonry stack on top of each other so perfectly you cannot slip a blade between the blocks. The Nazca Lines only legible from the air. Huascarán at 6,768 meters in the Cordillera Blanca, where hundreds of glaciers still cling to a range that is losing them fast. The floating reed islands of the Uros on Lake Titicaca. Iquitos, a city of half a million reachable only by river or air, where pink dolphins surface in the Amazon and shamans serve ayahuasca in lodges along the Nanay. Arequipa in white volcanic sillar stone with El Misti smoking on the horizon. And everywhere you go, food: ceviche with leche de tigre, lomo saltado with its Chinese soy-sauce inheritance, pachamanca cooked in underground stones, cuy that tastes more like rabbit than you expect, chicha morada made from purple corn.

## The People Keeping It

Peru today is a multiethnic country of 33 million. About 60 percent identify as mestizo, 22 percent as Quechua, 2 percent as Aymara, with smaller populations of Afro-Peruvian, Asian-Peruvian, and European-descended communities whose influence runs from the chifas of Lima's Chinatown to the Japanese Peruvian cuisine of Nikkei restaurants. The official languages are Spanish, Quechua, and Aymara, and in the Amazon dozens of indigenous languages hold on against enormous pressure. Patience helps here. Things start late. Buses break down. Altitude does what altitude does. The reward is a country whose travelers keep coming back, who find that one visit was not enough, that the food they ate in Cuzco stays with them years later, that the condor they saw in Colca Canyon wheeled in their memory long after they landed home.

## Flight Context

Centered near 12 degrees S, 76 degrees W, with 6q3f geohash over the capital region. Peru's 1,285,216 square kilometers span from near the equator to about 18 degrees S, with the Amazon basin extending east to 68 degrees W. Main international gateway is Lima's Jorge Chávez International (SPJC); major regional hubs at Cuzco's Alejandro Velasco Astete (SPZO) at 3,310 meters, Arequipa's Rodríguez Ballón (SPQU) at 2,559 meters, and Iquitos's Francisco Secada Vignetta (SPQT) in the Amazon. Cross-country flights typically cruise FL300-FL400 to clear the Andes. Expect altitude-related weather and notably thin air at highland airports; Cuzco approaches require particular caution. Dry season May through October for the sierra; avoid Amazon flights during rainy season afternoons.
