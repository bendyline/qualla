# Bagua province

A province in Peru's Amazonas region where four major rivers cut deep gorges - and where in 2009 indigenous Awajun and Wampis communities clashed with police over oil concessions on their land.

**Location:** -5.6386, -78.5311

**Categories:** Provinces of the Amazonas Region

## Introduction

On the morning of 5 June 2009, after nearly two months of indigenous communities blockading a road called the Devil's Curve, Peruvian National Police helicopters arrived above Bagua. The police came down to clear the road. The Awajun and Wampis people were defending what they considered their land, ancestral territory the government had just opened to oil and mining without consulting them. By the time the morning was over, Peru would be calling it the Baguazo. By any honest count, more than thirty people were dead - twenty-three police, at least ten civilians, possibly many more whose bodies the survivors say were carried away. The province has lived in the shadow of that day ever since.

## Where the Rivers Carve the Land

Bagua sits in the north-central part of Peru's Amazonas region, where the Andes start to give way to the western edge of the Amazon basin. Four major rivers do most of the work shaping the place. The Maranon, one of the two main source rivers of the Amazon, runs from southwest to northeast across the province. The Chiriaco runs north. The Nieva runs south to north. The Utcubamba runs east to northwest, and the city of Bagua sits on a plateau above its right shore - houses on the western side of town all face the river. Elevations swing from 400 meters along the Maranon and Santiago to 2,500 meters in the southern mountains. Pumas, ocelots, white-lipped peccaries, anteaters, and armadillos move through the forests. So do snakes the locals know by their old names: macanche, colambo, cascabel, the deadly shushupe.

## The Awajun and Wampis

The forests and rivers along Bagua's northern edge are home to the Awajun (also called Aguaruna) and Wampis (Huambisa) peoples. They are part of the broader Jivaroan family, who have lived in this transition zone between the Andes and Amazon for many centuries. Spanish colonial expeditions reached the region in the 1500s, but the Awajun and Wampis kept their independence in ways most Andean peoples did not. They were never fully conquered. They retained their language, their rivers, their forest knowledge, and a fierce sense of where their territory begins and ends. By the 21st century, Peruvian law had begun to formally recognize indigenous rights, including the right to free, prior, and informed consent before extractive industries could enter their land. The 2009 fight was about whether those rights would actually mean anything.

## The Baguazo

Trouble started with a series of decrees in 2008. President Alan Garcia signed laws he said were needed to implement the new free trade agreement with the United States. Indigenous organizations said the decrees would open roughly seventy percent of the Peruvian Amazon to oil and gas exploration without consulting the indigenous communities living there. Awajun and Wampis communities had watched what oil and toxic waste had done to other Amazonian peoples - the Achuar in the lower Pastaza, the communities along the Corrientes River - and they refused. Congress repealed two decrees that year but left the others in force. In April 2009, the protesters set up roadblocks. By June 5, the standoff had run nearly two months. That morning, the government sent the police in. The official body count - ten civilians and twenty-three police dead, one officer missing, and scores wounded - is still disputed by indigenous communities and journalists who reported corpses thrown into rivers and black vultures circling. Local news correspondents said flatly that the government's version did not adjust to the truth.

## The Aftermath

The Baguazo did not stop with the dead. Garcia's government repealed the most contested decrees within weeks. Indigenous leader Alberto Pizango fled to Nicaragua to avoid arrest. At Petroperu's Station 6, a pumping station on the oil pipeline where protesters had seized 38 police officers, about ten of the hostages were killed as the violence spread. The trials dragged on for years. Most of the indigenous defendants were eventually acquitted. Peru passed a new prior consultation law in 2011 - one of the more significant indigenous rights advances in Latin America - directly because of what happened at Bagua. The Awajun and Wampis kept fighting other extractive projects in the years after, with mixed results. The forest is still there. The oil infrastructure has not disappeared either. The province now lives with both.

## What Visitors Find

Tourism barely exists in Bagua - the place is hot, the roads are rough, and the recent history is uncomfortable. But the country itself is striking. The Cordillera de Colan Reserved Zone, set aside in 2002 and upgraded to a national sanctuary in 2009, protects cloud forest in the southern part of the province where endemic birds and plants live in a small biological pocket only recently surveyed. Butterflies draw the occasional collector, particularly in the wooded areas. The Plain of Bagua, where the Utcubamba widens and the temperature can hit 40 degrees Celsius, grows rice, coffee, cacao. Bagua's own draw is harder to package. It is a province where the country still feels recently fought over - because it is.
