# Cordillera Central Páramo

A high, wet, cold ecoregion straddling the Peru-Ecuador border where 60 percent of the plants exist nowhere else on Earth, and a frog count keeps creeping toward the endangered list.

**Location:** -6.9380, -78.5680

**Categories:** Ecoregions of Peru, Ecoregions of Ecuador, Páramos, Andes

## Introduction

Above the treeline at 3,200 meters and below the snowline at 4,500 meters, in the high Andes of northern Peru and southern Ecuador, lies a kind of country with its own name in Spanish: páramo. The word does not translate easily. Tundra is too cold. Moor is too European. Páramo is a wet, cold, perpetually cloudy world of tussock grasses and cushion plants, where temperatures dip below freezing most nights and the ground is rarely dry. About 1.2 million hectares of it form the Cordillera Central páramo ecoregion, and roughly six out of every ten plant species in it exist nowhere else.

## Above the Trees

Páramo begins where the cloud forest gives up. As you climb the eastern slopes of the Andes in northern Peru, the trees get shorter and gnarled and dripping with moss, and then they simply stop. From the treeline at around 3,200 meters to the permanent snow at 4,500, the landscape opens into rolling tablelands cut by deep valleys, with steep slopes rising toward the highest peaks. The Köppen climate classification gives this zone the designation Cfb: warm temperate, fully humid, with warm summers - which sounds gentler than it is. The reality is cold rain, near-constant cloud cover, and high winds. The geology is mixed: volcanic rock from the Tertiary period in some places, Paleozoic metamorphic rock in others, ancient Precambrian basement exposed where erosion has cut deepest.

## An Island of Endemism

The Huancabamba Depression - a deep transverse valley to the south - acts as a geological barrier between the northern and southern Andes. Plants and animals that evolved on one side could not easily migrate to the other. Combined with the natural isolation of any high mountain ecosystem, where each valley becomes its own little world, the result is staggering biological uniqueness. There are about 300 genera of seed plants here, totaling somewhere between 1,000 and 1,500 species. Roughly 60 percent of those species occur only in this ecoregion. Conservation biologists call this kind of zone a biodiversity hotspot, meaning a place with both exceptional endemism and serious threat. The Cordillera Central páramo qualifies on both counts. Other Peruvian hotspots include the Marañón dry forests, the central Peruvian Yungas, and the central Andean puna.

## Cinchona and Quinine

Among the notable plants in this ecoregion is Cinchona officinalis, the small tree whose bark gave the world its first effective treatment for malaria. Indigenous peoples of the Andes had used cinchona bark for centuries before Jesuit missionaries learned of it in the 1600s and brought samples back to Europe. The active compound, quinine, would shape colonial expansion across the tropics by giving Europeans a way to survive in malarial regions where they previously could not. Cinchona is the national tree of Peru and appears on the country's coat of arms. The fact that it grows naturally here, in the cloud-shrouded transition between forest and páramo, is a small reminder that some of the most consequential botanical discoveries in human history happened in places this difficult to reach.

## Mountain Tapir, Spectacled Bear

The fauna here arrived from three directions: the Amazon basin to the east, the tropical Andes themselves, and the northern desert zones. Among the larger mammals, the mountain tapir is the species that catches the eye - the smallest and most endangered of the world's four tapir species, with a thick wooly coat to handle the cold. Spectacled bears wander the upper forests and venture into the páramo. Northern pudús, deer the size of a small dog, slip through the underbrush. The little red brocket, another small deer, browses where it can find shelter. Three bird species are endemic to the ecoregion. Endangered birds include the bearded guan and the red-faced parrot, both struggling against habitat loss. The tally of endangered amphibians runs longer: Atelopus peruensis, Telmatobius brevipes, several others, the kind of accumulating list that suggests something larger is going wrong.

## Stable, for Now

The World Wildlife Fund classifies the ecoregion as 'Relatively Stable/Intact.' That phrase carries some weight. Many ecoregions in Peru and Ecuador have not earned it. People have been using the Cordillera Central páramo for centuries - settlements scattered across the plateau, highways crossing it, cattle and sheep grazing the high grasslands - but the system has held. Recent expansion of agriculture has begun to chip away at habitat near the larger towns. Overgrazing causes soil erosion. Mining waste sometimes contaminates streams that feed the rivers downstream: the Quirós, the Huancabamba, the Chinchipe, all flowing eventually into the Amazon system. Podocarpus National Park protects part of the ecoregion. Whether that protection is enough as the population pressure grows is the kind of question whose answer takes decades to become visible.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 6.94°S, 78.57°W, in the high northern Peruvian Andes near the Ecuador border. Elevation range 3,200-4,500 meters. Recommended viewing altitude: 6,000-8,000 meters AGL to clear the high terrain; the area is frequently shrouded in cloud. The deeply cut Huancabamba Depression to the south provides the most distinct topographic landmark. Nearest major airport is Cajamarca's Mayor General FAP Armando Revoredo Iglesias Airport (SPJR). Visibility severely limited much of the year due to persistent cloud cover; best chances in brief clear windows during the dry season.
