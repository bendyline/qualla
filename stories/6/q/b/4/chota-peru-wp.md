# Chota, Peru

A high-plateau town in northern Peru where Chilean troops once burned everything that would catch, and where bullfighters now headline the second-largest ring in the country.

**Location:** -6.5614, -78.6486

**Categories:** Cities in Peru, Cajamarca Region, Bullfighting, War of the Pacific

## Introduction

On August 29, 1882, Chilean soldiers marched into Chota and set the town on fire. They were retaliating for the defeat at the Battle of San Pablo, and they considered Chota the headquarters of northern Peruvian resistance. Thousands of houses burned. The church burned with them. The image of Mary Immaculate, the town's patron saint, was carried out of the flames by townspeople who refused to let her go - a small mercy in what was otherwise a complete erasure. The town that exists today, on a plateau 2,388 meters above sea level on the eastern slope of the Andes, was rebuilt from that ash.

## The Acunta Plateau

Chota sits on a flat shelf called the Acunta plateau, 150 kilometers north of Cajamarca and 215 kilometers east of Chiclayo, halfway up the slopes that drop from the Andean spine toward the Amazon basin. Three rivers fence the town in: the San Mateo to the north, the Colpamayo to the east, and the Rio Chotano along the southwestern edge. All of them eventually flow into the westernmost reaches of the Amazon system. The climate at this elevation is mild and temperate, averaging 17.8 degrees Celsius year-round, with a hot rainy summer from November to April. Because the equator is so close, the rainfall here is driven by El Niño cycles rather than ordinary seasonal patterns. When El Niño arrives, the slopes turn green almost overnight. When it does not, the dryness lingers.

## Layers of Inheritance

Long before any Spanish missionary arrived, this region was part of the Cajamarca kingdom, with archaeological remains dense enough to suggest serious population. The sites at Pacopampa, Negropampa, and Rejopampa show influence from the Chavín, Wari, and Moche cultures, the great civilizational waves that swept up and down the central Andes over thousands of years. Two local cultures held the land at various times: the Huambos to the west and the Cuismanco to the east. In the mid-fifteenth century, the Inca emperor Túpac Yupanqui absorbed the area into his expanding empire. Father Juan Ramírez founded the Spanish town of All Saints of Chota on November 1, 1552, slightly southwest of where the modern city stands. Each successive layer left something - a name, a wall, a road - and the land kept absorbing them all.

## Poisoning the River

The War of the Pacific, fought from 1879 to 1884, was Peru's worst nineteenth-century catastrophe. After the Chilean victory at the Battle of San Pablo, an Araucanian unit (named for the city of Arauco in southern Chile) marched on Chota. The townspeople made a desperate decision: they poisoned the waters of the Colpamayo River to deny the invaders a clean drink. The Chilean response was the burning of the town in August 1882. Whatever was made of wood or thatch went up. The patroness, Mary Immaculate, whose dogma had been formally proclaimed by the Catholic Church on December 8 of an earlier year, was rescued from the cathedral - and remains the focus of the town's ongoing religious devotion. The names of those who saved her have been mostly forgotten. The act is what survived.

## Fiesta de San Juan

Each June, Chota throws itself into the festival of San Juan Bautista. Activities begin June 13 and culminate on June 24, with the feast day filling the Sanjuanpampa farm at Corepuquio with concerts, traditional dishes, dances, Paso horse contests, motocross, and soccer. On June 22, the Identity of the Chotanos Day pays tribute to the independence hero José Manuel Silva and the poet Anaximando Becerra Vega Mateola, both native sons. The election of Flor de Chota - the festival queen, drawn from competitors across all the villages of the district - happens on the evenings of June 23 and 24. But the loudest event is yet to come.

## The Second-Largest Ring

On June 25, 26, and 27, bullfights fill the Plaza de toros El Vizcaino, a ring large enough to be the second biggest in Peru. Only the Plaza de toros de Acho in Lima - dating to 1766 and one of the oldest bullrings in the Americas - exceeds it. International matadors come for the cartel. The ring sits at altitude, the air thin enough that a bull's lungs and a fighter's lungs both labor. Chota calls itself the Bullfighting Capital of Northern Peru, and the title is earned in the simplest way: more spectators come for these three days each summer than the town has houses to put them in. The descendants of those who watched their town burn 140 years ago now watch matadors work in the rebuilt heart of the place. The ash is gone. The plateau remembers what it remembers.
