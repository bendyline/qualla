# Chachapoyas

The mountain basecamp for one of South America's most spectacular archaeological circuits, where 22-hour bed buses arrive at 5 AM from Lima and a cable car flies travelers across a 220-meter gorge to a pre-Inca fortress.

**Location:** -6.2167, -77.8500

**Categories:** Travel, Archaeology, Andean cities, Peru, Cloud forest

## Introduction

The bus from Lima leaves in the evening and arrives at five in the morning, twenty-two hours later, after climbing the Pacific desert, descending into the Marañón canyon, and grinding back up the eastern Andes. Travelers who book a bed on the lower deck of the double-decker can sleep through most of it. Those in the upper seats watch the road wind two kilometers down into a single-lane gorge that the Marañón has been cutting for millions of years. The destination is Chachapoyas, a small mountain city in northern Peru, and the reason to make the trip is what surrounds it: cliff-mounted sarcophagi staring out across valleys, a fortress whose walls run up to twenty meters high, a 771-meter waterfall, mummies recovered from cliff tombs above a remote mountain lake. The city itself is the staging ground. Almost everything you came to see is somewhere else.

## Getting Here

The fastest route from Lima is the daily ATSA flight; fares vary widely, so book ahead. Otherwise it is the bus: Movil Tours and Civa run direct overnight services from Lima, and Civa's bed bus has movies, meals, and sleeping pillows for around forty-five dollars. Cheaper options leave from Chiclayo (8 hours) and Trujillo. There is one daily service from Cajamarca, leaving at 4:30 in the morning with Virgen del Carmen, a route the operators describe as not for the faint-hearted because of the dramatic descent into the Marañón canyon. From Ecuador, an arduous two-day route enters at Balsas via San Ignacio. Once in Chachapoyas, daily vans and taxis leave from stations within a couple of blocks of the main square, heading to Kuélap, Leymebamba, and Luya.

## Kuélap and the Cable Car

Some of the largest pre-Inca ruins in South America sit on a cliff above the Utcubamba valley at about 3,000 meters. Kuélap is older than the Incas and built by the Chachapoya culture, whose people fortified themselves against neighbors with walls reaching up to twenty meters tall. For decades the only way to reach the ruins was a steep multi-hour climb. In 2017 a cable car opened, with the boarding station at Nuevo Tingo. The thrilling twenty-minute ride flies passengers across the deep gorge with the cars hanging up to 220 meters above the ground at the highest point, replacing what had been a two-hour drive around the canyon. After a section of the perimeter wall collapsed in April 2022, the visitor approach now involves a 1.5-kilometer walk that takes about thirty minutes, mostly uphill. Groups are limited to twelve people, the entire circuit is roped to control damage, and a guide is recommended because the site has no information signs. Day tours from Chachapoyas run about 100 soles.

## The Cliff Tombs

Karajía is the famous one: six anthropomorphic sarcophagi, each about 2.5 meters tall, perched on a narrow cliff ledge where they have stared out across the valley for roughly five hundred years. The figures are wrapped human burials placed inside clay-and-stick effigies, painted white with stylized faces and small skulls mounted on top of the tallest ones. Tours combine Karajía with Pueblo de los Muertos, the Town of the Dead, where similar sarcophagi share cliff ledges with stone houses built into the rock face. As of 2024, tour vans no longer take visitors down to the Pueblo de los Muertos sarcophagi themselves, only to a viewing platform about 200 meters away. The walk to Karajía proper is 1.5 kilometers and steep, though horses can be hired in the village of Trita for twenty soles. Revash adds another variation: cliff tombs that look like miniature houses, painted with red pictographs that have survived centuries of weather. The Museum of Leymebamba holds 219 mummies and thousands of artifacts recovered from cliff tombs above Laguna de los Cóndores in 1997.

## Gocta and the Cloud Forest

The waterfall that the German Stefan Ziemendorff brought to the world's attention after a 2002 expedition was announced in 2006 as 771 meters tall, making Gocta one of the world's highest. Local people had known about it for centuries; the outside world simply had not been paying attention. The hike from the village of Cocachimba is about 5.5 kilometers each way through cloud forest, going down two kilometers, then up one, and finishing relatively flat. Fast hikers complete the round trip in three and a half hours, normal pace closer to five. Tours from Chachapoyas run about ninety soles including guide, lunch, and transport. Yumbilla nearby reaches 895 meters, and Chinata 540 meters; both are accessible from the villages of Cuispes or San Carlos. The Gran Vilaya trekking route, a multi-day hike through cloud forest, threads between archaeological sites and reveals what the region looked like before roads were built. The first week of June brings Tourist Week with parades from villages across the Amazonas provinces. The festival of the Virgin of Asunta runs during the first two weeks of August, the patron saint celebration that anchors the year.

## Flight Context

Chachapoyas sits at 6.22°S, 77.85°W in northern Peru's Amazonas Region, at 2,335 m elevation. Chachapoyas Airport (SPPY) handles ATSA's daily Lima service. Jaén (SPJE) is the alternative, 4 hours west. Approach from Lima crosses the Andean spine; expect mountain weather, frequent low clouds at ridge heights between 2,000-3,000 m, and afternoon convection. From cruising altitude the area appears as densely forested ridges and deep canyons, with the Utcubamba River valley running south, the Marañón gorge to the west, and the Kuélap fortress visible on a high ridge southwest of the city.
