# Chachapoyas, Peru

A cloud-shrouded colonial city of tile-roofed mansions in the eastern Andes of Peru, perched on what may be the original Mountain of Mist named by the Quechua.

**Location:** -6.2300, -77.8700

**Categories:** Andean cities, Colonial Peru, Amazonas Region, Pre-Columbian, Spanish architecture

## Introduction

The fog rolls in nearly every afternoon during the rainy season, blanketing the wooded ridges around the city. The Quechua may have called this place sachapuyos, meaning mountain of haze, and watching the cloud descend over the red-tile roofs of Chachapoyas, the etymology feels less like a guess than an observation. The city sits at 2,335 meters in the eastern Andes of Peru, far enough from the coast that travelers used to arrive only after long, spectacular bus rides through the canyons of the Marañón. The Spanish conquistador Alonso de Alvarado founded it on September 5, 1538, and named it San Juan de la Frontera de los Chachapoyas. They had to move it three times before they got the location right. The current site, settled around 1545, has now held for nearly five centuries and still preserves the Andalusian architecture the conquistadors built into its plazas and casonas.

## An Isolated Capital

Chachapoyas serves as the capital of the Amazonas Region of Peru, a department whose name refers to the river drainage rather than the country to the east. Its 32,026 residents (2017 census) live in a transitional climate zone tucked between the arid Cordillera Occidental and Cordillera Central on one side and the rainforested Cordillera Oriental on the other. This balance of rain shadow and humid east winds gives the city a temperate, moderately rainy climate, with annual averages around 18 degrees Celsius and 778 millimeters of precipitation. Unlike the soaking towns farther east toward Moyobamba, Chachapoyas does not get the deluges that turn streets into rivers. Local agriculture supports sugar cane, orchids, and especially coffee. The roads connecting the city to the Peruvian coast were poor for centuries and only recently improved, which is why Chachapoyas retained so much of its original colonial character. There simply was no easy way to bring in the modern construction styles that have transformed other Andean cities.

## Casonas and Plazas

The Plaza de Armas anchors the western part of the city, a perfect quadrilateral one hundred meters per side. On its south side stands a monument to Francisco Bolognesi, the Hero of Arica, the colonel who refused to surrender to the Chileans during the War of the Pacific and died defending the fort named for him. Around the plaza, the wide colonial casonas survive almost intact, their interior courtyards opening onto large lounges, their roofs covered in red clay tiles. The street called Jirón Triunfo links the city's three principal plazas. It bears that name because the victorious Chachapoyans of the Higos Urco battle marched into the city through it on June 6, 1821, fighting the royalists during Peru's war of independence. There is also a legend from the viceroyalty: an indigenous chief named Pantoja asked the viceroy for permission to roof his house in gold. The viceroy refused. Pantoja's gold, and other treasures, were said to be hidden in one of the forty caves that surround the city, where they presumably still wait.

## Wells and Fountains

On Luya Urco hill, west of the city, stands the Yana Yaku well. The legend holds that Santo Toribio de Mogrovejo brought forth water from a rock here to end a drought that was killing the region. The well was built in the same spot. Local tradition says any man who drinks from its waters will be forever attracted by the charm of the city's women. Nearby, the Cuyana Fountain once had two jets, one carrying water that produced love and one carrying water that produced hate or negligence. A foreigner who drank from the fountain of love, the legend went, would be forever captivated by the women and the surrounding country. Below the city, the spot where the Kuélap stadium now stands was once a lagoon ringed with totoras (a Peruvian bulrush) and palm trees. The wood from those plants built the city's first churches. Twenty kilometers north in the Huancas district, the Petroglyphs of Pitaya preserve about thirty stone carvings of plants, animals, and hunting scenes from approximately 3,000 years ago.

## What the Distance Made Possible

The city's isolation produced a distinct cultural inheritance. The notable people born here include the Jesuit missionary Pedro de Añazco, the academic Toribio Rodríguez de Mendoza (a key intellectual figure of the Peruvian independence era), and the writer Blas Valera, whose chronicles of Inca history influenced generations of Andean scholarship. Modern Chachapoyas is the gateway to one of South America's most extraordinary archaeological landscapes: the fortress city of Kuélap, with walls reaching twelve meters high; the cliff-mounted sarcophagi of Karajía staring out from their narrow ledges; the painted tombs of Revash; the Museum of Leymebamba, which houses 219 mummies recovered from cliff tombs in 1997; and the Gocta Falls, plunging 711 meters down a wall of cloud forest. None of those sites is in the city. All of them depend on it. Chachapoyas earns its living now as a basecamp for the Chachapoya culture that preceded the Incas, the people who built fortresses on cliffs and buried their dead where no one could reach them.

## Flight Context

Chachapoyas sits at 6.23°S, 77.87°W in the Amazonas Region of northern Peru, at 2,335 m elevation in the eastern Andes. Chachapoyas Airport (SPCH) serves the city directly, with Jaén (SPJE) 4 hours west by road as a more frequent alternative. From altitude the city appears as a compact red-roofed grid on a forested ridge, with the Utcubamba River valley visible to the south. The terrain is steeply mountainous; expect strong updrafts and rapid weather changes. The afternoon cloud layer often blankets ridges between 2,000 and 3,000 m, which is reportedly the source of the city's name (sachapuyos, mountain of mist).
