# Cerro de la Sal

A loaf-shaped mountain in the eastern Andes whose surface vein of salt drew Asháninka traders, Franciscan missionaries, and conquistadors searching for lost cities.

**Location:** -10.6910, -75.2730

**Categories:** Mountains, Salt, Indigenous history, Peru, Amazon

## Introduction

For three months a year, the mountain was crowded. From July through September, when the rivers were navigable and the forest dried out, hundreds of Asháninka and Yanesha people climbed up the eastern slope of the Andes to mine salt. They cut the white vein in 20-kilogram blocks and carried each one down to the Paucartambo River, where as many as 600 balsa rafts a season would float the harvest down what colonial Spaniards called the River of Salt, distributing it to the peoples of the Amazon lowlands. Then the rains came back, the rafts could no longer launch, and the mountain emptied. A Spanish expedition in May 1691 climbed Cerro de la Sal expecting to find a thriving market and instead counted 44 people, only a handful of them mining. The Asháninka had simply gone home for the season.

## A Vein on the Surface

Eighteenth-century Franciscan missionary José Amich described the mountain as shaped like a loaf of bread, running about twelve kilometers to the southwest and many leagues more to the northeast. The salt deposit was not buried. It ran in a vein at the surface near the summit, twenty-three meters wide and mixed with stone and red clay. From the top of the ridge at about 1,750 meters, the land tilts down toward the Amazon basin to the east and toward higher Andean peaks to the west, peaks that rise another kilometer above. Below 1,550 meters the climate becomes true tropical rainforest. Above it the air cools into a damp subtropical zone. The mountain sits at the meeting place of two ecological worlds, which is exactly why it mattered: the salt was a highland resource that the lowland peoples needed and could not produce themselves. Trade routes spread Cerro de la Sal salt as far east as the Tupi peoples in what is now Brazil.

## The Missionaries Who Kept Coming Back

Spanish authorities understood the mountain's strategic value early. Where indigenous people gathered in numbers, missionaries could find converts and soldiers could find leverage. In 1635 the Franciscans established a mission at Quimiri and asked for fifty Spanish soldiers to control access to the salt. The Asháninka were not interested. In 1637 they killed the pioneering missionaries Jerónimo Jimenez and Cristóbal Larrios and five other Spaniards. The Franciscans tried again in 1641 and 1645 and lost five more priests. In 1674 a convert named Mangoré, angry at Franciscan attempts to abolish polygyny, killed five missionaries with arrows. Then in 1742 a charismatic indigenous leader named Juan Santos Atahualpa launched a messianic rebellion that swept the Spanish out entirely. For the next century the Asháninka controlled their salt mountain unmolested. Across the eighteenth century, Cerro de la Sal cost the Franciscans eleven dead missionaries.

## The Slow Conquest

Independence from Spain did not change much for the Asháninka. The newly Peruvian government built a fortress in 1842 at San Ramon, thirty-five kilometers down the Chanchamayo River, and began what one historian called a violent reconquest. Military expeditions in 1868 and 1869 failed to defeat the Asháninka but destroyed property and burned settlements. Beginning in 1873 the government took a different approach: it imported settlers from Europe. Several thousand Italians were placed south of the mountain in Asháninka territory. Germans and Austrians were settled to the north in Yanesha country. In the 1890s a British concession company sold land to British and Dutch settlers, while Andean Peruvians displaced by land pressure migrated in to claim what was left. Father Gabriel Sala visited the mountain in 1896 and 1897 and reported what was happening: settlers were preventing the indigenous people from mining for their own use and instead enslaving them to extract salt for commercial sale. The government had created a Salt Monopoly and was actively encouraging the new arrangement with tax exemptions.

## What Remains

Villa Rica was founded in 1928 by German-descended settlers from the older colony of Pozuzo, and coffee replaced salt as the dominant economy. The Asháninka and Yanesha continued to climb up to the mountain into the 1980s, carrying small loads of salt back to their homes in the deeper forest, but the seasonal congregation that had defined the place for centuries was finished. The mountain is still there, still loaf-shaped, still streaked with the white vein that drew everyone in the first place. The Spaniards who came looking for the legendary lost city of Paititi never found it. What they found instead was a salt mountain that had organized human life across half a continent for longer than anyone could remember, and a people who would not surrender it easily.

## Flight Context

Cerro de la Sal rises about 7 km north of Villa Rica in Pasco Department, Peru, at 10.69°S, 75.27°W. The mountain reaches roughly 1,750 m above sea level, surrounded by Andean peaks rising to 2,800 m. Nearest airports are Jauja (SPJJ) about 130 km southwest and Lima (SPJC) about 250 km west. From altitude the area appears as a forested ridge dropping eastward into the Amazon basin, with the Paucartambo and Chanchamayo rivers visible converging into the Perené. Mountain weather can be turbulent; afternoon clouds common.
