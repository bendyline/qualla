# Cabana District, Ancash

A high Andean district in northern Peru where pre-Hispanic ritual still threads through Catholic processions, and ancient stone fortresses crown the surrounding hills.

**Location:** -8.3929, -78.0086

**Categories:** districts, andean-culture, festivals, archaeology, peru

## Introduction

When the Spaniards arrived at Caguana in the early colonial era, they could not pronounce the name. So they shortened it. The Cully word for "to repair" or "to contemplate" became Cabana, and the new name stuck across centuries of changing maps and shifting borders. Today, the district that bears this misheard word stretches across the highlands of Pallasca Province in northern Peru's Ancash Region, a place where the past has not been so much erased as quietly absorbed into the rhythms of the present.

## A Name That Survived

The Cully language is gone. Spanish overwhelmed it during the colonial centuries, and by the 1800s only place names remained as scattered survivors of what people once spoke in the Conchucos region. Cabana is one of those survivors, mispronounced into permanence. The district sits within Pallasca Province, bordered to the north by Bolognesi and Huandoval, east and south by Corongo Province, and west by Tauca. Its capital, also called Cabana, was officially raised to district status on December 2, 1856, under the presidency of Ramón Castilla, then elevated to provincial capital in 1901 and granted the rank of City in 1914. Each promotion was a paper acknowledgment of what the highlands already knew: this place mattered, even if the borders around it kept shifting.

## The Festival That Lasts Two Weeks

Every July, the streets of Cabana refuse to sleep. The festival of Santiago Apostle runs from July 16 through July 25, ten consecutive days when music plays until four or five in the morning and food and drink flow freely to anyone who shows up. People fly in from Lima, from Chimbote, even from cities in the United States where the diaspora has settled. The central days are July 24 and 25, when the streets fill with masked dancers performing forms passed down through generations. There is no quiet evening in Cabana during this stretch. The festival has its own gravitational pull, and it does not let go until the last procession of Santiago has wound through the plaza.

## Dances With Memory

Three dances dominate the festival, each one carrying a different layer of the region's history. The Mojiganga, a Spanish word meaning something jocular or funny, was introduced by the colonizers and stages the social hierarchies of an earlier century: a wealthy patron and patrona in luxurious dress, shepherds in embroidered skirts and ponchos, and the Vilches, an intermediate figure draped in gold-thread embroidery and a crown of mirrored feathers. The Blanquillos remember the wars against Spanish domination, with ten or twelve dancers in masks and feathered crowns, processing alongside the saint's statue. And the Kiyayas, whose name in the lost Cully language means "consecrated women," preserve the memory of priestesses once dedicated to Catequilla, god of the Conchucos. The Spanish tried to suppress the practice. It survived as a dance.

## The Fortress on the Hill

Above Cabana rise the ruins of Pashash, a stone complex that gave its name to an entire pre-Inca culture. The Pashash culture flourished here between roughly 500 and 1000 CE, a regional development of the older Recuay tradition that shaped much of the northern Sierra de Ancash. Engraved stones from the site, some dating to around 500 CE, suggest a sophisticated tradition of ritual and craft. Local historians describe Pashash as the central defensive and ceremonial bunker of the wider Conchucos region, the seat of the cult of Catequilla, the same divinity the Kiyayas once served. Nearby stand the ruins of Mashgonga, smaller but part of the same network of stone-built sanctuaries that once watched over these valleys.

## Holy Week in the Highlands

Holy Week in Cabana follows a rhythm similar to the great Easter celebrations in Ayacucho. Palm Sunday opens the week with a procession recalling Christ's entry into Jerusalem. By Holy Wednesday, statues of Jesus and Mary meet in the streets in a staged encounter watched by the whole town. Holy Thursday brings the foot-washing ritual, then the procession of the crucified Christ. Good Friday at three in the afternoon, the cabanistas mark the moment of death; that night, the body of Christ is carried through the streets in a coffin. On Saturday, the resurrected Christ emerges. The week is dense with symbolism, and the entire population participates whether they consider themselves devout or not. In November, a different procession moves through the same streets: the Toro de Trapo, a cloth-and-frame bull danced around the statue of the Virgin of Llactabamba, follows her up the hill and back down to her temple.

## Flight Context

Located at 8.39 degrees south, 78.01 degrees west, in the Pallasca Province of Peru's Ancash Region.
