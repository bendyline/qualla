# Cabana, Peru

A small Andean city of four neighborhoods that sent a shoeshine boy to the presidency of Peru and still hosts one of the country's most exuberant patron-saint festivals.

**Location:** -8.3956, -78.0164

**Categories:** cities, andean-culture, presidents, festivals, peru

## Introduction

The boy who would become president of Peru shined shoes in this town. Alejandro Toledo was born in Cabana in 1946, one of sixteen children in a desperately poor highland family. From there, his trajectory ran through migrant work, a Peace Corps connection, Stanford University, a doctorate, and finally, in 2001, the presidential palace in Lima. Cabana keeps the connection in plain view: a small highland city in northern Peru whose most famous son became the first person of indigenous descent democratically elected to lead the country.

## Four Neighborhoods on a Hillside

The city divides itself into four neighborhoods: Huayumaca, Pacchamaca, San Jeronimo, and Trujillo. The names hold the same layered history as the city itself, mixing Quechua roots with Spanish saints. Cabana sits as the capital of both the Cabana District and the Province of Pallasca in the Ancash Region of northern Peru. It was officially founded as a Peruvian municipality on January 2, 1857, although people had been living on this hillside since long before the Spanish ever wrote anything down about it. The original name was Caguana, a word from the Cully language meaning "to repair" or "to contemplate." The Spaniards mispronounced it into Cabana, and the new spelling won.

## From Cabana to the Presidency

Alejandro Toledo's path out of Cabana is one of the more improbable journeys in modern Peruvian history. The family moved to Chimbote when he was a child, where he sold newspapers and worked as a shoeshine boy. American Peace Corps volunteers helped arrange a path to higher education abroad. He earned undergraduate and graduate degrees at the University of San Francisco and Stanford, eventually completing a doctorate in economics. He worked at the World Bank before returning to Peru and entering politics. In 2001, after a long contest with Alberto Fujimori, he became president, serving until 2006. His later legal troubles became their own chapter, but the basic arc remains: Cabana to Stanford to Peruvian presidency, a story rooted in this small highland city.

## Pashash, the Older Layer

Long before any of these names were spoken in Cabana, the people of Pashash built their stone complexes here. The Pashash culture, dating roughly from 500 to 1000 CE, gave its name to an archaeological site located near the modern city. The culture is now understood as a later development of the Recuay tradition that shaped the northern Sierra de Ancash, with related artifacts found in places like Chacas to the east. Engraved stones from the Cabana area, some of them dating back about 1,500 years, depict the iconography of a culture that combined defensive needs with ritual significance. The site is sometimes described by local historians as the central religious and defensive bunker of the wider Conchucos region, the place where the god Catequilla was venerated.

## Two Weeks for Santiago

Each July, Cabana commits itself fully to the festival of Santiago Apostle. Beginning July 16 and culminating on the central day of July 25, the city does not really sleep for ten days. Music carries through the streets until four or five each morning. Visitors fly in from Chimbote, Lima, and the Cabanista communities scattered through the United States. Food and drink flow without payment to anyone who shows up. On July 24 and 25, the dancers come out: the Mojiganga in their luxurious patron costumes and shepherd dress, the Blanquillos in their feathered crowns, and the Kiyayas, whose name means "consecrated women" in the lost Cully language. The Spanish tried to suppress the worship of indigenous gods. The dance survived as memory.

## The Cabanistas Abroad

Migration has spread the people of Cabana far beyond Pallasca Province. In Lima, the cabanistas have organized themselves into the Cabanista Association, the Sport Club Cabana, and the Brotherhood of July 25 of Resident Children of Cabana, plus newer cultural projects like Cantarria. Similar organizations exist in Chimbote, where many cabanistas migrated for work, and in cities across the United States. The festival of Santiago Apostle is now celebrated in all these places, in some sense exporting Cabana to wherever its children settle. The connection home runs through the music, the dances, and the long memories that travel in suitcases. Cabana stays small, but its diaspora carries the city outward into much larger geographies.

## Flight Context

Located at 8.40 degrees south, 78.02 degrees west, at high altitude in the northern Sierra de Ancash. The city sits on a hillside at the edge of valleys that drop toward the Pacific coast about 100 kilometers to the west. Visible from cruising altitude in clear weather. Nearest accessible commercial airports include Trujillo's Capitan FAP Carlos Martinez de Pinillos International (TRU) on the coast, with Anta Airport (ATA) near Caraz serving as the closer regional option.
