# Cajamarca

A high-altitude city in northern Peru where you can soak in the same hot springs the Inca emperor used, eat famously fine cheese, and wander a Plaza de Armas haunted by the moment an empire ended.

**Location:** -7.1575, -78.5175

**Categories:** cities, history, carnaval, hot-springs, peru

## Introduction

Soak in the same hot springs that the Inca emperor Atahualpa used, then walk a few blocks to the room where Spanish conquistadors held him captive before executing him in 1533. Few cities in the Americas compress this much history into this small a footprint. Cajamarca sits at about 2,700 meters above sea level in the northern highlands of Peru, an equatorial city of roughly 200,000 people where the air is mild and dry, the soil is fertile, and the Plaza de Armas still feels like the geographic center of a particular kind of ending.

## The Hot Springs and the Plaza

The Banos del Inca, five kilometers east of the city, are the natural hot springs that gave Cajamarca its first reputation. Atahualpa was bathing in them when Francisco Pizarro's expedition arrived in 1532. Today they are a public bath, accessible by microbus from Avenida Atahualpa for two soles, with an entrance fee of six soles for the pools. The springs sit in their own small town, also called Banos del Inca, and locals and tourists share the water. In the city itself, the steps of Santa Apolonia hill climb to the so-called Inca Seat, a stone formation where the emperor is said to have addressed his subjects. The view from the top covers the valley below, a useful orientation for understanding why the Inca chose to spend time here.

## Cheese and Mountains

Cajamarca's modern fame rests partly on dairy. The temperate climate and fertile valleys produce excellent milk, and the city is widely considered the cheese capital of Peru. Mantecoso, made from a fresh curd called quesillo, has become the area's signature, marketed under the collective trademark Poronguito since 2000. Andean Swiss-style hard cheeses appear alongside it, the result of a Swiss aid project supporting rural dairies in earlier decades. Visit any cheese shop in town and you will encounter a serious tradition. The food culture extends beyond dairy: cuy (guinea pig) appears on restaurant menus for the adventurous, and street vendors sell tamales and other items, particularly around dinner time. Cream-based sauces dominate the local cooking, an obvious extension of the dairy economy.

## Carnaval, the Annual Water War

The weeks before Lent, usually in February, are open season in Cajamarca. The city hosts what many Peruvians consider the country's most exuberant Carnaval, with parades, indigenous dances, and an enthusiastic public commitment to dousing each other with water and paint. Stores stock up on water balloons. Young people, mostly between the ages of six and twenty-five, organize themselves into informal groups and patrol the streets looking for targets. Wet patches mark the pavement everywhere. Local custom permits, even encourages, surprise attacks, and the unspoken rule is that anyone walking outside during this stretch has effectively volunteered. The festival winds down with the felling of the Yunza, a tree hung with gifts, in neighborhood after neighborhood, and ends with the burning of the effigy Ño Carnavalón, a farewell to the chaos.

## Cumbe Mayo and the Older Stones

Above and beyond Cajamarca, the landscape carries pre-Inca history that predates the city by millennia. Cumbe Mayo, a pre-Columbian aqueduct built around 1000 BCE, still carries water along channels carved into stone, its engineers having designed for the dry season more than three thousand years ago. The site sits at roughly 3,500 meters and can be cold and wet; tours from the Plaza de Armas run about eighteen soles. Closer to town, the Ventanillas de Otuzco are a pre-Inca necropolis carved into a cliff face, the small windows giving the place its name. A microbus from the market reaches it for one sol each way. Both sites long predate the Inca arrival, much less the Spanish; both remain as evidence of how thoroughly the highlands were inhabited and engineered before the colonial chapter began.

## Living at Altitude

Cajamarca's elevation is a daily fact for visitors. The first day or two demand an adjustment: drink water, skip the alcohol, take it easy. Sun at this altitude is fierce even when temperatures feel mild, and the nights are cold enough to require a jacket year-round. The reward for that adjustment is a city where most major sites are walkable. Mototaxis, the three-wheeled converted motorcycles that buzz through the streets, fill in the gaps for two or three soles a trip. Modern shopping malls like El Quinde and Real Plaza now anchor the eastern side of the city, evidence that Cajamarca is also a regional commercial center. The Yanacocha gold mine, 45 kilometers north, has been pumping economic activity into the region since the 1990s, with all the contradictions that mining-led development typically carries.

## Flight Context

Located at 7.16 degrees south, 78.52 degrees west, at approximately 2,700 meters elevation in the northern Peruvian Andes. Mayor General FAP Armando Revoredo Iglesias Airport (CJA) is 3.26 kilometers northeast of the main square, with daily flights from Lima Jorge Chavez International (LIM). Visible from cruising altitude as a distinctive urban grid in the high inter-Andean valley.
