# Cajamarca

The high Andean city in northern Peru where the Inca Empire effectively ended, where cheese is a serious tradition, and where Carlos Castaneda was born before he ever met any sorcerer.

**Location:** -7.1575, -78.5175

**Categories:** cities, history, inca, cheese, education, peru

## Introduction

On November 16, 1532, a Spanish force of fewer than 200 men led by Francisco Pizarro captured the Inca emperor Atahualpa in the central plaza of Cajamarca. Within a year, despite a famous ransom of gold and silver paid for his release, Atahualpa was dead. The largest empire in the pre-Columbian Americas had not been destroyed, exactly, but it had been decapitated in this Andean city, and what came after looked nothing like what came before. Cajamarca remembers this as the place where the Inca world cracked open. Today the city is a regional capital of about 100,000 people, set in a fertile valley at 2,700 meters where the climate stays mild but the nights run cold.

## The Climate That Made the Cheese

Cajamarca's location near the equator and at high altitude produces an unusually pleasant climate. January is the warmest month, averaging a maximum of 22 degrees Celsius and a minimum of 7. The coldest months, June and July, see daytime highs of 21 degrees but minimums of 3, with occasional frosts. This combination of mild temperatures, fertile soil, and steady moisture made the region one of the great dairy zones of Peru. Mantecoso cheese, made from a fresh curd called quesillo, has been produced in rural Cajamarca for decades and has become the local signature. In 1999, thirty-nine small and medium dairy enterprises organized themselves into the APDL, the official Cajamarca cheese-makers association, and in 2000 they secured a collective trademark called Poronguito to protect the traditional craft. Cajamarca is now widely considered the cheese capital of Peru.

## The Painters and the Anthropologist

Cajamarca has produced an unexpected concentration of writers, artists, and thinkers. Carlos Castaneda, the controversial author and anthropologist whose books about the Yaqui sorcerer Don Juan sold in the millions, was born here in 1925. The painter Mario Urteaga Alvarado, born in 1875, became one of the leading indigenist painters of early twentieth-century Peru, his work depicting the daily life of the Andean peasantry. Camilo Blas, born in 1903, joined the Grupo Norte intellectual community and produced paintings that grappled with similar themes. The philosopher Mariano Iberico Rodriguez, born in 1892, helped shape twentieth-century Peruvian thought. The writer Amalia Puga de Losada, born in 1866, was one of the city's distinguished literary voices. For a regional capital of modest size, Cajamarca's contribution to Peruvian culture has been disproportionately large.

## The University in the Highlands

Cajamarca is also a center of education for the northern Peruvian Andes. The San Ramon School, founded in 1831, ranks among the oldest high schools in Peru, and the Universidad Nacional de Cajamarca anchors a higher-education ecosystem that includes the private Universidad Antonio Guillermo Urrelo and the regional branches of five other Peruvian universities. This concentration of schools has shaped the city's character: a regional capital that draws students from a wide swath of the northern highlands, where bookshops and cafes radiate around the Plaza de Armas, where the rhythms of academic terms shape parts of the local economy. The city is small enough that you encounter the universities in the texture of daily life rather than as a separate enclave.

## Yanacocha and the Modern Tension

Forty-five kilometers north of Cajamarca sits the Yanacocha gold mine, an active operation that has reshaped the regional economy since the 1990s. The mine has driven employment, infrastructure, and tax revenue, but it has also generated significant environmental and social conflict, particularly over water resources in a region where high-altitude lakes feed agricultural valleys. Cajamarca is connected to the rest of northern Peru by Mayor General FAP Armando Revoredo Iglesias Airport, located 3.26 kilometers northeast of the main square, with daily flights to Lima. A railway has been proposed to connect the mining areas to a Pacific port, though the project has remained mostly an idea on paper. The presence of the mine sits in tension with the city's older identities as cheese capital, cultural center, and historical pilgrimage site.

## Carnaval, the Annual Reset

Once a year, Cajamarca puts on what many Peruvians consider the country's most uninhibited Carnaval. In late January and early February, before the start of Lent, the city fills with parades, indigenous dances, and an organized chaos of water and paint. The local custom is to spill water on friends and bystanders alike, and the practice escalates into what amounts to an all-out civic water war between men and women, mostly between the ages of six and twenty-five, armed with buckets and water balloons. Stores everywhere carry stacks of balloon packs during this stretch. Wet patches mark the pavement on every block. Groups of young people roam the streets looking for targets, and the implicit rule is that anyone walking outdoors during the height of Carnaval has consented to the game. The festivity ends with the burning of the Yunza tree, a Lenten farewell to a season the city throws itself into without restraint.
