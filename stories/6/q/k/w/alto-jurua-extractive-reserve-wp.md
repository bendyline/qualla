# Alto Juruá Extractive Reserve

The first extractive reserve ever recognized in Brazil, born from the rubber-tappers' fight that Chico Mendes died for, where 5,000 people still live by the rhythm of the forest.

**Location:** -8.7792, -72.1181

**Categories:** protected-area, amazon, brazil, extractive-reserve, rubber-history, indigenous-territories

## Introduction

On January 23, 1990, presidential decree 98.863 created the first extractive reserve in Brazilian history. That signature was the legal endpoint of decades of struggle by Amazonian rubber tappers, the seringueiros, who had insisted that the forest could be lived in without being destroyed. Chico Mendes had been killed for that idea in Xapuri thirteen months earlier. The Alto Juruá Extractive Reserve covers 537,946 hectares of the upper Juruá river basin in the state of Acre, against the Peruvian frontier. About 5,000 people lived inside it at the 2011 count.

## What an Extractive Reserve Actually Is

An extractive reserve is a Brazilian legal category that protects both forest and the people who depend on it. It is classed as IUCN protected area category VI, meaning sustainable use of natural resources is allowed and even encouraged. The model was developed by rubber tappers led by Chico Mendes in the 1980s as a counter-proposal to the cattle-ranching deforestation that was tearing up Amazonian land titles at the time. Instead of cutting down forest to plant pasture, the reserves let traditional populations continue tapping rubber, harvesting Brazil nuts, fishing, hunting, and farming small clearings, while binding them legally to keep the surrounding forest standing. Alto Juruá was the first to win this legal status, and is now administered by the Chico Mendes Institute for Biodiversity Conservation, ICMBio.

## The Land Itself

The reserve sits mostly within the municipality of Marechal Thaumaturgo, with smaller pieces in Jordão, Porto Walter, and Tarauacá. The Juruá River flows north through the western part of the reserve. The Tejo River enters from the east and joins the Juruá just upstream of Marechal Thaumaturgo, which sits on the left bank of the Juruá just outside the reserve boundary. Smaller streams drain a hilly terrain with slopes from 3 to 20 percent and altitudes from 250 to 300 meters. The international border with Peru forms the southern edge. The Serra do Divisor National Park adjoins to the northwest, and the Riozinho da Liberdade and Alto Tarauacá extractive reserves to the northeast. Together they form a contiguous block of protected forest along the western frontier of Brazil.

## Life Inside the Forest

Communities range from three to one hundred houses. Rubber tapping, the original economic activity that drew most of the residents' grandparents here from northeastern Brazil starting around 1890, is still practiced, though demand for it fell sharply in the 1980s. Family farming has filled some of the gap. Residents grow cassava, hunt, fish the Juruá and Tejo, build canoes from felled hardwoods, and produce cassava flour for sale. INCRA, the federal land agency, formally recognized the reserve as an agro-extractive project for 567 families on November 13, 2003. The deliberative council that gives residents a say in management decisions was created on July 21, 2011.

## Indigenous Neighbors

The reserve is surrounded and partially overlapped by indigenous territories: the Arara do Rio Amônia and Kampa do Rio Amônea to the west, and the Jaminawa/Arara do Rio Bagé, Kaxinawa do Baixo Jordão, Kaxinawa do Rio Jordão, and Kaxinawa/Ashaninka do Rio Breu to the east. The overlap has produced ongoing tensions, particularly around the Arara do Rio Amônia Indigenous Territory, where non-indigenous people have intruded into land legally reserved for the Arara people. An inter-agency working group was established on October 27, 2015 to address the issue. The boundary between extractivist communities and indigenous territories is, in practice, more cultural than physical: people on both sides have lived in the same forest for generations, sometimes intermarrying, often trading.

## The Forest That Voted for Itself

Alto Juruá is in the Amazon biome. December through February is the wettest period, June through August the driest, with average annual rainfall of 3,000 millimeters and temperatures averaging 30 degrees Celsius. Vegetation is mostly lowland forest and campina, with stretches of dense or open dry-land tropical forest and seasonally flooded alluvial forest. Field surveys have documented 130 species of mammals, including 16 primate species, jaguar, puma, giant otter, tapir, deer, and manatee. Reptiles include caimans. There are 84 amphibian species and 115 fish species. Bird counts have reached 527 species, including macaws, parrots, herons, and the harpy eagle. The Paititia neglecta butterfly, a protected species, is found here. The reserve is a working example of a different kind of Amazonian future: one where the people inside the forest keep the forest standing because the forest is what keeps them alive.

## Flight Context

Located at 8.78°S, 72.12°W in western Acre, against the Peruvian border. The reserve covers the upper Juruá and Tejo river basins. From cruising altitude, the unbroken green of the western Amazon stretches in every direction, with the Serra do Divisor visible on the western horizon. Nearest airport is Cruzeiro do Sul International (SBCZ); smaller airstrips serve Marechal Thaumaturgo and other municipal centers. Best viewing 8,000 to 16,000 feet AGL; cloud cover is heavy year-round, with clearer skies in the dry season June through September.
