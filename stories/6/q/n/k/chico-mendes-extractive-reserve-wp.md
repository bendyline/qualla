# Chico Mendes Extractive Reserve

Nearly a million hectares of Amazon rainforest in western Brazil, named for a murdered rubber tapper whose vision of working forests still struggles to outpace the cattle that came after.

**Location:** -10.5106, -69.7992

**Categories:** Extractive reserves of Brazil, Amazon rainforest, Protected areas of Acre, Conservation

## Introduction

On December 22, 1988, a rubber tapper named Chico Mendes stepped out the back door of his house in Xapuri to take a shower. A man hiding behind a coconut palm shot him with a 12-gauge shotgun. Mendes had spent his life trying to convince Brazil that the Amazon was worth more standing than cleared, that rubber tappers and Brazil-nut harvesters could live in the forest without destroying it. Less than fifteen months later, the government created the reserve that bears his name - 931,537 hectares of rainforest stretching across seven municipalities of the state of Acre, designed to do exactly what he had spent his life arguing for.

## The Forest Mendes Defended

The reserve sprawls across the southwestern corner of Brazil, pressed up against the border with Peru, with Bolivia just to the south, in a region where the Acre River Depression meets the Lower Amazon Plateau. Most of it sits below 300 meters elevation. The Iaco River forms one boundary; the Acre River forms another; the Xapuri River runs through the middle. Average annual rainfall is 2,054 millimeters - enough to keep the canopy dripping for most of the year. This is rainforest in the classical sense, dense and tall, and the wildlife reflects the abundance: collared peccaries and white-lipped peccaries crash through the undergrowth, capuchin monkeys travel in noisy bands, and the canopy is a haven for birds and butterflies, in an area biologists consider a center of endemism for birds and amphibians.

## Tapping the Trees

A rubber tapper walks his trail before sunrise, cutting diagonal slashes into the bark of Hevea brasiliensis trees scattered through the forest. The white latex bleeds down into a small cup. He returns later in the day to collect what has gathered. The work is repetitive but gentle: a properly tapped tree can keep producing for decades without being killed. The reserve was built around this practice, home to roughly ten thousand residents spread across 46 seringais, old rubber estates laced with long, looping tapping trails called estradas. In the 2010s their latex went to Natex, a state-run condom factory in the town of Xapuri, earning the average tapper around 800 reais a month, until the factory ran into crisis and halted production in 2018. Brazil-nut harvesting brings in additional income during the rainy season, when the heavy nuts fall from trees more than thirty meters tall.

## The Cattle Compromise

Almost every family in the reserve also raises cattle now. Cattle convert easily to cash; rubber does not. The utilization plan allows each household up to fifteen hectares of pasture, with fines for going over, but the 2009 census found families had crossed that line. A community leader put it bluntly in 2013: the son of a tapper born today wants to raise cattle. The pull is not difficult to understand. Rubber prices collapse. Brazil-nut harvests vary. A cow can be sold whenever the family needs cash. The vision Mendes died for - a forest economy built on extraction without destruction - now competes with the same logic that pushed deforestation across the rest of the Amazon. Land inside the reserve is sometimes quietly sold to outsiders who do not know, or do not care, that it is protected.

## Watching the Forest from Inside

In 2013, the Centre for Amazonian Workers and the Global Canopy Programme launched a community monitoring program. Forty residents were trained as monitors, gathering data on what was happening in the forest and interviewing their neighbors about how Acre's REDD program - the state's effort to reduce deforestation through carbon payments - was actually affecting them. The hurdles were predictable. Distances inside the reserve are vast, and traveling to meetings consumes days. Disputes over resources fractured the kind of cooperation the program needed. But the data came back, and federal and state agencies got something they had never quite had before: a picture of the reserve assembled by the people who actually live in it. Whether that data can change the trajectory remains the question Chico Mendes was asking when he was killed.

## Among the Largest of Its Kind

Chico Mendes Extractive Reserve is the second-largest extractive reserve in Brazil, after Verde para Sempre in Pará, classified by the IUCN as a Category VI protected area - sustainable use of natural resources by traditional populations. The model itself was largely Mendes's invention, a third path between fortress conservation and unrestricted exploitation. It depended on people whose ancestors had migrated to Acre during the rubber booms of the late 1800s and early 1900s, when newspapers in northeastern Brazil promised wealth in the forest. Most of those people died poor, but their grandchildren stayed, learned the forest, and made it home. The reserve is what they fought to keep. Whether it survives the twenty-first century in the form Mendes imagined will depend on prices, politics, and the quiet calculation of every family deciding whether to bleed a tree or graze a cow.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 10.51°S, 69.80°W, in the southwestern Brazilian state of Acre near the Peru and Bolivia borders. Recommended viewing altitude: 3,000-5,000 meters AGL to appreciate the unbroken canopy stretching to the horizon. The BR-317 highway runs along the eastern and southern edges, providing a visible boundary. Look for the meandering Acre and Iaco rivers as navigation references. Nearest airport is Plácido de Castro Airport (SBRB) at Rio Branco, about 200 km northeast. Visibility variable; clearest in the June-September dry season.
