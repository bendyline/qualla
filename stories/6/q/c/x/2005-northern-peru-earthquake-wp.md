# 2005 Northern Peru Earthquake

A deep magnitude 7.5 earthquake struck the Department of Loreto on a Sunday night, shaking buildings as far away as Bogotá and crushing the adobe houses of Lamas.

**Location:** -5.6780, -76.3980

**Categories:** earthquake, natural-disaster, peru, loreto, amazon

## Introduction

It was 8:55 in the evening on Sunday, September 25, 2005, when the ground beneath northern Peru began to move. The shock that arrived was magnitude 7.5, but its hypocenter was 115 kilometers down inside the subducted slab of the Nazca plate, deep enough that the energy spread out across most of the continent. People in Bogotá felt their buildings sway. So did people in much of Ecuador and western Brazil. The town that took the worst of it was Lamas, an old Inca-era hill town in the Department of Loreto, where 102 houses were destroyed and 3,000 people were left without homes.

## Deep, Not Shallow

Most damaging earthquakes are shallow. They rupture in the upper crust where buildings sit, and the energy stays concentrated. The September 25, 2005 event was different. Its hypocenter sat roughly 115 kilometers below the surface, inside the lithosphere of the Nazca plate that has been subducting beneath South America for tens of millions of years. The shock occurred about 39 kilometers northwest of Yurimaguas, on a normal fault inside the descending slab itself. Focal mechanism solutions from the United States Geological Survey suggest the rupture happened on a north-striking fault with up to several meters of slip near the epicenter. Because the energy traveled outward from such a deep source, the shaking was felt across an enormous area, but the maximum perceived intensity at the surface was only VI, strong but not severe.

## What Happened in Lamas

Lamas is a hill town founded in the 17th century, perched on a ridge above the surrounding jungle, with a population that includes a substantial Quechua Lamista community whose ancestors were resettled here generations ago. The town's older neighborhoods are built almost entirely of adobe. When the shaking arrived, the dry mud-brick walls cracked along their seams. One hundred and two houses were destroyed outright. Another 498 were severely damaged. Five people died in Lamas and sixty were injured. Three thousand more had nowhere to sleep that night. The damage pattern followed the geography of the town: the lower neighborhoods, on softer ground, fared worse than the church-crowned upper district. By morning the streets were full of families pulling possessions out of collapsed houses.

## A Wide Footprint

Beyond Lamas, the toll spread thinly across a vast area. One woman was killed by a collapsing house in La Libertad, far to the southwest. Another death occurred in San Martín Province. In Moyobamba, walls of some buildings collapsed, in a town that had still not fully recovered from the 1990 and 1991 earthquakes that hit the same general region. Damage and power outages were reported in Iquitos, Cajamarca, Chiclayo, Chimbote, Huacho, Tumbes, Sullana, Piura, and Pucallpa, a list that effectively traces the populated arc of northern Peru. In total, 20 people died, 266 were injured, and 1,316 homes were damaged or destroyed. Most of the affected structures were adobe.

## The Red Cross at the Door

Reaching Lamas in the days after a disaster is not easy. The town sits in the foothills of the Andes where they spill into the upper Amazon, and the road network is limited. Ten Red Cross volunteers from Moyobamba were the first organized responders to arrive. Despite difficult weather, they delivered 1,000 blankets, 50 tents, and 200 packs of clothing to the National Institute of Civil Defence. The international response was modest, partly because the death toll was relatively low for an earthquake of this magnitude, partly because the affected area was distant from the country's media centers. For the families of Lamas, the recovery would take years and would mostly be carried out by themselves, neighbor by neighbor.

## A Region That Keeps Shaking

Earthquakes of similar magnitude and mechanism occurred close to the 2005 event in 1990, 1991, 2019, and 2021. The pattern reflects the geometry of the subducting Nazca plate beneath northern Peru, where flat-slab subduction creates ongoing deformation of both the descending slab and the overriding South American plate above it. Deep intraslab earthquakes like the 2005 shock are part of the regular tectonic vocabulary of this region. They tend to be felt across very large areas and to produce comparatively localized severe damage where vulnerable buildings happen to sit on soft ground. The lesson, repeated every few years and learned more slowly than seismologists would prefer, is that adobe construction in the Peruvian Amazon is in continuous quiet conversation with the plate moving beneath it.

## Flight Context

Located at 5.68°S, 76.40°W in the Department of Loreto in northern Peru, where the Andean foothills give way to the upper Amazon basin. From cruising altitude, the green expanse of the Amazon is visible to the east, with the Andean ridges rising to the west. Nearest airports are Cadete FAP Guillermo del Castillo Paredes (SPST) at Tarapoto and Moisés Benzaquén Rengifo (SPYL) at Yurimaguas. Best viewing 8,000 to 14,000 feet AGL; tropical cloud cover is heavy year-round, with clearest skies typically July through September.
