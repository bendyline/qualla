# Chacas

An Andean town at 3,359 meters where an Italian priest's vocational school taught orphans to carve baroque balconies and run a stained glass factory said to be unique in South America.

**Location:** -9.1619, -77.3661

**Categories:** Andean towns, Peru, Catholic missions, Crafts, Tourism

## Introduction

An Italian priest named Father Ugo de Censi arrived in Chacas in 1976 and was struck by what he saw. The town sat high on the eastern slope of the Cordillera Blanca, beautiful and severely poor, with a 17th-century baroque altarpiece slowly decaying inside the parish church. He decided to do something about all of it at once. Two years later, in 1978, he founded the Don Bosco Workshop School for the poorest children and orphans in the district. They learned woodworking, stained glass, sculpture, and stonemasonry. Today their carved double-water balconies adorn the houses around the main square, the restored baroque altarpiece glows again with seventeenth-century gold, and Chacas runs a stained glass factory said to be unique in South America. In October 2023, the United Nations World Tourism Organization named it one of the Best Tourism Villages on Earth.

## On the Eastern Slope

Chacas sits at 3,359 meters in the buffer zone of Huascarán National Park, on the side of the Cordillera Blanca that faces the Amazon rather than the Pacific. The terrain rises and falls dramatically: the lowest part of the district drops to 2,800 meters in Upakasha, and the highest peak, the snow-capped Copa, climbs to 6,173 meters. The Perlilla peak reaches 5,586 meters to the southeast and Wakuy 4,702 meters to the west. The valley below the town is fed by 35 streams emptying into the Gun and Chakapata rivers, which join at Puruytumaq to form the Aquchaka. There are 32 lakes scattered across the district. The climate produces a daily whiplash typical of the high tropics: clear July days swing from 10 to 30 degrees Celsius during the day and crash to minus 3 to 10 at night. Wheat and other cereals grow in the Quechua zone where the town center sits.

## Layers of People

Long before the Spanish arrived, the territory around Chacas was occupied by the lordship of Huari, the dominant culture of the eastern Sierra Ancash after the Chavín culture faded. Caves and rock shelters in the region preserve some of the oldest evidence of human presence in this part of the Andes. The Inca conquest, beginning around 1460 under General Qhapaq Yupanqui acting on orders from his brother Pachacuti, was bloody and incomplete. After some seventy years of Inca rule, the Spanish arrived before what scholars call the cuzqueñización process had finished, which is why Ancash retained customs, religious practices, and dialects distinct from Cuzco. The hilltops above town are crowded with pirushtus, defensive stone constructions built by the warring lordships of the Conchucos confederation before Inca consolidation. The Spanish town was founded in April 1572 by Augustinian evangelizers, including Hernando García and Alfonso Espinoza, with Diego de Álvarez serving as encomendero. They built the temple directly on top of the most important pre-Inca ceremonial center in the territory, where the Piedra de Chacas, a sacred huanca stone, had stood. The stone remained in its original spot until the mid-twentieth century.

## Baroque in the Andes

The colonial layout was a perfect Spanish grid: 100 Spanish rods per side, four main streets, blocks forming a checkerboard. The first families to occupy the buildings around the plaza came from the original native settlements at Chagastunán and Macuash. The Spanish clergy and viceregal officials took the cloister of the temple. The town hall, school, and prison went on the plaza itself. By the 1860s mining had become the dominant industry, with Italian, English, French, German, and Croatian businessmen extracting gold, silver, lead, copper, and zinc from the Cordillera Blanca. The Pompeyo and Vesubio mining companies, founded in 1875, were the largest and most modern in Chacas. The 17th-century baroque altarpiece in the Mama Ashu shrine had been built when Captain Juan Tafur of Cordova, owner of the San José de Mushuqmarka mill, ordered it constructed. Three centuries later, when Father Ugo de Censi arrived, the altarpiece needed restoration. His Don Bosco artisans took it on.

## What the Workshop Built

The Don Bosco school produces work for export to Europe and the United States, but its most visible product is the town itself. Walk through Jirón Lima and the carved wooden balconies stand out against whitewashed walls, double-water carved gates frame doorways onto narrow streets, and the church and the plaza glow with renewed paint and gilding. The Festival of Mama Ashu on August 15, honoring the Virgin of the Assumption, fills the square with bullfights, the Race To Tapes competition, and the Mozo Danza ritual dance, which Peru has declared part of the nation's cultural heritage. The Provincial Museum holds about 400 pre-Columbian pieces, one of the largest collections in Áncash. In August 2013 the AN-107 highway opened, including the Punta Olímpica tunnel, the highest vehicular tunnel in the Americas at about 4,736 meters. The drive to Huaraz dropped from seven hours to three. A decade later the UNWTO honor arrived. Father de Censi died in 2018, but his workshop is still cutting glass, carving wood, and teaching orphans how to make beautiful things in a place that needed them.

## Flight Context

Chacas sits at 9.16°S, 77.37°W in Ancash Region, Peru, at 3,359 m elevation on the eastern slope of the Cordillera Blanca. Nearest airport is Anta/Huaraz (SPHZ) about 35 km southwest, much farther by road through the Punta Olímpica tunnel. From altitude the town appears as a small settlement on a plateau between dramatic peaks: Copa (6,173 m) to the west, Perlilla (5,586 m) to the southeast. The Cordillera Blanca's snow-covered ridges run north-south just west of town. Mountain flying conditions: expect strong winds, afternoon convection, and rapid weather changes. Best visibility in dry season (April-November).
