# Cordillera Blanca

The most extensive tropical ice-covered mountain range in the world, with 485 glaciers feeding northern Peru's water supply, shrinking faster than anyone wants to admit.

**Location:** -9.1167, -77.6000

**Categories:** Mountain ranges of Peru, Andes, Glaciers, Huascarán National Park

## Introduction

Spanish for 'white range' is the simplest possible name for what this is. Two hundred kilometers of tropical mountain running northwest through the Peruvian Andes, with 485 glaciers piled across its summits as of the most recent count, including the highest point in Peru. Huascarán rises to 6,768 meters by the most generous survey, 6,746 by another, and either number is enough to make airline pilots route around it. From the village of Musho at its foot, you cannot see all of Huascarán at once. The mountain has too much vertical to fit in your field of view.

## The Largest Tropical Icefield

The Cordillera Blanca is the most extensive tropical ice-covered mountain range on Earth. The Andes have other glacier zones, but none with this concentration, none this close to the equator. Satellite imagery in 2003 counted 485 glaciers covering 569.6 square kilometers. That number has been shrinking. Between the 1970s and 2006, the area of permanent ice cover dropped by about a third. The melt feeds the Santa River, which feeds the hydroelectric plant at the Cañón del Pato gorge, which produces five percent of Peru's electricity. The same melt provides drinking water to a long line of coastal cities and irrigation to the agricultural valleys that depend on year-round water. Glaciers in the tropics melt year-round, not just in summer, which is why these mountains have stayed reliable. They are becoming less so.

## The Roll Call of Peaks

There are seven peaks above 6,000 meters in the Cordillera Blanca, with topographic prominence great enough to count as independent mountains, and dozens more above 5,500. Huascarán Sur tops the list at 6,768 meters. Then Huantsán at 6,369. Huandoy at 6,360. Chopicalqui at 6,354. Chinchey, Palcaraju, Santa Cruz, all above 6,200 meters. The most beautiful mountain in the range, by general acclaim of climbers, is Alpamayo at 5,947 meters - a perfect granite pyramid whose southwest face is fluted with parallel ice ridges that make it look mathematically impossible. The Italian climbing magazine Alpinismo once polled mountaineers worldwide on which peak qualified as the most beautiful in the world, and Alpamayo won. It is the kind of mountain that ruins photographers.

## Yungay Below Huascarán

On May 31, 1970, an earthquake centered offshore in the Pacific shook loose a vast slab of ice and rock from the north face of Huascarán. The debris rocketed down the valley at speeds estimated above 200 kilometers per hour. The town of Yungay sat directly in its path. Within minutes, almost the entire town was buried under tens of meters of mud and rubble. Estimates of the dead in Yungay alone run to 20,000 or more. The catastrophe was the largest avalanche of the twentieth century. A few hundred people survived because they were in the town's elevated cemetery when the slide hit; the cemetery became an island in a sea of destruction. Today the original Yungay site is a cemetery itself, marked with palm trees and a few exposed building tops. The new Yungay was built half a kilometer away.

## Lakes of Cold Light

Along the western base of the range lie a series of glacier-fed lakes that draw visitors from across the world. The Llanganuco Lakes, accessible from Yungay, are two side-by-side basins of impossible turquoise water sitting beneath the twin peaks of Huascarán Sur and Huascarán Norte. Lake Palcacocha, higher up, is one of the lakes whose growth from glacial melt has prompted engineering interventions to prevent catastrophic outburst floods downstream toward Huaraz. Lake Querococha sits beside the road that climbs through the Cordillera. Lake Conococha forms the southern gateway to the Callejón de Huaylas, the long valley between the Cordillera Blanca and the Cordillera Negra. The water is clear, cold, mineral-rich, and almost luminous in the right light. Andean condors patrol the cliffs above.

## What Lives at Altitude

Almost the entire Cordillera Blanca lies inside Huascarán National Park, which has recorded more than 120 bird species - the Andean condor, the puna tinamou, the giant coot, the torrent duck that lives only in fast mountain streams. Spectacled bears, the only bear species native to South America, range through the higher forests. Vicuñas graze the high plains. Tarucas, the Andean deer, pick their way along ridgelines. The vegetation has adapted to the brutal combination of intense ultraviolet radiation, thin air, and freezing nights: most plants have fuzzy or hairy leaves that reduce water loss. The Polylepis trees, with their papery red bark, grow at altitudes where no other tree on Earth survives. The hot springs at Monterrey and Chancos draw visitors looking for warmth at altitude. Huaraz, the regional capital, sits at the foot of the range and runs on this dependence.

## Flight Context

Coordinates approximately 9.12°S, 77.60°W, with the range extending about 200 km in a northwest-southeast direction through the Ancash Region of Peru. Maximum elevation 6,768 meters at Huascarán. Recommended viewing altitude: 8,000-9,000 meters AGL minimum to clear the highest peaks safely; the range is a serious obstacle requiring careful weather assessment. Visibility best in dry season May-September; cloud and snow common in wet season. Nearest airport is Anta - Comandante FAP German Arias Graziani Airport (SPHZ) at Huaraz, on the western side of the range. Mountain wave turbulence common with strong winds aloft.
