# Caraz

The northernmost city of Peru's Callejón de Huaylas, an Andean town that survived the 1970 earthquake when others did not, and the gateway to glacier-fed lakes and the world's largest flower.

**Location:** -9.0483, -77.8108

**Categories:** cities, andes, trekking, earthquake, peru

## Introduction

Caraz survived. On May 31, 1970, the great Ancash earthquake reshaped the entire Callejon de Huaylas, burying the town of Yungay under a landslide and partially destroying Huaraz and Aija. Caraz, at the northern end of the valley, was damaged but spared the catastrophe. As a result, it remains one of the few cities in the corridor that still keeps its traditional architecture and the rhythm of an older Andean village. The other towns had to be rebuilt; Caraz still looks much like it did before the mountain came down.

## A Name Without a Definite Meaning

The origin of the name Caraz remains uncertain. One theory traces it to the Quechua word QARA-PUNKu, meaning leather door, on the grounds that the doors in Caraz were once covered with leather. Another suggestion, from the local historian Don Celso V. Torres, links it to the Quechua word KALLASH, meaning sterile or vegetationless, which fits the dry hillsides around the town. A third possibility points to QARAS, the Quechua name for the maguey plants that grow throughout the area. Multiple competing etymologies for a single name often signal a place whose origin is older than the records that describe it. Caraz sits at about 2,250 meters in the Cordillera Blanca range, a comfortable altitude where the climate stays gentle and the air is easier on visitors than in the higher towns.

## From San Martin to the Modern District

Caraz's official political history begins on February 12, 1821, when General Jose de San Martin, while staying in the village of Huaura north of Lima, founded four departments including Huaylas, with Caraz as its capital. In 1857, the original department was split, giving birth to the new province of Huaraz to the south. The District of Caraz was formally created by the Liberation administration of Simon Bolivar, legitimized by law on December 29, 1857, and sanctioned a year later. Caraz became the capital of the new Province of Huaylas on July 25, 1857. These dates matter for what they reveal: Caraz was on the political map at the very founding moment of the Peruvian republic, and it has remained there continuously.

## The Lake Above the Town

Thirty-two kilometers east of Caraz, at 4,200 meters in the Cordillera Blanca, sits Lake Paron. It is the largest lake in the white range, surrounded by 15 snow-capped peaks that mirror in its turquoise water. The drive up takes the visitor from temperate Andean farmland through progressively thinner air to a high alpine basin that feels closer to Patagonia than the tropics. The lake feeds the watershed that runs down through the Cañón del Pato to the coast. Caraz is also the gateway to the famous Santa Cruz trek, a four-day mountain route that begins 28 kilometers northeast of the city in the Santa Cruz valley and leads past two of the most photographed peaks in the Andes: Alpamayo at 5,947 meters, often called the most beautiful mountain in the world, and Artesonraju at 6,025 meters, whose silhouette appears on the Paramount Pictures logo at the start of every film the studio produces.

## The Flower That Lives Once

Forty-five kilometers west of Caraz, on the dry side of the Cordillera Negra at the Huashta Punta pass, grows Puya raimondii. This bromeliad is the largest member of its family and produces what is widely considered the largest inflorescence of any plant on Earth. A single Puya can take 80 to 150 years to flower, sending up a stalk three to ten meters tall covered with thousands of cream-colored blossoms, then dying after the seeds disperse. To see one in bloom is a matter of timing as much as travel. The plants live at 4,200 meters, in habitat that supports almost nothing else, surviving on minimal water and intense sun. Caraz sits low enough to be comfortable and close enough to be the practical base for getting up there.

## Tumshukayko and the Quieter Choice

Just one kilometer north of the Plaza de Armas, the ruins of Tumshukayko sit at the edge of town. The site is roughly 3,000 years old, with fine pre-Inca stonework that suggests it served as a place for worship. Most visitors to the Callejon de Huaylas head to Huaraz, the regional capital, but Caraz offers a quieter and often safer alternative. The weather, due to the altitude, stays gentle year-round, and the surrounding agricultural valleys produce a different texture of daily life than the busier city to the south. Daily flights from Lima reach the Anta airport (ATA) about 45 kilometers south of Caraz via LC Busre, flying 19-seat Fairchild Metroliner IIIs into thin mountain air. From Caraz, the entire upper Callejon spreads out: trekking routes north and east, the Cañón del Pato to the north, and Lake Paron above.

## Flight Context

Located at 9.05 degrees south, 77.81 degrees west, at about 2,250 meters elevation at the northern end of the Callejon de Huaylas in Peru's Ancash Region. Anta Airport (ATA) is 45 kilometers south near Huaraz; daily flights from Lima Jorge Chavez International (LIM) operate via 19-seat Fairchild Metroliner III aircraft. The town sits below the snow-capped peaks of the Cordillera Blanca, including Nevado Huascaran at 6,746 meters, all visible from cruising altitude in clear weather.
