# Cañón del Pato

A Peruvian gorge so narrow the river squeezes between rock walls just 15 meters apart, with cliffs rising thousands of meters above and a road that punches through 35 one-lane tunnels.

**Location:** -8.6667, -78.0500

**Categories:** canyons, andes, rivers, hydroelectric, peru

## Introduction

At one point near tunnel 18, the river runs between rock walls just 15 meters apart. The crests of two great mountain ranges rise on either side, in places reaching 6,000 meters above sea level. This is the Canon del Pato, the Duck Canyon, where the Cordillera Blanca and the Cordillera Negra collide at the northern end of the Callejon de Huaylas and squeeze the Santa River into one of the most dramatic constrictions in Peru. The road that follows it is narrow, single-lane, and tunneled through living rock 35 separate times. There are easier ways from the Andes to the Pacific. There is no more spectacular one.

## Where Two Ranges Meet

For 140 kilometers, the Cordillera Blanca and the Cordillera Negra run roughly parallel, separated by the broad inter-Andean valley of the Callejon de Huaylas, with crests typically about 16 kilometers apart and in places as much as 25. At the northern end of the corridor, the two ranges converge. The valley narrows. The Santa River, carrying meltwater from the white range and runoff from the black, gets channeled into an increasingly tight gorge. The result is the Canon del Pato. The walls are too steep and too arid for cultivation; in only a few places are the slopes even suitable for grazing animals. The canyon was carved by the river, but it now exists at the geological pinch point where two mountain ranges meet on the way to the sea.

## The Road With 35 Tunnels

Until 1952, getting through this canyon meant a narrow dirt track. That year, work began at Caraz, about 30 kilometers south of the canyon, on a highway extension that would punch through to the Pacific coast. The road runs along the Callejon valley until it crosses the river on a concrete bridge, then veers northwest onto the eastern face of the Cordillera Negra. From there, it pierces 35 one-lane tunnels through solid rock before emerging on the western face of the range, high above the town of Huallanca. The route is narrow, single-lane in the tunnels, and demands attention. A round trip from Caraz to the canyon and back takes roughly four hours. Drivers honk before each tunnel because there is no way to know whether someone is coming the other way until they appear out of the dark.

## Where the River Becomes Power

Just downstream of the canyon's narrowest point sits a diversion dam that pulls measured amounts of the Santa River's water into a man-made tunnel. The tunnel runs roughly ten kilometers, descending to a point roughly 103 meters directly above the Huallanca hydroelectric powerplant. At the tunnel's downstream end, the water plunges into the plant's six turbines a hundred meters below. The tunnel was built large enough to accommodate the small dump trucks that hauled out rock and debris during construction. The powerplant itself is entirely inside the mountain, in a manmade cavity carved a hundred meters deep into the rock at the eastern edge of Huallanca. The river that pulled this gorge into existence over millennia now drives turbines that light cities along the Peruvian coast.

## From the Canyon to the Coast

Past Huallanca, the Santa River turns westward and irrigates the farms of the arid coastal plain before emptying into the Pacific just north of the port city of Chimbote. The same road that climbed through the canyon descends to meet the Panamerican Highway and continues north to Trujillo. The drive from Caraz down to Chimbote alone runs roughly 170 to 200 kilometers, much of it through canyon country. The earthquake of 1970, the same one that buried Yungay further south, did so much damage to the railroad bed in this canyon that the railway connecting Huallanca to Chimbote was abandoned and has not been used since. The road remained, the tunnels remained, and the canyon kept funneling water from the high Andes to the sea.

## What It Feels Like From Inside

Driving through the Canon del Pato is not a routine activity. The cliffs above rise to glacier-capped peaks. The river runs hundreds of meters below in places, then surges up close to the road in others. The tunnels are short but unlit, and the rock around them is the same rock the river carved into a near-knife-edge. There is no shoulder, no guardrail in the older sections, and oncoming vehicles meet at pullouts negotiated by horn. Mountaineers use the route to access the climbing villages of the upper Callejon. Truckers use it to move goods between the Pacific coast and the highlands. Tourists use it because there is nowhere else in the Andes that compresses this much vertical drama into so narrow a passage. Fifteen meters of river, six thousand meters of mountain, thirty-five tunnels in between.

## Flight Context

Located at 8.67 degrees south, 78.05 degrees west, at the northern outlet of the Callejon de Huaylas in Peru's Ancash Region. The canyon cuts through the convergence of the Cordillera Blanca and Cordillera Negra. Chimbote's airport (CHM) about 75 km southwest is the nearest, with Anta Airport (ATA) near Huaraz about 90 km south-southeast and Trujillo's Capitan FAP Carlos Martinez de Pinillos International (TRU) on the coast to the northwest. The canyon and surrounding peaks above 6,000 meters are visible from cruising altitude in clear weather.
