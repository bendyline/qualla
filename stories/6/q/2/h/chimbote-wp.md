# Chimbote

A Peruvian fishing port that ballooned a hundredfold in a single decade, drowned in anchovy, then learned the price of catching too much.

**Location:** -9.0745, -78.5936

**Categories:** Cities in Peru, Port cities, Ancash Region, Fishing industry

## Introduction

In 1900, the Peruvian port of Chimbote had 1,400 people. By 1970, it had 170,000. Almost nobody who lived there in the boom years had been born there, and almost everyone had come for the same thing: the silver-blue clouds of anchoveta that boiled in the cold Humboldt Current just offshore. The fish were so plentiful that for a few years in the 1960s, Chimbote was the largest fishing port in the world by tonnage. Then the schools collapsed, an earthquake leveled the city, and the people who had migrated for wealth had to figure out what to do next.

## The Bay and the Current

Chimbote sits on Ferrol Bay, 130 kilometers south of Trujillo and 420 kilometers north of Lima, where the Pan-American Highway threads up the desert coast. The geography did all the work that mattered. The cold Humboldt Current sweeps north along the Peruvian coast, dragging deep-water nutrients to the surface, and where those nutrients hit sunlight, plankton blooms in quantities that defy imagination. Anchoveta - the small Peruvian anchovy - feeds on the plankton in oceanic densities, and seabirds, sea lions, and humans feed on the anchoveta. The bay protects ships from the open Pacific. The desert behind the city ensures clear weather most of the year, with thick fog rolling in overnight from May through November and almost no rain at all. From here, on a clear day, you can see snowcapped Huascarán in the Cordillera Blanca, 100 kilometers inland and the highest peak in Peru.

## From 800 to 170,000

In 1835, when General Andrés de Santa Cruz issued the town's first official acknowledgement, Chimbote had no more than 800 fishermen, descendants of huanchaqueros who had migrated from Huanchaco near Trujillo. In 1871, the railroad financier Henry Meiggs signed a deal to build a line into the interior. Chilean troops occupied the town during the War of the Pacific. By 1940 the population was still only 2,400. Then in 1943 the government created the Corporación Peruana del Santa, which built a hydroelectric plant in the dramatic Cañón del Pato gorge, opened a steel mill, and turned the port into a serious industrial operation. The real explosion came in the 1960s, when an Italian-Peruvian businessman named Luis Banchero Rossi industrialized the anchoveta fishery. Wages were high. Word spread to Cajamarca and the highlands. People came in waves.

## The Crash

Two things ended the boom almost at once. The first was overfishing: the anchoveta biomass was hauled out faster than it could regenerate, and the schools thinned year over year. The second was El Niño, the periodic warm-water disruption of the Humboldt Current that scatters the fish and crashes the food chain. When the warm water arrived, even what was left disappeared. Then on May 31, 1970, a magnitude 7.9 earthquake struck off the coast of Ancash. It killed roughly 70,000 people across the region and reduced much of Chimbote's infrastructure to rubble. Many of the migrants who had come for fishing wages had nothing left to stay for. Some moved to nearby Nuevo Chimbote. Some left entirely. The fish factories that survived imposed quotas to give the anchoveta any chance of recovering.

## The Steel Mill and the Bonito Liver

Before the anchoveta boom, Chimbote had two other industries worth knowing. SIDERPERU, the country's first steel mill, opened in 1958 and still produces long steel for construction and mining; it became part of the Brazilian Gerdau group in 2006. And during World War II, an unusual trade emerged: companies in Chimbote extracted livers from Pacific bonito tuna and sold them to overseas markets at high prices. The bonito liver business was a footnote, but it set the template for how a small port could rapidly industrialize whatever the Pacific would yield. Even now, more than 75 percent of Peru's fishing industry operates from Chimbote, and the city has more than thirty fish processing factories. Pittsburgh's Roman Catholic diocese has run a mission here since the 1960s, an old connection that keeps American visitors coming through.

## Saint Peter Goes to Sea

Every June 29, the patron saint of fishermen takes a boat ride. The festival of San Pedrito, dating to the sixteenth century when Huanchaco fishermen first settled here, runs from June 23 to 30, with the feast day on the 29th. The image of Saint Peter is carried through the streets in procession, then loaded onto a fishing boat and paraded around Ferrol Bay. The huge steel pylons of SIDERPERU stand on one side. The whitewashed Cerro de la Juventud, the Mountain of Youth crowned with a cross since 1985, looms behind the city. From the boat you can see Isla Blanca, the white-grounded island that gives the bay its strange luminous quality, and the long curve of the harbor where the descendants of the boom still pull a living from the sea.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 9.07°S, 78.59°W, on the north-central Peruvian coast at sea level. Recommended viewing altitude: 1,500-3,000 meters AGL to see Ferrol Bay, the SIDERPERU steel works, and the city's distinctive sprawl along the coast. On clear days, snowcapped Huascarán (6,768 meters) is visible 100 km to the east in the Cordillera Blanca. Nearest airport is Teniente FAP Jaime Montreuil Morales Airport (SPEO), serving the city. Coastal fog typical May-November overnight; clearer afternoons. Light winds and good visibility most of the year.
