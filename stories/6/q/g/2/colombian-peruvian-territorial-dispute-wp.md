# Colombian-Peruvian Territorial Dispute

A treaty signed nearly a century ago, a port seized by 48 civilians, and an island that emerged from the river itself - the long argument between Colombia and Peru over who owns the Amazon's edge.

**Location:** -7.0214, -74.1408

**Categories:** Territorial disputes, Colombia-Peru border, 20th century history, Amazon basin

## Introduction

Forty-eight people took the city. They were not soldiers, officially. The Peruvian government had ruled them out of the operation specifically because civilians could be disowned if things went wrong. So they organized themselves as the Civilian Recovery Army, gathered weapons through donations and charity, and on September 1, 1932, before dawn, they seized the Colombian river port of Leticia in protest of a treaty they considered an obscenity. They expected light resistance. They got none. Colombian authorities and police fled across the border into Brazil. Within weeks, the Peruvian Army formalized what the civilians had started, and a war that nobody officially wanted was underway.

## What the Map Was Worth

The treaty in question was the Salomón-Lozano Treaty of 1922, which Peru's president Augusto B. Leguía had signed and Congress had finally ratified in 1928. It handed Colombia more than 300,000 square kilometers of Amazon territory, including a strip of land that gave Colombia access to the Amazon River for the first time in its history. Much of that territory was occupied by Peruvian citizens. Leguía's calculation was strategic: Peru was already overwhelmed by territorial disputes with Ecuador and Chile, and he needed an ally on the eastern flank. The bargain worked diplomatically. Colombia subsequently backed Peru against Ecuador, and Ecuador broke off relations with Colombia. But in the Peruvian Amazon, where families had been told their land now belonged to Bogotá, the treaty was experienced as betrayal.

## The Leticia Plan

The conspirators met in the border city of Caballococha. Peruvian civilians Oscar Ordoñez and Juan La Rosa Guevara organized what they called the National Patriotic Junta of Loreto, with quiet support from a small number of military officers. Their manifesto, the Leticia Plan, denounced the Salomón-Lozano Treaty and announced a peaceful recovery of the port. Force would be used only if the Colombians resisted. La Rosa Guevara renounced his army commission to participate as a civilian. The takeover, originally planned for September 15, was moved up two weeks. When the 48 men crossed into Leticia in the early hours of September 1, the port surrendered without a shot. President Luis Miguel Sánchez Cerro then dispatched two regiments of regular soldiers to hold what the civilians had taken. Colombia severed relations in February 1933. The two countries fought for months.

## The Rio Protocol

The shooting ended when President Sánchez Cerro was assassinated in 1933 by a member of APRA, the leftist political party. The new Peruvian government wanted out of the war. The commission to settle the dispute met in Rio de Janeiro that October. Peru sent a delegation that included Víctor Andrés Belaúnde and Raúl Porras Barrenechea, both formidable diplomats who would shape Peruvian foreign policy for decades afterward. Colombia sent its foreign minister, Roberto Urdaneta Arbeláez. Peru tried to bring Ecuador into the negotiations, since the three countries all claimed overlapping territory in the upper Amazon, but Ecuador refused. On May 24, 1934, the diplomats signed the Protocol of Rio de Janeiro, ratifying the Salomón-Lozano Treaty as written. Leticia stayed Colombian. The map drawn in 1922 became the map both countries would teach in their schools.

## The Island That Made Itself

Sometime around 1970, a small island emerged from the Amazon River near the triple border with Brazil, breaking off from the Peruvian-administered Chinería Island through a natural process called fluvial fragmentation. The Civil Guard set up a small outpost and named it Santa Rosa, after Rose of Lima. The first formal settlement, Santa Rosa de Yavarí, was inaugurated on August 30, 1974. Brazilian neighbors attended the ceremony. Colombians did not. For decades the island sat quietly in Peruvian hands, its existence a footnote in the geography of the Tres Fronteras crossing. Then in 2024, during a border security meeting in Leticia, a Colombian foreign ministry official interrupted the mayor of Santa Rosa to insist that the island had never been formally assigned to Peru. The decades-old quiet ended in that interruption.

## Petro and Boluarte

By August 2025, Colombian President Gustavo Petro was posting on X that Peru had usurped Colombian territory. He moved the celebrations of the Battle of Boyacá to Leticia in protest. Peru created the new Santa Rosa de Loreto District by law on July 3, 2025, formalizing the island as administratively Peruvian. A Colombian Super Tucano aircraft was detected overflying the island. Two Colombian topographers were detained by Peruvian police. Peruvian President Dina Boluarte flew to Santa Rosa on August 15 and reaffirmed sovereignty in person. A Colombian flagpole erected on the island's southernmost point was discovered and removed. Petro's argument was that any island formed after 1929 required formal joint assignment, and that the assignment had never happened. Peru's argument was that Santa Rosa was simply part of Chinería Island, which had been Peruvian since 1929. The Amazon kept moving its banks. The map kept failing to keep up.
