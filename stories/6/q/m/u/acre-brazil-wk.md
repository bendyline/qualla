# Acre

Brazil's greenest state, born from a rubber-tappers' rebellion against Bolivia, half-protected forest, half-myth, half-state, depending on which Brazilian you ask.

**Location:** -9.1100, -70.5200

**Categories:** state, amazon, brazil, rubber-history, indigenous-territories

## Introduction

There is a running joke in Brazil that Acre does not exist. It is repeated as a kind of conspiracy meme, that this distant western corner of the country is somehow imaginary, a hoax cooked up by mapmakers. The joke is unfair on every level. Acre is the only state in the Brazilian federation that fought an actual war to join the country, and it is also the greenest, with more than half its territory under some form of environmental protection. Chico Mendes was born here. So was Marina Silva. Distance has not made Acre invisible, but it has made it easy to forget.

## How Acre Became Brazilian

Until the start of the 20th century, this territory belonged to Bolivia. The intense rubber-extraction industry of the late 19th century pulled Brazilian seringueiros, rubber tappers, into the region from many parts of the country, and by 1899 most of the people working the forest here were Brazilian. That year, Brazilian settlers in Acre declared independence and created the short-lived Republic of Acre. Bolivia tried to reassert control, the settlers revolted, and the resulting border conflict became known as the Acre War. On November 17, 1903, with the signing of the Treaty of Petrópolis, Bolivia formally ceded the territory to Brazil in exchange for two million pounds sterling, some land in Mato Grosso, and a promise to build the Madeira-Mamoré railway.

## Indigenous Acre

Long before rubber and longer before any of the borders that would later be drawn here, Acre was home to indigenous peoples of the Panoan language family, including the Kashinawa, Jaminawa, and Xanenawa. Three other groups speak languages from different families: the Madiha, also called Kulina, of the Arawan family; the Yine, called Manchineri; and the Ashaninka, called Kampa, both of the Arawakan family. Some of these communities are still here, including in territories adjoining the Alto Juruá Extractive Reserve in the west. Geoglyphs found in deforested areas of the state, dating back as far as 2,100 years, suggest that the Amazon rainforest here was shaped by complex pre-Columbian societies long before any European arrived.

## Cities in the Forest

Rio Branco is the capital and the largest city, with architecture that draws on forest themes and elements of indigenous culture. The Palácio Rio Branco and the Museu da Borracha, the Rubber Museum, document the region's history. Cruzeiro do Sul, in the far west, serves as the gateway to the Alto Juruá region and the Serra do Divisor. Xapuri, in the rubber country of the Acre valley, is where Chico Mendes lived; the town draws visitors to its monuments commemorating the Acre War and the Mendes house, with an eco-lodge at the Seringal Waterfall. Plácido de Castro sits across the Abunã River from Bolivia, named after the gaúcho commander who led the rubber tappers in the war for independence from Bolivian rule.

## What Grows Here

Acre is one of Brazil's largest producers of açaí, the deep-purple palm fruit that became an international superfood. Locally, the fruit is consumed differently. The fresh, unsweetened juice is mixed with manioc flour for crunch, then sweetened with sugar, condensed milk, or powdered milk. Tacacá is another regional dish that holds onto its strangeness: a broth of manioc water with jambu leaves, a plant that produces an effect somewhere between heat and mild numbness in the mouth. The bowl is sipped directly, Japanese-style. The cuisine here mixes Northeastern Brazilian, Pará, Bolivian, indigenous, and Arab influences, producing dishes like duck in tucupi, sun-dried meat with baião de dois, and fish stews built around the fruits and forest products of the western Amazon.

## The Serra do Divisor

On Acre's western edge, against the Peruvian frontier, the Serra do Divisor rises out of the Amazon plain in hills and low plateaus up to about 600 meters. In one month of fieldwork in 1991, ecologists recorded 43 large mammal species, more than 100 amphibian species, 30 reptile species, 485 bird species, 55 species of bats, and 29 spider families. The Nukini people live along the Juruá and Moa rivers in this country. Visitation requires authorization from ICMBio, the federal agency that manages the national park. The state highways total only about 200 kilometers, mostly clustered around Rio Branco. River transport on the Juruá, Moa, Tarauacá, and Envira does most of the work between November and June, when seasonal rains can make stretches of the BR-364 impassable. Acre exists, but it does so on its own terms.

## Flight Context

Located at 9.11°S, 70.52°W in the western Brazilian Amazon, bordering Peru and Bolivia. The state spans the upper Juruá and Acre river basins. From cruising altitude, the unbroken green of the Amazon stretches in every direction, broken only by river courses and the long line of the BR-364. Nearest airports are Rio Branco International (SBRB) at the capital and Cruzeiro do Sul International (SBCZ) for the western Alto Juruá region. Best viewing 10,000 to 18,000 feet AGL; cloud cover is heavy in the wet season November through April, with clearer skies June through September.
