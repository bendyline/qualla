# Acre

The only Brazilian state that fought an armed war to join Brazil, where rubber tappers became revolutionaries and a black caiman reputed to be the largest ever was killed in 1965.

**Location:** -9.0238, -70.8120

**Categories:** state, amazon, brazil, rubber-history, indigenous-history

## Introduction

In 1965, by a much-repeated account, someone in Acre shot a black caiman said to measure 7.7 meters long and weigh 1,310 kilograms. The report has never been confirmed, and scientists put the species' maximum at around five to six and a half meters, but it is still cited as the largest black caiman on record. That detail captures something about the state of Acre that statistics alone cannot. This is the place at the western end of Brazil where the Amazon does not relent, where a name perhaps comes from a misread letter, and where a war fought by rubber tappers in the early 20th century redrew an international border by force.

## A Name From a Misread Letter

The name Acre may come from the Tupi a'kir ü, meaning green river, or from a'kir, related to the verb ker, to sleep or rest. More likely, it derives from Aquiri, a European transliteration of the Ipurinã word Umákürü or Uakiry. According to one widely repeated account, in 1878 the agriculturist João Gabriel de Carvalho Melo wrote from the Purús River to a merchant in Belém asking for goods to be sent to the mouth of the Aquiri River. Either Gabriel's handwriting was unclear or he misspelled the word: when the goods arrived, the invoice was marked Acre. The letter that became the name became the state in 1962. Acre carries other names too: the End of Brazil, the Rubber Tree State, the Latex State, the Western End. People from here are called acrianos.

## Geoglyphs in the Cleared Forest

Since the 1970s, deforestation in Acre has revealed something unexpected: hundreds of geometric earthworks, geoglyphs cut into the land in precise circles, squares, and other shapes, dated to between roughly 1 and 1250 CE. Ondemar Dias is credited with the first discovery in 1977, and Alceu Ranzi later expanded the catalog by flying low over Acre with a camera. The structures are evidence of complex pre-Columbian societies organizing the Amazonian landscape on a substantial scale, contradicting the older view that the rainforest was a pristine wilderness untouched by humans. The BBC's Unnatural Histories series concluded the Amazon had been shaped by humans for at least 11,000 years, much of it through forest gardening that altered the species mix without clearing the canopy.

## Bolivian, Then Brazilian

Until the 19th century, Acre belonged to Bolivia under the Treaty of Ayacucho. The discovery of rubber-tree groves in the mid-1800s pulled migrants in from the Brazilian Northeast. By 1877 the population was overwhelmingly Brazilian. In 1899, when Bolivia set up a customs post in Puerto Alonso to tax the rubber, the seringueiros revolted, declared the Republic of Acre, and the Acre War began. Three short-lived republics, several Bolivian counter-expeditions, and one rubber-tappers' army led by the gaúcho José Plácido de Castro later, the Treaty of Petrópolis transferred the territory to Brazil on November 17, 1903. Brazil paid two million pounds sterling, ceded land in Mato Grosso, and agreed to build the Madeira-Mamoré railway to move rubber out of the region.

## Rubber Soldiers and the Forest Today

During the Second World War, when Japanese forces took over the rubber plantations of British Malaya, the Allies turned again to Amazonian latex. The Brazilian government recruited what it called the Rubber Soldiers, mostly drawn from drought-stricken Ceará in the Northeast, and sent tens of thousands of them to the western Amazon to tap rubber for the Allied war effort. Many never went home. Many died of disease and exhaustion in the seringais. Their descendants are still here. Acre's most famous son, Chico Mendes, the rubber-tapper turned environmentalist who was murdered in Xapuri in 1988, came directly out of this culture and changed how the world thinks about Amazonian conservation. The Chico Mendes Institute, the agency that now manages Brazil's federal protected areas, bears his name.

## The Greenest State

Acre covers about 164,000 square kilometers in the extreme west of Brazil, bordered by Amazonas, Rondônia, Bolivia, and Peru. The Amazon rainforest covers practically all of it. About 63% of the state's surface lies between 200 and 300 meters in elevation, with the highest point at 609 meters in the Serra do Divisor along the Peruvian border. Roughly half the state's territory is under some form of protection: indigenous lands, extractive reserves, national parks. The 2022 IBGE census recorded 830,018 people, about 5 inhabitants per square kilometer, making Acre the third least populous Brazilian state. Its exports, once almost entirely timber, are now led by soybeans and meat, alongside Brazil nuts and wood. Much of its wealth is still the standing forest, part of an Amazon biome that shelters at least one in ten of the world's species.

## Flight Context

Located at 9.11°S, 70.52°W in the western Brazilian Amazon, at a two-hour time difference from Brasília. From cruising altitude, the green expanse of the Amazon stretches unbroken in every direction, with the Serra do Divisor rising on the western horizon along the Peruvian border. Nearest airports are Rio Branco International (SBRB) at the capital and Cruzeiro do Sul International (SBCZ) in the far west. Best viewing 10,000 to 20,000 feet AGL; cloud cover is heavy in the wet season November through April, with clearer skies in the dry season June through September.
