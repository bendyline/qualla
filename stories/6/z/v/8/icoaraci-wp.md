# Icoaraci

In a district whose Tupi name means 'sun of the river,' potters in the Paracuri neighborhood reproduce vessel forms last made by the Marajoara a thousand years ago.

**Location:** -1.3000, -48.4667

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in Brazil, Pará, Belém

## Introduction

The name is a compound in an older language. Y means water, or river. Kûarasy means sun. Join them and you get Icoaraci - sun of the river - a Tupi phrase that survives on maps of Belém as the name of a district twenty kilometers north of the state capital, where Guajará Bay opens toward Marajó and the streets still number themselves by order of founding. First Street. Second Street. All the way out to Seventh, where the port sits and the boats leave for the islands. The district has around two hundred thousand inhabitants, give or take what the last IBGE census recorded, and some of them make their living doing something remarkable: copying, by hand, the pottery of a civilization that vanished before the Portuguese arrived.

## The Paracuri Potters

In the Paracuri neighborhood, a few streets back from the bay, the ceramic tradition is not folk art in the decorative sense. It is scholarship in clay. The potters here reproduce vessel forms from the Marajoara and Tapajonic cultures - the indigenous nations that shaped the Amazon before contact - working from pieces catalogued by the Museu Paraense Emílio Goeldi, the great Amazon natural history museum in Belém proper. Every curve of a Marajoara funerary urn, every geometric motif running around a Tapajonic bowl, exists in Paracuri because someone studied a museum specimen and learned to make it again. Mestre Cardoso, a pioneer of the art, still lives in the district. So does professor Etelvina, a pioneer of the folk dance tradition that runs parallel to the ceramics. The neighborhood's artisans' cooperative, COART, and the Museum of Popular Art anchor what is, functionally, a living archive.

## The Table

Walk along Cruzeiro Beach at dusk and the kiosks that line the shore of Guajará Bay send up smoke that carries the peculiar aromatics of Amazonian cooking. Tacacá, a hot broth of tucupi and jambu leaves and dried shrimp that numbs the tongue as it warms. Maniçoba, the week-long slow-cook of manioc leaves and pork that some people call Brazil's most laborious dish. Pato no tucupi, duck simmered in the yellow liquid pressed from bitter manioc. Caldeirada, the river-fish stew built on Amazon fruits that visitors rarely meet in any other form. The kitchens here cook what the region grew, not what the country exported, and the menu is closer to an indigenous inventory than to what most Brazilians eat in the south. Tourists come for the ceramics. They stay for the food.

## The Bay and the Islands

Icoaraci sits on the edge of something large. Guajará Bay, the broad estuarine mouth where the Pará River system feeds into the Atlantic, spreads west toward Marajó, the island at the Amazon's mouth that is itself roughly the size of Switzerland. From the port at Seventh Street, ferries leave daily for Marajó, for Cotijuba Island - which can only be reached by boat - and for the nearer Outeiro Island, also approachable by a bridge from the north end of the district. Cotijuba's beaches have only the infrastructure that boats can bring. The passage is short but the shift in register is total: from a port district with shopping warehouses to an island where the loudest sound is the river.

## The Streets That Number Themselves

The original nucleus of the district kept the old colonial habit of naming its streets simply. Travessas are lanes; ruas are streets, and the streets are numbered in the order they were laid out. The official names, when they exist, remember people. Manoel Barata, known locally as Second Street. Padre Júlio Maria, the Third. Fifteen de Agosto, the Fourth. Coronel Juvêncio Sarmento, the Fifth. And so on to Seventh, where the port is and the day begins with the unloading of fish from the bay. The open-air market at Oito de Maio, in the Campina neighborhood, is one of the busiest in all of Belém. Icoaraci's HDI sits far below the Brazilian average, a fact the district's own self-description is blunt about. But the cultural patrimony measured in clay and in dance steps is not something the income figures catch.

## Flight Context

Located at 1.30 degrees S, 48.47 degrees W on the north shore of Guajará Bay, about 20 km north of central Belém. Best viewed from 2,000-5,000 feet AGL on approach to Belém/Val de Cans International Airport (SBBE), which lies just southwest. The bay's wide waters and the adjacent Outeiro and Cotijuba islands give strong visual reference. The Atlantic mouth of the Amazon-Pará system opens to the north.
