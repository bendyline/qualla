# Icoaraci

In a district whose Tupi name means 'sun of the river,' some eighty workshops reproduce vessel forms the Marajoara made centuries before Europeans reached the Amazon.

**Location:** -1.3000, -48.4667

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in Brazil, Pará, Belém

## Introduction

The name is a compound in an older language. Y means water, or river. Kûarasy means sun. Join them and you get Icoaraci — sun of the river — a Tupi phrase that survives on maps of Belém as the name of a district twenty kilometers north of the state capital, where Guajará Bay opens toward Marajó and the streets still number themselves by order of founding. First Street. Second Street. All the way out to Seventh, where the port sits and the boats leave for the islands. Around 171,500 people lived here at the 2022 census, and a few hundred of them make their living doing something remarkable: copying, by hand, the pottery of civilizations that vanished before the Portuguese arrived.

## The Paracuri Potters

In the Paracuri neighborhood, a few streets back from the bay, the ceramic tradition is not folk art in the decorative sense. It is scholarship in clay. Roughly eighty workshops operate here, employing something like 220 artisans, and what they produce are vessel forms from the Marajoara and Tapajonic cultures — the Indigenous nations that shaped the lower Amazon before contact — worked from pieces catalogued by the Museu Paraense Emílio Goeldi, the great Amazon natural history museum in Belém proper. Every curve of a Marajoara funerary urn, every geometric band running around a Tapajonic bowl, exists in Paracuri because someone studied a museum specimen and learned to make it again. Mestre Cardoso, a pioneer of the art, worked in this district. So did professora Etelvina, a pioneer of the folk dance tradition that runs parallel to the ceramics. The artisans' cooperative, COART, the Paracuri Handicraft Fair, and the Museum of Popular Art anchor what is, functionally, a living archive.

## The Line That Ended Here

Icoaraci was not always called that. Carmelite friars held land here in the seventeenth century, and the settlement that grew around their farms was known for generations as Pinheiro. What turned it from a rural parish into a destination was rail: in 1906 the Pinheiro station opened as the terminus of a branch of the Belém–Bragança Railway, and the capital's comfortable families began coming out for the weekend. The district became a resort before it became a suburb. The trains stopped long ago, but the building survived, and in 2019 it reopened as the Icoaraci Cultural Station — a second use for a structure whose first purpose ended decades before.

## The Table

Walk along Cruzeiro Beach at dusk and the kiosks lining the shore of Guajará Bay send up smoke carrying the peculiar aromatics of Amazonian cooking. Tacacá, a hot broth of tucupi and jambu leaves and dried shrimp that numbs the tongue as it warms. Maniçoba, the long slow-cook of manioc leaves and pork that some people call Brazil's most laborious dish. Pato no tucupi, duck simmered in the yellow liquid pressed from bitter manioc. Caldeirada, the river-fish stew built around Amazon fruits that visitors rarely meet in any other form. The kitchens here cook what the region grew rather than what the country exported, and the menu reads closer to an Indigenous inventory than to anything served in the south. Tourists come for the ceramics. They stay for the food.

## The Bay and the Islands

Icoaraci sits on the edge of something large. Guajará Bay, the broad estuarine mouth where the Pará River system feeds toward the Atlantic, spreads west toward Marajó — the island at the Amazon's mouth that is itself roughly the size of Switzerland. From the port at Seventh Street, ferries leave daily for Marajó, for Cotijuba Island, which can only be reached by boat, and for the nearer Outeiro Island, also approachable by bridge. Cotijuba's beaches have only the infrastructure that boats can bring. The passage is short but the shift in register is total: from a port district with wholesale warehouses to an island where the loudest sound is the river. The waterfront at Vila Sorriso holds both worlds in one frame — ceramics on display in São Sebastião Square, the bay behind them, and the vessels coming and going.

## The Streets That Number Themselves

The original nucleus kept the old colonial habit of naming its streets simply. Travessas are lanes; ruas are streets, and the streets are numbered in the order they were laid out. Official names, where they exist, remember people. Manoel Barata, known locally as Second Street. Padre Júlio Maria, the Third. Quinze de Agosto, the Fourth. Coronel Juvêncio Sarmento, the Fifth. And so on to Seventh, where the port is and the day begins with fish coming off the bay. Beyond the kilns, the district works: an industrial park handling fishing, timber, carpentry, and heart of palm, and the open-air market at Oito de Maio in the Campina neighborhood, one of the busiest in all of Belém. Icoaraci's income indicators sit well below the Brazilian average. The cultural patrimony measured in clay and in dance steps is not something those figures catch — and the musicians Mestre Verequete and Nazaré Pereira, and the poet Antônio Tavernard, all came from this ledger too.

## Flight Context

Icoaraci lies at 1.30°S, 48.47°W on the eastern shore of Guajará Bay, about 20 km north of central Belém and part of the metropolitan area's continuous urban fabric. From the air it reads as dense low-rise development meeting a broad brown bay, with Outeiro and Cotijuba offshore to the northwest and the vast Marajó landmass beyond. Best viewed from 2,000–5,000 ft AGL on approach to Belém/Val de Cans International (SBBE), which lies roughly 10 km to the south — expect controlled airspace and arriving traffic. Near-equatorial convection produces afternoon thunderstorms almost daily; mornings offer the best visibility.
