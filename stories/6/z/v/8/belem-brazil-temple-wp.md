# Belem Brazil Temple

The 174th operating temple of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, an 89-foot spire of Brazilian granite at the mouth of the Amazon, dedicated in 2022.

**Location:** -1.3868, -48.4600

**Categories:** religious-architecture, brazil, amazon, landmarks

## Introduction

An 89-foot spire of Brazilian granite rises above a city at the mouth of the Amazon. On 20 November 2022, an apostle named Dale G. Renlund stood inside and read a dedication prayer across three sessions in a single Sunday, opening the 174th operating temple of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. The ninth temple in Brazil joined a national membership of about 1.5 million in a country where the church has been planting missions since the 1920s. But this particular temple is about geography. It is the church's second temple in the Brazilian Amazon, after Manaus, and the Saints who attend it travel river distances to reach it that the founders in Palmyra and Nauvoo would have found staggering.

## The Announcement

On 3 April 2016, during the church's general conference, church president Thomas S. Monson read a short list of new temples into the pulpit microphone: Quito, Ecuador; Harare, Zimbabwe; a second temple in Lima, Peru; and Belem, Brazil. The announcement was brief. The implications were not. Belem had a functioning stake center and a growing local membership, but a temple meant something categorically different. It meant the highest ordinances of the faith would be available inside city limits. For members in Para and Amapa, it meant the end of flights to Manaus or Recife or Sao Paulo to access those ordinances. For the church's builders, it meant acquiring land in a tropical port city and putting up a granite-clad building designed to function for generations in heat and humidity that would destroy most American buildings of the same size.

## Three Years from Groundbreaking

Marcos A. Aidukaitis, a general authority and then-president of the Brazil Area, conducted the groundbreaking ceremony on 17 August 2019. Three years later, the building was complete. An open house ran from 22 October through 5 November 2022, closed on Sundays, during which visitors of any faith or none could walk through the building's interior: two instruction rooms, two sealing rooms, a baptistry, and the celestial room that serves as the architectural summit of every temple. After dedication, entry would be restricted to members holding a current temple recommend, a credential renewed every two years through interviews with local and regional church leaders.

## Architecture of a Tropical Temple

The design keeps the familiar features of the church's modern temples, a single attached central spire with a gilt statue of the angel Moroni at the peak, a cladding of Brazilian granite chosen for durability in tropical conditions, but the particulars were tuned for the region. The architects drew on what the church has described as the cultural heritage of the Belem region, working geometric motifs drawn from Marajoara pottery and the Victoria amazonica water lily into the exterior and art glass, in the blues, greens, yellows and pinks of the rainforest. Renlund's dedicatory prayer explicitly invoked the Amazon River and the pororoca, the tidal bore where Atlantic swell and river current collide to produce standing waves that can reach four meters high.

## The People Who Use It

The temple's first president and matron, who served from 2022 to 2025, were J. Joel Fernandes and Maristela A. Fernandes. The congregations served by the temple stretch along the Amazon and Tocantins river systems, across Para and Amapa, into regions where members may travel two or three days by river to attend a temple session. Local wards have adopted the temple as a gathering point. Weddings, called sealings in church terminology, now happen here rather than requiring long journeys to distant temples, once a three-day bus ride to Sao Paulo. Vicarious ordinances, performed on behalf of deceased ancestors, bring members into the temple multiple times per month. The church had been present in Para for decades before the temple was announced. The building made that presence permanent in the way the church counts permanence, which is in dedicated granite rather than rented halls.

## A Temple and a Climate Summit

The temple sits in the city's northern suburbs, visible from highway approaches into Belem. Most visitors arriving for secular reasons, including the international delegations attending COP30 in November 2025 just three years after the dedication, would pass it without knowing what it was. For those who knew, it marked an organizational pattern: a global faith that makes permanent physical investments in places the commercial and political world rediscovers slowly and then intermittently. Belem had one temple before Belem had a UN climate conference. The Saints who tend its grounds maintain them quietly, through rains and droughts and the constant press of tropical vegetation, a spire of granite and a statue of gold rising above a city whose real skyline is the forest that begins where the suburbs end.

## Flight Context

Located at 1.39 S, 48.46 W in the northern outskirts of Belem, Para, Brazil, about 2 km east-southeast of Val-de-Cans International Airport (SBBE). Best viewed from FL100-FL200 in daylight, when the temple's white granite exterior and central gold-tipped spire are visible against tropical green suburbs. The proximity to the Amazon estuary means humid air and frequent afternoon convective buildups. Nearest alternate airport is Macapa (SBMQ) across the estuary. Expect clear skies and good visibility in the September-to-November dry season.
