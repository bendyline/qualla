# Belém/Val-de-Cans International Airport

Brazil's Atlantic gateway during WWII, named for a 19th-century Amazonian balloonist, still routing traffic between the Americas at the mouth of the Amazon.

**Location:** -1.3847, -48.4789

**Categories:** airport, aviation-history, world-war-ii, amazon, brazil

## Introduction

In 1934, a lieutenant named Armando Sierra de Menezes stood in a flat stretch of Amazonian scrubland twelve kilometers outside Belém and tried to imagine an airport. He had been sent by General Eurico Gaspar Dutra, then Director of Military Aviation, and the only instruction was to find a site. What he chose became Val-de-Cans, a strip of concrete and a single hangar eventually nicknamed the Yellow Hangar for reasons nobody troubles to explain. Within a decade, thousands of American warplanes would thunder across it on their way from Canadian factories to North African battlefields.

## The Airbridge South

When the Second World War broke out, geography turned Belém into one of the most strategically valuable places on the planet. The great circle route from the United States to Africa and Europe bends hard toward Brazil's northeastern shoulder, and Val-de-Cans sat almost exactly where that arc touches land. After protracted negotiations between Washington and Brasília, the Air Transport Command built two concrete-and-asphalt runways measuring 1,500 by 45 meters, modern for their era and capable of handling heavy transports. Bombers and fighters manufactured in Canada and the United States hopped down through Belém on their way across the South Atlantic to Sierra Leone, then onward to the fighting. When the Americans handed the airfield back to the Brazilian Ministry of Aeronautics in 1945, they left behind a facility transformed.

## The Balloonist They Honored

The airport carries an unusual name for a Brazilian airfield. On 13 April 2010, Law 12.228 formally christened it after Júlio Cezar Ribeiro de Souza, a researcher who lived from 1837 to 1887 and spent much of his working life designing dirigible balloons decades before powered flight existed. Ribeiro de Souza was a Paraense, a son of this same Amazonian state, and his work on steerable lighter-than-air craft was genuinely inventive for its time, even if it never achieved the commercial success its author hoped for. Naming the modern airport after him stitches together two eras of aviation separated by almost a century, and roots the facility in local history rather than in the anonymous geography of a neighborhood called Val de Cans, sometimes spelled Val de Caes, which is what it would otherwise commemorate.

## Terminals and Transitions

After the war, commercial aviation settled in quickly. Panair do Brasil, Pan American, and NAB built stations at Val-de-Cans and began selling tickets. In 1958 the Ministry of Aeronautics started work on the first real passenger terminal, opening it on 24 January 1959. Infraero took over administration in 1974 and, across decades, rebuilt the place twice. A brand new terminal rose beside the original in 1999; the old building came down to make room for an extension that added six jetways and a great deal of comfort. In August 2022, a consortium called Novo Norte, formed by the Brazilian firms Socicam and Dix, won a 30-year concession to operate the airport, ending nearly half a century of direct state management.

## The Harder History

Val-de-Cans shares its grounds with Belém Air Force Base, and the aviation history written on these runways is not only one of growth. Between 1955 and 1984, a string of accidents and hijackings carved the region's name into aviation safety records. A Douglas C-47 struck a tree on takeoff in 1955, killing all six aboard. A Fairchild Packet burned after engine failure in 1958. A DC-4 went into Carapi Island on night approach that August with only one of eleven survivors. On 14 March 1970, a Paraense Fairchild Hiller on final approach from Sao Luis crashed into Guajara Bay; three of forty on board survived. Hijackers took flights from here toward Cuba in 1969, 1970, and 1984, mostly without victims. These were real people, crews and passengers following ordinary routes between Amazonian cities, caught in the hard early decades of civil aviation in the tropics.

## A Living Hub

Today Val-de-Cans connects Belem to the rest of Brazil and outward to South America, North America, and Europe, with direct service to Lisbon that continues an old Atlantic affinity. The terminal hums with passengers heading for Sao Paulo and Brasilia, for Manaus and Macapa, for the smaller airfields scattered across the Amazon basin. In the air above the mango trees and the twisting channels of the Para River, pilots still follow versions of the routes first plotted during the war, approaching a city that grew rich on rubber, declined, and now sits 1.3 million strong at the gate of the greatest river system on Earth.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 1.38S, 48.48W. ICAO SBBE, IATA BEL. Approach over the Para River and Guajara Bay. Cruising altitude 35,000 feet offers excellent visibility of the Amazon delta and Marajo Island to the west in clear conditions. Nearby airfields include Macapa (SBMQ) 330 km north and Marabá (SBMA) 560 km south. Expect convective weather and afternoon thunderstorms year-round; morning arrivals generally smoothest.
