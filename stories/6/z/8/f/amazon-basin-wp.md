# Amazon Basin

A seven-million-square-kilometer drainage basin across nine countries, carrying a fifth of all river water on Earth and holding a tenth of all known species.

**Location:** -2.3000, -54.8833

**Categories:** amazon, rivers, biodiversity, ecosystems

## Introduction

Thirty-five and a half percent of South America drains into a single river system. The Amazon basin covers roughly 7 million square kilometers, larger than the contiguous United States and India combined, stretching across nine countries: Bolivia, Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, Peru, Suriname, Venezuela, and the French territory of Guiana. Rain falling on the eastern slope of the Andes in southern Peru finds its way through a branching network of tributaries, the Apurimac and the Maranon and a thousand others, into a main stem that flows 6,400 kilometers east to the Atlantic, delivering about 20 percent of all the fresh water on Earth to the ocean. Before the Andes rose, this water used to flow west into the Pacific. The mountains changed everything.

## The Water Column

The scale of the river is difficult to grasp from ground level. Where the Amazon meets the Atlantic at its mouth, the main stem is over 300 kilometers wide counting the outer islands. The pulse of fresh water entering the ocean is large enough to push salt water back 500 kilometers from shore. Satellites can see the sediment plume for hundreds of kilometers out to sea. The river itself runs through three chemically distinct flavors: whitewater rivers like the main Amazon, carrying heavy Andean sediment; clearwater rivers like the Tapajos, flowing off ancient shield rock; and blackwater rivers like the Rio Negro, stained amber by dissolved plant tannins and nearly free of suspended solids. Where two of these meet, at the Encontro das Aguas outside Manaus, the currents run side by side for miles without mixing, the dark Negro water and the tan Solimoes water held apart by differences in temperature, speed, and density.

## A Tenth of Everything

The basin holds an estimated 10 percent of the world's biodiversity. More than 1,400 mammal species live here, the majority bats and rodents, with larger ones including jaguars, ocelots, capybaras, pumas, and South American tapirs. About 1,500 bird species fly through, from Guianan cocks-of-the-rock to the clay-licking macaws of the western Amazon. The snake count includes green anacondas in shallow water and emerald tree boas in the canopy. More than 1,500 amphibian species keep their skins moist in the humidity, most of them arboreal rather than aquatic. And the fish tally is the most remarkable number in the basin. About 2,500 species are known; scientists estimate at least another 1,000 are undescribed. Forty-five percent of the known ones are endemic, found nowhere else on Earth. The Characiformes alone, the order that includes piranhas and tetras, account for 43 percent of the basin's fish. The smallest Amazonian fish, the sleeper goby Leptophilypnion, does not exceed one centimeter and is among the tiniest vertebrates on Earth.

## Fish That Walk, Jump, and Hide

The basin's fish have pushed into ecological niches that temperate fish do not. The splash tetra lays eggs on leaves above the water line and keeps them moist by splashing. The South American lungfish buries itself in the mud and waits out the dry season inside a mucous cocoon. Certain rivulid killifish jump overland between water bodies, sometimes moving uphill, to reach new pools or escape predators. An undescribed Phreatobius catfish spends its life not in streams but in the waterlogged leaf litter alongside them, a worm-like creature that has essentially left water behind. Cavefish of the genus Phreatobius live in underground aquifers in the Andean fringe of the basin. The Amazon contains species that climb, jump, dig, walk, and endure drought, collectively expanding the definition of what a fish can do.

## People, Past and Present

About 1.5 million people live in the Amazon basin today, most of them concentrated in a few river cities: Iquitos in Peruvian Loreto, Manaus in Brazilian Amazonas state, and Belem at the mouth of the river in Para. The rural population is scattered. Four hundred or more distinct tribal groups remain across nine countries, with perhaps 100 uncontacted groups still living in voluntary isolation. COICA, the Coordinator of Indigenous Organizations of the Amazon River Basin, federates the indigenous-rights groups across all of the Amazonian countries. The languages most widely spoken are Portuguese and Spanish. The hundreds of native languages that predate the colonial era are nearly all endangered. Recent archaeological work has overturned the older picture of the Amazon as permanently sparsely inhabited wilderness. Large pre-Columbian earthworks, road networks, and terra preta anthropogenic soils show that before European disease arrived, the basin supported substantial organized populations in multiple regions. What looks like untouched forest to a modern visitor is, in many places, the regrown successor of centuries of human management.

## The Biosphere Under Pressure

The Amazon vegetation stores around 56.8 billion metric tons of carbon above ground. Between 50 and 80 percent of the basin's rainwater is recycled within the forest itself, transpired by trees and returned to the sky to fall again as rain, a closed loop that drives weather systems across most of South America. Break the loop by removing the trees and the rainfall declines, with consequences extending to Argentina, Paraguay, and the Brazilian southeast. That break is in progress. Over the twenty years ending in 2021, the Brazilian Amazon emitted 13 percent more CO2 than it absorbed: the continent's largest carbon sink became, on net, a source. The principal drivers are cattle ranching, soy cultivation, and slash-and-burn expansion, with road-building opening access to previously forested land. What is at stake is not only an ecosystem. The hydrological cycle that the basin drives is one of Earth's major climate regulators, and a simplified or desertified Amazon would produce consequences at planetary scale. This was the argument Brazil brought to COP30 in Belem in November 2025. It is the argument that keeps being made from the world's largest river.

## Flight Context

The Amazon basin centroid near 2.30 S, 54.88 W falls in Para state, Brazil, near where the Tapajos joins the main stem of the Amazon. The basin as a whole spans roughly from 5 N in Venezuela and Colombia to 20 S in Bolivia and the Brazilian Mato Grosso, and from the Andean crest in Peru and Ecuador east to the Atlantic coast of Amapa. Best aerial viewing altitudes are FL300-FL400 for continental context. Major airports along the main stem include Belem Val-de-Cans (SBBE), Santarem (SBSN), Manaus Eduardo Gomes (SBEG), Tabatinga (SBTT), and Iquitos (SPQT) in Peru. Expect heavy convective weather year-round, with most intense storms in the afternoon. Dry-season burning haze (August-November) can reduce visibility considerably.
