# Monte Alegre várzea

A seasonally flooded Amazonian forest where the river rises six meters every year and the trees have learned to feed the fish.

**Location:** -2.0270, -55.6450

**Categories:** Ecoregions of Brazil, Amazon biome, Neotropical tropical and subtropical moist broadleaf forests

## Introduction

Every year the Amazon breathes in and out. For as long as eight months, the river swells and spreads across the lowlands along its central and lower basin, rising six meters and spilling miles into the forest. Trees that stood on dry ground in November stand in water by March. Fish swim through branches. And the forest, rather than drowning, feeds them. This is the Monte Alegre várzea, a living floodplain where the rhythm of inundation shapes everything from the color of the soil to the species of monkey that can survive here.

## The Breathing Forest

The várzea is the name Brazilians give to forests that flood. Unlike the higher terra firme jungle on either side, this is a landscape engineered by the river itself. Sediments washed down from the Andes during the Holocene created soils so rich they put the surrounding rainforest to shame. Each year's flood deposits a new layer, renewing nutrients that terra firme cannot match. Trees here have adapted to standing underwater for months at a time, growing shorter than their upland cousins, capping out at about 25 meters. They send out buttresses and prop roots to stay anchored in the soft, shifting mud. The understory is dense with Heliconia and ginger-family plants that thrive in the humidity.

## Fish in the Treetops

The strangest thing about the várzea is that during flood season, fish become the forest's primary seed dispersers. Trees like yellow mombim, socoró, and tarumã drop their fruit at exactly the moment the water is highest, and fruit-eating fish swim up to claim them. The seeds pass through the fish and are deposited elsewhere when the waters recede. The economically valuable palms Astrocaryum jauari and the açaí palm, Euterpe oleracea, depend on this partnership. It is one of the few forests on Earth where the aquatic and arboreal worlds are not separate at all. They are the same world, taking turns.

## The Animals That Live Between

Two hundred mammal species call the várzea home, from jaguars and ocelots prowling the drier edges to capybaras and tapirs navigating the margins. But the water mammals are the signatures. Amazon river dolphins, pink and ghostly, chase fish through flooded forest. Tucuxis follow. The endangered Amazonian manatee grazes on floating grasses. Giant otters, also endangered, hunt in family groups along oxbow lakes. Black caimans lurk where the water is deepest. Green anacondas wrap themselves around branches above the tideline. And 681 species of birds have been recorded here, including endangered sun parakeets and wattled curassows, plus the namesake várzea piculet, a tiny woodpecker found nowhere else.

## Primates of a Shifting World

Primates in the várzea face a puzzle that primates in terra firme never have to solve: the ground disappears for half the year. Spider monkeys, red howlers, squirrel monkeys, and tamarins have all found ways to live in canopies that become islands during flood season. Several primates are endemic to this ecoregion, found nowhere else in the world, including Hoffmanns's titi, the ashy black titi, the Santarem marmoset, and the white-footed saki. The endangered white-cheeked spider monkey, white-nosed saki, and pied tamarin still hold territory here, though shrinking. Every year the flood defines which tree islands hold enough food for a troop to survive until the water recedes.

## Critical and Endangered

The World Wildlife Fund rates the Monte Alegre várzea as Critical or Endangered. Ranchers clear and burn floodplain forests to create pasture for cattle. Gold mining contaminates water and fish with mercury. Deforestation upstream causes sediment loads to shift, changing which trees can survive where. And the várzea is low and flat, meaning there is nowhere uphill for species to retreat to as temperatures rise. A forest that evolved for a specific six-meter flood cycle cannot easily adjust when that cycle changes. The várzea has existed as it is for thousands of years. Whether it survives the next hundred depends on choices being made now, in Brasília and Beijing and Berlin, by people who may never see a pink dolphin surface in their lifetime.

## Flight Context

Centered near 2.03°S, 55.65°W, the Monte Alegre várzea stretches along the Amazon River in central Pará. From cruising altitude the seasonal flooding is dramatic in wet season, with silver-brown water spread across tens of kilometers of forest. Nearest airports: Santarém-Maestro Wilson Fonseca (SBSN) to the southwest and Monte Alegre Airport (SNMA) directly over the ecoregion. The Amazon's silver ribbon and oxbow lakes stand out clearly between 15,000 and 35,000 feet.
