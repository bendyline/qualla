# Belterra, Pará

Henry Ford's second attempt to grow American rubber in the Amazon—a flat-topped plateau of transplanted Michigan, complete with bungalows, rubber trees, and fire hydrants.

**Location:** -2.6358, -54.9369

**Categories:** plantation, industrial-history, amazon, brazil, rubber, ford

## Introduction

Henry Ford never once set foot in Brazil, but he owned 10,000 square kilometers of it for more than two decades, and he spent roughly 20 million dollars trying to grow American-owned rubber inside the Amazon rainforest. His first attempt, Fordlandia, opened in 1928 on a site chosen mostly because the timber company that sold it to him wanted to unload it. The place failed spectacularly. So in the early 1930s, Ford's engineers moved 100 kilometers north and started again, on a flat plateau above the Tapajos River where the soil was better and the terrain permitted the use of machinery. They named the new plantation Belterra.

## Why Henry Ford Cared About Rubber

During the 1920s, the British-controlled rubber plantations of Southeast Asia had driven prices up to what Detroit considered intolerable levels. Every automobile needs tires, and every tire then required natural latex from Hevea brasiliensis, a tree native to the Amazon basin but, by a historical accident, successfully cultivated only in British Malaya and Ceylon. The US Department of Commerce encouraged American companies to find alternative supplies. Ford took the challenge seriously. The logic was simple: Hevea brasiliensis came from Brazil. Grow it in Brazil, ship it to Dearborn, free Ford Motor Company from British price-setting. The execution was harder. The leaf-blight fungus Microcyclus ulei, which had co-evolved with the tree and kept wild populations sparse enough to resist infection, thrived on plantation monocultures.

## The Plateau

Belterra's site sat 165 meters above sea level at the edge of the Planalto escarpment, some 40 kilometers south of Santarem. The flat topography, which had disqualified Fordlandia, was the reason Belterra got built; machinery could work the ground where Fordlandia had demanded entirely manual labor. Soil scientists now know the plateau is also famous for something older: it carries some of the best-documented deposits of terra preta, the anthropogenic dark earth that indigenous Amazonian societies created through centuries of composting, burning, and farming. Radiocarbon analyses of ceramic fragments pulled from Belterra's terra preta show that people have been cultivating this ground intensively since at least 500 BC. Ford's agronomists may not have fully understood what they had, but the indigenous farmers who built that soil over millennia gave his plantation one of its few real advantages.

## The American Town in the Amazon

At its peak in the late 1930s, roughly 50 square kilometers of Belterra were cultivated with Hevea brasiliensis. The plantation employed 8,000 to 10,000 people at its fullest extent, most of them Brazilian workers living in a landscape engineered to resemble Michigan. Ford built identical wooden bungalows with screened porches, installed a water tower that would have looked at home in any Midwestern suburb, planted lawns, opened a school, built a hospital, and enforced rules against alcohol, tobacco, and dancing. The agronomists here applied new breeding methods using local varieties in an effort to defeat the leaf blight that had wrecked Fordlandia's monoculture. The effort succeeded biologically, up to a point, but it was enormously labor-intensive and therefore expensive.

## What Ford Left Behind

By 1945 the whole experiment had become untenable. Synthetic rubber, developed during World War II, made natural latex far less strategic. Global demand for raw rubber collapsed, and Belterra's high labor costs could not compete with Asian producers. Ford Motor Company sold the plantation to the Brazilian government, which continues to operate it today under EMBRAPA, the federal agricultural research corporation. The trees are still there, older and taller now, scattered across perhaps 10 square kilometers of working grove. The town persists, with 1,000 to 2,000 inhabitants, mostly plantation workers and their families. A 2010 census counted 16,324 people across the full district including surrounding villages. You can walk through the remaining bungalows and the water tower still stands, a Detroit skyline in miniature rising above the Tapajos forest.

## The Forest Around

Belterra's municipality includes part of the Tapajos National Forest, a 549,067-hectare sustainable-use conservation unit created in 1974. The forest is a working preserve, home to traditional communities, rubber tappers still harvesting wild latex, and research stations studying Amazonian ecology. The contrast between Ford's imported plantation and the native forest that surrounds it tells a longer story about how Amazon latex was actually produced for most of its history: not by monoculture plantations but by tappers working wild trees scattered through the forest, each one cut on its own schedule and bled by hand. That older system collapsed the year the rubber boom ended in 1912. Ford tried to replace it with industrial monoculture. The forest won. The plateau of Belterra is now one of the stranger places in the Amazon, a retired American company town slowly dissolving back into the green.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 2.64S, 54.94W. Located on the escarpment above the Tapajos River, approximately 40 km south of Santarem. Visible from cruising altitude as a distinct rectangular grid of plantation rows cut into the forest, with the old town clearly geometric compared to surrounding natural vegetation. Nearest major airport is Maestro Wilson Fonseca (SBSN/STM) at Santarem, approximately 50 km north. Tropical monsoon climate with wet season January to June.
