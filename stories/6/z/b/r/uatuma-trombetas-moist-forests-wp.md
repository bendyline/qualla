# Uatuma-Trombetas Moist Forests

A 47-million-hectare ecoregion north of the Amazon River, still classed as relatively intact, where 235 tree species can share a single hectare and the Guiana Shield meets the Amazon basin.

**Location:** -0.0870, -55.8050

**Categories:** Ecoregions of Brazil, Amazon biome, Neotropical tropical and subtropical moist broadleaf forests

## Introduction

North of Manaus, in a single hectare of forest, you can find 235 species of trees. That number is not a headline. It is the working number ecologists use to describe what diversity means in the most extreme corner of the Uatuma-Trombetas moist forests ecoregion. The ecoregion stretches across 47 million hectares north of the Amazon River, from close to the Atlantic coast west to the Rio Negro and the Branco. Small slivers cross the border into Guyana and Suriname. The World Wildlife Fund still classes the region as relatively stable and intact, which means the forest is mostly still standing. The qualifier matters. Along the roads, around Manaus, and in the patches cleared for cattle and agriculture, the ecoregion bleeds at the edges like everywhere else in the Amazon.

## Where Two Geologies Meet

The ecoregion straddles a geological boundary that is older than the Amazon itself. The northern reaches climb into the quartzite and sandstone uplands of the Guiana Shield, one of the most ancient rock formations on Earth, with tabletop mountains and plateaus that were dry land when most of what is now the Amazon basin lay underwater. The south sits in the newer sedimentary basin of the Amazon River, laid down during the Tertiary period. High plains, rolling hills, and lowlands fold into each other as the shield gives way to the basin, and the soils shift accordingly. Mostly kaolinite. Sandy podzols on the slopes. Some pockets of fertile clay loam where the ancient and the modern combine. The ecoregion is laced by blackwater and clearwater rivers: the Trombetas, the Jari, the Uatuma, the Curuapanema, the Paru, the Araguari, each carrying a different water chemistry and nurturing a different community of fish and plants.

## A Refugium Theory

Scientists studying the Uatuma-Trombetas near Manaus have proposed that this is one of the places where organisms that separated during Pleistocene ice ages came back together during warmer periods. The hypothesis, long debated, suggests that when the Amazon's forests shrank into isolated refugia during glacial maxima, species evolved independently in each pocket. When the rains returned and the forests reconnected, those independently evolved lineages met again, mingled, and produced the extraordinary biodiversity you now see. Whether or not the refugium theory holds, the forests north and east of Manaus are among the most species-rich on Earth. Every hectare contains more kinds of tree than most entire European forests. Common canopy species include Protium hebetatum, Eschweilera coriacea, Manilkara bidentata, and Duckeodendron cestroides, a species so unusual it sits alone in its own genus.

## Who Lives Here

One hundred seventy-five mammal species have been recorded, more than eighty of them bats. Primates include the red-faced spider monkey, the pied tamarin, the black bearded saki, the Venezuelan red howler, and the red-handed tamarin. Jaguars hunt here. Cougars too. Forty-two frog species include the Surinam horned frog. Sixty-two snake species include the bushmaster, the common lancehead, and the boa constrictor. Four hundred eighty-two bird species include the sun parakeet, whose saffron-yellow plumage is so brilliant that it has been pushed toward endangered status by the pet trade. The upper canopy, 30 to 40 meters high with emergent trees reaching 50 meters, feels still from the ground but buzzes constantly with movement that can only be seen from aerial platforms. Most of what lives here is never seen by anyone walking the forest floor.

## What Holds, What Falls

The interior is largely intact. The edges are another story. Deforestation bites along the main roads and rivers, around Manaus, and in the region from Obidos to Monte Alegre along the Amazon. Cattle ranchers have cleared large areas. Commercial plantations have eaten into the forest around the Jari River, and forestry concessions in the eastern part of the ecoregion have changed canopy structure across thousands of hectares. Upland areas in the Guiana Shield have been destroyed by mining operations that also pollute the downstream rivers. The Balbina Dam in the ecoregion's southwest flooded enormous areas of upland forest when its reservoir filled in the 1980s, and the dam still produces far less electricity than its environmental cost suggested it should. Against all this, the Rio Trombetas Biological Reserve protects 3,850 square kilometers, and the Jari Ecological Station another 2,271.

## Seasonal Forest in the Dry Corners

East of Obidos, the climate shifts toward drier. Summer brings months of little rain, and the forest changes character accordingly. The canopy drops below 20 meters. The tree community becomes semideciduous, with leaves shed in the driest months. Mesophyllous and xeromorphic species predominate, plants adapted to handle water stress. The dry hills north of Obidos hold strange communities of species you would not expect in an Amazon ecoregion: cycads like Zamia lecointei, drought-tolerant legumes, and plants whose nearest relatives are found in Brazilian savannas to the south. These transitional forests and meadows remind observers that the Amazon is not a single thing. It is a mosaic of biomes pressed together, some saturated with rain, others adapted to survive the dry season, each holding a slice of the biodiversity that makes this region a planetary treasure.

## Flight Context

Centered at 0.09 degrees south, 55.80 degrees west, north of the Amazon River in central-northern Brazil. Visible from cruising altitude as a vast expanse of dark-green canopy punctuated by the winding Trombetas, Jari, and Uatuma rivers. Nearest airports: Manaus (SBEG) to the west, Santarem (SBSN) to the south, Macapa (SBMQ) to the east. Best viewing in the dry season from July through October.
