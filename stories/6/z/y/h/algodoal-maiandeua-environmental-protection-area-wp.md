# Algodoal-Maiandeua Environmental Protection Area

Two car-free Atlantic islands off the coast of Para, with mangroves, dunes, scarlet ibis flocks, and a fishing culture that arrived a century ago and never left.

**Location:** -0.6135, -47.5625

**Categories:** protected-area, coastal, brazil, amazon

## Introduction

No cars reach Algodoal. You ride 40 minutes by boat from the port of Maruda, and then you walk, or you hire a horse cart, or you do not go. Since 2007, regulations have kept the 3,100 hectares of this two-island protected area free of motor vehicles, and the silence that produces on a Para beach is its own kind of preservation. The islands sit on the Atlantic face of the Amazon coast, guarded by mangroves on the sheltered side and by dunes and surf on the ocean side. The name Algodoal comes from a native plant, the algodao de seda, whose seed tufts drift through the air like cotton. The first fishing families moved here in the 1920s. Their descendants still pull the nets.

## Two Islands and a Tide

Algodoal proper is the smaller of the pair, 605 hectares of beach, restinga scrub, mangrove, and the three named shores called Farol, Princesa, and the central beach at the main village. Maiandeua is the larger island, 2,495 hectares holding three smaller villages, Fortalezinha, Mocooca, and Camboinha, separated by mangroves and tidal channels. Between the two runs a tidal channel called the Furo Velho, the Old Passage, which vanishes at low water and returns with the flood. On the map the complex is wedged between the Maracana River estuary to the east and the Marapanim River estuary to the west. The Mocooca channel separates the whole from the mainland; a 5 minute crossing from the municipality of Maracana lands you at the village of that same name.

## The Mangrove Engine

Most of the life that matters here begins in the mangroves. The prop-rooted forests that fringe every tidal channel are nurseries for a cascade of species: pescada amarela and white mullet, dourada and xareu, Atlantic goliath grouper and shrimp. Crabs take over the mudflats. Mussels, oysters, and the wood-boring turu colonize submerged roots. Birds work the whole system. Scarlet ibis turn the canopy red at sundown when they flock home to roost. Roseate spoonbills stalk the shallows. Rufous crab hawks pick through the mud. Migrant shorebirds show up in season: Hudsonian whimbrels, sanderlings, ruddy turnstones, collared plovers. Anything that cannot eat mangrove eats something that lives in mangrove. Remove the swamp and the rest comes apart. Algodoal-Maiandeua is, in part, a practical demonstration of why coastal Para still has fisheries at all.

## Two Climates, One Year

The year here splits cleanly in two. January through March is the wet season, when the sky opens and the rain is continuous and warm. September through December is the dry season, when the dunes bake and the rivers retreat from their banks. The annual average temperature is 27 degrees Celsius, and it rarely drifts far. Beyond the beach, the dunes support a fruit garden of opportunistic plants: ajuru, cashew, murici, carambola, coconut, mango. Terra firme patches on Maiandeua hold sloths, coatis, anteaters, margays, white-eared opossums, small monkeys, raccoons, red-footed tortoises, and caimans that locals still call alligators. Foxes move at dawn. Iguanas, which locals call chameleons, watch from restinga bushes. It is, at 3,100 hectares, a compressed survey of everything Para keeps on its coast.

## The People Who Made It Stay

The protection came late. The state law creating the APA dates to 27 November 1990, covering 2,378 hectares then, expanded since. The management council did not form until 2006. The motor vehicle ban came in June 2007, not as a tourism marketing choice but as a statement about what this place was and what it would not become. The management plan was approved in 2011 and published at the end of 2012. Through every step, the villages have continued their pre-existing life: artisanal fishing from wooden boats, cassava flour ground in community mills, the carimbo praiano danced on the beaches at night. Handicrafts made from local plants sell alongside the guided tours and fishing trips run by residents. Tourism arrived long ago and has been absorbed without erasing the fishing economy, which is harder than it looks and not the universal outcome for Brazilian coastal communities pressed against Rio and Recife. Algodoal has held its ground partly because it has stayed slow and partly because it has stayed walking.

## Pedra Chorona

On Maiandeua, the sand trails eventually reach a locality called Pedra Chorona, the Weeping Stone, one of the island's small named places alongside Camaleão and Passagem. Little is written about it beyond its name. A pilgrim walking up the beach from Algodoal village in the afternoon heat finds a stretch of coast where the only engine noise is wind and the only vehicles are the horses that brought the cart drivers down from the far villages. This is what a protected area on a tropical coast can still feel like, nearly a century after the first families crossed the Mocooca and stayed.

## Flight Context

Located at 0.61 S, 47.56 W on the Atlantic coast of northeastern Para, Brazil. Best seen from FL100-FL250 in daylight, when the two islands show clearly against the mouths of the Maracana and Marapanim rivers. Nearest airport is Belem Val-de-Cans (SBBE) about 135 km southwest; Macapa (SBMQ) lies far to the northwest across the Amazon mouth. Expect afternoon convective cells in wet season (January-March) and clear mornings in dry season (September-December). Note tidal sandbars extending offshore near low water.
