# Pará mangroves

A continuous belt of red mangrove forest along 650 kilometers of Atlantic coast, where saltwater reaches 45 kilometers inland and scarlet ibis roost at sunset.

**Location:** -0.8070, -46.8530

**Categories:** Neotropical ecoregions, Ecoregions of Brazil, Amazon biome, Environment of Pará

## Introduction

The mangroves of Pará stretch along 650 kilometers of Atlantic coast and extend 45 kilometers inland, because this part of Brazil is so flat that the tide does not stop at the beach. It keeps going. The tidal reach pushes saltwater up estuaries and riverbeds, carrying with it the salt-tolerant trees that build entire forests out of the mud the Amazon spits into the sea. Together with the Maranhão mangroves to the east, they hold most of Brazil's mangrove cover, one of the planet's largest remaining intact mangrove systems.

## Mud the Amazon Made

The Amazon carries more sediment to the sea than almost any other river on Earth, and most of it falls out of suspension where the freshwater meets the Atlantic. Islands and mudflats build up from that fine clay, some ephemeral, some permanent, all of them colonized by mangroves before they have even fully settled. Tides here range from 5 to 7 meters. The terrain is so level that saltwater reaches 45 kilometers upstream in places, allowing mangroves to grow far from what you would normally call coast. The climate is tropical and humid, with mean annual rainfall of 2,300 millimeters and a dry season from July to December when monthly rain drops below 50 millimeters.

## Six Species, One Forest

Six species of mangrove tree dominate the Pará coast. Rhizophora mangle, the red mangrove, is the most common, reaching 25 meters in height and lining the estuaries most exposed to open ocean. Rhizophora harrisonii fills the intermediate zones, and Rhizophora racemosa grows, within Pará, only in Marajó Bay. Avicennia germinans thrives on higher ground with less flooding and more salt. Avicennia schaueriana, less common but taller, can reach 45 meters where conditions allow. Laguncularia racemosa lines forest edges in brackish water. Each species occupies its own slice of the salinity gradient, stacked in zones you can see from a boat as the water changes color with every bend.

## A Nursery for the Atlantic

Mangroves are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth, and they earn that title by what they do for other species. Their flooded root systems provide shelter for juvenile fish, crabs, and shrimp that will later migrate out to reef and open-ocean habitats. Coastal fisheries along northern Brazil depend on species that spend their early lives among mangrove roots. Artisanal fishermen along the Pará coast harvest crabs, shrimp, and wood directly from the mangroves. Local communities have built their economies around this resource for centuries, trading mangrove crabs in the markets of Belém and Bragança.

## The Wading Birds of Dusk

Sunset is the best time to see a Pará mangrove, because the wading birds come home to roost. Scarlet ibis, the electric-red wading bird that is a national bird of Trinidad and Tobago and one of the defining creatures of this coast, gather in flocks of thousands against the dark green of the mangroves. Wattled jacanas walk across floating vegetation on toes so long they distribute weight like snowshoes. Tucuxis, the small grey dolphins of the Amazon system, chase fish in the muddy channels. West Indian manatees drift through the brackish inland stretches. Green sea turtles and leatherbacks use these coastal waters. It is a coast that becomes more crowded at low tide than at high.

## Still Standing, Still Vulnerable

The World Wildlife Fund rates the Pará mangroves as Relatively Stable and Intact, rare praise for a Brazilian ecoregion. The mangroves survive in part because they are hard to reach and harder to develop. Population density is low, and most of the coast outside Belém's immediate surroundings remains remote. But threats are growing. Subsistence agriculture, livestock, tourism, logging, and mining all push into mangrove territory. Urban expansion around Belém has already erased significant coastal forest. Sea-level rise and changes in Amazon flow will test the mangroves' resilience in ways the last few thousand years did not. What has endured because it was inaccessible is now accessible, and what comes next depends on choices being made right now along a coast few outsiders have ever visited.

## Flight Context

The Pará mangroves stretch along the Atlantic coast near 0.81°S, 46.85°W, from Marajó Bay east to the Maranhão state border. Best viewed on approaches to Bragança (no major airport) or on flights between Belém (SBBE) and São Luís (SBSL). From cruising altitude the mangroves form a dark green fringe separating the brown Amazon estuary water from the blue Atlantic. At low tide, exposed mud flats reveal the scale of the ecosystem. Clear views in dry season, July to December.
