# Estação das Docas

Three iron warehouses built to load rubber, reopened in 2000 as Belém's waterfront cultural complex - with the dissolved ruins of a 1665 fort serving as its amphitheatre.

**Location:** -1.4486, -48.5001

**Categories:** Geography of Pará, Belém, Pará, 2000 establishments in Brazil, Tourist attractions in Pará

## Introduction

In 1841 the question of what to do with a ruined fort travelled a very long way. The São Pedro Nolasco Fort had guarded the Belém waterfront since 1665, and the Cabanagem had left it badly damaged. Now the province wanted a new pier exactly where it stood. Rebuild, or remove? Bernardo de Sousa Franco, president of the province, put the question to the Minister of War, José Clemente Pereira, who put it to Emperor Pedro II - a teenager barely into his reign, ruling from Rio de Janeiro, more than two thousand kilometres away. The emperor ordered the remains dissolved. The waterfront got its pier. What survives of the fort is now an amphitheatre at the edge of a restaurant complex.

## Guarding Feliz Lusitânia

Rui Vaz de Siqueira, captain-general of the captaincy of Grão-Pará, built the São Pedro Nolasco Fort in 1665. It was one of three works meant to protect the colonial settlement then called Feliz Lusitânia - Happy Lusitânia - alongside the Presépio Fort and the São José Redoubt. The name Belém came later. The core those guns covered is the historic centre the city still keeps.

Then came the Cabanagem. Between 1835 and 1840 the poor of the Amazon rose against the imperial government in what is usually described as the only revolt in Brazilian history in which the popular classes took control of a provincial capital. It was also one of the deadliest, with estimates of the dead running into the tens of thousands. The fort took heavy damage in the fighting, and within a year of the rising's end the province was deciding whether it was worth keeping at all.

## English Iron, American Cranes

The docks that replaced the fort took another few decades to become serious infrastructure. From 1909 the port built its first 120-metre run of quay and a 2,000-square-metre warehouse, assembled in English iron with steam engines driving the machinery - a construction language already half a century old by the time it reached the Amazon, and none the worse for it. The external cranes came from the United States.

What they replaced were the Ver-o-Peso and Reduto docks, which the record describes without much affection as precarious. What they were built to move was rubber, and everything a rubber economy buys in exchange. The scale of the ironwork tells you how much traffic Belém expected. So does the fact that a century later the sheds were still standing, long after the trade that paid for them had gone.

## Three Sheds, Reopened

Belém inaugurated the Estação das Docas on May 13, 2000, after restoring three of the old warehouses across a 32,000-square-metre site with roughly 500 metres of frontage on Guajará Bay. The division is straightforward. Warehouse 1 holds the Boulevard das Artes, a cinema and the Maria Sylvia Nunes Theater. Warehouse 2 is given over to food. Warehouse 3 takes fairs, exhibitions and the Museu do Porto de Belém. The amphitheatre occupies the fort's footprint.

The port museum's collection was assembled by a team led by the historian Dantas de Feitosa and tells the story of navigation in Pará through photographs and objects, a good number of them dug up during the restoration itself. There is a tidy circularity in that: the excavation that prepared the site also stocked the museum. A social organisation, Pará 2000, runs the complex today.

## The Name on the Theatre

The theatre that opened here in 2002 carries the name of Maria Sylvia Ferreira da Silva Nunes, a professor and theatre director from Pará who in 1962 founded what is now the School of Theatre and Dance at the Federal University of Pará. She did it with her husband, the writer Benedito Nunes. She died in Belém in 2020, at ninety.

Naming a converted cargo shed after a drama teacher is a specific statement about what the waterfront is for now. For most of its working life this quay handled other people's exports; things were counted here, weighed, and shipped out of the Amazon. The iron columns and the crane rails are still visible above the tables. The building has not been disguised - it has simply been given a different job.

## Flight Context

Located at 1.449 degrees S, 48.500 degrees W on the Guajará Bay shoreline in the Campina neighbourhood of Belém. The three long parallel warehouse roofs and the row of external cranes make this one of the easiest landmarks to identify on the Belém waterfront, immediately north of the Ver-o-Peso market complex. Best viewed from 1,500-3,000 feet AGL on a north-south run along the shore. Nearest field is Belém/Val de Cans-Júlio Cezar Ribeiro International (SBBE), about 7 km north. The bay is wide and brown with sediment; late afternoon light picks out the quay edge and the moored boats cleanly.
