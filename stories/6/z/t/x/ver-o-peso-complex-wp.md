# Ver-o-Peso Complex

Named for a colonial weighing house, Belém's great riverside market is 35,000 square meters of working commerce - fish, açaí, herbs and medicine - run by vendors who once faced down the engineers who wanted to bury it.

**Location:** -1.4536, -48.5036

**Categories:** Markets, Food and drink, Historic districts, Amazon, Brazil

## Introduction

The market's day starts on the tide, in the dark. Boats come down from the islands and up from the channels with the night's fish and with rasas of açaí picked the previous afternoon, because the fruit ferments fast and there is no waiting. By the time Belém's offices open, that cargo has been unloaded, weighed, argued over, sold and dispersed into the city. Ver-o-Peso is a heritage monument and a tourist photograph, but first it is several thousand people's job, and the job has an early shift.

## See the Weight

The name is a colonial instrument that outlived its purpose. In 1625 the Portuguese built the Casa de Haver-o-Peso at the mouth of the Igarapé do Piri, a customs house whose function was to weigh whatever came into Belém and take the Crown's cut. Everything the Amazon produced passed over those scales, along with cattle driven down from the herds of Marajó Island and manufactured goods arriving from Europe. In 1803, under the Count of Arcos, the creek was filled in to let the city expand, and its mouth was rebuilt as the Ver-o-Peso dock with a stone slab, the Pedra do Peixe, where the weighing was done. The tax office is long gone. Four centuries on, the phrase that meant 'check the weight' is still what everyone in Pará calls the place.

## The Iron Market

In 1897 the firm La Rocque Pinto & Cia won the public auction to build Belém a proper municipal fish market. The old weighing house came down in 1899 and construction began the same year to a design by Henrique La Rocque. What opened in 1901 is the building everyone photographs: a dodecagon of 1,197 square meters, its Vieille-Montagne zinc structure prefabricated in England and New York, shipped across the Atlantic and brought the last stretch by river. It was rubber-boom taste - French art nouveau lines, ordered by catalogue, assembled on the equator - and it was also, practically, the city's first municipal fish market. It has since been voted one of the Seven Wonders of Brazil, and the complex around it, 35,000 square meters in all, was listed by IPHAN in 1977.

## The Erveiras

Along one edge of the market stand the tables of the herb sellers, mostly women, ranged behind bottles of oils and resins and stacked bundles of bark, root, seed and leaf brought in by boat from the interior. Some of what they sell is culinary, some is perfume, and much of it is medicine - remedies for ailments that people here bring to the erveiras before, or instead of, a clinic. This is not folklore staged for visitors. It is a body of applied botanical knowledge, built from generations of practice with Amazonian plants, serious enough that researchers have studied and documented how the erveiros and erveiras of Ver-o-Peso learn, organize and transmit what they know. A vendor who can identify several hundred species by smell and name their uses is a specialist, and should be treated as one.

## Fish, Meat, Açaí

The complex is really several markets sharing a waterfront. The Ver-o-Peso dock operates as a free public fish market, where the catch is sold straight off the boats. Behind the Forte do Presépio, the Açaí Fair supplies the whole city with fruit grown on the surrounding islands, moving through in the pre-dawn hours in woven baskets. The Francisco Bolonha Municipal Meat Market, Belém's first, handles the other half of the protein trade. The result is the dish that Pará eats and the rest of Brazil finds startling: fried fish with açaí, the pulp served thick, unsweetened and savory, the way it was eaten here long before anyone abroad had heard the word.

## The Rest of the Ground

Ver-o-Peso is bounded by pieces of the city's own self-image. Boulevard Castilhos França, one of Belém's first broad avenues, runs along it. Siqueira Campos Square, built in 1930, carries an iron tower whose clock was brought from England, donated by the intendant Antonio Faciola. The neoclassical Solar da Beira became a restaurant and events venue in 1985. Dom Pedro II Square, the first square the city ever laid out, sits just inland. And the dock is still there at all only because of the vendors: when port engineers proposed filling it in as unsanitary at the turn of the twentieth century, the traders protested until the plan was abandoned. The commerce was older than the port, and it won.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 1.4536 S, 48.5036 W, on the Guajará Bay shoreline at the western edge of Belém's Cidade Velha. The twelve-sided iron market with its turreted roofline is unmistakable from 1,000-2,000 ft AGL; approaching from the bay on an easterly heading lines up the Forte do Presépio, the market and the Lauro Sodré Palace in sequence, with the working docks of the Port of Belém extending northeast. Belém/Val de Cans International (SBBE) is about 8 km north-northeast; Belém/Brigadeiro Protásio de Oliveira, Júlio César (SBJC), is nearer the center. Early morning is both the visually clearest window and the hour the market is busiest; afternoon convection over the bay is routine and can build quickly.
