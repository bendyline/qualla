# Utinga State Park

A 1,393-hectare patch of Amazon rainforest at the edge of Belem that supplies drinking water to 60 percent of the metropolitan region's two million people.

**Location:** -1.4242, -48.4457

**Categories:** State parks of Brazil, Protected areas established in 1993, 1993 establishments in Brazil, Protected areas of Pará

## Introduction

Belem's drinking water comes from two lakes inside a rainforest. The Agua Preta and Bolonha lakes, inside the 1,393 hectares of Utinga State Park, supply roughly 60 percent of the metropolitan region's population of more than two million people. The park sits a few kilometers from downtown Belem, close enough that the traffic hum reaches into the canopy on quiet mornings, yet preserved enough that capybaras drink at its margins and tarantulas still live under the leaf litter. In 1993, in the same week of early May, the state government of Para created both the Metropolitan Belem Environmental Protection Area and, inside it, the Utinga State Park. Decree 1551 and decree 1552 were signed on May 3. The city had finally admitted that its taps depended on its trees.

## Water and Forest Together

The logic of an urban watershed protection zone is usually hidden until it fails. When the forest is cut, the lakes silt up. When the lakes silt up, water treatment costs climb. When treatment costs climb, the city either passes the cost to residents or lets water quality slide. Utinga was not designed as a nature reserve first. It was designed as a water-supply insurance policy with rainforest as the mechanism. The protection area covers the full catchment basin of the Agua Preta and Bolonha lakes and the Aura River. Sustainable use is permitted across the broader APA, but the park itself is a fully protected conservation unit. No hunting. No fishing in the lakes. No timber extraction. The park is a safe deposit box for water, with biodiversity as the principal and interest.

## The Monkey Trek

Visitors come on foot. There are eight established trails through the park, and the most popular is the monkey trek, a forty-minute walk from Bolonha lake to the visitor center. The name does not promise, but often delivers. Robust capuchin monkeys and common squirrel monkeys forage the canopy. Pale-throated sloths hang somewhere in the branches, visible only if a guide points. Coatis and pacas cross the trails. Birds are easier to spot than mammals, and the park supports 193 species including toucans, hummingbirds, the Amazon kingfisher, the great kiskadee, the anhinga, and the tropical screech owl. Guides lead groups of seven to thirty-five visitors. On weekends, Belem families picnic near the visitor center and learn about the water treatment plant that turns Bolonha's green-tinted lake water into what comes out of their taps.

## A Short Catalog of Urban Fauna

A quick ecological assessment of the park once identified 25 fish species, 7 amphibians, 26 reptiles, 193 birds, and 26 mammals. The list includes creatures that most city dwellers would prefer not to think about living this close to home. Brazilian wandering spiders. Tarantulas. Recluse spiders. Coral snakes and their harmless lookalikes the false corals. Bothrops jararaca, a pit viper responsible for most snakebite cases in Brazil. Boa constrictors and, in the wetter corners, anacondas. Spectacled caimans patrol the lake margins. Much of this fauna predates Belem as a city, persisting in fragments of rainforest that Utinga protects by keeping city expansion outside its boundaries. The trees that hold the spiders and the snakes also hold the water.

## The Samauma and the Medicinal Seeds

Among the trees of Utinga, one stands taller than the others. Ceiba pentandra, the samauma, is the giant of the Amazon canopy, with trunks that flare into enormous buttresses at the base and crowns that emerge above the surrounding forest. Carapa trees, known locally as andiroba, produce seeds from which a traditional medicinal oil is pressed, used in Amazonian homes for centuries to treat bruises, inflammation, and insect bites. Brazil nuts grow here too. The park's flora is not exotic curiosity. It is the working pharmacy and building material yard of the forest peoples who used this land before the Portuguese founded Belem in 1616, and whose traditional medicinal knowledge the park now helps preserve by keeping the trees alive.

## What Protection Means in Practice

The park's management plan was updated in 2013, prepared by Imazon, the Institute of Man and the Environment of the Amazon. The update replaced the 1994 plan that had not kept pace with modern conservation science. Even with current planning, the pressures persist. As of 2013 the process of indemnifying former landowners was incomplete, meaning parts of the legal ownership of park lands had not been fully resolved. Issues include illegal entry, poaching, fishing in the protected lakes, construction of unauthorized housing on park edges, disposal of household waste and sewage into the lake catchment, and the trimming of trees beneath ELETRONORTE and COSANPA power corridors that cross park boundaries. The monkey trek passes through some of these pressures. What makes Utinga remarkable is not that it is pristine. It is that in a metropolitan area of more than two million people, enough of the forest has been held in place to still deserve the protection it has.

## Flight Context

Located at 1.42 degrees south, 48.45 degrees west, inside the metropolitan area of Belem, Para. Visible from altitude as a distinct green patch of rainforest against urban gray, with the Agua Preta and Bolonha lakes as dark water bodies in the park's heart. Nearest airport: Val de Cans International (SBBE), about 10 kilometers northwest. Best viewed in the dry season from August through November when the forest canopy is dense and cloud cover over Belem is reduced.
