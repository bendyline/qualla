# Church of Our Lady of Mount Carmel (Belém)

Belém's only stone-faced church, carved in Portugal between 1750 and 1756 and shipped in pieces across the Atlantic to be reassembled at the edge of the rainforest.

**Location:** -1.4588, -48.5058

**Categories:** church, colonial-architecture, baroque, brazil, belem, portuguese-empire

## Introduction

Between 1750 and 1756, two master stonemasons named Jose Pereira and Manuel Gomes cut the facade of this Belem church out of Portuguese lioz limestone in a workshop somewhere in Portugal. They carved every element, numbered the pieces, and watched the finished facade loaded onto a ship. It arrived in Belem in stages, a flat-pack Baroque front for a colonial church, and was reassembled on a building that had already been under construction for sixty years. It remains the only stone facade in the entire city. Everything else in Belem's old quarter is plastered masonry or painted wood; this one church wears cut stone in a place where cut stone had to cross the Atlantic.

## Carmelites on the Amazon

The Discalced Carmelites arrived in Belem in 1626 from Maranhao with orders to catechize indigenous peoples and the children of Portuguese soldiers. On 31 May 1627 they settled on land donated by Captain Major Bento Maciel Parente, a wattle-and-daub country house on what was then called North Street, now Siqueira Mendes, in a damp lowland the Tupinamba had named Alagadico da Jucara. They built a small chapel on the donated ground and used the existing house as a convent, one of the first religious foundations in the captaincy of Grao-Para. Friar Andre da Natividade became the first vicar. For sixty years the modest chapel served; by 1690 the original buildings had fallen apart, and the Carmelites demolished what remained to begin something far more ambitious.

## A Construction Interrupted

Governor Antonio de Albuquerque Coelho de Carvalho laid the foundations of the new church in 1690. Work progressed for six years. Then on 2 July 1696, the bishop issued a formal ban on construction because the Carmelites had buried an excommunicated person in the unfinished building. Construction stopped. The order appealed to the Portuguese Crown Court, which was about as high as a colonial Brazilian Carmelite could appeal, and the court deliberated for four years before handing down a favorable decision on 21 April 1700. Work resumed. The church was finally completed in 1721 under Friar Victoriano Pimentel. The 95-year gap between the Carmelites' arrival and the finished church is not unusual for colonial religious architecture in the Portuguese tropics, where materials, money, and consecrated ground could all be easier to request than to deliver.

## The Pombaline Facade

The facade Jose Pereira and Manuel Gomes carved in Portugal reflects what architectural historians call the Pombaline style, named after the 1st Marquis of Pombal who dominated Portuguese politics during the mid-18th century. The design is sober, controlled, marked by Tuscan pilasters and restrained ornament. The central structure divides into two floors and three panels, topped by a mixed pediment with pinnacles shaped like vases with small chimneys at the top. An iron cross rises above the pediment. Its tympanum is pierced by an oculus. The central block is framed by slightly bulbous side towers with domes pierced by additional oculi. Some authors have detected traces of Francesco Borromini in the window pediments, though the overall effect is calmer than any Borromini building. The design introduced several Lisbon-derived elements, the undulating pediment chief among them, to Brazil through this specific facade.

## Antonio Landi and the Italians

Giuseppe Antonio Landi, the Italian architect who redesigned nearly every building in colonial Belem after his arrival in 1753, was among the architects who worked on Our Lady of Mount Carmel. The scagliola decorating the walls and altarpieces, in which colored plasters are worked to imitate marble, was applied in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, a technique Landi had also used in Belem. The ceiling of the central nave was painted sky blue with gold stars in the same period. The high altar's altarpiece is classified as transitional between the Portuguese national and Joanine styles: gilded woodwork carved by local artisans with acanthus leaves and allegorical birds, with a wooden altar table covered in Portuguese silver standing in front of it. IPHAN listed the church as a national heritage site in 1941.

## Who Sleeps in the Atrium

The atrium floor holds the remains of several figures central to Para's history. Captain General Pedro Albuquerque. Governor Francisco Coelho de Carvalho. Ombudsman General Mateus Dias da Costa. Felix Clemente Malcher, a leader of the Cabanagem, the violent 1835 to 1840 popular revolt against Brazilian imperial authority in Para that killed an estimated 30 to 40 percent of the region's population. To walk across the atrium is to pass above the bones of colonial and imperial Para. The church received a major restoration between roughly 2013 and 2015, funded by the Archdiocese of Belem in partnership with the mining company Vale and with IPHAN support through the Rouanet Law. Workers cleaned the lioz facade, restored the parietal paintings, and installed modern fire protection systems, reopening the church in March 2015. The facade that crossed the Atlantic in the 1750s emerged from its scaffolding looking the way it looked when Pereira and Gomes first finished carving it.
