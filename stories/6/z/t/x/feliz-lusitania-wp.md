# Feliz Lusitânia

The name the Portuguese gave to their 1616 settlement at the mouth of the Amazon has become an umbrella term for the fort, square, palace, and churches that still mark the birthplace of Belém.

**Location:** -1.4552, -48.5045

**Categories:** Belém, Pará, Portuguese Empire, colonial architecture, Iberian Union

## Introduction

There is a kind of place name that expresses less a location than a wish. Feliz Lusitânia, Happy Lusitania, was the phrase Captain Francisco Caldeira Castelo Branco used when he and 150 men landed on the east bank of Guajará Bay on January 12, 1616, and began nailing together a wooden fort. Lusitânia was the old Roman word for Portugal. The captain was calling what he had found a happy piece of Portuguese ground, and he was calling it that while standing on Tupinambá and Pacajá land in the middle of an Amazon that technically, under the Treaty of Tordesillas, was not Portuguese at all. The name stuck. Four hundred years later it labels a cluster of buildings in the Cidade Velha district of Belém, the starting point of everything that became the city.

## The Iberian Trick

The reason a Portuguese captain was building a fort on nominally Spanish territory has to do with a temporary crisis of European royalty. From 1580 to 1640, Portugal and Spain were joined under the Iberian Union, a personal union of the two crowns sometimes called the Philippine Dynasty. In practice this meant Portuguese subjects could operate in Spanish territories without quite needing a separate sovereign's permission. The king who dispatched Castelo Branco was Philip III of Spain, ruling simultaneously as Philip II of Portugal, and what he wanted was the Amazon. Dutch, English, and French ships had been pushing into the estuary, setting up trading posts, and building alliances with indigenous groups. To protect the so-called drogas do sertão, the sertão drugs, the spices and natural products that could be extracted from the rainforest, somebody had to plant a fort. Castelo Branco was that somebody.

## Three Ships and 150 Men

The expedition arrived in three vessels, the patache Santa Maria da Candelária, the caravelão Santa Maria das Graças, and a large speedboat called Assunção. The engineer-major Francisco Frias de Mesquita directed the construction of the Forte do Presépio, later the Forte do Castelo, at the mouth of a creek called the Piry. The fort was initially a wooden stockade; it became masonry later. What happened next was predictable and terrible. The foundation of Feliz Lusitânia opened decades of warfare against the Dutch, English, and French who contested the region, and simultaneously opened decades of colonization and enslavement against the Tupinambá and Pacajá whose land it was. The Portuguese economic model depended on indigenous labor, and the early municipality ran on a system of forced labor that reshaped and decimated the peoples who had lived there.

## What the Name Now Means

Today Feliz Lusitânia is not a historical memory so much as a visitor designation. Formally the Conjunto Arquitetônico e Paisagístico Feliz Lusitânia or Complexo Turístico Feliz Lusitânia, it groups together the heritage buildings of the colonial core: the Forte do Presépio, the Dom Frei Caetano Brandão Square, the Palace of the Eleven Windows, the Santo Alexandre Church, and the Igreja da Sé, the Belém Metropolitan Cathedral. IPHAN, Brazil's national heritage institute, lists all of them. The project was developed specifically to valorize and restore the historic area, which had been slowly decaying under the pressure of urban sprawl and what Portuguese calls verticalização, the vertical densification of cities that replaces low-rise historic buildings with high-rise apartment blocks. Belém was suffering that pressure acutely. The Feliz Lusitânia project was a deliberate line in the sand.

## Inside the Complex

Forte do Presépio still holds its original cannons, pointing out over the water at threats that have not materialized for centuries. Inside the fort, glass cases display Marajoara and Tapajó ceramics, painted vessels and funerary urns older than any European contact with the region. A short walk away, the Palace of the Eleven Windows looks out over the bay from its eighteenth-century colonial façade; it was once a military hospital and now holds a cultural center. The Saint Alexander Church and College, completed in 1719 in a vigorous Amazonian baroque and built by Jesuits whose convent here is considered the most important Jesuit complex in Brazil, now houses the Museum of Sacred Art. The metropolitan cathedral, the Igreja da Sé, stands where the first parish church of Belém once stood. The Dom Frei Caetano Brandão Square, completed in 1900, anchors the complex at its center with its bronze bishop and palm-lined walks.

## The Oldest Streets

Two candidates vie for the title of first street in Belém. One is Rua Siqueira Mendes, formerly called Rua do Norte. The other is Ladeira do Castelo, Castle Hill, the ramp that runs between the Forte do Presépio and the Feira do Açaí. The question matters in regional historiography because the layout of Portuguese colonial cities followed strict north-south and east-west grids; whichever street was laid out first established the orientation for everything after. Rua Siqueira Mendes connects the Largo da Sé, now the Dom Frei Caetano Brandão Square, with the Largo do Carmo, following the old Portuguese pattern. Ladeira do Castelo, pressed directly against the fort, runs more naturally with the topography. The truth is probably that both were laid out nearly at the same time, in the first decade or two after 1616, by men making it up as they went. You can still walk both of them. The stones are not the originals, but the routes are.

## Flight Context

Feliz Lusitânia occupies the historic core of Belém's Cidade Velha district at 1.4552°S, 48.5045°W, right at the water's edge of Guajará Bay. From altitude the complex reads as a tight cluster of red-tiled colonial buildings around a star-shaped masonry fort. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,000 feet. Nearest airport is Belém/Val de Cans International (SBBE), 9 km north. Morning light is ideal; afternoon thunderstorms are frequent during the December-May rainy season.
