# Belém

The Amazon's Atlantic gateway, where 17th-century Portuguese forts meet rubber-boom palaces, and the afternoon rain comes on a schedule you can set your watch by.

**Location:** -1.4558, -48.5039

**Categories:** city, travel, amazon, colonial, brazil, gastronomy

## Introduction

Every afternoon around three o'clock, the sky over Belém breaks open. The rain arrives on a schedule so reliable that locals plan their errands around it, ducking into a market stall or an air-conditioned cafe for twenty minutes until the sun returns and the steam starts rising off the mango-lined streets. This is life 1.4 degrees south of the equator, at the mouth of the greatest river on Earth, in a city that has been receiving afternoon thunderstorms since the Portuguese dragged their first fort out of the forest in 1616.

## The Gateway

Belem sits 100 kilometers up the Para River from the Atlantic, on the Bay of Guajara, where a braid of islands and river mouths forms the eastern edge of the Amazon estuary. The city of 1.3 million people is, by any practical reckoning, the gate of the Amazon. Its port handles boats bound for Macapa and Santarem and Manaus, with hammocks slung between the rails of the cheaper decks and air-conditioned cabins on the larger craft. Travelers who time their journey right can watch pink river dolphins surface at dawn and eat jambu-laced soup for breakfast. The airport connects to most major Brazilian cities, and buses grind down from the south on twenty-seven-hour runs from Fortaleza. However you arrive, the river shapes everything.

## Colonial Bones, Rubber-Boom Skin

The heart of the old city is Cidade Velha, where the Forte do Castelo still guards the waterfront it was built to defend in 1616, and Campina, the neighborhood that absorbed the wealth of the late-19th-century rubber boom. Among the high-rises of modern Belem, those colonial and Belle-Epoque buildings still stand in surprising density: the Casa das Onze Janelas with its eleven windows facing the water, the tiled facades borrowed from Lisbon, the Praca da Republica with its statues and the neoclassical Teatro da Paz of 1878. Many of the grander mansions now house government bodies, and an active preservation effort has been pushing the survival rate upward. First impressions can deceive a visitor into seeing only glass towers, but a walk through the old quarters reveals a layered, textured city beneath.

## Ver-o-Peso

The market Ver-o-Peso, meaning roughly see the weight, is the first and most consequential stop. It sits on the waterfront in the iron pavilion prefabricated in Europe and shipped across the Atlantic in pieces around 1900, and it has been the economic center of Para state for centuries. Inside you find fish that have never existed in any other hemisphere, fruits with names that do not translate, herbs sold by women who can still tell you what each plant treats and which spirit it pleases. Nearby, the restored warehouses of Estacao das Docas have been converted into restaurants and bars facing Guajara Bay. Walk out along the river at dusk and the old iron of the rubber-boom-era buildings glows orange in the light, as boats of every description still work the water below.

## Flavors Found Nowhere Else

Belem has been part of UNESCO's Creative Cities Network for gastronomy since 2015, and its cuisine is a reason to come. Tacaca is a shrimp soup in tucupi broth made from bitter cassava, served brutally hot in a gourd called a cuia, with jambu leaves that leave your lips pleasantly numb. Pato no tucupi uses the same broth to poach duck. Manicoba simmers manioc leaves with salted pork and smoked meats for days until the toxins cook out. Caldeirada paraense combines fish and shrimp with egg, potato, and the unfailing jambu. For dessert, the regional ice creams rotate through Amazonian fruits no supermarket carries: acai, cupuacu, taperaba, bacuri, graviola. Sorveteria Cairu, with branches including one at Docas, is the place locals send outsiders.

## Cirio de Nazare

On the second Sunday of October, Belem hosts one of the largest Catholic processions in the world. The Cirio de Nazare draws more than two million faithful who walk through the streets for roughly five hours behind a small statue of the Virgin that, according to local tradition, was discovered centuries ago on the edge of a creek by a farmer named Placido Jose de Souza. When he carried the image home, the story goes, it returned on its own to the place of its finding. Today the procession incorporates a river pilgrimage, a fireworks display sponsored by the Stevedores' Union, and a candlelit march that ends at the Se Cathedral. The full festivities extend two weeks, and the city treats the event as what it is: the Christmas of the Amazon.

## Beyond the City

When you need relief from Belem's heat, the river offers it. Ilha do Mosqueiro lies 80 kilometers north, its freshwater beaches actually receiving waves thanks to the sheer scale of the estuary. Icoaraci is closer, with a bayside walk and car ferries to Ilha do Marajo, an island roughly the size of Switzerland, where water buffalo outnumber people and the sunsets stretch flat across horizons that feel oceanic. Three hours by road brings you to Salinopolis and proper Atlantic beaches, though expect weekend crowds that turn the sand into something like a parking lot. For slower travel, consider the two- or three-day boat journey upriver to Santarem. Like most things in Belem, the trip runs on its own schedule, and the river sets the pace.
