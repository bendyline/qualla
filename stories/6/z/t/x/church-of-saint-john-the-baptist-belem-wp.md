# Church of Saint John the Baptist (Belém)

A small octagonal baroque chapel in Belém where an Italian architect painted walls that look like marble but are not, and the paintings hid for decades beneath later interventions.

**Location:** -1.4574, -48.5027

**Categories:** church, baroque, landi, colonial-architecture, brazil, belem

## Introduction

The pink and green walls of this small church are lying. They are not marble and never were, but Giuseppe Antonio Landi, an Italian architect trained in Bologna at the Accademia di Belle Arti, painted them so skillfully in the 18th century that nobody was quite sure for a long time. Later restorers covered his trompe-l'oeil with plaster, and for decades the original illusion lay hidden beneath renovations. It was rediscovered in a 1987 prospection and fully uncovered during a 1996 restoration. When the church reopened, Landi's painted marble had returned to a chapel that is itself an unusual shape: two overlapping squares, with the larger one forming an irregular octagonal nave capped by a dome, a form common in Europe but rare in colonial Brazil.

## An Italian in Belem

Giuseppe Antonio Landi arrived in Belem in 1753 as part of a technical commission sent from Portugal. He was nearly forty, born in Bologna in 1713 and trained at the Accademia di Belle Arti there, already an established architect and engraver with published books of ornamental engravings. He came as a draftsman with the commission demarcating Portugal's northern borders, and he settled in Belem. Landi remained for nearly four decades until his death in 1791, and during that time he redesigned or built nearly every significant public building in Belem. The Church of Saint John the Baptist, known affectionately as Sao Joaozinho or Little Saint John, is one of his designs. It stands in Libano Square, also called Largo de Sao Joao, and it is small enough that a first-time visitor can miss it entirely amid the taller buildings around.

## The Geometry

Most colonial churches in Brazil follow a straightforward rectangular plan, derived from Portuguese parish architecture of the 16th and 17th centuries. Sao Joaozinho does something unusual. Its plan consists of two overlapping squares: a larger one forms the nave, internally shaped into an irregular octagon, and a smaller one forms the main chapel flanked by annex rooms. The octagonal nave is topped by a dome, one of the few true domes in Belem's colonial architecture, with slit windows cut into four of its eight panels to admit light. The dome follows the same octagonal geometry as the nave below. The plan was a deliberate quotation of contemporary European church architecture, particularly from the Emilia-Romagna region around Bologna, where complex central-plan churches had been a local specialty for more than a century by the time Landi left.

## Painted Architecture

The two altars installed on the wider sides of the nave carry trompe-l'oeil paintings in Landi's hand. These are quadratura, a technique of illusionistic ceiling and wall painting developed at the Accademia di Belle Arti in Bologna and associated especially with the Galli da Bibiena family, renowned 18th-century set designers whose theatrical perspective work influenced painters and architects across Europe. Landi painted niches that are not there, balustrades that stop at the painted wall, windows opening onto imaginary rooms, garlands and vases of flowers, inverted volutes, and a lamp with the symbolism of the Holy Spirit that appears in multiple Landi projects. He calculated the natural light to emphasize the scenographic effects. Under the right afternoon sun, a visitor cannot tell which architectural elements are real and which are painted until touching the wall.

## The Canvases and the Missing One

Three canvases once hung above the altars, each depicting an episode from the life of John the Baptist: the pilgrimage, the beheading, and a third subject whose identity is known only through historical references. They are attributed to the Portuguese artist Francisco de Figueiredo, and their frames were made in Lisbon in 1774. The third canvas has disappeared; when, and how, is not recorded. The two that remain still hang above the altars in those frames. Between 1899 and 1959 the Augustinian Order took over the chapel and installed three neo-Gothic altarpieces that clashed badly with Landi's Baroque geometry. These were removed during the 1996 restoration and eventually transferred to the Archdiocese of Belem. Around 150,000 reais went into the full restoration, funded through Brazil's National Program to Support Culture, the PRONAC, and by the Archdiocese of Belem through donations collected via the city's town hall.

## A Miniature Baroque

The facade is sober, classicizing Baroque, the style Landi had learned in Bologna filtered through his Portuguese patrons' preferences. Compared to the massive stone facade of Belem's Church of Our Lady of Mount Carmel, completed around the same period, Sao Joaozinho is modest, almost domestic in scale. It is this modest scale, combined with the sophistication of its interior geometry and the quality of Landi's illusionistic painting, that makes the church a small jewel of Brazilian colonial architecture. IPHAN has protected it as a national heritage site. The restoration work completed in 1996 stabilized the building, recovered the original paint scheme where that was possible, and returned Sao Joaozinho to something close to how Landi finished it. The pink and green walls are still marble that has never been marble, the dome still floats above an octagon, and John the Baptist's pilgrimage still unfolds above the altar where Figueiredo's brush left it.

## Flight Context
