# Dom Frei Caetano Brandão Square

The oldest open space in Belém began as the meeting ground between Portuguese and Tupinambá in 1616, and is now a shaded plaza of palm trees and colonial churches at the city's historic core.

**Location:** -1.4552, -48.5054

**Categories:** Belém, Pará, colonial architecture, public squares, Portuguese Empire

## Introduction

Before there were streets there was a clearing. When the Portuguese landed at Guajará Bay in January 1616 and threw up their wooden fort, the flat ground just inland became the meeting place between colonizers and indigenous Tupinambá, a trading post, a parade ground, and the mustering point for a colonial enterprise that was about to swallow the lower Amazon. For four centuries that clearing has kept its function even as its names have changed. Largo da Matriz, then Largo da Sé, and finally Praça Dom Frei Caetano Brandão, it is the oldest public space in Belém and the place where the city, in a real sense, began.

## The First Ground

Belém was founded in 1616 on the exact spot where this square now sits. The first structure was the Forte do Presépio, the wooden fortification that would eventually become the masonry Forte do Castelo. The Largo da Matriz spread out beside it as the civic and religious center of the new settlement. Before the rubber trade transformed the city in the nineteenth century, the place was barely urbanized. Wide enough for troops to drill, flat enough for mass to be celebrated outdoors, close enough to the bay to hear the boats arriving, the square did all the things a plaza in a Portuguese colonial town was supposed to do. Its most important buildings went up around its edges rather than in its middle. A small chapel to Saint Francis Xavier, and the Saint Alexander Church and College, begun in 1653 by the Jesuit Missionary Company, still define the square's religious character.

## The Rubber Century

Everything changed after 1880. Amazon rubber became one of the most profitable commodities in the world, and by 1910 it represented roughly 40% of Brazil's total exports. The money flowed through Belém and reshaped everything it touched. The city's powerful intendant, Antônio Lemos, widened avenues and drained swamps, installed iron monuments imported from European foundries at the Ver-o-Peso Market and the Largo da Pólvora, now Republic Square. The Largo da Sé was not exempt. Lemos's hygienist policies, fashionable at the time across Latin America, called for the cleaning and ordering of public space, and the square was restored accordingly. The Dom Frei Caetano Brandão Square, completed in 1900, stands today as a concrete expression of an era when rubber money briefly convinced Belém it was a small European capital.

## Who Was the Bishop

The man at the center of the square, cast in bronze, is Dom Friar Caetano Brandão, a Franciscan friar who served as Bishop of Pará from 1782 until 1789, then as Archbishop of Braga in Portugal until his death in 1805. Brandão arrived in Belém when the city was a distant and difficult posting, and distinguished himself with the kind of administrative and pastoral work that tends to get a person a square named after them two centuries later. The statue is the focal point, surrounded by boulevards, flower beds, and walking paths paved with slate stone. Palm trees now line the space, replacing mango trees that once grew there: the mangoes shaded everything pleasantly but their dense canopies blocked the view of the surrounding architecture. The trade-off was deliberate.

## Inside the Feliz Lusitânia

The square is part of the Complexo Feliz Lusitânia, the architectural and landscape project that takes its name from the original 1616 settlement. The project bundles together the fort, the square, Saint Alexander Church, the Palace of the Eleven Windows, and the Our Lady of Grace Cathedral into a continuous heritage zone. Walking through it you move across centuries quickly. The fort dates to 1616, the Church of Saint Alexander to 1719, the cathedral to 1771, the Palace of the Eleven Windows to the eighteenth century. All of them survived, more or less intact, in part because Belém was a small enough city through most of the twentieth century that nobody bothered to tear them down. The Feliz Lusitânia restoration pulled them into a coherent visitor experience.

## What the Square Is Now

On any given morning the square fills with the expected mix for a warm-weather plaza: vendors arranging stalls for coconut water and snacks and typical Paraense foods, all of them officially registered with the Municipal Secretariat of Economy. Tourists arrive by the busload to start their walking tours of the historic center. Locals cut across the square on their way to jobs in the ministries and museums that now occupy the old colonial buildings. The bishop stands in the middle, his bronze back to the river, facing the cathedral he did not live to see finished with any of its current ornamentation. The palms rustle. Four centuries in, the Largo da Sé still does exactly what a plaza is supposed to do in a Portuguese city: collect people in the open air, and let the surrounding architecture do the rest of the work.

## Flight Context

Dom Frei Caetano Brandão Square sits at the heart of Belém's Cidade Velha district at 1.4552°S, 48.5054°W, within 100 meters of the Forte do Castelo and the Guajará Bay waterfront. From altitude it reads as a green rectangular break in the dense grid of red-tiled colonial roofs. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,000 feet. Nearest airport is Belém/Val de Cans International (SBBE), 9 km north. Morning light highlights the cathedral's white façade and the palm lines of the square itself.
