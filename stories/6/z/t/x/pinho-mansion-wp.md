# Pinho Mansion

A rubber-era commander's house in Belém's old quarter, restored for millions, abandoned, restored again, and only lately put back to work.

**Location:** -1.4611, -48.5048

**Categories:** Palaces in Brazil, National heritage sites of Pará, Belém, Tourist attractions in Pará

## Introduction

The 1897 date of the Pinho Mansion is not incidental. It is the year the building went up, and it is also precisely the year Belém was rich. The rubber cycle that transformed Pará between 1870 and 1920 was at full tilt. Commander Antônio José de Pinho had the means to build what the city considered an innovative mansion, and he had the social ambition to make it a destination. Rubber money paid for the mansion. The crash that came two decades later did not kill it, but it started a long slide that lasted the better part of a century, through administrations that kept announcing restorations and a building that kept falling apart.

## Built on Latex

In 1897 Belém was at the peak of its rubber boom. Ships left the Port of Belém loaded with latex destined for the tire factories of Europe and North America. Rubber tappers deep in the Amazon bled the Hevea brasiliensis trees for their sap, which concentrated into the black rubber that the industrial revolution could not do without. Commander Antônio José de Pinho, a figure of Belém's commercial elite, commissioned the architect Camilo Amorim to design a mansion that would showcase his fortune. Amorim produced a three-story building at 586 Doutor Assis Street in the Cidade Velha, the old colonial center. It was considered innovative for its time, a mix of styles and spatial solutions that broke with more traditional Belém residences.

## A House for Parties and Politics

On the second floor, a large central hall hosted family parties that poured into flanking side halls. Music rooms, games rooms, and a library filled the remaining space. The second floor contained most of the bedrooms and bathrooms. On the third floor, a belvedere offered views of Guajará Bay, the same bay that was shipping Belém's rubber wealth out to the world. The mansion became what rubber-era Belém wanted: a place where the commercial elite could hold intense social, cultural, and political activity, where deals could be negotiated between dances, where the city's ruling class could see itself reflected in the chandeliers.

## The Long Decline

The Pinho family occupied the mansion for decades. But by 1978 the heirs could no longer afford to preserve it, and the building was auctioned off. The buyers stripped the interior decorations. In 1982 a supermarket chain bought it and used it as a warehouse for flammable materials. The city expropriated it in 1992, beginning a legal dispute that dragged on. The mansion stayed closed. The chandeliers were gone, the furniture scattered, the library emptied. What was left was a beautiful, empty shell in a historic district that had also fallen on hard times. Cidade Velha, the oldest part of Belém, had slowly lost the economic life that once animated its streets. The mansion was one of many buildings whose protection nobody could quite afford.

## Restored, Abandoned, Restored Again

In 2011, after years of legal battles, the mansion was restored at a cost of R$7.8 million, funded by the mining company Vale and the utility Eletrobras through Brazil's Rouanet Law, which offers tax incentives for cultural projects, plus city government funds. The restored mansion opened on Belém's 395th anniversary with an exhibition of more than fifty works from the Belém Museum of Art. Then it fell into disuse. Paint flaked. Structures deteriorated. By the late 2010s the mansion was again in visible decline. In 2022, a new R$5 million restoration project began, this one transferring administration to the Municipal Department of Education, to turn the building into a home for its Center for Arts, Culture, and Education. The first phase was handed over in February 2024, when the mansion took in the city's arts school, and in November 2025 the city and a private partner opened it as a sustainability and innovation hub during the COP30 climate summit.

## A Listed Building

In August 1986, the National Institute of Historic and Artistic Heritage, IPHAN, listed the Pinho Mansion as a national heritage site for representing an important example of late 19th century architecture. IPHAN's note suggested the protection should extend to the surrounding area, especially the neighboring two-story houses and a walled plot across the street, because a single restored mansion in a neglected block does not preserve a sense of place. Whether that wider protection will ever materialize is another question Cidade Velha has been asking for decades. The Pinho Mansion still stands at 586 Doutor Assis Street, its windows still facing the same old colonial street. Whether its newest uses last depends on whether the current generation of Belém can make good on the promises the previous two made.

## Flight Context

Located at 1.461°S, 48.505°W in Belém's Cidade Velha district, about five blocks inland from Guajará Bay. From Belém/Val-de-Cans International (SBBE), approaches over the city give views of the compact colonial core. The three-story Pinho Mansion sits on Doutor Assis Street, a few blocks from the Palace of the Eleven Windows and the Castle Fortress. Best viewed in dry-season morning light, July to November.
