# Trairao National Forest

A 2,575 square kilometer national forest in Para named after a predatory river fish, created in 2006 as a bulwark against the advancing arc of deforestation.

**Location:** -4.9070, -55.5450

**Categories:** 2006 establishments in Brazil, National forests of Brazil, Protected areas of Pará

## Introduction

The forest is named for a fish. Hoplias lacerdae is a thick, toothy predator of Amazonian rivers, and the municipality of Trairao borrowed its name, and then the national forest borrowed it from the municipality. Look at a map of the southwestern Para conservation corridor and the Trairao National Forest sits almost dead center in a belt of protected Amazonia that federal planners assembled in the mid-2000s to hold the line against what they called the advancing arc of deforestation. Created on February 13, 2006, it covers 2,575 square kilometers of rainforest in a watershed where the Tapajos and Xingu basins meet, and where, by the time of its creation, the BR-163 highway corridor had already lost more than ten percent of its trees.

## A Fish and a Highway

The BR-163 runs north to south through Para and functions in the Amazon the way interstate highways once functioned on the American frontier: it carries everything. Logs, soy, cattle, chainsaws, and the migrants who come to cut and plant. Roads into the Trairao were engineered deliberately off the BR-163 to reach logging and ranch sites, and by the mid-2000s the ecosystems along those roads were already fragmented. The federal government classed the forest under IUCN protected area category VI, a designation that allows timber extraction under a management plan. The idea, in theory, was to create an economic reason to keep forest standing, to give legal loggers an alternative to outlaw ones, and to preserve enough intact canopy to keep biodiversity from collapsing in the corridor.

## The Shape of Biodiversity

Most of the forest sits at about fifty meters of elevation. Rainfall averages two thousand millimeters a year, heavy between December and May, lighter from June through November. Seventy percent of the canopy is classed as dense rainforest, thirty percent as open. The watershed divide creates habitat variation out of proportion to the gentle topography. Waterfalls, igapo flooded forests, riparian zones, and terra firme forest each host different communities. A short herpetofauna survey at two locations in September 2009 recorded ninety-four species of amphibians and reptiles, including thirty-one snake species, twenty-three lizards, and the smooth-fronted caiman. Some of what the surveyors collected may yet prove new to science. Sixty-four fish species have been identified, most of them characins, the order that includes the piranhas and the tetras.

## The Rivers You Cannot Use

Rivers here look promising on maps. In practice, they are barely navigable. The Branco, Itapacura, and Cupari rivers wind through the forest, but rapids, shallow stretches, fallen trees, and sharp curves make boat travel difficult. Flood season brings its own problem: the channels vanish and identifying the deepest water becomes guesswork. Getting in and out of the Trairao overland depends on the BR-163 and the tributary roads that branch east from it. Santarem, the nearest major port on the Tapajos, is fifteen hours away by boat from Itaituba, the nearest larger town. That inaccessibility has been, in equal measure, the forest's best protection and the reason illegal operators have long found ways to work it quietly.

## Management in Theory, Pressure in Fact

The official management plan was published in March 2010. It identified the south and southwest as especially important for biodiversity. It also acknowledged what was already visible on the ground: illegal logging, illegal extraction of non-timber products, hunting, fishing, unauthorized roads, and the creep of small farms. Conversion of rainforest to pasture, as has happened elsewhere in Para, tends to be terminal for the herpetofauna because amphibians and reptiles depend on a humid microclimate that pasture cannot hold. The Trairao sits in a neighborhood of protected areas, with the Jamanxim National Park to its south and the Riozinho do Anfrisio Extractive Reserve to its east. Together, federally protected land in this corridor approaches fourteen million hectares. Whether that scale of paper protection translates into functional forest depends on enforcement budgets and political will that shift with each administration.

## Why This Corner Matters

Conservation strategy in the Amazon treats corridors differently from isolated parks. A single reserve, no matter how large, becomes an island when the surrounding matrix is degraded. The Trairao's role is to bridge other reserves. It sits between the Tapajos National Forest and the Jamanxim complex, letting species move across a broader landscape than any one unit could support alone. The forest is quiet country from the air: dense canopy broken by stream valleys, the occasional clearing near an old logging road, the long line of the BR-163 drawing its asphalt frame to the west. What has kept this piece of the Amazon green is not wilderness value alone. It is the bureaucratic decision, renewed year after year, that the forest is worth more standing than cut.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.91 degrees south, 55.55 degrees west, in southwestern Para. Visible from altitude as an expanse of dense canopy bordered on the west by the BR-163 highway. Nearest airports: Itaituba (SBIH) to the west, Santarem (SBSN) to the north. Best viewing in the dry season from July through October, when clouds are lower and smoke from seasonal burns has not yet obscured visibility.
