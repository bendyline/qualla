# Trincheira-Bacaja Indigenous Territory

An indigenous territory of 1.65 million hectares along the Bacaja River in Para, home of the Xikrin people, under siege from illegal loggers, miners, and land grabbers.

**Location:** -4.7304, -51.4611

**Categories:** Indigenous Territories (Brazil), Protected areas of Pará

## Introduction

The Xikrin remember arriving at the Bacaja between 1926 and 1927. They moved into a river valley already occupied by the Parakana, the Arawete, and the Asurini. What followed was a century of contact: conflict with neighbors, then with the Brazilian state, then with the extractive economy that now defines the pressures on their land. In November 1959, sertanistas from the federal Indian Protection Service found a Xikrin community near where the Igarapé Golosa meets the Bacaja. The epidemics that followed that first official contact killed enough Xikrin that survivors fled deeper into the forest. Official demarcation of the Trincheira-Bacaja Indigenous Territory took until 1993. Ratification came in October 1996. The territory covers 1,650,939 hectares in the state of Para and, as of the 2010 census, is home to 746 people.

## A River, A Century, A People

The Bacaja is a tributary of the Xingu, which is itself a tributary of the Amazon. The valley the Xikrin moved into in the 1920s had been contested territory for far longer than the dates of official paperwork suggest. The Xikrin are part of the larger Kayapo nation, known regionally as the Xikrin do Bacaja to distinguish them from the Xikrin communities on the Catete River farther south. Their early contact story is the standard tragedy of Amazonian frontier history: first the epidemics brought by outside contact, then the slow negotiation with a federal agency that treated them as subjects to be managed rather than sovereigns to be consulted. The territory spans parts of four municipalities, among them Sao Felix do Xingu, a frontier municipality whose economy and politics have repeatedly spilled over the territory's boundaries.

## The Bridge That Should Not Have Been

In April 2020, agents from Ibama, Brazil's federal environmental agency, destroyed a bridge inside the territory. It had been built over a 300-meter landfill across a stretch of the Negro River, a tributary within the indigenous land. The bridge existed to move stolen timber out. The landfill existed because trucks could not ford the river otherwise. Ibama fined the Municipality of Sao Felix do Xingu for supporting invaders of indigenous lands in the Medio Xingu region. Within weeks of the action being publicized, Jair Bolsonaro's environment minister, Ricardo Salles, dismissed Hugo Loss, the Ibama coordinator of inspection operations who participated in the raid, and the general coordinator of inspection, Renê Luiz de Oliveira, as well. The dismissal sent a clear message through the ranks of federal enforcement about what kind of intervention would be tolerated.

## Operation Samauma

In July 2021, agents from Ibama, FUNAI, the Federal Police, and the National Public Security Force entered the territory together in a coordinated operation named for the giant tropical tree. They destroyed two facilities and a bridge used by deforesters and illegal miners. They pulled down fences that unauthorized occupiers had strung across stretches of forest to claim land as theirs. In the two years after Samauma, additional operations in and around Sao Felix do Xingu dismantled more camps, houses, and bridges built to enable illegal clearing. The pattern is a cycle. Invaders build infrastructure. Enforcement destroys it. New invaders arrive and build it again. In May 2022, the Federal Public Ministry sued FUNAI, the federal government, and Ibama for failing to monitor the territory, asking the court to order emergency inspections.

## The Belo Monte Shadow

The Trincheira-Bacaja sits in the shadow of a much larger controversy. The Belo Monte hydroelectric dam on the Xingu River flooded more than 400 square kilometers and diverted most of the water from the Volta Grande, the great bend of the Xingu into which the Bacaja flows. The dam was constructed over intense indigenous protest and ongoing legal opposition. For the communities who relied on the Xingu's seasonal floods and its fisheries, the dam's operation changed the river they had known. Hydroelectric expansion, timber theft, and illegal mining now coexist with livestock farming as overlapping pressures on the territory. The Xikrin themselves have reported invaders approaching their villages, raising fears of direct confrontation. Enforcement has often been reactive, arriving after damage is done.

## What the Xikrin Defend

Indigenous territories in Brazil exist as constitutional protections written into the 1988 charter, which guarantees indigenous peoples the permanent possession of lands they traditionally occupy. In practice, that guarantee depends on political will and enforcement capacity that has fluctuated administration by administration. The Xikrin do Bacaja know this. When the president of the country signals that demarcations will be reviewed, as Bolsonaro did after his 2018 election, the logging roads multiply. When enforcement personnel are dismissed after successful operations, as happened after April 2020, the extractors understand what that means. The Trincheira-Bacaja from the air still looks mostly green, an expanse of rainforest along the sinuous Bacaja River. Zoom closer and the access roads appear, the clearings, the sawmill scars. What the Xikrin are fighting for is not only the forest. It is the right not to be erased.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.73 degrees south, 51.46 degrees west, along the Bacaja River in southern Para. Visible from cruising altitude as dense rainforest cut by the winding Bacaja and scarred by illegal logging roads. Nearest airports: Altamira (SBHT) to the west, Maraba (SBMA) to the east. The Belo Monte dam complex on the Xingu River lies about 180 km to the north-northwest. Best viewed in the dry season from July through October, though smoke from seasonal burns can dominate the sky late in the season.
