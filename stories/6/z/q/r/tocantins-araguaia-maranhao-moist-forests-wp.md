# Tocantins-Araguaia-Maranhao Moist Forests

The eastern edge of the Amazon biome, where rainforest dissolves into pasture and Belem's two million people draw a line that forest regeneration can no longer cross.

**Location:** -2.9740, -47.3150

**Categories:** Ecoregions of Brazil, Amazon biome, Neotropical tropical and subtropical moist broadleaf forests

## Introduction

Fly east from the Amazon's mouth and the green below begins to fray. Rectangular clearings interrupt what the forest wants to be continuous. Highway traces appear where no highway should stretch. This is the Tocantins-Araguaia-Maranhao moist forests ecoregion, the most easterly stretch of the Amazon biome and the most punished. Over a third of its forest has been cleared. The patchwork you see from altitude is the ecoregion's census: remnant canopy, secondary growth, cattle pasture, fire scars, and the suburban sprawl of Belem, capital of Para, a city of more than two million people perched at the delta's edge.

## The Forest Before the Road

Before highways drew the modern boundary lines, this region was a transitional zone where Amazonia slowly surrenders to the drier cerrado to the south. The Tocantins River, a tributary of the Amazon, marks the western edge. The Mearim draws the southern boundary. To the northeast, mangroves fringe the Atlantic coast. Scientists suspect this forest was once a refugium during past ice ages, a place where species survived when drier climates shrank the broader Amazon. That history left it unusually rich: a diversification center for trees, a transitional zone where Amazonian species mingled with drier southern vegetation, producing a botanical complexity that taxonomists are still untangling.

## Blackwater and Tide

The rivers here are moody. The Gurupi, Capim, and Guama flow into the Amazon's mouth and feel the ocean's pull twice daily. Tides force water back upstream, flooding riverbank forests on a twelve-hour clock. Biologists split the flooded forests into two types. Igapo forests get daily baths from clear blackwater rivers, their soils acidic white sand, their trees shorter and less diverse. Varzea forests are flooded by whitewater rivers thick with soil, nutrients, and organic matter. The same palm species appear in both, but varzea grows taller, denser, more exuberant. Above the flood line, terra firme forest takes over. There, canopy trees reach upward unchecked, and epiphytes and lianas weave the understory into near-impenetrable green.

## What the Numbers Count

The species list reads like inventory from a lost world. One hundred forty-nine mammal species recorded, more than eighty of them bats. Five hundred seventeen bird species, including two herons uncommon elsewhere in the Amazon. Seventy-six snake species. Endangered mammals include the white-cheeked spider monkey, the black bearded saki, and the giant otter. Endangered sea turtles, green and hawksbill, nest along the coast. The mahogany that once made this forest synonymous with valuable timber has been logged so thoroughly that Swietenia macrophylla now survives mostly along the upper Capim and Guama rivers, a species hunted nearly out of its range.

## The Calculus of Loss

Between 2004 and 2011, this ecoregion lost forest at 0.51 percent per year. That rate sounds small until you multiply it by the decades before and after. The Tucurui Dam, built on the Tocantins below Maraba, flooded 2,430 square kilometers of low-lying forest when it filled its reservoir. Fires used to clear land for cattle escape into the remaining forest every dry season. The pastures left behind are hostile ground for rainforest seedlings. The trees do not regenerate on dry, eroded soil. What burns here tends to stay burned. The Caxiuana National Forest, 2,000 square kilometers of federal protection, offers a stronghold. But the arithmetic of fragmentation still runs against the forest.

## Belem at the Edge

The ecoregion's most visible feature from altitude is its largest city. Belem rose at the Amazon's mouth as a colonial outpost in the seventeenth century and grew into the commercial heart of the eastern Amazon. Paragominas, Braganca, and Maraba cluster in its orbit, with cattle ranches and soy fields filling the spaces between. This is the Amazon where humans clearly won. The question now is whether what remains of the forest, a patchwork of reserves, indigenous territories, and regrowth, can be knit back together before the climate changes what grows here at all. Global warming will force tropical species uphill. A flat, hot, degraded ecoregion like this one has nowhere left to climb.

## Flight Context

Centered near 2.97 degrees south, 47.31 degrees west. Visible from cruising altitude as a mosaic of remnant dark-green forest, pasture rectangles, and river ribbons. The Tocantins River forms the western boundary. Nearest major airports: Belem's Val de Cans International (SBBE) and Maraba (SBMA). Best viewing in the dry season from July through October, when cumulus is lower and smoke has not yet risen from seasonal burns.
