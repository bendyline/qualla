# Belo Monte Dam

The world's fifth-largest hydroelectric project, built over three decades of indigenous resistance on the Xingu River the Juruna call 'house of God.'

**Location:** -3.1278, -51.7758

**Categories:** hydroelectric, indigenous-rights, amazon, brazil, infrastructure, environmental-history

## Introduction

In 1989, a young Kayapo woman named Tuira raised a machete to the face of an Eletronorte engineer named Jose Antonio Muniz Lopes in front of television cameras in Altamira. She did not strike. The gesture was enough. Behind her stood more than six hundred indigenous people from across the Xingu basin who had gathered to confront the Brazilian state's plan to dam their river, and the image of her blade against his cheek ran on front pages around the world. The original project, called Kararao, was shelved. Three decades later, under a changed name and a redesigned plan, most of it got built anyway.

## A River Called the House of God

The Xingu winds north through the state of Para to meet the Amazon, and along a 100-kilometer stretch called the Volta Grande, or Big Bend, the river falls roughly 87 meters in a series of rapids and channels. Eighteen ethnic groups live along this river, totaling more than 25,000 people, among them the Juruna, the Arara, the Kayapo, and the Xikrin. For them the Xingu is not a resource. The word the Juruna give the river translates as house of God. Their seasonal rhythms depend on floods and fish migrations that the river's natural pulse makes possible. The loss of that pulse, many have said, has been more than material; it has been, as one academic paper put it, a spiritual shift in indigenous worldviews.

## A Dam Designed Twice

Plans first emerged in 1975 under Brazil's military dictatorship, when Eletronorte commissioned a hydrographic study that identified five possible dam sites on the Xingu and one on the Iriri. The scheme was leaked in 1987 and met an antagonistic public. The First Encounter of the Indigenous Nations of the Xingu in 1989, the gathering at which Tuira confronted Muniz Lopes, forced the removal of the five upstream dams from planning. Kararao was renamed Belo Monte at the request of the indigenous people whose sacred site carried that name. Between 1989 and 2002 the project was redesigned, the reservoir shrunk from 1,225 to 440 square kilometers, and two diversion canals added to route water away from indigenous territories. One engineer, speaking to a reporter, said: God only makes a place like Belo Monte once in a while. This place was made for a dam. The displaced disagreed.

## Construction Against the Courts

Between 2010 and 2019 the dam complex was built amid an unending procession of federal court battles. The Federal Public Prosecutor filed eleven lawsuits. IBAMA issued a partial installation license in January 2011 despite a FUNAI staff report warning of inadequate consultation with the Paquicamba indigenous community. Construction was halted, resumed, halted again, resumed again. In August 2012 a federal court ordered work stopped because the indigenous peoples had not been consulted as the Brazilian constitution requires; the Supreme Federal Court overturned the ruling within weeks. Norte Energia, the consortium operating the project, failed to obtain what international law calls free, prior, and informed consent from the Juruna and Arara. The first turbines came online in May 2016. The 18th and final turbine was installed in November 2019.

## What the Numbers Do Not Hold

Roughly 20,000 people were directly displaced by the reservoir flooding. An estimated 100,000 migrants arrived during construction to work the camps, many staying when jobs ended. Beyond the forced moves and the lost homes, riverbank communities report decreased fish catches, higher fuel costs for boats now navigating altered channels, and worse water quality. The Xingu River's 600 endemic fish species include the zebra pleco, found nowhere else on Earth, whose habitat the dam either dried out or drowned. An independent expert review concluded the proposed flow through the Volta Grande would not maintain the river's species diversity and risked extinction of hundreds of species. The UN Human Rights Council has published statements denouncing Brazil's handling of the project; the International Labour Organization pointed out that the Brazilian state was in violation of the Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention it had ratified.

## The Machine Itself

By installed capacity of 11,233 megawatts, Belo Monte is the fifth-largest hydroelectric dam complex in the world, behind China's Three Gorges, Baihetan, and Xiluodu dams, and Brazil's own binational Itaipu. It cost somewhere north of 16 billion US dollars. The complex comprises three dams, over a dozen dikes, and diversion canals that feed water from two reservoirs into the main Belo Monte powerhouse, which holds 18 Francis turbines each rated at 611 megawatts. Because of the seasonal oscillations of Xingu flow, the dam's guaranteed minimum output runs about 4,571 megawatts, or 39 percent of that maximum. External researchers have noted that most of this energy is routed toward Brazil's aluminum industry, rather than to the people living nearest the dam.

## After the Turbines

The dam runs now. Chief Raoni Metuktire, who prophesied in 2010 that there will be a war so the white man cannot interfere in our lands again, traveled to Paris with a petition against the project and stood with James Cameron, who called his own anti-Belo Monte documentary Message From Pandora. The war Raoni named did not come in the form he described. It came as the slow war of a river transformed, of fish that no longer run, of a Big Bend permanently bent. The Juruna and the Arara still live along the Xingu. Their children inherited a river that now obeys a turbine schedule rather than the seasonal rains. Whether that counts as cost or benefit depends on where you stand, and what you were willing to lose.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 3.13S, 51.78W. The dam complex spans roughly 50 km of the Xingu River near Altamira, Para. Visible from cruising altitude as a distinct reservoir pattern with two straight diversion canals, each 500 meters wide by 12 kilometers long, running south-west to the main powerhouse. Nearest airport is Altamira (SBHT/ATM), about 40 km north-east. Convective weather year-round over the Amazon basin; morning flights generally offer best visibility.
