# Tucurui Dam

Brazil's first large Amazon hydroelectric project, Tucurui generates 8,370 megawatts, displaced 32,000 people, and cost an activist her life.

**Location:** -3.8325, -49.6491

**Categories:** Dams completed in 1984, Energy infrastructure completed in 1984, Energy infrastructure completed in 2007, Dams in Pará, Hydroelectric power stations in Brazil, Gravity dams, Locks of Brazil, Tucuruí

## Introduction

Tucurui means grasshopper's water in Tupi, and the name now belongs to a concrete wall 78 meters high and nearly 7 kilometers long that stops the Tocantins River in eastern Para. When Phase II finished in 2010, the plant's 25 generating units together produced 8,370 megawatts, enough electricity to power 13 million people and to anchor the aluminum industry that transformed the lower Amazon into one of the most industrialized stretches of the rainforest. The dam was the first large-scale hydroelectric project in the Brazilian Amazon. Its reservoir flooded 2,430 square kilometers of forest. The dam features in the closing scenes of the 1985 John Boorman film The Emerald Forest, playing itself.

## A River, A Dictatorship, A Loan

Engineers from the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation and USAID first surveyed the Tocantins in 1964. The studies moved through various Brazilian committees through the late 1960s and early 1970s. In 1973, during the military government of President Emilio Medici, funding decisions were put on the table. Two sites competed: Tucurui and a Santo Antonio Dam on the Tocantins, unrelated to the later Santo Antonio Dam on the Madeira River. Tucurui won approval in 1974 under President Ernesto Geisel. The World Bank declined to fund the project. Brazilian institutions, led by Eletronorte and Eletrobras, assembled the financing. Construction under the Camargo Correa consortium began in 1980. Phase I, with its twelve 350-megawatt Francis turbines, came online in 1984. Phase II, adding capacity to reach 8,370 megawatts, ran from 1998 to 2010.

## What the Reservoir Flooded

The reservoir impounds 45 cubic kilometers of water, with a live volume of 32 cubic kilometers. The spillway is the second largest in the world, capable of releasing 110,000 cubic meters per second through twenty floodgates. Those are the engineering superlatives. The other numbers matter more. Between 25,000 and 35,000 people were removed from the future reservoir zone in the early 1980s. The government relocated 14,000 of them. Of those, 3,750 were resettled onto newly created islands within the reservoir that lacked adequate roads, water systems, and health infrastructure. The river below the dam lost more than a hundred fish species. Once it held 280 species. Only 178 remain. Manatees, river dolphins, and amazon dolphins that had inhabited the Tocantins are not expected to survive the altered flow regime over the long term.

## The Indigenous Cost

Before construction, the Parakana, Asurini, and Parkatiji peoples fished, farmed, and mined in what became the flooded zone. An impact study before construction calculated that 900 tons of the 1,500 tons of fish these communities relied on for subsistence would no longer be available once the dam closed. The communities had little political standing in the Brazil of the military regime to press their case. The study recorded the loss. The dam was built anyway. Later research into the reservoir's operation found that eutrophication, excess nutrients flooding the stagnating water, created ideal breeding grounds for malaria-carrying mosquitoes and explosive growth of aquatic weeds. Malaria cases spiked in the construction migrant camps. AIDS followed the labor force into the region.

## Dilma Ferreira Silva

Dilma Ferreira Silva was a regional coordinator for the Movement of Dam-Affected Peoples, a Brazilian organization that has tracked the displacement caused by hydroelectric projects across the Amazon since the 1990s. She spent her career documenting what Tucurui had cost the communities upriver and downriver of the wall. On March 22, 2019, assassins killed her at her home in Para. Her husband and a friend were killed alongside her. Silva had been arguing a point that Brazilian energy policy did not want to hear: hydroelectric power, marketed globally as clean and renewable, is not environmentally neutral when the reservoirs submerge tropical forest. The decomposition of inundated vegetation releases methane for decades. The displaced communities bear the social costs. Her killing was part of a larger pattern of violence against Amazonian land defenders that Brazilian environmental organizations have documented in grim detail.

## What the Dam Built

Power from Tucurui runs north to the aluminum smelters at Barcarena near Belem, and through transmission lines built in the 2010s, it now reaches Macapa to the east and Manaus to the west. Sixty percent of the plant's output goes to industry, creating just under two thousand jobs at the heavy smelters. The social calculation favored by engineering consultancies has always been that this is a reasonable trade: thirty thousand displaced, thirteen million electrified, Brazilian industry anchored in the north. The people who were displaced have argued back that the trade was never theirs to make. From the air, the reservoir is a dark inland sea among the trees, its shoreline crooked with drowned tributary valleys. The dam wall is a gray stripe across the Tocantins. Below it, the river runs quieter than it did before 1984, with fewer fish and fewer dolphins and a Brazil that needs the electricity more than ever.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.83 degrees south, 49.65 degrees west, on the Tocantins River in eastern Para. The reservoir is the largest visible water body in the region, extending roughly 170 kilometers upriver from the dam. Nearest airport: Carajas (SBCJ) to the southwest and Tucurui's local airfield (SBTU). Best viewed in the dry season from July through October, when reservoir levels are lower and drowned forest remnants emerge from the water.
