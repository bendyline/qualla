# Aruã People

An Arawakan-speaking people of the Amazon mouth, whose language an ethnographer took down from a 75-year-old elder in 1877, and whose urn cemeteries still emerge from the earth on Caviana Island.

**Location:** -3.5611, -49.6083

**Categories:** indigenous-peoples, history, amazon, brazil

## Introduction

In 1877, an ethnographer named Ferreira Penna walked through the river town of Afua in the Amazon estuary and sat down with an old man. The man was about seventy-five. He was the last Aruã left in Afua. He called himself and his people Aroanauinta, a word Penna wrote down carefully along with a vocabulary of a few dozen other words, and in doing so produced most of the surviving linguistic record of a nation that had held islands at the mouth of the Amazon through the 1600s and 1700s. The Aruã were Arawakan speakers, distant linguistic cousins of the Taino of the Caribbean and the Yine, or Piro, of the Peruvian Amazon. Their stronghold was Caviana Island, which they called Uyruma. Their story is a small one in historical memory and a large one in what it tells us about what colonial contact meant, stretched over two hundred years.

## A Nation Written Down by Strangers

The Aruã enter European records by spelling, not by voice. An Irish settler named Bernard O'Brien put their name in writing in 1621 as Arrua. The Dutch cartographer Joannes de Laet mapped islands north of Marajo as Arouen in 1625. The Walloon Huguenot Jesse de Forest wrote about the Arouen who wear their hair long like women. Later maps label them Aruans. Today, the accepted spelling in academic work at the Museu Paraense Emilio Goeldi and the Federal University of Para is Aruã, with the nasalized final vowel marked by a tilde. In northern Amapa, close to French Guiana, you occasionally see Aruá, with an acute accent, a French Creole inheritance. The same people, in other words, were filed under half a dozen spellings by writers from several European trading empires trying to put them on maps.

## Caught Between Three Empires

The Aruã lived through one of the most unstable colonial neighborhoods in the Americas. The mouth of the Amazon was contested by three European powers at once. The Portuguese held Belem and pushed outward. The French held Cayenne and pushed west. The Dutch held what would become Guyana and pushed south. All three wanted trade, captive labor, or both. The Aruã tried to survive by siding with whichever empire was not pressing them at the moment. When the Portuguese tried to force them into hard labor and failed, they were put to work as sentinels, watching the approaches. When the French offered trade, they listened. Between 1725 and 1728, the French nonetheless attacked them repeatedly, together with a group from Mexiana Island under a leader named Gaaimara. Their lives were spent navigating shifting alliances with colonizing powers that viewed them as pieces on a map rather than as people.

## The Treaty of Mapua, and What Followed

In 1654, the Portuguese launched a military expedition from Belem against the Aruã and other indigenous nations. The following year, 1655, the Jesuit missionary Antonio Vieira, preaching from the pulpit and writing to the Portuguese Crown, succeeded in having the laws that sanctioned enslavement of indigenous peoples abolished in the region. It was a partial and temporary victory. By 1658, the Portuguese were preparing another armed expedition. That expedition never launched. Instead, in 1659, the Portuguese and several indigenous nations signed the Treaty of Mapua, a formal agreement that was supposed to regulate their relations. The Aruã were there with their chief, Piye, who refused to swear an oath of obedience to the Portuguese crown. The treaty proved fragile: in 1661, settlers short of captive labor forced the reinstatement of Indigenous slavery, and it collapsed after just two years. Between 1784 and 1798, Portugal depopulated the entire coastline of Brazilian Guiana to forcibly bring the Aruã back into Portuguese territory. A village was founded near Belem to settle them. Most escaped by canoe back to Guiana. In 1793, those Aruã who had remained around Chaves on Marajo were transferred to the lower Tocantins River. Their new village, called Moru, is now part of the municipality of Breu Branco.

## The Urn Fields of Caviana

On the east side of Caviana Island, in what the Aruã called Uyruma, there are still funeral urn fields. Large ceramic vessels were buried in the ground, holding the bones of the dead in secondary burials. Archaeologists today classify them as protected archaeological sites. Near Chaves on Marajo Island, ceramic fragments related to Aruã pottery have long been found along the coastline. But the Vieira Grande Bay, a broad inlet on the northern Marajo shore, has been encroaching on that coast in recent decades, and much of the pottery evidence has been washed away by the advancing water. The urns of Caviana remain above the tide. To walk among them is to walk through the material record of a people whose spoken language is gone and whose descendants have been forcibly relocated at least twice by colonial authority.

## What Remains

Scholars in Belem still return to them: A Terra dos Aruã, an ecological history of the Marajo archipelago published by the Museu Paraense Emilio Goeldi in 2012, takes its very title from them. Their descendants live on. Aruã who settled along the Uaca River in Amapa merged with Galibi, Marworno and other neighbors into the people who today call themselves the Galibi-Marworno, still living on the Uaca alongside the Karipuna. Others live among the mixed populations of the Marajo islands, who trace ancestry that the formal records ignored. The elder Ferreira Penna met in 1877 spoke his people's name in his own language one more time: Aroanauinta. A life, a people, a word. All three still echo, in the urn fields of Caviana and on the maps that bear their name in half a dozen spellings.

## Flight Context

Centered near 3.56 S, 49.61 W in the lower Tocantins basin of Para, Brazil, close to the village of Moru, now part of Breu Branco, where the Portuguese forcibly resettled Aruã from Marajo in 1793. The Aruã homeland extended north across Marajo Island and Caviana Island near the Amazon mouth at around 0 to 1 S latitude. Best viewing altitudes are FL200-FL280. Nearest airports include Tucurui (SBTU) for the Tocantins area and Belem Val-de-Cans (SBBE) for the Marajo archipelago. Expect dry-season haze from ranching fires between August and November.
