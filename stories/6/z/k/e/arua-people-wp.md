# Arua People

An Arawakan-speaking people of the Amazon mouth, whose last speaker was 75 years old in 1877, and whose urn cemeteries still emerge from the earth on Caviana Island.

**Location:** -3.5611, -49.6083

**Categories:** indigenous-peoples, history, amazon, brazil

## Introduction

In 1877, an ethnographer named Ferreira Penna walked through the river town of Afua in the Amazon estuary and sat down with an old man. The man was about seventy-five. He was the last fluent speaker of the Arua language anyone would ever record. He called himself and his people Aroanauinta, a word Penna wrote down carefully along with a vocabulary of a few dozen other words, and in doing so produced nearly the entire surviving linguistic record of a nation that had controlled the islands at the mouth of the Amazon for centuries. The Arua were Arawakan speakers, distant cousins of the Taino of the Caribbean and the Piro of the Peruvian Andes. Their stronghold was Caviana Island, which they called Uyruma. Their story is a small one in historical memory and a large one in what it tells us about what colonial contact meant, stretched over two hundred years.

## A Nation Written Down by Strangers

The Arua enter European records by spelling, not by voice. An Irish settler named Bernard O'Brien put their name in writing in 1621 as Arrua. The Dutch cartographer Joannes de Laet mapped islands north of Marajo as Arouen in 1625. The Walloon Huguenot Jesse de Forest wrote about the Arouen who wear their hair long like women. Later maps label them Aruans. Today, the accepted spelling in academic work at the Museu Paraense Emilio Goeldi and the Federal University of Para is Arua, with the nasalized final vowel marked by a tilde. In northern Amapa, close to French Guiana, you occasionally see Arua without the diacritic, a French Creole inheritance. The same people, in other words, were filed under half a dozen spellings by half a dozen different European trading empires trying to put them on maps.

## Caught Between Three Empires

The Arua lived through one of the most unstable colonial neighborhoods in the Americas. The mouth of the Amazon was contested by three European powers at once. The Portuguese held Belem and pushed outward. The French held Cayenne and pushed west. The Dutch held what would become Guyana and pushed south. All three wanted trade, slaves, or both. The Arua tried to survive by siding with whichever empire was not pressing them at the moment. When the Portuguese tried to force them into hard labor and failed, they were put to work as sentinels, watching the approaches. When the French offered trade, they listened. Between 1725 and 1728, the French nonetheless attacked them repeatedly, together with a group from Mexiana Island under a leader named Gaaimara. Their lives were spent navigating shifting alliances with colonizing powers that viewed them as pieces on a map rather than as people.

## The Treaty of Mapua, and What Followed

In 1654, the Portuguese launched a military expedition from Belem against the Arua and other indigenous nations. The following year, 1655, the Jesuit missionary Antonio Vieira, preaching from the pulpit and writing to the Portuguese Crown, succeeded in having the laws that sanctioned enslavement of indigenous peoples abolished in the region. It was a partial and temporary victory. By 1658, the Portuguese were preparing another armed expedition. That expedition never launched. Instead, in 1659, the Portuguese and several indigenous nations signed the Treaty of Mapua, a formal agreement that was supposed to regulate their relations. The Arua were there. Their chief, Piye, signed. But formal treaties proved fragile. Between 1784 and 1798, Portugal depopulated the entire coastline of Brazilian Guiana to forcibly bring the Arua back into Portuguese territory. A village was founded near Belem to settle them. Most escaped by canoe back to Guiana. In 1793, those Arua who had remained around Chaves on Marajo were transferred to the lower Tocantins River. Their new village, called Moru, is now part of the municipality of Breu Branco.

## The Urn Fields of Caviana

On the east side of Caviana Island, in what the Arua called Uyruma, there are still funeral urn fields. Large ceramic vessels were buried in rows in the ground, holding the remains of the dead. Archaeologists today classify them as protected archaeological sites. Near Chaves on Marajo Island, ceramic fragments related to Arua pottery have long been found along the coastline. But the Vieira Grande Bay, a broad inlet on the northern Marajo shore, has been encroaching on that coast in recent decades, and much of the pottery evidence has been washed away by the advancing water. The urns of Caviana remain above the tide. To walk among them is to walk through the material record of a people whose spoken language is gone and whose descendants have been forcibly relocated at least twice by colonial authority.

## What Remains

Academic scholarship now treats the Arua as one of the most important Arawakan-speaking peoples of the eastern Amazon. The book A Terra dos Arua, published by the Museu Paraense Emilio Goeldi in 2012, argues that Arua ecological knowledge and political organization shaped the pre-colonial landscape of the Marajo archipelago more deeply than the colonial record suggests. Their descendants are still there, scattered among the Galibi-Marworno, the Karipuna, and other Amapa peoples in the north, and among the mixed populations of the Marajo islands who trace ancestry that the formal records ignored. The last fluent elder Ferreira Penna met in 1877 spoke his name in his own language one more time: Aroanauinta. A life, a people, a word. All three still echo, in the urn fields of Caviana and on the maps that bear their name in half a dozen spellings.

## Flight Context

Centered near 3.56 S, 49.61 W in the lower Tocantins basin of Para, Brazil, close to where the forcibly relocated Arua of 1793 founded the village of Moru, now part of Breu Branco. The Arua homeland extended north across Marajo Island and Caviana Island near the Amazon mouth at around 0 to 1 S latitude. Best viewing altitudes are FL200-FL280. Nearest airports include Tucurui (SBTU) for the Tocantins area and Belem Val-de-Cans (SBBE) for the Marajo archipelago. Expect dry-season haze from ranching fires between August and November.
