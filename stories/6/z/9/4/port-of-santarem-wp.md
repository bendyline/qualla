# Port of Santarém

A military-government port built in 1974 to move Amazon soy to China, now the fastest-growing cargo terminal in Brazil.

**Location:** -2.4172, -54.7347

**Categories:** Ports and harbours of Brazil, Buildings and structures in Pará

## Introduction

The Port of Santarém is what the Brazilian military government built when it decided the Amazon needed to produce. Inaugurated on February 11, 1974, the port was the anchor of the National Integration Plan, a suite of infrastructure projects that also included the BR-230 Transamazonian highway and the BR-163 road connecting Cuiabá to Santarém. The idea was to open the Amazon to development. The execution was brutal in places, permanent in its ecological effects, and, when measured purely in tonnage moved, spectacularly successful. In 2019, Santarém ranked first among all 19 Brazilian ports that saw growth in cargo volume. More than 12 million tons left through its docks that year, most of it soy and corn bound for Asia and Europe.

## Where the Tapajós Meets the Amazon

The port sits at Ponta do Salé, on the right bank of the Tapajós River, about three kilometers from the point where the Tapajós pours into the Amazon. It is 876 river-kilometers upstream from Belém. The Tapajós here runs clear and greenish-blue, colored by forest sediment. The Amazon runs silty and brown, carrying Andean soil toward the sea. The two rivers meet but resist mixing for dozens of kilometers, creating the encontro das águas, the meeting of the waters, one of the Amazon's famous visual spectacles. Santarém grew up at this junction because it is a natural trading post, where boats from the Tapajós could transfer cargo to boats going down the Amazon. The port makes that logic modern.

## Built for Grain

Santarém was designed to move solid bulk and general cargo, with emphasis on vegetable grains from Brazil's interior. Today the port handles soy, corn, fertilizer, and liquid bulk including fuels and LPG. Cruise ships and river ferries use the passenger terminal. The throughput is heavily grain-weighted. In 2015 and 2016, the Santarém Complex handled 4.5 million and 4.3 million tons of solid vegetable bulk, respectively. Grain movement works in two directions: barges from Porto Velho in Rondônia bring grain down the Madeira and up the Amazon, delivering 80% of the inbound cargo. Another 20% comes from Miritituba in Pará, a transshipment point that receives grain trucked from Mato Grosso. All of it ships out to buyers abroad.

## China and the New Trade

Soy destined for China leaves Santarém by the boatload, alongside shipments to the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, France, Spain, and Italy, with smaller flows to Saudi Arabia, Germany, Egypt, and Algeria. The global market for Brazilian soy has tripled over the last two decades, driven primarily by Chinese demand for animal feed. Corn leaves Santarém for Algeria, Egypt, the Dominican Republic, and other destinations. The Amazon port is shorter and cheaper than the traditional southern route. Mato Grosso soy can reach Santarém via 1,000 kilometers of road on the BR-163, versus about 2,000 kilometers to the Port of Santos. Shorter distance means lower trucking costs, lower emissions per ton, and an economic case that has been closing the south-north gap.

## The Road That Changed Everything

BR-163 was begun by the military government in the 1970s but spent most of its life as an unpaved, seasonally impassable track. Paving was finally completed in late 2019. That single infrastructure change transformed Santarém's trajectory. The port's cargo volume jumped. The port went from a respectable regional terminal to one of the fastest-growing cargo nodes in Brazil. The road also transformed the land it crosses. Between Cuiabá and Santarém the highway runs through rainforest and cerrado that was once remote. Deforestation followed the pavement. The paved BR-163 has made Santarém boom, while behind the boom, the forest that shelters the highway is disappearing at rates Brazilian monitoring agencies continue to raise alarms about.

## What Comes Next

Access to Santarém today runs through the Tapajós and Amazonas rivers for ships and the BR-163 and BR-230 for trucks. There is no rail link. A proposed railway, the Ferrogrão, would connect Mato Grosso's grain fields directly to Santarém, bypassing the road bottleneck and potentially doubling throughput. The railway has faced legal challenges from indigenous groups whose lands it would cross and from environmental groups concerned about the additional deforestation a rail corridor would trigger. Santarém remains a working port at the center of debates that are actually about what kind of Amazon Brazil wants to have. The port moves more grain each year. The choices about what happens upstream, around it, under its sphere of influence, will shape the region for decades.

## Flight Context

Located at 2.417°S, 54.734°W on the Tapajós at its confluence with the Amazon. From Santarém-Maestro Wilson Fonseca International (SBSN), the port is directly visible on approach. The contrast between blue-green Tapajós and brown Amazon water is striking from altitude. Barges, cargo ships, and river boats cluster at the quay. The meeting of the waters extends downstream in visible streaks. Best viewed in dry-season morning light, July to November.
