# Dom Eliseu

A Pará frontier town that began life as a roadside warehouse at Kilometer Zero, boomed on Amazon timber, and has been reinventing itself around guava and cattle ever since the chainsaws fell silent.

**Location:** -4.2850, -47.5050

**Categories:** Pará, Amazon frontier, deforestation, agriculture, Brazilian municipalities

## Introduction

It started with one man and a warehouse. In the mid-1960s, at the precise junction where the federal highway BR-010 met the new state road PA-70, a construction worker named Leopoldo Cunha decided not to move on after his crew finished the job. He had been laid off from Delta Engenharia, the company that built PA-70. Instead of leaving, he set up a warehouse at the crossroads, brought his family down from wherever they had been, and before long opened a small restaurant for truckers. That restaurant was the first building in what is now Dom Eliseu, a Pará municipality of roughly 58,000 people whose entire existence is downstream of one man's refusal to pack up and go home.

## Kilometer Zero

The village that grew up around Cunha's roadhouse was originally called simply km 0, because that was the mile-marker at the start of PA-70. The name would not last. In July 1973, Filinto Müller, a Brazilian senator and former head of the political police, died when Varig Flight 820 crashed on approach to Paris. Müller had been politically powerful in his home state of Mato Grosso and nationally controversial for his role in the authoritarian Estado Novo period. The tiny Pará settlement at km 0 had no obvious connection to him, but the country was in the middle of military rule, and naming a new town after a recently dead politician was one of the ways villages curried favor. Km 0 became Filinto Müller, and then, in 1983, when the village became a district of Paragominas, it was renamed once more, this time for Dom Eliseu Maria Coroli, a Catholic bishop. Formal emancipation as a municipality followed in 1988, and the first mayor, Antônio Jesus de Oliveira, elected with 65% of the valid votes, took office on January 1, 1989.

## The Age of Sawmills

In the 1970s, Serraria Alves Marques Ltda. opened the first logging mill at the junction. Others followed quickly. For roughly three decades the economy of Dom Eliseu ran almost entirely on timber. Veneer mills, sawmills, and plywood factories processed the Amazon hardwoods being hauled out of forests to the north and west, and the town prospered. Then, in the 1990s, two forces collided: the accessible trees within trucking distance were gone, and Brazilian environmental agencies finally began enforcing the laws that had been on the books for years. The mills closed, one after another. By January 2008, the federal Ministry of Environment placed Dom Eliseu on a list of thirty-six municipalities responsible for more than half of all Amazon deforestation, a distinction the town carries along with its forest loss: from 46% forest cover in 2003 down to 37% by 2008.

## Guava and Cattle

When logging died, agriculture grew. Cattle raising became the economic flagship, with herds distributed throughout the municipality and a milk basin that now ranks eighth in all of Pará. Meat and milk flow out to the markets of Belém, Marabá, and São Luís. But the more unexpected success story is guava. In 1999 a local food processing company, needing raw fruit for its sweets and pastes, began encouraging farmers to plant guava trees. With support from Emater and the creation of a demonstration plot in 2004, interest spread quickly. By 2005, Dom Eliseu surpassed 25,000 tons of guava sweets produced in a single year. Today about 6,000 hectares of guava grow within the municipality. Japanese immigrant families, meanwhile, brought the cultivation techniques behind another local crop, black pepper.

## The Carajás Vote

Dom Eliseu sits in the southeast corner of Pará, closer geographically and culturally to the mining region around Carajás than to the distant state capital of Belém. In the 1990s the town joined the Association of Municipalities of Araguaia and Tocantins and pushed, through the Comitê Municipal Pró-Carajás, for Pará to be split and a new state of Carajás to be created. On December 11, 2011, a plebiscite was held. Within Dom Eliseu more than 90% of voters backed the split. Across Pará as a whole, however, 66.60% voted against. Dom Eliseu became one of just thirty-nine municipalities in the state that voted for separation. The state stayed whole. The resentment that had driven the vote did not entirely disappear.

## Ordinary Things

The town has an airport with the ICAO code SSFU, 37 primary schools, five secondary schools, and a municipal IDEB score of 5.4 in 2023, the highest in Pará for its grade level. Most houses are brick and concrete, roofed in corrugated shingles; a handful are made from shipping containers. Outside the bus terminal, donkey carts still wait for passengers alongside the taxis. In December 2013 a Christmas event called the Natal do Bob Esponja brought in mascots of SpongeBob SquarePants and Minnie Mouse. In August 2018 a pickup truck with armed men opened fire near the Banco do Brasil branch shortly after midnight, scattering bullet shells across the road. The town is small enough that it remembers everything and large enough that everything eventually happens to it.

## Flight Context

Dom Eliseu lies in southeastern Pará at 4.285°S, 47.505°W, at the junction of BR-010 and BR-222 (formerly PA-70), at an elevation of 180 meters. From the air the town appears as a regular grid sprawling along the highway crossroads, surrounded by pastureland and guava orchards where primary rainforest once stood. Aeroporto Dom Eliseu (SSFU) serves the town directly. The larger Carajás Airport (SBCJ) is about 340 km southwest. Visibility is best in the dry season (May through September).
