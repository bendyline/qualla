# Ilha do Marajó

A river island the size of Switzerland at the mouth of the Amazon, where a sophisticated mound-building society flourished for centuries before Europeans arrived, and where police now patrol the flooded grasslands on water buffalo.

**Location:** -0.9400, -49.4500

**Categories:** Islands, Archaeology, Indigenous history, Wildlife, Amazon, Brazil

## Introduction

Start with an urn. It stands perhaps half a meter high, painted in red and black on a white slip, its surface worked into interlocking geometric bands and the stylized figure of a woman. Inside are cremated human remains, and a bowl was set over the mouth to close it. It was made on Marajó, at the mouth of the Amazon, by potters working within a tradition that lasted centuries and produced two distinct grades of ware - plain vessels for the kitchen, elaborate ones for the dead. For most of the twentieth century, the standard account of Amazonia held that the rainforest was too poor to sustain a society capable of making such a thing. The urns were the evidence that the standard account was wrong.

## The Tesos

The Marajoara built upward, because the island floods. Their settlements sit on tesos - earthen mounds raised in layers, often on top of ground that was naturally a little higher to begin with. Anna Curtenius Roosevelt, excavating and surveying in the 1980s, mapped more than four hundred of them spread across upwards of 2,000 square kilometers, linked into villages and ceremonial centers. Os Camutins, one of the largest complexes, has around forty mounds and may have held ten thousand people; Fortaleza has twenty. Roosevelt estimated the island's population at its height at more than a hundred thousand. Her excavation at Teso dos Bichos pointed to a conclusion that unsettled the field: this society had developed on Marajó itself, rather than arriving fully formed from the Andes as Betty Meggers had argued on the premise that tropical soils could never support such complexity.

## A World Organized Around Women

What the mounds contain is as striking as their scale. Houses had tamped earth floors and were arranged in clans that appear to have been matrilineal, with households organized around the women who stayed put while husbands moved in. The iconography agrees: female figures dominate the ceramic art as mythic ancestors, creators and culture heroes. Even the settlement layout carries meaning, aligned east to west in a pattern consistent with a cosmology divided along lines of gender. The culture flourished from roughly 800 to 1400 AD, though people had been living at some of these sites as far back as a thousand years before Christ. Why the mounds were finally abandoned - structures left unrepaired, sites left quiet - is still unresolved.

## After Contact

The Europeans arrived in the sixteenth century, and within a few generations roughly ninety percent of the indigenous population of the region was dead, overwhelmingly from Eurasian diseases against which they had no immunity. That is not a footnote to the archaeology; it is the reason the archaeology exists in the form it does. The mounds and their contents are what remains of communities that were destroyed before anyone thought to write down how they lived. Marajoara design survived the people who invented it and became one of the visual signatures of Pará, reproduced on ceramics, tiles and civic ornament across the state - a style detached from its makers, still recognizably theirs.

## Half a Million Buffalo

The animal that now defines Marajó is not native either. Asian water buffalo number around 450,000 on the island, comfortably outnumbering the people, and how they got here is a matter of competing legends - a ship that sank offshore, or escapees from French Guiana who crossed the mangroves on buffalo-back. Whatever the truth, they suit the terrain better than cattle do, and better than machinery. The military police detachment at Soure patrols on them: officers mounted on buffalo can cross flooded field and deep mangrove mud at speeds no horse or motorcycle can manage, which is why theirs is the only unit in Brazil doing it. The practice goes back about thirty years. Buffalo also supply the island's kitchens with meat and with the cheese that turns up on menus from Soure to Belém.

## Water Decides

At about 40,100 square kilometers - close to the area of Switzerland - Marajó is the second-largest island in South America, bounded by the Amazon, the Pará and the Tocantins rivers and by the Atlantic. From December to June the eastern half is under water. Lake Arari swells to some 400 square kilometers in the wet season and loses four-fifths of that in the dry. Where the ocean tide meets the river, the pororoca sends a wall of water upstream that can reach four meters. Life is arranged around this. Soure and Salvaterra draw most visitors, with the long empty river beaches at Pesqueiro, Araruna and Praia Grande and colonial ruins at Joanes; Breves, on the western side, is the largest town. Ships from Belém take about four hours, speedboats ninety minutes, an air taxi twenty to thirty. The buffalo were simply the first outsiders to work out how to move here.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 0.94 S, 49.45 W, roughly 120 km west-northwest of Belém across the mouth of the Amazon. Best appreciated high - 6,000-10,000 ft gives a sense of the scale, with the dark forested western half, the open savanna and gallery forest of the east, and the vast pale sediment plume where the river meets the Atlantic. In the wet season the eastern grasslands read as sheet water with tree islands, and the tesos are occasionally visible as regular low rises above the flood. Belém/Val de Cans International (SBBE) is the main gateway; small airstrips serve Soure, Salvaterra and Breves, and air taxis cross from Belém in under half an hour. Expect heavy equatorial convection, low ceilings and rain from December to June, and haze from agricultural burning in the drier months.
