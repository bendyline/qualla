# Treaty of the Mapua

A 1659 treaty signed on Marajo Island between Jesuit missionary Antonio Vieira and the indigenous nations of the archipelago, which lasted only two years before Portuguese colonists reinstated slavery.

**Location:** -1.1167, -50.3000

**Categories:** 1659 treaties, Treaties of the Kingdom of Portugal, Colonial Brazil, History of Pará

## Introduction

The treaty lasted two years. In August 1659, at a gathering on the Mapua River whose proceedings went almost unrecorded, Portuguese Jesuits led by the missionary Antonio Vieira met with indigenous leaders from the Marajo Archipelago to negotiate what both sides hoped would hold. Vieira named only one of the indigenous leaders in his surviving writings: Chief Piye of the Arua, who lived on the island of Caviana near the Amazon's mouth, and who refused to swear obedience to the Portuguese king. The treaty granted the Portuguese unhindered canoe passage through the straits around Breves and the right to establish Jesuit missions across the archipelago. It promised, implicitly, an end to the military expeditions and the slaving raids. Then the colonists in Belem revolted over a shortage of enslaved labor, blamed the Jesuits, and by 1661 the agreement was dead.

## A Line on a Map No One Could See

The Treaty of Tordesillas had drawn a line through the Atlantic in 1494, dividing the New World between Portugal and Castile. The line was supposed to fall 370 leagues west of Cape Verde. The treaty never specified how long a league was or which Cape Verdean island the measurement started from. The Portuguese took the most advantageous reading, pushing the line westward until it ran through the Marajo Archipelago at the mouth of the Amazon. That interpretation placed the river's estuary inside the Portuguese claim. By the seventeenth century, the competition for control was not with Spain but with the French operating out of Cayenne and the Dutch pressing in from the north. Each European power needed the indigenous population as allies, laborers, or trading partners. Each also raided them when useful.

## Antonio Vieira

Vieira arrived in the Amazon in 1653 with a complicated reputation. Five years earlier, he had written to the Portuguese Crown recommending that the Captaincy of Pernambuco be handed over to the Dutch in exchange for peace. The suggestion was pragmatic and politically toxic. When he reached Belem as a Jesuit missionary, the colonists who would have preferred to keep him far from their slaving interests had to deal with him anyway. In 1658, Portuguese forces in Belem prepared yet another armed expedition against the Arua and other indigenous groups of the Marajo Archipelago. Vieira's alternative was to negotiate. A year later, on the Mapua River, he managed what earlier missionaries had not. He got indigenous leaders to sit down.

## What Was Signed and What Was Not

The surviving record is thin. Vieira names Chief Piye. He does not name the other leaders present, nor the particulars of the negotiations, nor the specific concessions offered to the indigenous signatories in exchange for their concessions to Portugal. The written terms were lopsided. The Portuguese got canoe access through a critical trade route linking Belem to Macapa and French Guiana. They got the right to plant Jesuit missions across the archipelago. What the indigenous peoples received in the documents was mostly an absence: an implied end to military raids, a promise of missionary protection against slavers. Vieira reported the treaty to Lisbon on February 11, 1660. The Crown received it as a triumph. It had closed the mouth of the Amazon to Dutch ambitions and secured the region for Portuguese America. The peoples whose lives had been bargained over were, in the correspondence, barely mentioned.

## Two Years and a Revolt

Christianization began immediately after the signing. Many indigenous people did not wait to find out what mission life would bring. They fled north into Brazilian Guiana, the region that is now the state of Amapa, and into French Guiana beyond it. In 1661, the colonists of Belem rose against the Jesuits. Their complaint was blunt: the Jesuits had disrupted the slave supply. Secular clergy and rival Catholic orders joined the colonist revolt, resenting the Jesuit monopoly on the governance of indigenous peoples. The colonists reinstated slavery. Slave-raiding expeditions resumed. Whatever protection the treaty had offered collapsed. Vieira, already on thin political ice, was expelled from the region along with thirty-one other Jesuits in that same year.

## An Agreement Between Unequal Hands

History has tended to treat the Treaty of the Mapua as a footnote in the larger story of the Portuguese consolidation of the Amazon, and its footnote status reflects how colonial history gets told: the peoples whose consent was extracted under military pressure and theological persuasion become background to the European signatures in the archives. Chief Piye lived on Caviana, in the archipelago where the Arua had fished and traded for generations. His name survives because Vieira wrote it down. The names of the other indigenous leaders who sat across from the Portuguese that August do not. The canoes kept moving through the straits at Breves. The missions went up. The slaving continued under different management. Flying over the Marajo Archipelago today, you see the wide brown sweep of the Amazon's mouth and the scattered green islands where those negotiations happened. The signatures are gone. The geography is unchanged.

## Flight Context

The treaty location is approximately 1.12 degrees south, 50.30 degrees west, on the Marajo Archipelago at the mouth of the Amazon River. Visible from altitude as a cluster of large low-lying green islands between the Para River channel and the Atlantic. Nearest airport: Macapa (SBMQ) to the north, Belem's Val de Cans International (SBBE) to the southeast. Best viewed in the dry season from August through November when the Amazon's flow is lower and island edges are distinct.
