# Marajó Archipelago Environmental Protection Area

The largest river-and-sea archipelago in the world, a landscape of salt and sweet water in seasonal alternation, protected across twelve municipalities and sixty thousand square kilometers.

**Location:** -0.8192, -49.7863

**Categories:** 1989 establishments in Brazil, Environmental protection areas of Brazil, Protected areas established in 1989, Protected areas of Pará

## Introduction

As Marajó's favorite story tells it, a ship carrying buffalo from India to French Guiana wrecked off the island, and the animals swam ashore, found the flooded grasslands and endless vegetation exactly to their liking, and stayed. Today their descendants outnumber the human population across much of the archipelago - hundreds of thousands of water buffalo wading through savannas that become lakes in the wet season and grasslands in the dry, a species completely reshaping a landscape they were never native to. This is the kind of detail that makes Marajó strange even by Amazonian standards. The Marajó Archipelago Environmental Protection Area, established in 1989, protects what Brazilian researchers call the largest fluvial-marine archipelago on Earth: about three thousand islands and islets spreading across almost sixty thousand square kilometers where the Amazon and Tocantins rivers converge and pour into the Atlantic. Marajó island itself, the centerpiece, is roughly the size of Switzerland.

## Twelve Municipalities, One Delta

The protected area is divided administratively among twelve Pará municipalities, each holding a percentage of the total. Chaves takes the largest share at 22.44 percent, Breves the next at 16.15 percent, Afuá 14.2 percent, Anajás 11.78 percent. Smaller slices belong to Cachoeira do Arari, Curralinho, Muaná, Ponta de Pedras, Salvaterra, Santa Cruz do Arari, Soure, and São Sebastião da Boa Vista. All twelve face the same environment and the same pressures: a population of roughly 250,000 people spread across a delta where the infrastructure is rivers and the highways are canoes. The APA contains, within its boundaries, several smaller protected zones - Charapucu State Park in Afuá, the Mapuá Extractive Reserve, the Terra Grande-Pracuúba Reserve, the Soure Marine Extractive Reserve - a nested structure of protection and community management.

## Salt and Sweet

What makes Marajó genuinely unusual is the seasonal alternation between salt water and fresh. In the dry season, Atlantic tides push saline water up into the archipelago's channels. In the wet season, the muddy floods of the Amazon and Tocantins push the salt back out and drown the low-lying grasslands in sweet river water for three or four months. The vegetation responds in bands and zones: alluvial rainforest on the higher ground, savanna and campo on the flooded fields, mangroves and salt marshes where the tides reach, sandbanks and pioneer plant communities at the edges. Species that define the Amazon rainforest grow here alongside species that define brackish coastal environments - Brazil nut and açaí palm alongside Cedrela odorata (Cuban cedar) and Virola surinamensis (baboonwood), with buriti palm filling the margins of permanent water. The average temperature hovers near thirty degrees Celsius year-round.

## Two Manatees and Flocks of Scarlet Ibises

The wildlife inventory is its own argument for protection. Two endangered species of manatee live in the archipelago's waters: the West Indian manatee (Trichechus manatus) in the more saline channels, and the Amazonian manatee (Trichechus inunguis) in the freshwater stretches - the Amazon's mouth is where the two species' ranges meet. Scarlet ibises flock by the thousands along the mudflats, their feathers turning Marajó's dawn skies pink. The mix of terrestrial and aquatic species reflects the mix of terrestrial and aquatic habitats: a place that is not quite land and not quite water, or rather is both at different times of year. And then there are the buffalo - the shipwreck descendants of legend, alongside herds imported from the 1890s on - whose numbers are now measured in the hundreds of thousands. They have become so culturally central to Marajó that the island's cheese and butter industry, its police force (famously mounted on buffalo), and its tourist imagery all depend on them. The buffalo are invasive, ecologically problematic, and utterly identified with the place.

## The Plan That Still Isn't

The APA was created on October 5, 1989, under Article 13 of the Pará state constitution. The stated objectives are ambitious: ecological and economic zoning, biodiversity conservation, preservation of endangered species, support for scientific research and environmental education and ecotourism. A presidential decree in February 2007 established an inter-ministerial executive group to coordinate federal agency activity in the archipelago, and a sustainable development plan was published in September 2007. But as of 2014 - twenty-five years after the APA's creation - no management plan had been formally adopted for the protected area itself. Brazilian conservation law requires management plans for APAs, their buffer zones, and their ecological corridors, integrated with the socio-economic life of surrounding communities. The gap between the law and the ground is a familiar Amazonian pattern. For the people who actually live on Marajó - fishing, tapping açaí, herding buffalo, making pottery in the Marajoara tradition whose ceramic art gave the island its archaeological fame - the protection has always been, in practice, whatever local communities themselves can manage.

## Flight Context

Centered at approximately 0.82 degrees S, 49.79 degrees W, northwest of Belém at the mouth of the Amazon. The archipelago spans roughly 60,000 km² and includes thousands of individual islands; Marajó island itself is the dominant landmass. Best viewed from 5,000-10,000 feet AGL - the scale of the delta and the pattern of channels, beaches, and flooded grasslands is genuinely impressive from altitude. Belém/Val de Cans International Airport (SBBE) to the southeast provides the main air connection.
