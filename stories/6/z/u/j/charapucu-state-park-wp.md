# Charapucu State Park

A 65,000-hectare flooded forest on the western edge of Marajó Island, where jaguars, manatees, and rare fish share a landscape the Amazon rewrites every tide.

**Location:** -0.3702, -50.5234

**Categories:** state-park, protected-area, amazon, marajo, brazil, rainforest

## Introduction

The Charapucu State Park has no entrance gate, no visitor center, and in some patches no record of human entry at all. It sits on the western shoulder of Marajo, the largest fluvial island in the world, where the Amazon River finally breaks apart into the Atlantic. The park's 65,181 hectares include forests that have never been systematically explored, which is a rare honest statement from a government conservation document. The water rises and falls on the rhythm of ocean tides more than any local weather; forests that look solid at low tide become navigable channels six hours later.

## The Edge of Marajo

Marajo is so large that maps have trouble with it. At roughly 40,000 square kilometers, it is bigger than Switzerland, a flat interior island formed by the slow sedimentation of the Amazon and Tocantins rivers where they meet the Atlantic. Charapucu State Park occupies a piece of its western flank, in the municipality of Afua, bounded by the Vieira Grande Bay to the northwest. The Cajari River runs to the east of the park and empties into the bay. A channel called the Furo Charapucu cuts across the park's southern edge, linking the Cajari back to another part of Vieira Grande. All of this sits within the much larger Marajo Archipelago Environmental Protection Area, a sustainable-use conservation unit created in 1989 covering nearly six million hectares of the delta.

## Water That Has No Single Color

Two different kinds of water meet inside the park, and they do not mix easily. From the Amazon comes what scientists call white water, muddy and gray-brown with sediment scraped from the Andes, carrying the dissolved nutrients that make floodplain forests some of the most productive ecosystems on Earth. From the island's interior come blackwater rivers, their tea-dark color produced by tannins leached out of decomposing leaves in the sandy, acidic soils behind the main channels. Blackwater carries fewer nutrients but harbors entirely different fish communities and supports different plants along its banks. Watching the two waters swirl past one another in the park's tidal channels is one of the stranger sights in Amazonia; the boundary can be sharp enough to draw with a ruler.

## A Canopy Above Water

The trees here are varzea and igapo species, adapted to standing in water for months of each year. Virola surinamensis, whose nutmeg-like seeds flavor Amazonian cuisine; Carapa guianensis, or crabwood, whose oil is used for medicine; the enormous kapok tree Ceiba pentandra, whose buttressed roots make its trunk look like a cathedral's flying buttresses; Calycophyllum spruceanum, with its smooth, peeling bark. The buriti palm Mauritia flexuosa raises its crown above the water in dense stands, and Euterpe oleracea, the acai palm whose berries have become an export commodity, grows here in its native habitat rather than as a plantation crop.

## The Animals

Charapucu harbors several species that have been driven out of more accessible parts of the Amazon. The Kaapori capuchin (Cebus kaapori) is critically endangered; this is one of the monkeys' few remaining habitats. The Amazonian manatee (Trichechus inunguis) and the West Indian manatee (Trichechus manatus) both use the park's tidal channels, and this overlap of the two manatee species in a single protected area is unusual. Jaguars (Panthera onca) and pumas (Puma concolor) still hunt here. The Arrau turtle (Podocnemis expansa), the largest river turtle in South America, nests on the park's exposed sandbars when the water drops. Spectacled caimans, giant anteaters, and South American tapirs round out a fauna that reads like a field-guide index of Amazonian mammals and reptiles.

## A Park Still Being Negotiated

Governor Ana Julia Carepa created Charapucu State Park by state decree 2592 on 9 November 2010, with the stated purpose of preserving the ecosystems, supporting scientific research, and advancing a proposal to have UNESCO recognize a Marajo Biosphere Reserve. The park's management has been a slow and deliberate process. A working group to develop the management plan was appointed in April 2013, a consultative council established soon after, and a full managing council convened by July of that year. In July 2015 the Institute of Forest Development and Biodiversity, known as Ideflor-Bio, began consulting with the Afua municipality communities to identify which parts of the park they historically used for hunting, fishing, and gathering, and whether the boundaries or conservation status should change to reflect that use. These consultations continue. Marajo's human geography, spread across dozens of small river communities, resists any top-down planning that tries to draw straight lines across a landscape the tides rewrite twice daily.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 0.37S, 50.52W. Located on the western side of Marajo Island at the mouth of the Amazon River. Visible from cruising altitude as a mosaic of forest and tidal channels, with the broad Vieira Grande Bay to the northwest. No airstrip within the park; nearest airports are Belem Val-de-Cans (SBBE/BEL) approximately 180 km southeast and Macapa (SBMQ/MCP) approximately 200 km north. Tropical climate with consistent rainfall and convective afternoon storms.
