# Fordlândia

Henry Ford's abandoned Amazon rubber colony sits on the Tapajós River, a 45-meter water tower still marking the skyline of a failed American experiment that now shelters 3,000 Brazilians.

**Location:** -3.8314, -55.4975

**Categories:** Pará, Amazon, Henry Ford, Ford Motor Company, abandoned towns

## Introduction

A water tower rises 45 meters above the east bank of the Tapajós River, 150 feet of American industrial iron. Climb it, after asking the guard for permission, and you get an aerial view of a town that was meant to be a piece of Michigan transplanted to the Amazon and did not quite become one. This is Fordlândia, a small river town of about 3,000 people in Pará that began as Henry Ford's rubber plantation in 1928 and was given up by the Ford Motor Company in 1945. It failed so completely that its failure is more famous than most people's successes.

## What Ford Was Trying to Do

In the late 1920s, Henry Ford bought a large area of Amazon jungle for one reason: rubber. The automobile industry needed it. British and Dutch producers controlled most of the world supply through plantations in Southeast Asia, and Ford wanted his own source. The plan was to recreate an entire American industrial community in the middle of the Amazon rainforest. Workers would live in American-style houses, eat American food at set American mealtimes, work American hours, and presumably behave in American ways. The settlement was meant to export Dearborn, Michigan, to Pará, Brazil, complete with the kind of company-town discipline Ford liked. Workers disagreed. The food was unfamiliar, the hours cruel in tropical heat, and the cultural imposition was resented from the start. Combined with the fact that nobody at Ford's operation actually understood tropical agriculture, the Fordlândia plantation was failing within a few years. From 1934 the main planting moved downriver to Belterra, which also eventually failed, and Ford sold both to the Brazilian government in 1945.

## Getting There

There is no quick way to reach Fordlândia. As of 2017, boats left Santarém from the Praça Tiradentes port Monday through Saturday at 4 pm and Sunday at 2 pm. The trip takes 12 hours and costs about R$45, with passengers hanging hammocks on the deck for the overnight ride. Hammocks cost R$25-40 at the shops around the port. You arrive around 4 am and can stay strung up until sunrise. Faster speedboat services, called lanchas, run from Pontão de Lancha near Praça Tiradentes on Sundays, Tuesdays, and Fridays at 1 pm for R$50, taking four to seven hours. Coming from the south, boats from Itaituba leave daily at 4 pm and take four hours, speedboats about an hour. There is just one road into town, and a four-wheel drive is recommended in the rainy season.

## Vila America

About 2.3 kilometers from the town center sits Vila America, the district where Ford's American managers and technicians once lived. Walking through it now is a slow inventory of what happens when you leave a planned town mid-build and do not come back: American-style houses slowly crumbling, American fire hydrants still set in concrete at intersections, fragments of a paved sidewalk, an abandoned swimming pool slowly filling with tropical green. As of 2017, all the houses had inhabitants except one decaying structure partly hidden behind vegetation. Previous travelers have noted that in some of the abandoned houses you can still see 1930s beds, bathtubs, and sinks, shipped upriver from the United States, frozen in place by the end of the project.

## Going Around

Fordlândia is small enough to walk, and there are several mototaxis for anyone who does not want to. The water tower is the most recognizable landmark, visible from almost everywhere in town. There is no organized tourist infrastructure, no tour company, no visitor center; you figure it out as you go. You can find internet at a few small cafés, though the connections are slow. There are no ATMs, which is worth remembering. In an emergency a motorcycle workshop in town has the only card payment equipment and can be used to extract small amounts of money. A few simple pousadas near the dock offer rooms. Travel light and bring more cash than you expect to need.

## Food and Life

One travel guide jokes that you can eat rice and vegetables like the original Fordlândia workers, as long as you bring them yourself. There is truth behind the joke. The failure of the American food imposition, more than any other factor, symbolized what was wrong with the project. Ford managers insisted on Midwestern American food and rigid mealtime schedules in a region where workers had always eaten cassava, river fish, fruit, and whatever else the forest provided. Today the town functions on its own terms, a Pará river community that happens to share its streets with the ghost of an industrial utopia. Boats going upriver to Itaituba leave around 4 am, and boats going downriver to Santarém leave around 8 pm. Life continues around the abandoned fire hydrants.

## Flight Context

Fordlândia lies on the east bank of the Tapajós River in Pará at 3.8314°S, 55.4975°W, roughly 180 km south-southwest of Santarém. From altitude the town is a small cleared rectangle in dense Amazon rainforest, distinguished by its water tower and its geometric American street grid that contrasts sharply with the organic Brazilian settlements along the river. Nearest major airport is Santarém/Maestro Wilson Fonseca (SBSN), about 180 km north-northeast. Itaituba Airport (SBIH) is closer but smaller. The Tapajós is one of the Amazon's great clearwater tributaries, greenish from the air, though gold-mining silt now muddies stretches of it.
