# Tucurui Transmission Line

A 1,800-kilometer high-voltage corridor from the Tucurui Dam to Manaus and Macapa, with 295-meter towers crossing the Amazon River nearly as tall as the Eiffel Tower.

**Location:** -1.5866, -52.7607

**Categories:** Energy infrastructure completed in 2011, Electric power infrastructure in Brazil, High-voltage transmission lines, 2011 establishments in Brazil

## Introduction

Two towers rise from Jurupari Island in the middle of the Amazon River, and they weigh 2,500 tonnes each. They stand 295 meters tall. The Eiffel Tower is 324. Between those towers hangs a 2.2-kilometer span of stranded aluminum cable, four conductors thick with steel cores, carrying 500 kilovolts of electricity from the Tucurui Dam across the Amazon to Manaus and on north to Boa Vista. Before this line was completed in 2013, none of Brazil's states north of the Amazon were connected to the national electrical grid. Manaus, a city of two million, ran its lights on diesel, thermal generators, and a Balbina Dam that never delivered more than 20 percent of what the city needed. The Linhao de Tucurui changed that.

## Why a Line Had to Cross the Amazon

The Amazon is not just a river. At its widest, it is an inland sea with islands in the middle, and no cable-stayed bridge has ever spanned its lower reaches. For engineers designing a continental power grid, the Amazon was the obstacle and the prize. North of the river, the states of Amapa and Roraima plus parts of Para and Amazonas depended on subsidized thermal power that was expensive to run and polluting to operate. The Tucurui Dam, with 8,370 megawatts of capacity, had more power than it could push through the southern grid alone. The Linhao de Tucurui, or Tucurui-Macapa-Manaus Interconnection, would wire the underserved north to the cheapest available hydro source. The project consisted of seven double-circuit power lines totaling 1,800 kilometers, connecting eight substations, and using 3,600 transmission towers with an average span of 500 meters between them.

## Barges That Could Not Barge

Between the Xingu and Amazon rivers lies the Alagados region, a landscape that floods in the wet season and turns to deep mud in the dry. Large barges could not operate without causing unacceptable environmental damage, and smaller craft could not carry the weight needed for tower construction. The engineers built light barges propelled from the front by hydraulic excavators. The excavator scoop dug into the mud and pulled the barge forward meter by meter. To find the deepest water channels through the swamp, crews followed buffalo trails left by the cattle that had been crossing this country for a century. The Isolux Corsan contractor responsible for lots A and B kept 4,000 workers housed in fourteen camps through the construction phase. A 70-kilometer section of the line passed through swamp that would have been impassable in conventional engineering practice.

## The Towers That Wanted to Be Eiffel

The main crossing of the Amazon near Jurupari Island required structures that are, in engineering terms, unusual. Two 295-meter towers flank a 2.2-kilometer span. The total strain section, from one end of the river crossing to the other, extends 3,908 meters. The Zhejiang Electric Power Transmission and Transformation Corporation of China, along with the China Cable Corporation and Zhejiang Shengda Steel, built the two largest towers and the main span. Four 779.4-millimeter-diameter stranded aluminum conductors with steel cores carry the electricity. The pylons are lattice towers, steel frameworks that look from the air like a photographic negative of the Eiffel. When the sun catches them, they flash against the river like small mountains that should not be where they are. Smaller 150-meter towers handle a secondary 1.2-kilometer span, and additional crossings over the Trombetas and Uatuma rivers required similarly outsized structures.

## Whose Territory the Line Must Cross

In 2015, the concession for an extension from Manaus north to Boa Vista, Roraima, had not yet broken ground because the route crossed the Waimiri Atroari Indigenous Territory. The chosen alignment along the BR-174 highway, the shortest and least environmentally damaging of the four routes considered, would run 123 kilometers through indigenous land and require 250 support towers inside the territory. A federal judge issued an injunction in February 2016 halting construction until the Waimiri Atroari were consulted as required by the Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention of 1989. Public hearings had been held in nearby cities but not inside the territory itself. A Waimiri Atroari leader told reporters that FUNAI could not speak on their behalf. The Waimiri Atroari had already suffered violent pacification campaigns in earlier decades and flooding of sacred sites by the Balbina Dam. In March 2016, a different federal judge suspended the injunction, ruling that judicial interference had been undue given the project's strategic national importance.

## Carbon Saved, Forest Cut

The environmental math of the Linhao de Tucurui is contested. The line's operation is estimated to avoid 1,460,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent emissions per year by displacing thermal generation. Against that, the transmission corridor itself required forest clearance, and the maintenance paths beneath the wires, up to 70 meters wide in some sections, create habitat gaps that many wildlife species cannot cross. In 2013, a federal investigation examined whether trees had been illegally removed from Mindu Park in Manaus in violation of environmental licenses. In 2015, 450,000 reais were allocated to the Uatuma Sustainable Development Reserve to offset irreversible damages from the line's construction. The Roraima extension runs through the Adolfo Ducke Forest Reserve, an important research site, where scientists worry the cleared corridor will isolate portions of the reserve and cause local extinctions. The power reached Manaus in 2013. Total investment: 2.29 billion US dollars.

## Flight Context

The main Amazon crossing at Jurupari Island is located at roughly 1.59 degrees south, 52.76 degrees west. Visible from altitude as twin steel pylons rising above the forest and river. The line corridor is visible as a ruler-straight cut through rainforest, generally running alongside the BR-163 and BR-174 highways. Nearest airports: Santarem (SBSN), Manaus (SBEG), Macapa (SBMQ). Best viewed in the dry season from July through October when cloud cover is lower.
