# Gurupa Várzea

Nowhere on Earth does the sea reach so far up a river as here, where Atlantic tides push 700 kilometers inland and flood a maze of islands, savannas, and forest that 148 mammal species call home.

**Location:** -1.9100, -53.2680

**Categories:** Amazon ecoregions, Pará, várzea, Amazon River, tropical ecosystems

## Introduction

Twice a day, hundreds of kilometers inland from the Atlantic, the river runs backward. In the Gurupa várzea, an ecoregion of 984,195 hectares stretching along the lower Amazon from the mouth of the Tapajós down to the mouth of the Xingu, the ocean tide dams the river's flow and water levels rise and fall in a rhythm that has nothing to do with rain and everything to do with the moon. Elevations here are no more than five meters above sea level. During the peak of the rainy season, when the Amazon is in full flood, the tidal fluctuation still ripples measurably upstream, and the river behaves, at the scale of hours, more like an estuary than a river at all. This is a landscape the Atlantic Ocean reaches into.

## Black Water, White Water

The Gurupa várzea takes its name from Ilha Grande de Gurupá, a large alluvial island sitting in the mouth of the Amazon. Geographically it marks a zone where the whitewater Amazon, heavy with sediment from the eastern Andes, meets several blackwater tributaries: the Xingu, the Jari, and the Tapajós. Blackwater rivers run dark, stained with tannins from forest decay, and carry almost no sediment. Where they meet the turbid Amazon, the waters initially run side by side without mixing, a phenomenon visible from orbit. The soil across the várzea is made up of the Andean sediments carried here by the rivers during the current Holocene epoch. It accumulates where the flow slows, building up the natural levees along the riverbanks and leaving low-lying floodplains behind them.

## Savanna Where Forest Should Be

Most of the Amazon you have seen in photographs is dense rainforest that closes overhead like a green roof. The Gurupa várzea has some of that, clustered along the levees where tropical trees reach heights of around 25 meters. Behind the levees, though, the landscape changes radically. Here the várzea is mostly savanna rather than forest, a distinctive Amazonian grassland called canarana that grows on clay soils and tolerates the daily tidal flooding. The flooded savanna supports an entire dictionary of aquatic grasses with Latin-tag names: Echinochloa polystachya, Hymenachne amplexicaulis, Paspalum fasciculatum, several species of wild Oryza rice. Sedges and small shrubs fringe the transition zones between grass and water. It is one of the least-photographed landscapes of the Amazon, partly because it is hard to reach and partly because it does not match the popular image of what the Amazon is supposed to look like.

## The Animals in the Water

In its waters live fish large enough to feed a village. Pirarucu, Arapaima gigas, is one of the largest freshwater fish on Earth, sometimes reaching over two meters. Tambaqui, Colossoma macropomum, a heavy-bodied relative of the piranha, eats fallen fruit from flooded forest trees. Pacu, piranha, the broad round discus fish Symphysodon aequifasciatus, armored catfish by the dozens of species, cichlids, characins, tetras. The endangered Arrau turtle, Podocnemis expansa, lays its eggs on exposed sandbanks during the dry season. The giant otter, Pteronura brasiliensis, one of the most charismatic and endangered of all Amazonian mammals, hunts fish in family groups in the quieter backwaters.

## The Canopy Above

The mammalian count is 148 species. Ocelots and margays stalk the levees. The South American tapir, Tapirus terrestris, crashes through the floodplain forest at night. Capybaras, the world's largest rodents, graze along the water's edge. The kinkajou moves through the branches. White-lipped peccaries travel in herds through the understory. And the primates: the red-faced spider monkey, the Venezuelan red howler whose morning calls carry for kilometers, and several species found nowhere else. The red-handed tamarin, the bare-eared squirrel monkey, the silvery marmoset, the red-bellied titi, these are endemic to the region. Endangered primates include the white-cheeked spider monkey and the white-nosed saki. Bird diversity runs to 558 recorded species: herons and egrets, whistling ducks, the roseate spoonbill glowing pink in the shallows, the dark-winged trumpeter running along the forest floor, the iridescent crimson topaz hummingbird flashing between flowers.

## Critical and Endangered

The World Wildlife Fund, as of 2017, classified the Gurupa várzea as Critical/Endangered. The pressures are what you would expect and a few you might not. Selective logging has taken most of the commercially valuable timber, including Virola surinamensis and Ceiba pentandra, the kapok tree sacred in several Amazonian cosmologies. Cattle and water buffalo ranching convert savanna to pasture. Mechanized rice production continues to damage wetland systems. In the past, large-scale jute cultivation badly degraded large areas. Urban sprawl from the small city of Monte Alegre eats into the ecoregion's edges. Small-scale farmers, the subsistence ribeirinhos who have worked this floodplain for generations, largely practice forest-friendly agriculture that does not degrade the land; their way of life may turn out to be the best hope the várzea has. The tide keeps rising. The river keeps running backward twice a day. The question is what survives the next hundred years.

## Flight Context

The Gurupa várzea stretches along the lower Amazon River in Pará, centered near 1.91°S, 53.268°W, between the mouths of the Tapajós and Xingu rivers. From altitude the ecoregion reads as a complex maze of channels, sedimentary islands, and flooded savanna, with the white-water Amazon meeting the black-water Xingu visible as two differently-colored flows running side by side before mixing. Recommended viewing altitude 5,000-8,000 feet for the regional context. Nearest airports are Santarém/Maestro Wilson Fonseca (SBSN) to the west and Porto de Moz (SNMZ) to the east. Rainy season December through May brings dramatic flooding patterns.
