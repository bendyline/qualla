# Pará

Brazil's second-largest state, where the Amazon meets the sea, indigenous cuisine dominates the table, and about 40 Indigenous peoples live, some still in isolation.

**Location:** -3.0667, -52.7333

## Introduction

Pará is where the Amazon ends. After 7,000 kilometers of travel through South America, the world's largest river spreads into an estuary so wide that from the air it looks less like a river and more like an inland sea. Here in Brazil's second-largest state, the Amazon empties into the Atlantic, and everything Pará is flows from that geography: the food that tastes like nowhere else in Brazil, the colonial capital that styled itself Paris n'America, the river island the size of Switzerland, the indigenous groups who have never spoken with outsiders, and the massive iron mines that reshape mountains. Pará is the Amazon turned into a state.

## A State the Size of a Country

Pará covers 1.2 million square kilometers, second only to Amazonas in Brazilian area, larger than most European nations. It contains 144 municipalities and borders six Brazilian states plus Guyana and Suriname. The east is densely populated around Belém and the coast. The west is barely developed rainforest being pushed back further each year by ranching and logging. About 40 indigenous groups still live in the state, some of them uncontacted as of recent decades. The Assurini lived in isolation until 1971. That contrast between colonial cities and deep forest, between global commodity ports and peoples who still speak their own languages, is the state's defining tension.

## The Food of the Amazon

Pará is the Brazilian state where indigenous cooking most shapes the modern table. Start with tucupí, a yellow gravy boiled down from manioc root that smells bitter and tastes like nothing else on Earth. Tacacá is a soup of tucupí, dried shrimp, and jambú, a leaf that numbs your mouth as you eat it. Pato no tucupí is duck cooked in that same gravy. Maniçoba, easily shortlisted for the worst-looking food ever invented, is a stew of pork and manioc leaves boiled for days to neutralize natural cyanide. Açaí here is not a smoothie bowl but a thick purple porridge eaten with dried shrimp or fish. Brazil nuts, castanhas-do-Pará, literally mean nuts from Pará.

## Belém, the Paris of the Amazon

The capital Belém sits near the mouth of the Amazon on Guajará Bay. During the rubber boom of the late 19th century, when latex from Pará's forests made a handful of people rich, the city styled itself Paris n'America. Intendant Antônio Lemos lined the streets with mango trees and designed boulevards modeled on Haussmann's Paris. The boom collapsed twice, first in 1920 when British plantations in Malaya flooded the market, then again after 1945. What remained was a city of tiled facades, wrought-iron balconies, and the Círio de Nazaré, an October procession from the cathedral to the Basilica of Nazaré that draws some two million faithful each year. The Port of Belém still ships bauxite, iron, and wood out to the world.

## Islands and Beaches

Just offshore from Belém sits Marajó, claimed as the world's largest fluvial-maritime island, with an area of about 40,000 square kilometers, roughly the size of Switzerland. It has distinct birdlife, enormous water buffalo herds introduced in the late 19th century, and remnants of the vanished Marajoara culture that produced the ceramics still made in Belém today. Ilha de Mosqueiro, closer in, offers 17 kilometers of beaches with freshwater tides. Alter do Chão, on the Tapajós, is often called the Brazilian Caribbean for its white sand and clear green water. Salinópolis, a beach resort about 300 kilometers from Belém, draws crowds during the dry season from July to November.

## Fordlândia and the Other Histories

In the 1920s, Henry Ford tried to break the British rubber monopoly by building a prefabricated American town in the middle of the Pará rainforest. Fordlândia was designed for 10,000 workers and included a dance hall, a golf course, and a hospital. It was built on exact Michigan specifications and collapsed within a decade. Leaf blight decimated the rubber trees. The Brazilian workers rejected the Midwestern diet and curfews, leading to a 1930 riot. Ford eventually abandoned the project. Today its buildings still stand in a river town upriver from Santarém, a monument to industrial hubris. In the southeast, the Serra dos Carajás holds one of the world's largest iron ore mining complexes, where nearly 180 million tons a year are mined, much of it bound for China, Japan, and Europe.

## Flight Context

Pará stretches across northern Brazil near 3.07°S, 52.73°W. Main air gateways: Belém/Val-de-Cans International (SBBE) on the eastern coast and Santarém-Maestro Wilson Fonseca (SBSN) in the interior. From cruising altitude, the silver ribbons of the Amazon, Tapajós, and Xingu rivers dominate the visual field. In October, dramatic storm systems build over the interior. Marajó Island sits clearly visible in the estuary. Dry season (July to November) gives the clearest views.
