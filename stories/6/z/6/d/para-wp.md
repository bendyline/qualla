# Pará

A Brazilian state the size of two Frances, where the Amazon empties into the Atlantic, rubber built a copy of Paris, and a single mountain range feeds China's steel mills.

**Location:** -3.8500, -52.4000

**Categories:** Pará, Federative units of Brazil, North Region, Brazil, States and territories established in 1772, 1772 establishments in Brazil

## Introduction

Pará means sea in the Tupi language, a word the first European arrivals would have understood immediately if they had bothered to ask. Vicente Yáñez Pinzón sailed into the mouth of the Amazon in 1500 and thought he had found an ocean, because the freshwater plume reached 300 kilometers out into the Atlantic and the northern bank of the river was too far away to see. That confusion of river and sea defines the state. Pará holds the Amazon's final miles, from first approach through the complex delta islands to the open water beyond. In between is the country's second-largest state by area, a territory of 1.2 million square kilometers that produces a quarter of Brazil's iron ore and almost all of its bauxite, while keeping some of the last truly untouched rainforest on the planet.

## A Sea Called Pará

The state's name comes from the Tupi word for sea, sometimes applied to very large rivers. The Pará River, one of the Amazon's distributary channels, gave the state its name. The Amazon mouth is so wide here that the river functionally becomes ocean, with tides, salinity gradients, and wave action. Marajó Island sits in the center of the estuary, claimed as the largest fluvial-maritime island in the world at 50,000 square kilometers. The Tupi people recognized this geography long before Pinzón did. The colonists who came later adopted their word for it, which is one of the small ways the indigenous Amazon still names the land that was taken from it.

## Paris in the Amazon

The first European to reach Belém stayed. In January 1616, Portuguese captain Francisco Caldeira Castelo Branco founded the Fort of the Nativity at the site that became the state capital. Two and a half centuries later, during the rubber boom of the 1870s to 1920s, Belém reinvented itself as Paris n'America. Intendant Antônio Lemos imported mango trees from India, lined the streets with them, and designed boulevards and plazas inspired directly by Haussmann's Paris. Rubber money paid for iron pavilions, tiled facades, and an opera house. When the boom collapsed, first from British plantations in Malaya flooding the market after 1920, and again after 1945, Belém kept its mango trees and its Parisian bones. You can still feel the city the rubber barons wanted to build.

## Cabanagem and the Blood on the Flag

The two red stripes on Pará's flag commemorate the Cabanagem, a rebellion that remade and nearly destroyed the state between 1835 and 1840. The cabanos were poor mixed-race, indigenous, and Black Pará residents who rose against the regional elite after independence. Tens of thousands died in the fighting and the reprisals. By the time the Brazilian Empire regained control, as much as a third of Pará's population had perished. It remains one of the largest popular uprisings in Brazilian history and one of the deadliest. The state government marks it quietly. The flag marks it in red. Every schoolchild in Pará learns the word cabanagem, even when the full weight of what happened is glossed over in the textbooks.

## The Iron Mountain

In the 1960s and 70s, something different happened in the south of the state. Prospectors flying over the Serra dos Carajás noticed iron-rich rocks visible through thin forest. Geologists confirmed one of the largest iron deposits on Earth. Companhia Vale do Rio Doce, now Vale, built the mine, the railway, the port, and the company town. By 2007, Carajás alone was producing 296 million tons of iron ore a year, shipped from Pará to Japan, Germany, Italy, France, and Spain. Pará also produces almost all of Brazil's bauxite, most of its manganese, and significant copper, nickel, gold, and tin. In 2017 the state's mining sector was worth nearly R$38 billion. The wealth does not always stay. Most of the processing happens elsewhere.

## Japanese Papaya, Lebanese Merchants, Italian Traders

Pará is Brazil's third-largest Japanese community. The first immigrants landed in 1926 at Tomé-Açu after a voyage from the Port of Kobe. They introduced jute, which briefly boomed in the 1930s, and later black pepper, Hawaiian papaya, and melon, all of which still dominate the state's tropical agriculture. Before them, Lebanese immigrants came during the rubber boom, settling in Belém, Santarém, Óbidos, and a dozen other cities. Italians from Calabria and Campania ran trading houses along the Amazon, and the Italian consulate eventually opened an office in Óbidos to serve the community there. The Portuguese arrived first and stayed most obviously, leaving their language, their architecture, their Catholicism. But Pará's cultural mix is layered, a record of who came up the river and who stayed.

## Flight Context

Pará sits near 3.85°S, 52.40°W, spanning northern Brazil from the Amazonas border to the Atlantic. Main gateways: Belém/Val-de-Cans International (SBBE), consistently ranked among the world's top 10 airports, serves 2.7 million passengers a year; Santarém-Maestro Wilson Fonseca (SBSN) serves the interior. From cruising altitude the Amazon, Tapajós, and Xingu dominate the landscape as silver ribbons. Marajó Island is visible in the estuary. The mining scars of Carajás are visible in the southeast as red patches in the forest. Dry season (July to November) gives the clearest views.
