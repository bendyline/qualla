# Cavalcante

An old gold-mining town in northern Goiás, now surrounded by the largest Kalunga quilombola territory in Brazil.

**Location:** -13.7978, -47.4578

**Categories:** Municipalities in Goiás, Quilombola territories

## Introduction

In 1736, a prospector named Julião Cavalcante and a small company of gold seekers crossed into the Serra da Cavalhada in what is today northern Goiás. Near a stream they called Lava Pés - Wash-Your-Feet - they struck gold. The news traveled. More prospectors came. A settlement grew around the diggings, taking its name from the man who had led the expedition there. For seventy years, gold flowed out of these mountains. Then, around 1806, the seams ran dry. The miners left. The town shrank. What remained of Cavalcante settled into a quieter existence, ringed by cerrado, surrounded by people whose story had nothing to do with gold.

## Gold, Then Not

The Brazilian gold rush reshaped the interior of Goiás. Colonial mining towns sprang up along the Serra Dourada and the Chapada dos Veadeiros, pulling labor - much of it enslaved - into remote valleys to work the alluvial deposits. Cavalcante was among the most productive of these towns for a time. But gold mining follows a predictable arc: find it, extract it, exhaust it, leave. By 1806 the Lava Pés stream was yielding little, and the bandeirantes moved on. The town dropped to vila status in 1831, then became a municipality in 1953. In 1957, the district of Colinas do Sul split off. In 1988, Teresina de Goiás did the same. Cavalcante today retains some colonial architecture from the gold period, though much has been lost or modified across the intervening centuries. The old streets testify to an era when silver candlesticks and religious imagery came across the sertão on muleback, and fortunes were made and lost in the course of a decade.

## The Kalunga Territory

The history that has lasted longer than the gold belongs to the Kalunga people. When Portuguese colonists were running mining and agricultural operations with enslaved African labor, some of those enslaved people escaped into the mountains. In remote valleys where pursuers struggled to follow, they built settlements called quilombos - self-governing communities of formerly enslaved people and their descendants. The Kalunga communities around Cavalcante maintained near-total isolation from Brazilian society until the 1960s. They developed their own dialect, their own festivals, their own fusion of Catholicism and West African spiritual tradition. Today about 4,500 Kalunga people live across Cavalcante, Monte Alegre, and Teresina de Goiás. Their main population centers are Contenda, Vão do Calunga, Vão de Almas, Vão do Moleque, and the former Ribeirão dos Negros, renamed Ribeirão dos Bois. In 2019, Brazil formally recognized much of this territory as a Historic Site, protecting a cultural landscape that has survived against long odds.

## The Lost Springs

The Serra da Cavalhada that brought the first prospectors here holds more than gold. Hidden in these low mountains are mineral springs - some hot, some cold - that gave their name to an entire lost town. Água Quente, literally Hot Water, was once a mining settlement built around the thermal springs. The miners left. The town died. The springs remain, now reachable only on foot across rough country. Cavalcante's altitude ranges from 360 meters to over 1,600 meters within its boundaries, creating conditions for an unusually rich mix of cerrado ecosystems. More than 150 waterfalls have been cataloged within the municipality. Most are reachable only by trail, which is why local guides have become an essential part of the tourism economy that has slowly grown here since the Chapada dos Veadeiros National Park was established to the south.

## A Town of Modest Numbers

Cavalcante's economy remains small by any national measure. Cattle - about 60,777 head in 2006 - is the main agricultural activity. Corn and rice are the leading crops, planted on only 1,500 hectares of corn despite 331,175 hectares of total farm area. Most of that land is pasture or natural cerrado. One bank, Banco do Brasil, anchors the commercial center. The Human Development Index here sits at 0.609 - 241st out of 242 Goiás municipalities, reflecting the remoteness and the long historical marginalization of the region. Literacy in 2000 stood at 61.7 percent, one of the lowest rates in the state. Infant mortality was 30.02 per thousand. These are sobering numbers. They are also a reminder of how much of Brazil's interior still waits for investment and attention that coastal cities have long taken for granted.

## A Changing Economy

Three forces are slowly reshaping Cavalcante. Tourism has grown - visitors coming to hike the waterfalls and trails of the national park, staying in pousadas that didn't exist a generation ago. Hydroelectric power generation at nearby dams, including the Serra da Mesa artificial lake, has brought infrastructure and some employment. Mining continues in a smaller form. And over everything hangs the question of how to integrate Kalunga communities into the broader economic life of the state without eroding what makes their culture distinct. The tension is real: recognition has brought some support, but it has also brought pressure. For now, Cavalcante remains what it has been for two centuries - a remote place where several different Brazilian histories continue to unfold in parallel, shaped by the same cerrado landscape that first drew prospectors, then gave refuge to those who had run from them.
