# Gruta da Lagoa Azul State Park

A limestone chamber in Mato Grosso holds a pool of impossibly blue water — and has been locked shut for more than two decades because visitors loved it nearly to death.

**Location:** -14.5744, -55.9647

**Categories:** State parks of Brazil, Protected areas of Mato Grosso, Caves, Karst, Conservation

## Introduction

The most photographed room in Nobres has had almost no visitors since 2002. Behind a gate in the limestone country of central Mato Grosso lies a chamber where groundwater from the Saloba River pools into a lagoon so saturated with blue that photographs of it look retouched. Columns rise from floor to ceiling, some over five metres tall and a metre thick, built one mineral drip at a time across a span of years no human calendar covers. Brazil's environmental agency closed the cave because too many people had come to see it, and the damage they left behind could not be undone by asking them politely to stop.

## The Blue Room

Water does the work here. The Saloba River runs partly underground through this karst, and where it surfaces inside the cave it fills a pool that flooded even the main entrance — you enter, in effect, through the lagoon itself. The colour is not pigment but physics: light bending through water clear enough to see the bottom of, over pale limestone that throws it back. Around and above the pool hang the slow architecture of the place, stalactites reaching down, stalagmites reaching up, and in a few places the two joined into columns thick as a person. Archaeologists suspect there are remains in the cave worth studying properly. Nobody has yet had the chance to find out.

## Twelve Thousand Hectares of Cover

The cave is the headline, but the park around it is the reason the cave still exists. Gruta da Lagoa Azul State Park covers roughly 12,513 hectares of the municipality of Nobres, and it absorbed a much smaller predecessor — the 512-hectare Gruta Lagoa Azul Reserve — when the state created it in 1999. Above ground it is deciduous forest, the kind that drops its leaves through the dry months and stands grey and open until the rains bring it back. Howler monkeys announce dawn from the canopy with a sound that carries kilometres. Tapirs move through the understorey; jaguars hunt them; macaws cross overhead in pairs, loud and unmistakable. There are other limestone caves in the park too, less famous and less battered.

## Signatures on the Walls

What ruined the Lagoa Azul was not malice so much as volume and thoughtlessness. Formations were snapped off and carried away. Names were scratched into surfaces that took millennia to grow. Writing appeared on the walls. Local agriculture pressed in from outside. The people of Nobres knew exactly what was happening — the cave was both a source of pride and a source of income, and they could see it being worn down. When the question of closing it came up, residents largely backed the shutdown, choosing a cave they could not visit over a cave that would not survive being visited.

## Breath and Light

A cave is a machine that runs on stability. Temperature and humidity barely move across a year, and the organisms inside are calibrated to that stillness. Add people and you add carbon dioxide from their breathing and heat from their lamps, and both shift the chemistry: fungus and lichen take hold on speleothems that have never had to fight anything before, and the formations begin to degrade from the surface in. The speleological management plan drawn up for the site catalogued more than a dozen kinds of bacteria present in the cave environment, some of them a genuine hazard to human health. The risk, it turned out, ran in both directions.

## The Long Way Back

Reopening a cave takes longer than closing one. An ordinance on 30 May 1999 barred public visits until a management plan existed; a decree that December created the park, and a state law confirmed it in December 2000. The speleological management plan itself was not approved until 10 February 2014, and a consultative council followed on 15 December that year. Mato Grosso's environment authorities have since said the cave should reopen by the end of 2026, with infrastructure work and a concession to run access under strict limits. If it happens, it will be a cave visited on the cave's terms — small groups, controlled routes, and no more names on the walls.

## Flight Context

Located at 14.57°S, 55.96°W in the karst country of Nobres, central Mato Grosso, roughly 130 km north of Cuiabá. Best viewed from 3,000-5,000 ft AGL: the giveaway is the transition from open cerrado pasture into darker deciduous forest over pale limestone, cut by the clear-water courses of the Saloba drainage. Nearest major field is Cuiabá / Marechal Rondon International (SBCY, elevation 617 ft), about 40 minutes' flying to the south-southwest. Dry season (May-September) offers the most reliable visibility; the wet season brings afternoon convective buildups and haze from regional burning in August and September.
