# Goiás

The central Brazilian state whose name comes from a word meaning "people of the same origin" - a high, flat land of cerrado savanna, colonial gold towns, river beaches, and pequi fruit you cannot bite.

**Location:** -15.8833, -49.8333

**Categories:** states, brazil, goias, central-west, cerrado, travel

## Introduction

The word guaiá meant "the same person" or "people of the same origin" in the language of the people the Portuguese called Goyaz. That word became the name of a region, then a captaincy, then a state. Goiás today is the most central of Brazilian states and the most populous of the Central-West Region - a high, flat landscape of cerrado savanna broken by rivers, canyons, waterfalls, and two capitals. One is modern: Goiânia, the planned city founded in 1933. The other is colonial: the City of Goiás, Brazil's former state capital, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Between them lie the surprises of the Brazilian interior - hot springs, a national park on a plateau, river beaches that appear when drought lowers the water, and a fruit that can injure your mouth if you bite it wrong.

## The Plateau and Its Rivers

Goiás sits on the Brazilian Central Plateau, elevation mostly between 500 and 1,000 meters. The landscape is plateau country - flat-topped tabletop hills, broad valleys, cerrado savanna with its distinctive twisted trees adapted to fire and dry winters. Major rivers drain the state: the Araguaia runs along the western boundary with Mato Grosso; the Paranaíba and Tocantins system drain the east and north. From June to September, the drought season, the Araguaia's water level drops enough to expose almost two kilometers of river beaches - sand bars wide enough for hammocks, campfires, and the slow summer tourism of inland Brazilians who come for fishing and the particular peace of a river that has retreated from itself. The Chapada dos Veadeiros National Park covers a high plateau of canyons, valleys, rapids, and waterfalls - access is through Alto Paraíso de Goiás or the village of São Jorge.

## Two Capitals

Goiânia was drawn on paper in the 1930s and built as a state capital with concentric rings and art-deco buildings - a confident modernist experiment that grew past its plan. The City of Goiás, 148 kilometers to the northwest, is the opposite: a colonial gold-rush settlement from 1727 that UNESCO recognized in 2001 for its preserved 18th-century architecture. When the state government moved to Goiânia in 1937, the old capital shrunk and in shrinking, kept its stone churches, narrow streets, and the Procession of the Fogareu that runs at midnight on the Wednesday before Easter. Other notable towns include Pirenópolis, an old goldmining settlement a hundred kilometers from Brasília, and Caldas Novas, a hydrothermal resort whose hot springs attract Brazilian families on weekends. Anápolis sits between the two capitals as a commercial hub. Catalão is the industrial east.

## Eco-adventures at Altitude

Chapada dos Veadeiros is the state's great natural draw - a UNESCO-listed national park on a high quartzite plateau at around 1,200 meters elevation, with deep canyons, crystal-clear rivers, waterfalls dropping eighty or more meters, and trails leading to circular pools called cristalinas. The village of São Jorge, at one main entrance, has grown into a laid-back hub for trekkers. Aruanã sits on the Araguaia and draws fishermen and river-beach tourists; Niquelândia and Uruaçu give access to Lago da Serra da Mesa, a large reservoir with beaches and water sports. Hot springs run in Caldas Novas, Rio Quente, and Lagoa Santa. For something rarer, the state holds the access point to Bananal Island - the largest river island in the world, covering roughly 20,000 square kilometers in the Araguaia, with indigenous reserves and pantanal-like wetlands.

## The Fruit You Cannot Bite

Pequi is the fruit Goianos love most and outsiders learn to approach carefully. It has a yellow flesh with a distinctive smell - some say cheese, some say garbage, most say both - and it hides fine, sharp thorns just below the pulp. Bite it and the thorns lodge in your mouth and throat. The only safe way is to gnaw the flesh off without breaking the surface. Pequi has been a staple of Goiano cooking since the 18th century, used in rice - arroz com pequi - with chicken, with pasta, with fish, as a liqueur. The cerrado provides other fruits too: jabuticaba, guabiroba, jatobá, all with their own flavors and seasons. Pamonha, the green corn dough stuffed with cheese and wrapped in corn straw, is the region's signature street food, savory or sweet. Empadão goiano combines chicken, sausage, potato, cheese, and the bitter heart of palm called guariroba into a double-crust pie. Chambaril is a broth made from beef shin. The cuisine uses saffron and ginger generously.

## Sertanejo and Rock

Goiás is the cradle of sertanejo, the Brazilian country music genre closely tied to rural life. Two main types of sertanejo coexist: modão, the older traditional form, and sertanejo universitário, the modern radio style that dominates Brazilian pop charts. The state's music scene grew up connected to the countryside - cattle ranches, small farms, the long drives between villages on dirt roads. In Goiânia specifically, a rock scene emerged in the 1980s as a counter-identity to the dominant sertanejo style, and the city's nightlife became a test ground for bands that would eventually reach Brazilian national audiences. The electronic DJ Alok, one of the highest-earning DJs in the world, was born in Goiânia. So is the country music genre most Brazilians associate with dusty roads, cowboy hats, and the specific rural melancholy of the Central-West. Both sounds live in the same state. The festivals in Goiânia, the City of Goiás, and Pirenópolis draw large crowds every year.

## Flight Context

Goiás state covers roughly 340,000 km² of central Brazil, centered near 15.88°S, 49.83°W. The largest airport is Santa Genoveva International (SBGO) in Goiânia with domestic and international flights. Nelson Ribeiro Guimarães Airport (SWKN) in Caldas Novas handles local flights. Brasília International (SBBR) is close to the eastern border and often a practical alternative. Cruise at 5,000-7,000 feet to see the characteristic cerrado plateau - high flat country between 500-1,200 m elevation, broken by the gorges of the Chapada dos Veadeiros (around 1,200 m elevation to the northeast) and the broad Araguaia valley to the west. Look for the planned concentric streets of Goiânia and the colonial grid of Goiás city 148 km northwest. Climate is tropical wet/dry with sharp seasonal contrast - wet Oct-Apr, dry May-Sep. Road network is extensive with more than 92% paved.
