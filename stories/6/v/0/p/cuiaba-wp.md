# Cuiaba

A state capital founded in 1719 after bandeirantes found gold, whose name means 'arrow-fishing' in Bororo and whose obelisk once marked the geographical center of South America.

**Location:** -15.5958, -56.0969

**Categories:** cities-brazil, state-capitals, mato-grosso, colonial-history

## Introduction

The name comes from an indigenous Bororo word meaning arrow-fishing, a technique in which fishermen shot arrows through the shallow water to spear fish they could see but not reach. The Bororo had been doing it for generations on the river when bandeirantes from Sao Paulo arrived in 1718, lost a battle with the Coxipone, regrouped, and promptly found gold. By 1719 the rush was on. Cuiaba was founded to extract that gold using enslaved indigenous labor, and when the gold gave out, the settlement that remained eventually became the capital of Mato Grosso, an agribusiness hub at the geographical center of South America, and the southern gate to the Amazon.

## Bandeirantes and Gold

The first Portuguese to arrive in this region were not settlers but slavers. Bandeirantes, the expedition leaders from Sao Paulo, drove west through the Brazilian interior hunting indigenous people for forced labor and looking for anything of value. Manoel de Campos Bicudo visited Cuiaba in 1673 and 1682 and founded an early village where the Coxipo River meets the Cuiaba. In 1718, Pascoal Moreira Cabral arrived and attacked the Coxipone seeking captives; the Coxipone defeated him. Retreating down the river, his party found gold. Cabral sent word to the Captaincy of Sao Paulo on April 8, 1719, a letter that triggered a gold rush. Prospectors poured in, more indigenous people were enslaved for mining work, and Cabral positioned himself as guarda-mor regente, managing disputes among the new arrivals. The violence that founded the city is not decorative history. It is the core of how Cuiaba came to exist as a Portuguese settlement.

## The Searing Heat

Cuiaba is famous throughout Brazil for being one of the country's hottest cities. Summer temperatures routinely exceed 40 degrees Celsius, and the Chapada dos Guimaraes mountain range blocks polar masses coming from the south, holding the heat in place. Average annual rainfall is 1,351 millimeters, concentrated from October to April, and the dry season brings humidity below 15 percent at times, sparking respiratory problems and fire risk. July is the coolest month, with an average low of 16.6 degrees, and occasional cold fronts can drop temperatures to 10 degrees for a day or two before the heat reasserts itself. The climate is a daily fact of life here, shaping when people work, when they walk outside, when they drink mate cocido, the chilled yerba mate that is the traditional defense against the furnace afternoons.

## The Center That Moved

In the middle of Cuiaba stands an obelisk that for most of the twentieth century marked the exact geographical center of the South American continent, as calculated in 1909. Generations of schoolchildren came on field trips to see it. Then, in the 1990s, more accurate measurements relocated the continental center 45 kilometers northeast of the city, to a spot near Chapada dos Guimaraes. The obelisk still stands, a monument to a claim that science eventually revised. Cuiaba sits in a transition zone between three of Brazil's great biomes, the Amazon to the north, the Cerrado across the plateaus, and the Pantanal wetlands to the south, which gives the city its title as Southern Gate to the Amazon. From Cuiaba, the Interoceanic Highway runs west to the Pacific Ocean, and the BR-364 runs east to the Atlantic, making the city a crossroads even if not quite the center it once claimed to be.

## Arena Pantanal

In 2010, Cuiaba demolished its old Estadio Jose Fragelli and began building a new one, the Arena Pantanal, to host matches for the 2014 FIFA World Cup. The stadium, completed in April 2014 at a capacity of 42,788, staged four group matches and has since become home to Mixto EC and Cuiaba EC, the local clubs. The architects GCP Arquitetos emphasized sustainability, and the corner zones are planted with trees and vegetation, visible green patches in the concrete bowl. A parallel story unfolded beside it. A 22-kilometer light rail line, intended to connect Cuiaba with Varzea Grande and the international airport before the World Cup, was launched, stalled, resumed, and finally abandoned as one of the most notorious corruption scandals of the Brazilian hosting effort. Sections of track were built near the airport but never used. No trains ever ran. The light rail stands today as a monument to unspent billions, the reverse image of the successful stadium nearby.

## Between Three Worlds

Cuiaba's cultural life draws from Portuguese, African, and indigenous roots, and the museums around Centro catalog that mix: the Museum of Sacred Art, the SESC Arsenal, the Museum of the Image and Sound of Cuiaba. The patron saint is Saint Benedict, a Black saint whose processions during the year bring out drums and singing that reach back through centuries. Saint Benedict being the patron reflects the African presence that has been part of Cuiaba since the gold-rush days, when the enslaved labor force that extracted the wealth included both indigenous and African people. The city's off-season Carnaval, called micarecuia, fills the streets with blocos and music that would not sound the same anywhere else. This is a city formed by the collision of three worlds in the middle of a continent, heat beating down on all of it, the Pantanal starting an hour's drive south.

## Flight Context

Cuiaba lies at 15.60 S, 56.10 W, near the geographical center of South America. Marechal Rondon International Airport (SBCY) is 10 km from downtown. Cruise at 3,500 to 5,500 feet to observe the city laid out at the base of the Chapada dos Guimaraes highlands, with the Pantanal wetlands stretching south. Summer temperatures routinely exceed 40C and dry-season humidity can drop below 15 percent.
