# Araguaia State Park

A quarter-million hectares of Mato Grosso that becomes a shallow inland sea every year, holding the seam where the cerrado gives way to the Amazon.

**Location:** -12.3500, -50.8100

**Categories:** State parks of Brazil, Protected areas of Mato Grosso, Cerrado, Wetlands, Amazon rainforest, Wildlife

## Introduction

Once a year this park stops being land. The rains come, the Araguaia and the Rio das Mortes rise past their banks, and 223,169 hectares of savanna and forest go under shallow water that stands for months. Marsh deer wade where cattle would drown. River dolphins push into flooded woodland that was dry ground in August. Then the water pulls back, the grass returns, and the whole cycle resets. Araguaia State Park protects one of the last large stretches of eastern Mato Grosso where that rhythm still runs uninterrupted.

## The Seam Between Two Worlds

Biomes do not stop at a line; they overlap, and the overlap is usually the richest ground. This park sits in the transition between the Amazon rainforest and the cerrado, Brazil's vast tropical savanna, and it collects species from both. Robust capuchins and howler monkeys work the forest canopy — Amazonian animals, at the southern edge of their range. Out on the open savanna the maned wolf hunts, long-legged and red as rust, and pampas deer graze. In the wetlands there are marsh deer and capybara. The rivers hold Amazon river dolphins, giant otters and turtles. Few places let you see a wolf of the dry grasslands and a dolphin of the flooded forest in the same landscape.

## Between the Araguaia and the River of Deaths

The park lies in the municipality of Novo Santo Antônio, hard against the borders of São Félix do Araguaia, Ribeirão Cascalheira and the state of Tocantins. It occupies the wedge between the Araguaia and its tributary the Rio das Mortes — the River of Deaths, a name given by colonists after the violence that played out along its banks between bandeirante expeditions, prospectors and the Indigenous peoples whose land it was. The Xavante know that river by their own name for it. Araguaia State Park forms the southernmost link in a chain of protected areas running up the right bank of the Araguaia, which means it functions less as an island than as a doorway: fauna can move north through connected habitat rather than dead-ending against fence line and soy.

## Which Land Gets Saved

Protection in Mato Grosso has never been evenly distributed, and the numbers say why. Since the 1970s, driven by federal and state tax incentives, enormous areas of Amazon forest and cerrado in this state have been converted to large-scale ranching and cropping. What survived was largely what nobody could farm easily — remote, inaccessible, or already flooded. The arithmetic is stark: roughly 83.77 percent of permanently flooded land in the region is protected, against just 3.87 percent of open treed savanna. Conservation here has often followed the path of least economic resistance. A park like this one, sitting on annually inundated ground, exists in part because the water made the argument for it.

## Paper and Promises

Mato Grosso created the park by state law 7.517 on 28 September 2001, setting aside around 230,000 hectares to protect water resources, keep corridors open for native fauna, preserve a representative sample of the region's ecosystems, and allow controlled public use, education and research. It was classed as an IUCN category II protected area — a national park in all but name — and handed to the State Environment Foundation, FEMA, with a five-year deadline to produce a management plan. That deadline slipped. The park's boundaries were altered by law 8.458 in January 2006; the management plan appeared in 2007 and was finally approved in December 2008, seven years after the park was declared.

## The Exchange That Was Refused

In June 2011 the state government published a law proposing an unusual swap. It would have moved the Xavante of the Marãiwatsédé Indigenous Territory into the state park, and in exchange regularised the holdings of the squatters occupying their land. The Xavante had been forced off Marãiwatsédé in 1966, when 233 people were flown hundreds of kilometres south to a mission; measles swept through them almost immediately, and within two weeks the group had been cut to around 160. They spent nearly half a century trying to get home. Funai, the federal Indigenous affairs agency, rejected the exchange outright — it had no interest in the trade, and Indigenous territory in Brazil is constitutionally inalienable and cannot be swapped away. In December 2012, following a Supreme Court ruling that October, courts ordered the illegal occupants removed and the Xavante returned to Marãiwatsédé. The park stayed a park, and the land stayed theirs.

## Flight Context

Located at 12.35°S, 50.81°W in far eastern Mato Grosso, in the wedge between the Rio Araguaia and the Rio das Mortes, on the Tocantins state line. From the air the park is easiest to identify by hydrology rather than boundary: braided channels, oxbow lakes and — in the wet season, roughly November through April — sheet water standing across gallery forest and savanna. Best viewing is 2,000-4,000 ft AGL along the Araguaia. Nearest field is São Félix do Araguaia (SWFX / SXO), elevation 653 ft, single 1,450 m asphalt runway 12/30, about 80 km north; no scheduled service. Nearest major alternates are Palmas (SBPJ) to the northeast and Cuiabá / Marechal Rondon (SBCY) far to the west. Dry season (May-September) gives clear air but heavy smoke haze is common in August and September.
