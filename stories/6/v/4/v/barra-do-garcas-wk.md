# Barra do Garças

At the confluence of the Garças and the Araguaia, beneath a mountain range named for the noise it makes, a Brazilian river town built a landing pad for flying saucers — and waits.

**Location:** -15.8900, -52.2569

**Categories:** Cities in Mato Grosso, Araguaia River, Cerrado, Folklore and legend, Outdoor recreation

## Introduction

The mountain is named for a sound. When the wind funnels through the canyons of the Serra do Roncador, it produces a low roar that gave the range its name — roncador, the snorer. Barra do Garças sits at its foot, on the Mato Grosso side of the state line, at the point where the Garças river empties into the Araguaia. Nearly seventy thousand people live here. There is a Christ the Redeemer statue on a hill above town, which is why locals call the place the Rio de Janeiro of the West, and there is a concrete pad on the outskirts built to receive spacecraft, which is why a rather different set of visitors comes.

## Where Two Rivers Meet

Barra means bar or river mouth, and the name is literal: this is the place where the Garças joins the Araguaia, one of the great rivers of central Brazil. The Araguaia here is wide, shallow and sandy, and in the dry season it retreats to expose freshwater beaches that draw Brazilian families the way an ocean coast would. The town was founded on 13 June 1924 and became a municipality of its own on 15 September 1948. It now ranks among the largest cities in Mato Grosso, sitting at the midpoint of the BR-070, the highway that runs from Cuiabá to Brasília — roughly 470 road kilometres to the state capital in one direction, 530 to the federal capital in the other. That central position on the map is more than a boast: the Serra Azul escarpment just outside town holds the marker for the geodetic centre of Brazil.

## The Mountain That Roars

The Serra do Roncador is a long escarpment of cerrado-covered sandstone, and it dominates the country north and east of town. Part of it is protected as Serra Azul State Park, 11,002 hectares of savanna, waterfalls and cliff edges that have become a favourite launch site for paragliders — the thermals off the rock face in the dry season are reliable enough to hold a wing up for hours. Hot springs bubble out of the ground nearby, and water parks have grown up around them. On a clear morning from the top of the escarpment you can see the two rivers converge and the town spread out in the fork between them.

## A Landing Pad and No Landings

In the 1990s the municipality poured concrete for a discoporto — a spaceport for flying saucers. Nothing has ever landed on it. That has not slowed the phenomenon it was built to serve: Barra do Garças has become one of Brazil's centres of ufological tourism, drawing people who believe the Roncador is a place of unusual activity. Residents report strange lights over the escarpment. Visitors arrive convinced that the range hides crystal skulls, or a subterranean city, or a portal that opens onto Machu Picchu and links this place to the origins of the Inca. None of that is established fact, and the town does not particularly need it to be. What is documented is the belief itself, and the steady stream of seekers it brings up the BR-070 — a Brazilian Roswell, complete with its own souvenirs, its own local celebrities, and its own patient concrete circle waiting for a guest.

## The Man Who Walked Into the Green

Some of the mystique predates the flying saucers by decades. In 1925 the British explorer Percy Fawcett went into the Mato Grosso backlands looking for a ruined city he called Z, taking his eldest son and his son's friend with him. They were never seen again, and the Serra do Roncador is among the regions long associated with their disappearance. What actually happened to the three men has never been established — a century of expeditions, rumours and rival theories has produced no body and no ruin. The unresolved ending is exactly the kind of thing a landscape like this holds onto. Fawcett's lost city and the Roncador's lights are separate stories, but they feed the same appetite: the sense that this country has not finished being explained.

## River Town, Working Week

Strip away the mystery and Barra do Garças is a busy regional hub. Cattle and grain move through it. The BR-070 keeps trucks rolling past at all hours. A small airport handles light aircraft, with most traffic arriving from Cuiabá. Kids swim off the sandbars in the dry months. The city is close enough to Goiânia, Brasília and Campo Grande to serve as a natural stop on a long crossing of the interior, and far enough from all of them to feel like the frontier it recently was. The Christ statue looks out over both — the rivers and the ordinary town — from a hill that, on some nights, people insist is not the only thing watching.

## Flight Context

Located at 15.89°S, 52.26°W, at the confluence of the Rio das Garças and the Rio Araguaia on the Mato Grosso / Goiás border. Barra do Garças Airport (SBBW / BPG) sits at 1,158 ft with a single 1,598 m asphalt runway, 07/25 — adequate for light and business aircraft, no scheduled jet service. The Serra do Roncador escarpment north and east of the field generates strong afternoon thermals and mechanical turbulence in the dry season; the same lift is what makes the ridge a paragliding site. Best sightseeing altitude is 2,500-4,000 ft AGL following the Araguaia. Alternates: Cuiabá / Marechal Rondon (SBCY) about 470 km west, Goiânia / Santa Genoveva (SBGO) about 390 km east. May-September is dry and clear; August-September brings smoke haze from regional burning.
