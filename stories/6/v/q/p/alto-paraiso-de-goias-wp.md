# Alto Paraíso de Goiás

A municipality founded as a 1750s cattle ranch in cerrado highlands, now an ecotourism hub for Chapada dos Veadeiros and home to a rural Esperanto school.

**Location:** -14.1328, -47.5100

**Categories:** brazil, goias, municipality, cerrado, ecotourism, chapada-dos-veadeiros

## Introduction

An internationally supported Esperanto organization runs a rural school here where the teachers greet students in Esperanto. That alone would make Alto Paraíso de Goiás strange, but the school called Bona Espero - Good Hope, in the invented language Zamenhof built to unite humanity - is only one layer of the peculiarity stacked on this cerrado highland town. Twenty-two hundred kilometers from the coast, 1,200 meters above sea level, the municipality covers a patch of Brazilian Highlands that geologists call Chapada dos Veadeiros. Francisco de Almeida started a cattle ranch here in the 18th century, on land cold enough in winter that he could grow wheat alongside the cattle - a rarity in Brazil - and called the region Veadeiros for the deer that ran through it.

## The Long Road to Municipality

Alto Paraíso's legal identity came in stages, each one pulling it further out from under the shadow of its larger neighbors. The settlement appeared as a district of Cavalcante in 1938, carrying the name Veadeiros. In 1953 it broke off to form its own municipality. In 1963 it took its current name, Portuguese for High Paradise of Goiás - a rebrand with a direct sales pitch embedded in it. The municipality stretches across 2,593 square kilometers of savanna and escarpment, thin on people at 2.56 inhabitants per square kilometer. The urban population in 2007 sat at 4,585, with another 2,043 in the surrounding countryside. Growth has been modest by Brazilian standards - around 1.87 percent per year - but tourism has steadily pulled the local economy away from its farming roots.

## The Cerrado at Elevation

This is the Brazilian Highlands at its most dramatic, a patch of the central plateau where rivers cut down into red sandstone and quartzite canyons. The local economy still rests partly on soybeans - 3,800 hectares planted - and corn, with a cattle herd that hit 30,500 head in 2006. But the agricultural data tells only part of the story. Since 2001, the entire municipality has sat inside the Pouso Alto Environmental Protection Area (APA), a designation that restricts development and pushes economic activity toward ecotourism. Chapada dos Veadeiros National Park lies just outside town, accessible through the village of São Jorge 35 kilometers to the west. Between them they draw tens of thousands of visitors each year, filling inns, booking guides, buying the crystal souvenirs the region has become known for.

## The Village and the Park

São Jorge is technically still inside the municipal boundary of Alto Paraíso, but it might as well be a different country. The village sits at the park entrance, with dirt roads and minimal infrastructure - a deliberate choice by locals who want it to stay rustic. During holiday seasons it fills with university students looking for waterfalls and noisy parties. The city of Alto Paraíso itself, by contrast, has hotels, ATMs, drugstores, and restaurants lined along Avenida Ary Valadão Filho. The Maytreia Garden, the Canyon of Rio Preto, the falls at Almécegas and Cristais and Santa Bárbara and Anjos e Arcanjos - they all lie within day-trip range. Balloon rides launch from the grasslands near the small local airport. Gastronomic festivals cycle through the calendar, drawing weekenders from Brasília.

## Bona Espero

An international network of Esperantists runs one of the most curious schools in rural Brazil here. Bona Espero serves children from poor families in the surrounding region, and the instruction emphasizes Esperanto - the constructed international language invented by L. L. Zamenhof in 1887 to ease communication across language barriers. The school fits the municipality's broader character in an odd way. Alto Paraíso attracts unusual projects. Spiritual communities, health retreats, Esperantists, crystal healers, balloon pilots, waterfall guides - the mix reflects a town that has consciously defined itself as a place where atypical things happen, often in languages and frequencies outside the Brazilian mainstream. Nine schools served 2,636 students in 2006. Bona Espero is only one of them, but it is the one that keeps showing up in European Esperanto newsletters.

## The Seasons of the Cerrado

Anyone planning a visit has to respect the calendar. The cerrado dry season runs May through September, and that is the window when the landscape is most photogenic and the trails most navigable. Rain comes hard between October and March, turning dirt roads into clay traps and pushing flash floods down river systems like the Rio São Miguel. The best waterfalls - Vale da Lua, the Couros cataracts - sometimes become dangerous during the wet. Local tourist information centers, the CAT, keep current conditions posted. Cyclists are increasingly common on the GO-239 toward São Jorge, the only regional highway with a proper bike lane. Most visitors arrive by car from Brasília, 220 kilometers away, or by bus from the federal capital's interstate station. The runway at the local airport handles only small private aircraft.

## Flight Context
