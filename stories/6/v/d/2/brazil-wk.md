# Brazil

A continent-sized country of rainforests, beaches, favelas, and colonial towns - the only place in the Americas where Portuguese is the official language.

**Location:** -14.0000, -53.0000

**Categories:** Countries, South America

## Introduction

Flying across Brazil, you cross time zones without crossing borders. Roraima ticks on Brasília-time-minus-one while Acre sits two hours behind the capital. Fernando de Noronha, a volcanic outcrop in the Atlantic, runs an hour ahead. This is a country that spans the equator and the Tropic of Capricorn, touches every South American nation except Chile and Ecuador, and contains within its borders about 60 percent of the Amazon, the world's largest wetland, 7,491 kilometers of Atlantic coastline, and the largest population of Portuguese speakers on Earth. Brazil is not one place. It is many, held together by language, football, and the long memory of the Atlantic.

## The Five Brazils

Geographers divide Brazil into five regions, and the divisions are more than administrative. The Southeast is where São Paulo's 21 million people stack skyscrapers into the horizon and Rio de Janeiro tucks beaches between granite peaks. The South feels more like Argentina - cooler, more European, sometimes snowy in the highlands above 1,000 meters. The Northeast holds Salvador, the first capital, where Yoruba gods survive inside the shells of Catholic saints. The Center-West contains Brasília and the cerrado, a vast tropical savanna half the size of the Amazon itself. The North is the Amazon - Manaus and Belém, river-boats the size of hotels, a rainforest that remains the single most biodiverse place on the planet.

## A Continent of Climates

The Amazon receives rain almost without end, the wettest months stretching from November to March. The coastal Northeast has frequent winter rains. The interior Northeast runs semi-arid, with droughts that have killed hundreds of thousands across Brazilian history. The Center-West drops below 30 percent humidity in the dry season, and the savanna browns and burns. The Southeast coast is warm and humid year-round. The South has actual winter: frost, sometimes snow on the higher slopes of Santa Catarina and Rio Grande do Sul. A country this size is never one climate. Packing for a trip between regions is often packing for two seasons in one suitcase.

## Cities That Each Feel Like a Country

São Paulo is the big one: the largest, richest, and most cosmopolitan city in South America, shaped by immigration from Italy, Japan, Korea, Germany, Lebanon. Rio de Janeiro trades off its physical setting - Sugarloaf, Corcovado, the long curve of Copacabana - for a reputation earned as the former imperial capital. Salvador, Brazil's first capital before Rio, still holds the densest African cultural presence in the Americas outside Haiti. Recife spreads across islands linked by bridges like a tropical Venice. Manaus sits in the middle of the rainforest, 1,600 kilometers up the Amazon from the Atlantic, yet once so rich from the rubber boom that Europeans shipped it an opera house. Brasília, built from nothing in four years, is the country in its modernist moment. Florianópolis is beaches and forests on an island in the southern Atlantic.

## The Wild Places

Beyond the cities, Brazil's nature is what most visitors come for. The Pantanal - the world's largest wetland, straddling the Center-West - teems with jaguars, caimans, anacondas, giant otters, and more bird species than all of Europe. The Chapada dos Veadeiros, a plateau north of Brasília, holds waterfalls and cerrado species found nowhere else. Fernando de Noronha, a marine national park and UNESCO site in the Atlantic, is so protected that visitor numbers are capped by law. Lençóis Maranhenses is a landscape of bright white sand dunes that fills with fresh-water lagoons during the rainy season, a place that looks like nowhere else on Earth. Iguaçu Falls at the Argentine border drops across 275 individual cascades over nearly three kilometers of river, louder and wider than Niagara.

## Getting Around

Brazil is too big for anything but flying between regions. Air travel grew from 70 million passengers in 2009 to nearly 120 million in 2019, and the country's 2,500 airports form the second-largest network in the world after the United States. The train network, once among the largest in the tropics, has been mostly dismantled since 1945 - a legacy of Brazilian transport policy that favored roads. Long-distance buses remain the workhorse for most Brazilians, with some routes taking more than a day to complete. First-class overnight sleeper buses (leito) offer reclining seats and bathrooms. The road network, at 1.72 million kilometers, is the largest in Latin America, though quality varies wildly - highways in the South are often excellent, while Amazonian roads become impassable during the wet season.

## The Language That Binds

Brazilian Portuguese is not Portuguese Portuguese. Brazilians drop words that Europeans keep, soften consonants, open vowels, and speak with a rhythm shaped by African and indigenous influence. It is the only country in the Americas with Portuguese as an official language, surrounded by Spanish-speaking neighbors who can often understand Brazilians but are rarely understood back. Indigenous words saturate the vocabulary - the pau-brasil tree that gave the country its name, the names of foods (tapioca, mandioca, acai), rivers, states (Paraná, Paraíba, Piauí). Over a hundred indigenous languages are still spoken, mostly in the Amazon, and dozens are endangered. English is not widely spoken outside tourist zones. Learning a hundred Portuguese words before a trip transforms the experience.

## Flight Context

Approximate center: -14°S, -53°W. Brazil spans four time zones (UTC-2 to UTC-5) and over 7,000km of coastline. Major international gateways are São Paulo-Guarulhos (SBGR) and Rio de Janeiro-Galeão (SBGL). Other important airports include Brasília (SBBR), Salvador (SBSV), Recife (SBRF), Manaus (SBEG), and Belém (SBBE). The Amazon dominates the north, the cerrado and Pantanal the center-west, and populated urban corridors hug the Atlantic coast.
