# Brazil

A country named for a red-dyewood tree, home to more than 213 million people and the planet's largest rainforest.

**Location:** -14.0000, -53.0000

**Categories:** Countries, South America, Federal republics

## Introduction

The country is named after a tree. When Portuguese ships reached the coast in 1500, they found forests thick with pau-brasil - a hardwood whose inner bark produced a deep red dye prized by European textile makers. The Portuguese called the land Terra da Santa Cruz, the Land of the Holy Cross, but the sailors and merchants who worked the trade simply called it the Land of Brazilwood. Within a century, the commercial name had eclipsed the religious one on official maps. A country named not for a saint or a king or a geographic feature, but for a tree European weavers needed to turn cloth red.

## Before 1500

Long before Pedro Álvares Cabral's ships dropped anchor in April 1500, people lived here. Luzia Woman, one of the oldest human remains in the Americas, was found near Pedro Leopoldo, Minas Gerais, dating back at least 11,000 years. The earliest pottery yet discovered in the Western Hemisphere came out of the Amazon near Santarém, radiocarbon-dated to more than 8,000 years ago. The Marajoara culture flourished on Marajó Island in the Amazon delta from AD 400 to 1400, developing social stratification, mound building, and sophisticated ceramic traditions. When the Portuguese arrived, an estimated seven million indigenous people lived across what would become Brazil - Tupi, Guarani, Gê, and Arawak among them. Most spoke languages of the Tupi-Guarani family. Most lived by hunting, fishing, gathering, and migrant agriculture.

## The Slave Ships and the Sugar

By the mid-16th century, sugar had become the colony's largest export, and sugar needed labor. Portugal responded by importing enslaved Africans - nearly five million between 1500 and the 1850s, more than any other country in the Atlantic slave trade. This was a generational catastrophe whose scale is difficult to grasp. Enslaved people arrived from Angola, Mozambique, and the Bight of Benin, and their labor built the sugar economy, the gold rush of the 1690s, and the coffee plantations of the 19th century. Slavery in Brazil lasted until 1888, longer than anywhere else in the Americas. The Golden Law that abolished it, signed by Princess Isabel while her father was abroad, ended the institution but left approximately 700,000 formerly enslaved people without land, compensation, or protection. Brazil's racial composition today - 45.3 percent pardo or mixed, 43.5 percent white, 10.2 percent Black, 0.6 percent indigenous according to the 2022 census - is the demographic signature of that history.

## A Peculiar Independence

Brazil's independence did not follow the Spanish American pattern. When Napoleon invaded Portugal in 1808, the entire Portuguese royal court fled to Rio de Janeiro, making Brazil the only European colony that ever hosted its own empire's capital. King John VI returned to Lisbon in 1821, leaving his son Pedro as regent. When the Cortes in Lisbon tried to pull Brazil back into colonial subordination, Pedro sided with Brazilian elites instead. On 7 September 1822, on the banks of the Ipiranga stream outside São Paulo, he proclaimed independence. He became Emperor Pedro I of Brazil - a Portuguese prince ruling an independent country that was still a monarchy. For 67 years Brazil remained an empire, first under Pedro I, then under his son Pedro II, whose long and relatively tolerant reign ended only when the military overthrew him in 1889.

## Coups, Republics, and Recovery

The 20th century brought several republics and several ruptures. Getúlio Vargas took power in a 1930 revolution, ruled as president and then dictator through the Estado Novo, was ousted in 1945, and returned via election before taking his own life in 1954. Juscelino Kubitschek built Brasília in four years. A 1964 coup installed a military dictatorship backed by the United States that ruled until 1985, imprisoning and killing thousands of opponents through Operation Condor. Democracy returned through gradual opening and the 1988 constitution. Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, a former metalworker, was elected president in 2002, again in 2006, and after a prison sentence later overturned, returned to win a third term in 2022. The week after his inauguration, supporters of outgoing president Jair Bolsonaro stormed the Congress, Supreme Court, and Planalto Palace. Bolsonaro was charged with attempted self-coup in February 2025.

## The Megadiverse Country

Brazil ranks first among the 17 megadiverse countries. It holds most of the Amazon basin - home to the world's largest river system and the largest remaining tropical forest. More than 70 percent of the world's catalogued animal and plant species occur here: 55,000 plants, 3,000 freshwater fish, over 689 mammals, 1,832 birds, 744 reptiles. The Amazon rainforest, the Cerrado savanna, the Atlantic Forest, the Pantanal wetlands, the Araucaria forests of the south - each is its own world. Since 1970, more than 600,000 square kilometers of Amazon rainforest have been cleared. Deforestation threatens not just local species but global climate. In 2024, a drought called the strongest in Brazilian records covered nearly 60 percent of the country's territory. What Brazil does with its remaining forest is one of the planet's defining questions.
