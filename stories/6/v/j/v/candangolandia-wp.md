# Candangolândia

The first camp built for the workers who raised Brasília, and the neighbourhood that kept their name after the city they made had moved on without them.

**Location:** -15.8544, -47.9500

**Categories:** Administrative regions in the Federal District (Brazil), History, Labor history, Brasília

## Introduction

Before it was Candangolândia, it was Lonalândia — the land of tarpaulin. The first shelters went up here in 1956 out of canvas and burlap sacking, which earned the place a second nickname, Sacolândia, the land of sacks. Novacap, the state company charged with conjuring a capital out of empty savanna, sited its first official construction camp on this red slope between the Plano Piloto site and the road south. Everywhere else in the Federal District was drawn on paper and then built. This place was built first, and named later, by the people living in it.

## Land of Tarpaulin

Novacap cut three wide avenues and a set of cross streets into the ground in late 1956 and put up roughly five hundred houses along them. Canvas gave way to timber quickly, because the camp was meant to run a construction project the size of a country. It held the company's headquarters, housing for technical and administrative staff, a strongroom for the payroll, a health post, a hospital, a police station, two restaurants, and a school for the residents' children. The founding date on the paperwork is 3 November 1956. A short walk away, another settlement was growing with a different job to do: Cidade Livre, later Núcleo Bandeirante, where merchants were allowed to trade tax-free and where the bars, shops and dance halls ended up. One camp administered the work. The other sold to the people doing it.

## The Word They Were Given

Candango was not a compliment. The term came into Portuguese from an African language and carried the sense of coarse, ordinary, no good; by one account it was what enslaved Africans called their Portuguese overlords, and it later attached itself to itinerant labourers with no property and no schooling. More than sixty thousand such workers came to the Planalto Central between 1956 and 1960, from Minas Gerais, from Goiás, and above all from the drought-worn Northeast, where the alternative to leaving was often nothing at all. They came for wages and for the promise that the country was building something. Somewhere in those four years the insult turned over. Government publicity cast the candangos as the men and women making a nation's future, and they took the word for themselves. Today anyone born in Brasília may claim it.

## What the Work Cost

Juscelino Kubitschek had promised a finished capital by 21 April 1960, and the camps ran day and night to deliver it. They were company towns in the plainest sense — housing, canteens and policing all supplied by the same authority that issued the wages, with the Guarda Especial de Brasília keeping order. On the night of 8 February 1959, at the Pacheco Fernandes Dantas camp in what is now Vila Planalto, a dispute that began with workers complaining about spoiled food ended with armed guards firing into the barracks. The police inquiry recorded one man dead and others wounded. Survivors described far worse, and no bodies were ever produced to settle it. Sixty-odd years on, the Massacre da Pacheco Fernandes remains unresolved — an argument about how many people died building a capital, conducted over the heads of the people who were there.

## Velhacap

In 1959 Novacap packed up and moved into the Plano Piloto it had been assembling, and the camp it left behind acquired a new nickname: Velhacap, the old capital. What stayed behind was a functioning neighbourhood with services most of the region still lacked. Its hospital, the Hospital Juscelino Kubitschek de Oliveira, had opened on 6 July 1957 as the first hospital in the new Federal District — twenty-three wooden buildings that treated construction injuries, delivered babies and closed in 1968. The complex was placed under heritage protection in 1985 and reopened on 26 April 1990 as the Museu Vivo da Memória Candanga, a museum of the construction years housed in the wards where the construction years were bandaged.

## A Region of Its Own

Officially, Candangolândia spent nearly four decades as an appendage of Núcleo Bandeirante. Law 658 of 27 January 1994 finally made it Administrative Region XIX, with its own regional administrator responsible for water, power, sanitation and roads. Around sixteen thousand people live there now, on a wedge of land bordered by Brasília to the north, Lago Sul to the east, Park Way and Núcleo Bandeirante to the south and Guará to the west, with streams running down toward Lake Paranoá and the city zoo at its northern edge. Wooden houses from the construction era still stand among the newer brick. The neighbourhood is a reminder of an arithmetic that shaped the whole Federal District: most of the people who built the Plano Piloto never lived in it. They settled the towns around it instead, and Candangolândia was the first.

## Flight Context

Candangolândia sits at 15.854°S, 47.950°W, roughly 1,050 m above sea level on the Planalto Central. From 3,000–5,000 ft AGL the neighbourhood reads as a compact grid of low houses under heavy tree cover immediately southwest of the Plano Piloto, wedged between the BR-040 corridor and the southern arm of Lake Paranoá. Brasília–Presidente Juscelino Kubitschek International (SBBR) lies about 8 km east-northeast, and the Esplanada dos Ministérios and the Monumental Axis give an unmistakable visual reference to the north. The dry season from May to September delivers reliable visibility, though smoke haze thickens in August; the November–January wet season brings strong afternoon convection over the plateau.
