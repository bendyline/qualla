# Pirenópolis

The Greek-named gold town that homesick Catalans christened after the Pyrenees, where Charlemagne's knights still ride every Pentecost.

**Location:** -15.8526, -48.9589

**Categories:** Municipalities in Goiás, Historic towns of Brazil, Brazilian colonial architecture

## Introduction

The name is a small act of homesickness. Pirenópolis - from the Greek, "city of the Pireneus" - refers to the mountain range that cradles the town. And those mountains got their name, according to local tradition, from Spanish immigrants, probably Catalans, who looked at the quartzite ridges rising out of the Brazilian cerrado and thought of the Pyrenees they had left behind in Europe. Never mind that the two ranges share almost nothing geologically. The Catalans were looking at stone and remembering home. The name stuck.

## Meia Ponte

The town was founded in 1727 with a more practical name: Minas de Nossa Senhora do Rosário de Meia Ponte. Meia Ponte meant "half a bridge" - because the bridge over the Almas River had been half-washed-away by a flood. Portuguese colonizers arrived first, chasing gold that was easily panned from the Rio das Almas. From 1750 to 1800, the town entered its golden age. Four churches went up. Pirenópolis competed with Vila Boa - today's Cidade de Goiás - for the title of richest town in the province. By 1830 the settlement was publishing the first newspaper in Goiás, the Matutina Meiapontense, printed by Joaquim Alves de Oliveira. Then the gold ran out. The commercial routes shifted north to Anápolis, and Meia Ponte became economically invisible. In 1890 the town renamed itself Pirenópolis, swapping a practical Portuguese name for a romantic Greek one - a change that foreshadowed, in its way, what the town would eventually sell: its own history.

## The Festa do Divino

The Cavalhadas arrived in Pirenópolis in 1826, carried there by Portuguese settlers as part of the Festa do Divino Espírito Santo - the Festival of the Holy Spirit, observed 50 days after Easter on Pentecost. The ritual reenacts, loosely, a battle from the era of Charlemagne: Christian knights versus Moorish knights, fought on horseback through the streets of the town and climaxing in a mock combat in the city's bullring. The riders wear papier-mâché helmets and medieval costume - blue for the Christians, red for the Moors. The battle lasts three days. A bugle fanfare opens the parade. The knight's pages ride first, then the mounted warriors displaying their colors. The outcome is fixed: the Moors are defeated, convert to Christianity, and the feast concludes. It attracts thousands of visitors every year, many from Brasília 150 kilometers east, who come specifically to watch this medieval Portuguese ritual unfold in a Brazilian cerrado town on horseback.

## The IPHAN Listing

In 1989 IPHAN - the Institute of National Historic and Artistic Heritage - listed the entire historic center of Pirenópolis as protected patrimony. The decision was partly preservationist and partly a recognition of what the town already was: an 18th-century core that had survived because no one had bothered to modernize it. The First Church of Our Lady of the Rosary dates from 1728-1732. Our Lady of the Carmo and the Church of Our Lord of Bonfim both went up between 1750 and 1754. The Theater of Pirenópolis, a hybrid of colonial and neo-classical styles, opened in 1899 and still hosts performances. The Cine-Pireneus was originally built in 1919 and rebuilt in art-deco in 1936, giving the town one of the more architecturally interesting cinema facades in central Brazil. The House of Chamber and Jail, constructed in 1919, is itself a faithful restoration of the 1733 original. Everything stacks on everything in Pirenópolis.

## The Stone That Built Brasília

When Brasília started rising out of the cerrado in the late 1950s, its architects needed stone - a lot of it, and good. They found it 150 kilometers west at Pirenópolis. The famous Pedra de Pirenópolis is a micaceous quartzite, silvery and hard, and during the 1960s and 1970s it was mined intensively to clad the monumental buildings of the new federal capital. If you have walked across Praça dos Três Poderes in Brasília, you have walked across Pirenópolis. The irony, as the tourism boom arrived, was that the town's oldest industry - quarrying - helped build the city that now sends most of its weekend visitors. The town itself is crossed by the Almas River, a tributary of the Tocantins that flows north to form the artificial Serra da Mesa lake. A restored wooden bridge crosses the river near the center, carrying the modern version of the same traffic the old Meia Ponte served three centuries ago.

## Twenty Waterfalls and an Ecovillage

Within fifteen kilometers of the center, more than twenty waterfalls drop off the quartzite shelf of the Serra dos Pireneus. Some are enormous - the Salto de Corumba disappeared for decades when prospectors diverted its waters for dredging. Others tumble in cascades through fazendas that charge small entrance fees to hikers. The source of the Corumbá River, one of the longest in Goiás, lies near town. The municipality also contains part of the 2,833-hectare Pireneus State Park, created in 1987. In the 1980s a new kind of migration began - the hippies, with handicrafts, silversmithing, and alternative communities. One of those communities grew into the IPEC, the Institute of Permaculture and Ecovillage of the Cerrado, which hosts foreign students studying sustainable design. An 18th-century gold town, a federal-capital quarry, a tourist preserve, a hippie enclave - Pirenópolis has been all of these, and somehow all of them are still visible.

## Flight Context

Historic center sits at the foot of the Serra dos Pireneus, identifiable from above by red tile roofs, church towers, and the quartzite ridge to the north. Almas River crosses the center.
