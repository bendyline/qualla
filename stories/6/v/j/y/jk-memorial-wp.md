# JK Memorial

The man who built Brasília died with his political rights annulled; five years later his widow commissioned the tomb that put him back at the centre of his own city.

**Location:** -15.7844, -47.9131

**Categories:** Modernist architecture in Brazil, Oscar Niemeyer buildings, Monuments and memorials, Tourist attractions in Brasília

## Introduction

Juscelino Kubitschek died a private citizen. The president who moved a national capital into empty savanna in under four years had his political rights annulled by the military regime in 1964, spent time in exile, came home in 1967 to a country that would not let him stand for anything, and was killed in a car crash on the road between Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo on 22 August 1976, along with his driver. Some 350,000 people came to the burial in Brasília. The memorial that now holds him did not open for another five years, and when it did, the argument was not about the man. It was about the shape of the thing his architect had put over him.

## Sarah's Commission

The request came from Sarah Kubitschek, his widow and Brazil's former first lady, and it went to the obvious person: Oscar Niemeyer, who had drawn most of the buildings her husband's capital was famous for. The two men had been the central partnership of the Brasília project, the politician supplying the deadline and the political will, the architect supplying the curves. Niemeyer designed the memorial as a windowless block of white marble set on the Monumental Axis — a sealed, almost mute object among his open, floating government buildings, closer in spirit to a mausoleum than to the Palácio da Alvorada. It was inaugurated on 12 September 1981, at a moment when the dictatorship that had erased Kubitschek from public life was still in power and beginning, slowly, to unwind.

## The Argument Over a Curve

Outside stands Honório Peçanha's statue of Kubitschek, four and a half metres of bronze weighing about 1,500 kilograms, raised on a slender pedestal that lifts him nearly thirty metres into the sky. The pedestal does not go straight up. It curves over at the top and encloses the figure, and to a good many Brazilians in 1981 that silhouette looked unmistakably like the sickle of the hammer and sickle. Niemeyer, a lifelong member of the Brazilian Communist Party, conceived the shape and denied the reading. The controversy ran hot before installation and has never fully cooled — which is arguably the most Brasília outcome imaginable: an argument about ideology conducted entirely in the language of geometry, over a monument to a president the left and the right both tried to claim.

## Inside the Block

The doors open into something quieter than the exterior suggests. Above the catafalque, a stained-glass composition by the French-born artist Marianne Peretti floods the tomb chamber with coloured light, and work by Athos Bulcão sits elsewhere in the interior. Around them is the archive of two public lives: photographs, personal objects, the presidential sash, and the clothes Juscelino and Sarah wore to the inauguration of the capital on 21 April 1960. It is a museum built around a grave, and it does not pretend otherwise. Visitors walk past the evidence of a career and arrive at the body of the man who had it. The memorial is open Tuesday to Sunday, from nine in the morning until six.

## Fifty Years and Five

Kubitschek campaigned on a promise of fifty years of progress in five, and Brasília was the proof he chose to build. The bill came due in inflation and debt that his successors inherited, and historians have argued about the trade ever since. What is harder to argue with is the speed: a capital laid out, built and inaugurated inside a single presidential term, on a plateau that had almost nothing on it. His death has been argued over too. In 2013 a São Paulo state truth commission concluded that the crash had been an assassination; reviewing the evidence, the national truth commission did not sustain that finding, and the case remains contested rather than closed.

## Where He Ends Up

Position matters in a city laid out as an argument. Kubitschek's tomb sits on the Monumental Axis, the spine that runs from the television tower down to the Praça dos Três Poderes, placing him on the same line as the institutions he installed here. He stands west of the ministries, on the same drawn line. A president stripped of his rights by one government was given, by a later one, the most deliberate address in the country he had redrawn. The white marble gives away nothing from outside. That is the point: you have to go in.

## Flight Context

The memorial sits at 15.784°S, 47.913°W on the western Monumental Axis, around 1,150 m above sea level. From the air the giveaway is not the low white block but the statue — a bronze figure on a tall curving mast, casting a long shadow across open lawn just west of the Praça do Cruzeiro and the TV tower. Run the Monumental Axis from the National Congress westward and it appears on the north side. Best viewed from 1,500–3,000 ft AGL. Brasília–Presidente Juscelino Kubitschek International (SBBR), named for the man buried here, lies about 13 km east-southeast; expect airspace restrictions over the government district. Late afternoon in the May–September dry season gives the cleanest light and the longest shadow.
