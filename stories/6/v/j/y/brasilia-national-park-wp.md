# Brasília National Park

Brazilians call it Água Mineral and swim in its spring-fed pools, but the park is also the reservoir, firebreak and cerrado refuge that keeps the capital drinking.

**Location:** -15.7156, -47.9756

**Categories:** National parks of Brazil, Protected areas of the Federal District (Brazil), Cerrado, Wildlife

## Introduction

Ask for the national park and a brasiliense may hesitate. Ask for Água Mineral and you will be given directions, a warning about weekend crowds, and an opinion about which of the two pools is colder. The name comes from spring water rising into basins along the Córrego Acampamento that were dug out during the construction years, before there was a capital to swim near. Everything else about the place is bigger and less comfortable than a swimming hole: 42,355 hectares of cerrado pressed against the northwest edge of a metropolis of nearly three million, holding the water that city drinks.

## Cold Water, Warm Concrete

The pools are the reason most people come. Spring water surfaces clear and cold, fills two open basins ringed by paving and shade trees, and drains onward down the stream. It is an unglamorous piece of infrastructure that became a civic ritual: families arrive early on Sunday with coolers, teenagers colonise the shallow end, and joggers loop the paved circuit past them. The Capivara trail takes about twenty minutes on foot. The Cristal Água trail takes closer to two hours and puts you into scrub where the city noise finally drops away. Around 251,000 people visited in 2019, which for a protected area of this size is a remarkable amount of traffic — and the main argument its managers make for keeping it funded.

## The Tap

Two river basins drain the park: the Torto, covering about two-thirds of it, and the Bananal. Inside its fence sits the Santa Maria dam, and behind that dam sits close to a third of the Federal District's drinking water. This is the quiet reason a national park was drawn here in the first place. When Decree 241 created the park on 29 November 1961 — signed by Prime Minister Tancredo Neves, in the brief parliamentary interlude that followed Jânio Quadros's resignation — the stated purpose was to hold forested land around the new capital. Water was the practical translation of that idea. Protecting the headwaters was cheaper than treating what a subdivided watershed would have sent downstream, and the arithmetic has held for six decades.

## The Upside-Down Forest

Cerrado does not look like much to a visitor expecting Amazon. The trees are short, crooked and thick-barked, spaced far enough apart to walk between, with leaves like sandpaper. Almost all the growth happens underground: root systems that drive many metres down to reach water, which is why the vegetation shrugs off a dry season that lasts from May to September and delivers, in June, July and August combined, about two percent of the annual rain. Then October turns and the sky opens. Eighty-four percent of the year's rainfall arrives between October and April, peaking in December. Along the watercourses the cerrado thickens into gallery forest, dark ribbons of tall closed canopy that thread across the open savanna and hold the streams cool.

## Who Else Lives Here

The maned wolf is the animal people hope for and rarely see — a russet, long-legged canid built for looking over tall grass, more fox-shaped than wolf-shaped, and increasingly squeezed everywhere else in its range. Giant anteaters, giant armadillos and marsh deer share the park, all of them on Brazil's threatened list. Birds are easier: greater rheas striding through the grassland, red-legged seriemas announcing themselves with a rising scream, toco toucans crossing the gallery forest, curl-crested jays working the scrub, king vultures and lesser yellow-headed vultures riding the thermals that build over the plateau by mid-morning. Burrowing owls stand at the edge of the parking lot as if supervising it.

## Fire and Fence

Cerrado burns; that is part of how it works, and many of its plants need heat to seed. What it cannot absorb is the modern frequency. In 1994 fire took roughly seventy percent of the park in a single season, and burns have kept returning — around a tenth of the area in 2017 alone. Most ignitions come from the boundary rather than the sky, which is the park's other permanent problem. Its perimeter is fenced and legally fixed, expanded in 2006 by federal law to its present size, but it runs against subdivisions, roadside settlements and landfill ground on the Brasília side. A park this large surrounded this closely is an unusual thing to have kept. It survives because the capital cannot afford to lose it.

## Flight Context

The park's visitor area lies at 15.716°S, 47.976°W, on a plateau around 1,100 m above sea level. Approaching from the south, the Monumental Axis points almost directly at it: the park opens out immediately northwest of the built city, an obvious block of unbroken green against the grid, with the Santa Maria reservoir a distinct dark finger in its northern half. Best viewed from 3,000–6,000 ft AGL. Brasília–Presidente Juscelino Kubitschek International (SBBR) is about 20 km southeast; note the airport's controlled airspace overlies the approach. May–September is dry with excellent visibility until smoke from regional burning settles in during August and September; October–April brings towering afternoon cumulonimbus over the plateau and rapid visibility loss in rain.
