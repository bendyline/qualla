# Praça dos Três Poderes

An open triangle where Brazil's three branches of government face each other as equals — and where, on 8 January 2023, that arrangement was physically attacked.

**Location:** -15.8008, -47.8613

**Categories:** Squares in Brazil, Government buildings in Brazil, Modernist architecture in Brazil, Oscar Niemeyer buildings, Tourist attractions in Brasília

## Introduction

The flag is too big to last. Two hundred and eighty-six square metres of cloth, about ninety kilograms of it, hangs from a hundred-metre steel mast at the centre of the plaza, and the plateau sun and wind shred it steadily enough that a fresh one goes up on the first Sunday of every month. The changing is a formal ceremony: the Presidential Guard Battalion, the Independence Dragoons in their imperial-era uniforms, sometimes the president of the republic. Guinness records it as the largest flag flown regularly anywhere in the world. It is the loudest object in a square whose whole design argument is about restraint.

## A Triangle, Not a Hierarchy

Lúcio Costa laid the plaza at the far eastern end of his Monumental Axis and Oscar Niemeyer filled it, and the geometry carries the whole idea. Three institutions occupy three corners of an open space roughly 120 by 220 metres: the Palácio do Planalto for the executive, the National Congress for the legislature, the Supreme Federal Court for the judiciary. None of them sits above the others. None of them stands at the head of a processional approach, the way a palace or a cathedral would. They face inward across paving and reflecting pools, at a distance that forces you to turn your head to take them all in. The plaza is an argument about the separation of powers, made in stone and empty space rather than in a constitutional clause.

## The Warriors in the Square

Bruno Giorgi's bronze Os Candangos stands out on the paving — two tall, elongated figures side by side, also known as Os Guerreiros, the warriors. They commemorate the candangos, the construction workers who came mostly from the drought-pressed Northeast and built the capital in under four years, many of whom never got to live in it. In front of the Supreme Federal Court, Alfredo Ceschiatti carved A Justiça in granite: a seated, blindfolded woman, monumental and heavy, as unlike the airy court building behind her as a sculpture could be. Beneath the paving on the north side is the Tancredo Neves Pantheon of the Fatherland and Freedom, a Niemeyer cenotaph of 1986 spread over three floors and some 2,105 square metres, honouring Brazilians recognised as national heroes.

## Everyday Ground

For most of its life the plaza does something unremarkable and rather good: it works as a public square. Tourists photograph the flag. School groups queue for the Planalto. Civil servants cut across it between buildings, and demonstrators use it because it is the address where being heard counts. The absence of walls is deliberate and, until recently, uncomplicated. Costa and Niemeyer designed a seat of government you can walk up to, in a country that had known plenty of governments you could not. That openness is the plaza's best feature and, as January 2023 demonstrated, its most exposed one.

## 8 January 2023

A week after Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva was inaugurated, a crowd of supporters of the defeated former president Jair Bolsonaro broke into the plaza and then into the buildings around it — the Supreme Federal Court, the National Congress and the Planalto Palace — smashing windows, furniture and equipment across all three. The damage reached the art. A 1962 Emiliano Di Cavalcanti canvas was slashed seven times. An eighteenth-century clock by Balthasar Martinot, a French royal gift to the Portuguese crown with only one known counterpart, at Versailles, was destroyed. A wooden sculpture by Frans Krajcberg was broken in several places. More than 2,000 people were detained in the days and weeks that followed, most of them in the mass roundup at the protest encampment outside Army headquarters; the Supreme Court has since reported convictions of more than three hundred, on charges including attempted coup and damage to protected heritage.

## What Was Put Back

Restoration began almost immediately, and conservators returned twenty-one repaired works in time for the second anniversary in January 2025. Some pieces could not be recovered. The plaza itself was cleaned and reopened within days, which was partly the point of the hurry. In November 2024 the square was tested again, when a pair of explosions went off nearby on the evening of the 13th; the only person killed was the man who set them off, and no one else was injured. The flag went up again on the first Sunday of the following month, as it does. A place built to argue for the peaceful division of power has become, without ever intending to, a place where that argument is periodically defended.

## Flight Context

The plaza lies at 15.801°S, 47.861°W, at roughly 1,140 m elevation on the eastern tip of the Monumental Axis, where the peninsula runs out toward Lake Paranoá. It is the easiest landmark in Brasília to find from the air: follow the axis east past the double row of identical ministry blocks to the twin white towers and paired domes of the National Congress, and the plaza opens immediately beyond them, with the flag mast throwing a needle of shadow across it. Best viewed from 1,500–4,000 ft AGL. Brasília–Presidente Juscelino Kubitschek International (SBBR) is about 12 km west-southwest; the government district carries flight restrictions, so verify current NOTAMs before manoeuvring overhead. Dry-season mornings from May to September offer the sharpest visibility.
