# Guayaquil

The city that Simon Bolivar and Jose de San Martin met in secret to decide the fate of South America - and that pirates, yellow fever, and a pandemic in turn nearly destroyed.

**Location:** -2.1900, -79.8875

**Categories:** cities, history, independence, ports

## Introduction

On July 26, 1822, two men met in a room in this port city and changed the shape of South America. Simon Bolivar had come down from Gran Colombia. Jose de San Martin had come up from Peru. Between them, they controlled the armies that had driven the Spanish from a continent, and now they needed to decide who would finish the job. What exactly they said in that room has never been fully known - both men kept the conversation private - but San Martin left Guayaquil the next day, resigned his command, and retired to European exile. Bolivar stayed. The independence of Peru, and of what would become Bolivia, would be his to complete. The Guayaquil Conference belongs to the city's long history of being the room where decisions got made.

## The Legend of Guayas and Quil

The city's name is older than the Spanish arrival. One story - romantic, probably apocryphal, never written down until centuries later - claims that a local leader named Guayas and his wife Quil chose to fight to the death rather than submit to the conquistadors, setting fire to their own town as their final act. A more scholarly view points to pre-1543 Spanish documents that reference a chief named Guayaquile who ruled a settlement in the region occupied by the Chono people. Researcher Angel Veliz Mendoza counted at least seven such references. Whichever origin is correct, when the Spanish conquistador Francisco de Orellana founded the formal city in 1538, he named it Muy Noble y Muy Leal Ciudad de Santiago de Guayaquil - Most Noble and Most Loyal City of Santiago of Guayaquil - combining the patron saint of Spain with the name already on the ground. The founding date is celebrated July 25 each year, even though an earlier formal act of foundation took place on August 15, 1534, when Diego de Almagro founded Santiago de Quito near Riobamba - the settlement that would eventually be moved to the coast and become Guayaquil.

## Pirates and Yellow Fever

Being the chief Spanish shipyard on the Pacific coast made Guayaquil a target. On April 20, 1687, English and French pirates under George Dew, Picard, and Groniet raided the city; 75 defenders died, more than 100 were wounded, and the invaders took local women as concubines. Quito paid the ransom to secure their release and to save Guayaquil from being burned. In 1709, another English raid arrived under the command of Woodes Rogers and William Dampier with a crew of 110, but yellow fever broke out among the pirates and they fled without collecting the ransom. The disease saved the city. Through the colonial period, the port served as a stopover on the Manila galleon trade route - a commercial chain that ran from Philippines to Acapulco and down to Callao in Peru, with Guayaquil as a way station. In the late eighteenth century, the majority of the region's enslaved people were held in Guayaquil, living under a practice called jornal that allowed them limited independence while their owners retained legal title to them.

## The First Ecuadorian City to Become Free

On October 9, 1820, almost without bloodshed, a group of Guayaquil civilians supported by the Granaderos de Reserva battalion and led by the Peruvian colonel Gregorio Escobedo arrested the Spanish authorities and declared the Provincia Libre de Guayaquil. The city became the first in what would become Ecuador to achieve permanent independence. Jose Joaquin de Olmedo - poet, statesman, future author of the Guayaquil anthem - was named Jefe Civil. From Guayaquil, General Antonio Jose de Sucre led the army that won the Battle of Pichincha, confirming the independence of Gran Colombia. The Free Province was then annexed to Bolivar's Gran Colombia, and when that union collapsed in 1830, Guayaquil became the principal port of the new Republic of Ecuador. But the trouble was not quite finished. In 1829, the Peruvian Army invaded and occupied the city for seven months.

## Strike and Fire

In 1922, Guayaquil's workers went on a general strike that lasted three days. The repression was violent: at least 300 people were killed by military and police, though the full count has never been established. The strike is remembered each November 15 as a marker of Ecuadorian labor history. Two decades earlier, in 1896, a fire had consumed large sections of the city, destroying wooden colonial buildings that had stood for two centuries. The disasters, political and natural, became part of the city's story: the 1860 Battle of Guayaquil, in which forces under Gabriel Garcia Moreno defeated the Peruvian-backed Guillermo Franco; the 1942 Rio Protocol negotiations that followed another war with Peru; the 2016 earthquake that collapsed a bridge over Avenida de las Americas and killed two people. And then, in early 2020, COVID-19 arrived.

## The Pandemic That Overwhelmed the City

By April 2020, Guayaquil had become one of the worst pandemic hotspots in Latin America. The city's medical system and mortuary services were completely overwhelmed. Bodies lay in the streets for days because hospitals could not admit them and morgues could not hold them. Families placed relatives in caskets on sidewalks waiting for collection that did not come. Cardboard coffins replaced wood when wood ran out. Almost 6,000 more deaths were recorded in the first two weeks of April than the average for the same period in previous years. The images reached international press and shocked the world. The city recovered, slowly, as vaccines arrived and the initial surge passed. But for the families who lost relatives in those two weeks - elderly, frontline workers, those who simply could not be saved - the pandemic in Guayaquil was not statistics. It was the worst thing most Ecuadorians had lived through in their lifetime. The city, founded on a river, had endured pirates, fires, wars, and strikes. The new century brought a different kind of test, and Guayaquil remembers.

## Flight Context

Guayaquil sits at approximately 2.19 degrees south, 79.89 degrees west, on the west bank of the Guayas River near the Gulf of Guayaquil and the Pacific Ocean. Jose Joaquin de Olmedo International Airport (SEGU / GYE) has a field elevation of roughly 20 feet and serves commercial traffic from across the Americas. Metropolitan population is approximately 2.7 million. From altitude, Guayaquil appears as the largest urban concentration on Ecuador's coast, with the wide Guayas River channel feeding into the Gulf of Guayaquil to the south. Look for the downtown skyscraper cluster, the Las Penas district at the river's northern bend, and the international port facilities south of the city. The Daule and Babahoyo rivers converge north of town to form the Guayas. Tropical savanna climate brings heavy rains January through April; best aerial visibility is May through December with the Humboldt Current suppressing cloud buildup.
