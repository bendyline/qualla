# Alausí

Founded in 1534 on the lands of the Lausí people, this Ecuadorian highland town preserves its colonial streets, 18th-century churches, and a railway heritage built on the graves of Jamaican laborers.

**Location:** -2.2014, -78.8464

**Categories:** Populated places in Chimborazo Province

## Introduction

On June 29, 1534, Sebastián de Belalcázar paused in a small valley at the foot of Cerro Gampala, along the Chanchán River, and named the place San Pedro de Alausí. Two choices went into that name. The saint was June's - it was Saint Peter's day. And Lausí came from the people who already lived there, the Indigenous community the Spanish called the Lausies. Four hundred and eighty-five years later, the Ecuadorian Ministry of Tourism declared Alausí a Pueblo Mágico, officially acknowledging what locals had known for centuries: the town had aged well, its cobbled streets and century-old houses preserved by a kind of remote grace. What the Pueblo Mágico designation does not mention, but the town remembers, is that some of those streets were built over the graves of men from Kingston and Spanish Town who never wanted to see Ecuador at all.

## First Settlement of the Royal Audience

Ecuador before independence belonged to what the Spanish Crown called the Real Audiencia de Quito - a jurisdictional district of the Viceroyalty of Peru. Alausí, founded the same year as Quito itself, is considered the first settlement in that administrative territory. The city took its formal shape through repeated foundations - Belalcázar's initial naming, later legalization under the simpler name Alausí, then gradual absorption into the Government of Quito as a minor political and administrative entity. When Quito launched its independence movement in 1810, Alausí recognized the new government and sent Captain José Antonio Pontón to represent it. Pontón signed the Carta de Estado de Quito in 1812, and in recognition of the town's loyalty Bishop Cuero y Caicedo resolved to elevate its status from town to Villa. Small honors for a small place - but the civic pride they represent still surfaces in Alausí's November 13 observances, the anniversary of the town's 1820 declaration of independence.

## The Railway and Its Dead

September 8, 1902, the railway arrived. The Guayaquil-Quito line, driven through the Andes against engineering odds that had defeated earlier attempts, reached Alausí and transformed the town into a commercial center between coast and mountains. The most difficult section - the Nariz del Diablo, the Devil's Nose - descends nearly 500 meters in just 12 kilometers through two switchbacks blasted into the face of a vertical rock wall. American engineers Archer and John Harman oversaw the construction; the labor came from multiple sources. Wikipedia records plainly that the Nariz del Diablo was the tomb of many Jamaican workers contracted to dynamite the mountain. They were brought thousands of miles from the Caribbean to do the deadliest work on the project. Many did not go home. The number is not precisely known. Their names, mostly, are not preserved in the monuments to the engineers or the politicians. The train that tourists now ride down the switchbacks passes through stone that their hands cut. This is part of Alausí's story too.

## The Old Stone Church and the Saint on the Hill

Most of the houses in Alausí are more than 100 years old - the kind of stone-and-plaster construction that ages into the landscape rather than against it. The Iglesia Matriz de Alausí, the town's principal church, was built in the 18th century. Its stones came from the mines of Chiripungo, about two kilometers outside the city. It faces the Parque 13 de Noviembre, named for the date in 1820 when Alausí declared itself free of Spanish rule. Above the town, on the Loma de Lluglli, stands the Monument to Saint Peter - a 21-meter sculpture by Ecuadorian artist Eddie Crespo, completed in 2001. At that scale the statue dominates the skyline; from anywhere in Alausí you can look up and find the patron saint gazing down. In the Eloy Alfaro Square, another sculpture commemorates the president who willed the railway into existence, along with four murals depicting moments of his liberal revolution.

## Bulls, Bells, and the Festival of San Pedro

Alausí celebrates Carnaval the way highland Ecuador generally celebrates it - with parades, water fights, flour, and the neighborhoods of the city competing in processions. The signature event, though, is the running of the bulls. Modeled loosely on Pamplona's San Fermín tradition, a closed street channels released bulls to their destination at the bullfighting ring. The larger festival is San Pedro, celebrated June 22 through July 2 - days of traditional dance, music, folklore, cockfights, bullfights, and markets. The celebration has persisted since the colonial era. Spanish influence is inescapable in its forms, but the specifics - the dances, the costumes, the food - reflect what happens when a European calendar is absorbed into an Andean town over four centuries. Tourists come in increasing numbers, but the festival remains primarily local, an Alausí celebration of Alausí.

## The Town That Trains Remember

Alausí today is a town of about 21,000 people in the urban parish and 43,000 more across the rural canton. Tourists come for the Devil's Nose train and leave with photographs. Buses connect the town to Quito, Cuenca, Guayaquil, and smaller destinations along the Pan-American corridor. The bus station sits three blocks down from the train station on 5 de Junio. Around those anchors, the town continues its long rhythm - the schools (several named after local notable figures like Federico González Suárez and Manuel Galecio), the markets, the slow walks to the church plaza in the evening. Bolivar Arellano, born in Alausí in 1944, became a prominent Ecuadorian-American photographer - one of several locals who left and made names elsewhere. Most stay. The railway that once shaped the town now defines it as heritage. Stone streets and century-old facades do not modernize easily, and in Alausí that limitation has become an advantage.
