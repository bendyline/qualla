# El Cajas National Park

Gateway to the snowy mountains: an Andean paramo of 270 glacial lakes, nineteen endemic plants, and the world's largest hummingbird.

**Location:** -2.8461, -79.2203

**Categories:** national-parks, ecuador, paramo, andean, biodiversity

## Introduction

Cajas, in Quichua, might mean gateway to the snowy mountains. Or it might come from caxa, the Quichua word for cold. It has also been linked to the Spanish cajas, meaning boxes - a possible reference to the ice-carved basins that hold the park's 270 alpine lakes. The etymology is unsettled, and perhaps it matters less than the place itself. At 3,100 to 4,450 meters above sea level, the paramo landscape of El Cajas National Park stretches across 28,544 hectares of the Ecuadorian Andes thirty kilometers west of Cuenca. In 2013, UNESCO recognized it as one of 759 biosphere reserves worldwide. It is not quite the snowy mountains the name suggests - the altitude here is too low for permanent snow - but the cold reaches deep into the bones of anyone who hikes it.

## An Ecosystem of Rain and Altitude

The ecology of Cajas depends on one particular combination: high humidity, high altitude, low atmospheric pressure. The combination produces soils that accumulate organic material at an unusual rate and hold water like a sponge - the definition of paramo. The grassland ecosystem contains plants suitably adapted to these exact conditions, and nineteen of them are endemic to Cajas itself, growing nowhere else on Earth. The dominant grass is Calamagrostis intermedia, the tall straw grass that gives the landscape its pale gold color. Above 3,300 meters the quenua or paper tree forest takes over - Polylepis, a genus uniquely adapted to extreme altitude with twisted trunks and shredding red bark. Cajas is one of the few places where a rare fuchsia species, Fuchsia campii, is predicted to hold a second protected population. In the lower ravines near the brooks and rivers, cloud forest and perennial high mountain forest appear, supporting a different set of species in a different microclimate.

## The Condor and the Giant

Fewer than 150 Andean condors remain in all of Ecuador. Some of them fly over Cajas. The condor (Vultur gryphus), South America's largest raptor and one of the largest flying birds on Earth, glides on thermals over the high peaks with a wingspan that can approach three meters. Also in residence is the curiquinga (Carunculated Caracara), a large black and white raptor common enough to be sighted regularly. And then there is Patagona gigas, the giant hummingbird - the largest hummingbird species in the world, which feeds on nectar from a range of high-altitude flowers including Puya, Agave, and tall cacti. The violet-throated metaltail (Metallura baroni), another hummingbird, is endemic to Cajas and the valleys immediately surrounding it. In total, 157 bird species have been cataloged in the park, making it one of the most important bird-watching sites in Ecuador. For birders attempting to see the endemic species of the Andean cloud forests and paramo, Cajas is often the first serious stop on a southern Ecuador itinerary.

## Mammals, Including the Ones That Are Only Here

Forty-four mammal species have been identified in the park. The list includes opossums, small cats, various bats, pumas, coatis, weasels, skunks, foxes, porcupines, pacas, shrews, rabbits, and a collection of other rodents. Two species are endemic to the park: the Cajas water mouse (Chibchanomys orcesi), a member of the small rodent group Ichthyomyini adapted for life in fast-flowing mountain streams, and Tate's shrew opossum (Caenolestes tatei), a marsupial of the high Andes. Neither is easy to see - both are small, nocturnal, and specialized enough that they keep to habitat most humans cannot access. The presence of pumas in the park is confirmed though rarely witnessed; the cats range widely across the Andes and use the protected forest corridors of Cajas as movement routes. Llamas, introduced and managed by local communities, graze in some areas and contribute to the paramo landscape that tourists expect to see.

## The Roads Before the Park

The Canari, the pre-Inca people of southern Ecuador, built three interregional roads connecting Guapondeleg - the settlement that later became Tomebamba and today Cuenca - with the Pacific lowlands to the west. One of these roads ran through what is now Cajas National Park, crossing the continental divide on paved stone. A control point called Paredones still stands within the park, used during the Canari and Inca periods to monitor trade moving between the highlands and the coast. After the Inca conquest of the region in the 1470s, Inca road engineers incorporated the Canari roads into their own network, adding new stretches and fortifications. Twenty-eight archaeological sites have now been identified in the park and its immediate vicinity, spanning pre-Incan and Incan periods. During the colonial era the high fields were used for livestock, a tradition that continued in pockets until the park was formally established on November 5, 1996, by resolution N 057.

## International Recognition

Cajas holds several international conservation designations. In August 2002, it was listed as a Ramsar Wetland of International Importance - the Ramsar Convention's designation for wetlands of global significance, and Cajas qualified on the strength of its paramo, its glacial lakes, and the Amazonian and Pacific watersheds that originate here. The park is also categorized as an Important Bird Area by BirdLife International, reflecting the endemic and endangered species that use it. It is part of the UNESCO biosphere reserve network. Currently, Cajas is a candidate for designation as a World Natural Heritage Site by UNESCO, which would place it among the most protected natural sites on Earth. The existing protections combine to form a conservation framework unusual for a single park, reflecting the real ecological importance of the paramo landscape it contains.

## The Door of the Snowy Mountains

Whatever Cajas means - whether gateway to the snowy mountains, or simply cold, or perhaps boxes - the park offers access to a kind of landscape that exists in only a few places on Earth. The high Andean paramo sits in a narrow elevation band between the tree line and the permanent snow line, a zone that requires very specific conditions of temperature, moisture, and latitude to form. Most of the world's paramo is in South America, and most of South America's paramo is in Colombia, Ecuador, and northern Peru. Cajas protects one of its most accessible and biodiverse examples. The park feeds the drinking water of Cuenca, one of Ecuador's major cities, and provides the high-altitude home for endemic species that survive nowhere else. Its combination of archaeological depth, ecological rarity, and accessibility from Cuenca makes it a rare kind of protected area - one that does everything a national park is supposed to do, within an hour's drive of a UNESCO World Heritage city.

## Flight Context

Cuenca Mariscal Lamar International (SECU) the nearest airport, with flights from Guayaquil crossing park airspace on approach. 270 glacial lakes form distinctive mirror pattern from altitude.
