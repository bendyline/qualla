# Cuenca, Ecuador

A UNESCO World Heritage city built on the ruins of an Inca capital, where Canari, Inca, and Spanish histories stack in plain sight.

**Location:** -2.8974, -79.0045

**Categories:** cities, unesco, colonial, andean, historical

## Introduction

Guapondeleg, in the language of the Canari people, meant land as big as heaven. For roughly a thousand years, from around 500 AD, that was the name of this valley between four rivers in the southern Andes. Then, less than half a century before the Spanish arrived, the Inca came and renamed it Tomebamba. They built a city here they said rivalled Cuzco and called it the second Cuzco, the door of the puma. Then the Spanish came and renamed it Cuenca, after a valley town in Spain the viceroy's mother was from. Three empires, three languages, one valley. The city that stands here today - declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1999 - has not forgotten any of them.

## Before the Inca

Archaeological evidence of human presence at the Cave of Chopsi near Cuenca dates to 8060 BC. The earliest inhabitants were hunters following the paramo herds and the rhythms of the Andean seasons. By about 5585 BC the culture reached its fullest expression, but the record falls quiet between 5000 and 2000 BC before picking up again with more organized settlements: Chaullabamba, Huayco, Pirincay, Monjas, Putushio, Huancarcucho, and Jubones. By 500 AD the Canari people had developed the valley into Guapondeleg, a settlement with water management, astronomical knowledge, and an agriculture that included potatoes, quinoa, squash, and domesticated guinea pigs and camelids. The Canari endured for nearly a millennium before the Inca arrived in the 1470s. The conquest was bitter and violent. The Inca, rather than destroying Canari achievements, absorbed them - a characteristic strategy of an empire that preferred to incorporate what worked.

## The Second Cuzco

After consolidating control in the 1470s, Inca emperor Tupac Yupanqui ordered construction of a grand city called Pumapungo, the door of the puma. Spanish chroniclers recorded indigenous accounts of golden temples and marvelous wealth, descriptions that would eventually fuel rumors of El Dorado. Some historians have considered Tomebamba a candidate for the mythical golden city the conquistadors pursued. Whatever it had been, the Spanish arrived too late to see it whole. The Inca emperor Huayna Capac, who had been born in Tomebamba and favored it as a secondary capital, died around 1527, and the civil war between his sons Huascar and Atahualpa that followed tore the empire apart. By the time Francisco Pizarro made his way south in the 1530s, Tomebamba had been sacked, depopulated, and reduced to ruins. Whether its inhabitants destroyed it ahead of the Spanish or whether it fell in the Inca civil war, Tomebamba's splendor did not survive the transition.

## Gil Ramirez Davalos, 1557

The Spanish city was founded on April 12, 1557, by the conquistador Gil Ramirez Davalos. He acted on orders from Andres Hurtado de Mendoza, Viceroy of Peru, who directed that the new settlement take the name of his hometown - Cuenca, a medieval city in Castile whose name, like the Ecuadorian valley, means basin. Cuenca was a late addition to the Spanish urban network in the region. Quito had been founded in 1534, Guayaquil in 1538, Loja in 1548. By the time Cuenca appeared on Spanish maps, the shape of colonial rule was already set. The city grew slowly through the colonial centuries, building the stone and whitewashed architecture that still defines the historic center. Independence came on November 3, 1820, early in the wars that would break Spanish control across the continent, and Cuenca briefly served as one of three provincial capitals in the nascent republic.

## The New Cathedral's Mistake

The Catedral Metropolitana de la Inmaculada Concepcion - the New Cathedral - was completed in 1975 after nearly a century of construction. At its inauguration, it could hold 9,000 of Cuenca's then 10,000 inhabitants in a single service. The three sky-blue domes with white trim have become the city's visual signature, reproduced on every postcard and tourist map. They are also shorter than their architect intended. A calculation error early in construction meant that the foundation would not have supported the full-height towers originally designed, and the builders had to truncate them. The pink marble in the nave floor was imported from Carrara in Italy; the alabaster and local marble in the facade came from nearby quarries. Across the plaza, the Old Cathedral of El Sagrario, built in 1557 and no longer consecrated, houses a museum where visitors can see restored seventeenth-century murals and trace the outlines of the colonial faith that built this city.

## The Athens of Ecuador

Cuenca calls itself the Athens of Ecuador, and the claim rests largely on its universities. The Universidad de Cuenca was founded in 1867, making it the country's third oldest after the Universidad Central del Ecuador in Quito and the Universidad Nacional de Loja. The city has since accumulated four universities and a reputation for producing writers, artists, and intellectuals out of proportion to its size. In 2011 the National Assembly formally designated Cuenca the City of Universities. The writer Jeffrey Herlihy-Mera observed that the plateau has been a place treasured by empires - Canari, then Inca, then Spanish, each renaming it in their own language. The pattern persists in a different form. The historic center was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1999, drawing international attention and an expatriate population of several thousand North Americans and Europeans who have discovered Cuenca as a retirement destination, drawn by the climate, the architecture, and the cost of living.

## Four Rivers, One Valley

The geography that gave the city its name has not changed. Four rivers - the Tomebamba, Yanuncay, Tarqui, and Machangara - still run through Cuenca, and the first three still rise from the paramo of El Cajas National Park to the west. The Tomebamba's banks form one of the loveliest urban walks in the Andes, with colonial buildings on the north bank and newer neighborhoods climbing the south. The climate is temperate highland - the Koppen classification Cfb, averaging 14.7 degrees Celsius year-round, with bright mornings and afternoon showers during the March-to-May wet season. The 2022 census recorded 596,101 people in the city proper, making it Ecuador's third largest, and around 730,000 in the metropolitan area. A tram line opened in 2020 connecting the industrial park in the north through the historic center to Banos in the south - a modern strand threading through a city that has worn its history for nearly fifteen hundred years.

## Flight Context

Cuenca at 2.90 S, 79.00 W, 2,538 meters elevation. Mariscal Lamar International Airport (SECU) is Ecuador's third-busiest, serving roughly 1.4 million passengers annually via Avianca Ecuador and LATAM Ecuador connections to Quito. The airport sits at 2,530 meters on the northeast edge of the city, approach over surrounding mountains. Cajas National Park to the west visible as distinctive blue-lake-dotted paramo at 3,500-4,400 meters. Morning departures recommended; afternoon thunderstorms common over the Andes.
