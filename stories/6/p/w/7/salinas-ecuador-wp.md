# Salinas, Ecuador

The westernmost point of mainland Ecuador, where the Humboldt Current keeps a tropical beach town cool and where silver coins from a 1654 shipwreck rest beside pre-Columbian figurines.

**Location:** -2.2083, -80.9681

**Categories:** Populated places in Santa Elena Province, Surfing locations in Ecuador

## Introduction

The coast here is tropical by latitude and subtropical by feel. Salinas sits almost exactly on the equator, two degrees south, and by every rule of geography should be humid and hot. Instead, the Humboldt Current pulls frigid water up from Antarctica past this westernmost point of mainland Ecuador, and the Pacific stays stubbornly cool. The result is a beach town where the weather behaves like somewhere a thousand miles farther from the sun - warm and dry, but rarely sweltering, the air sharp rather than soft. It is the reason Ecuadorians with money built yacht clubs here instead of somewhere closer to home.

## From Fishing Village to Yacht Club

Until 1929, Salinas was a cluster of fishing boats pulled up on the sand - a rural parish of Santa Elena, known mostly to the people who lived there. That June, it was formally established as a parish. Eight years later, on December 22, 1937, General Alberto Enriquez Gallo, commander in chief of the country, signed the decree that elevated Salinas to the rank of canton. The transformation that followed was rapid and uneven. Two yacht clubs now mark the water - the smaller Salinas Yacht Club and the larger Puerto Lucia Yacht Club, the latter with its hotel, private beach, and marina full of masts. The fishing boats still come and go, but they share the harbor now with sailboats whose owners fly in on weekends.

## The Currents Collide at La Chocolatera

Follow the peninsula to its outermost tip and you reach La Chocolatera, a cliff that earns its name from the color of the water below. Here the Humboldt Current running north meets the equatorial current pushing south, and the collision churns silt and sediment into a rich brown foam that stains the sea the color of melted chocolate. Sea lions haul out on the rocks, indifferent to the tourists watching them. The air smells of salt and the particular iodine tang of cold Pacific water. Offshore, whales pass on their annual migration - humpbacks coming north from Antarctic feeding grounds to calve in warmer equatorial seas, which, here, are not actually all that warm.

## What the Ground Remembers

The Salinas Museum 21st Century, tucked into the town grid at Malecon and Quil streets, holds a deeper history than the resort hotels suggest. Its archaeological room displays pottery and figurines from the Valdivia culture (3500-1500 BC), the Machalilla culture that followed (1500-1100 BC), and the Chorrera variant called Engoroy. Later still came the Jambeli-Guangala and the Guancavilca - the last of whom were still here when the Spanish arrived in 1531. The museum's most evocative exhibit is a scale model of a Guancavilca Manteña raft, reconstructed from a description written by the Spanish cosmographer Samano in 1526. These black-hulled vessels traded Spondylus shells and copper axes as far as Mexico and Peru, a coastal trade network that flourished for a thousand years before Europe arrived.

## The Captain's Treasure

Another exhibit commemorates the wreck of the galleon Jesus Maria de la Limpia Concepcion, known to sailors as 'The Captain,' which ran aground off Chanduy in 1654. Divers recovered silver cobs - crude coins in denominations of 1, 2, 4, and 8 reales, struck for transport to Panama and onward to the Caribbean and Europe. There were fragments of majolica pottery, tin bars, bronze cannonballs, iron shot for muskets, and a gold cross etched with Latin inscriptions. An earring set with pearl, a silver buckle, agate ornaments - the personal possessions of passengers who never reached Spain. The objects sit now in climate-controlled cases a few miles from where they spent three and a half centuries on the sea floor.

## Surfing the Ram's Point

Ten minutes south of Salinas, Punta Carnero - 'Ram Point,' named for the rocky headland at its southeast end - offers some of Ecuador's most consistent waves. The beach runs about 1.6 miles of white to gray sand, medium waves rolling in against a steady inland breeze. Salinas hosted the ISA World Junior Surfing Games in 2009, and the international competitions keep coming back. Surfers show up year-round because the Humboldt's effect holds the climate remarkably stable. There is no wet season to plan around, no hurricane threat to track. The current that cools the beach also smooths the weather.

## Flight Context

Salinas sits at 2.21 degrees south, 80.97 degrees west, at the westernmost tip of mainland Ecuador. Fly low along the Santa Elena Peninsula to see La Chocolatera's cliff jutting into the Pacific where the currents meet. General Ulpiano Paez Airport (SESA/ESLS) serves the town. Salinas lies about 90 miles west of Guayaquil's Jose Joaquin de Olmedo International (SEGU/GYE), a short hop over coastal lowlands. Clear conditions year-round thanks to the Humboldt Current - expect cool coastal air rather than tropical humidity.
