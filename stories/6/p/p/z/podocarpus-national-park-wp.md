# Podocarpus National Park

Four ecosystems collide inside a single Andean park, making this corner of southern Ecuador one of the most botanically dense places on Earth.

## Introduction

Four ecosystems meet here, and the collision is what makes this place extraordinary. Northern Andes, Southern Andes, Amazon basin, and Pacific slope all press into the same mountains in southern Ecuador, and their overlap has produced something geographers call a megadiverse zone and botanists simply call the Botanical Garden of America. More than 4,000 plant species grow across Podocarpus National Park's 1,463 square kilometers, including the romerillo tree that gives the park its name and the cinchona, Ecuador's national tree, whose bark once treated malaria across the world. The numbers sound improbable, but at these latitudes, on these slopes, they make sense.

## Where Four Worlds Meet

The park sprawls across two provinces, Zamora Chinchipe and Loja, with about 85 percent on the Amazonian flank and the rest looking west toward the Pacific. Elevation does most of the work. Lower montane rain forests begin around 1,000 meters and climb through cloud forest and then elfin forest, those stunted, moss-draped woodlands that cling to peaks wrapped in permanent mist. Above 3,000 meters, the forest surrenders to paramo, the high Andean grassland where a complex of more than 100 lagoons glitters between tussocks of grass. The best known of these is Lagunas del Compadre, reachable only by a two-day hike with a guide. Four ecosystems colliding in a single park is what generates the diversity, and the same geology that concentrates rainfall also concentrates life.

## A Botanical Garden Without Walls

The romerillo trees can reach 40 meters, tall enough that their crowns disappear into cloud. Podocarpus glomeratus is the species that lends its name to the park, but it grows alongside a staggering variety of companions: wild cinchona, arrayan, pumamaqui, cedar, walnut, and a cascade of orchids that have drawn botanists from around the world. The surrounding foothills hold more than 4,000 plant species, and researchers still find new ones. Moth specialists call these slopes a global diversity hotspot. For birds, the numbers tilt toward the absurd. Five hundred sixty species have been registered here, roughly six percent of every bird species on the planet, and forty percent of every species ever recorded in Ecuador. BirdLife International named the park an important area for neotropical bird conservation in 1995, and the list of what lives here keeps growing.

## Spectacled Bears and Mountain Tapirs

The mammals that walk these forests include four species on Ecuador's Red List. The mountain tapir, pinchaque, moves through the cloud forest like a small rhinoceros with a prehensile upper lip. The spectacled bear, named for the pale rings around its eyes, is South America's only bear and the living inspiration for Paddington. The northern pudu, barely knee-high, is one of the world's smallest deer, and the jaguar still passes through the lower elevations. Sixty-eight mammal species in all have been recorded, alongside hog-nosed skunks and grey shrew opossums and countless smaller creatures. Gold miners work illegally in some sectors, and the pressure on these animals grows as roads push deeper. The park remains their refuge, but the word refuge gets harder to earn every year.

## Trails Through the Clouds

Two main entrances let visitors touch this complexity without disappearing into it. The Cajanuma sector, fifteen minutes south of the city of Loja, opens into the elfin forests and paramo between 2,900 and 3,400 meters. Short trails visit the spectacled bear habitat and the cloud forest, and a longer five-kilometer climb ends at a mirador with views across Loja and the valleys beyond. The Bombuscaro sector, fifteen minutes from Zamora, drops visitors into lower montane rainforest along the Bombuscaro River, where the Poderosa waterfall roars into a pool deep enough to swim. Rainy seasons differ by flank. East of the divide the rain comes March through July, while the western valleys stay wet October through December. Temperatures average 18 degrees Celsius in the lower forests, 12 degrees on the paramo. Layers matter.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.28 degrees south, 79.00 degrees west, in the far south of Ecuador where the Andes narrow toward Peru. From altitude the park appears as a long ridge of dark green forest with cloud caps clinging to the higher elevations. The nearest major airport is Catamayo Airport (SETM) in Loja, about 40 kilometers north-northwest of the Cajanuma entrance. Guayaquil (SEGU) and Quito (SEQM) offer wider connections. Best visibility comes in the dry months for each flank. Expect persistent cloud cover over the eastern slopes, where Amazonian moisture rises and condenses against the mountains.
