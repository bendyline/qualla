# Tefe

Founded by Carmelite friars in the 1690s on a lake of the Solimoes, Tefe is one of the oldest permanent European settlements on the Amazon and was once the capital of the language Henry Walter Bates heard for 2,500 miles.

**Location:** -3.3539, -64.7108

**Categories:** amazon, colonial history, missions, brazil, municipalities

## Introduction

In 1620, Portuguese Carmelite friars could already count converts among the Mura people living around Lake Teffe. The mission that became Tefe was founded later in the century, one of eight aldeias that Carmelite priests strung along the Solimoes and Negro rivers between 1697 and 1751. By the standards of European settlement in the Amazon, this was very early. By the standards of the people who had lived here for millennia, it was yesterday. The town that grew from that mission is now the largest commercial center of the middle Solimoes and the jumping-off point for the Mamiraua reserve. Its name has cycled through three versions, but the place has stayed where it is for more than three hundred years.

## The Mission That Moved

The original Carmelite mission was called Parauari, established on the Ilha dos Veados, three leagues across the water from where Tefe stands today. A smallpox outbreak in the early eighteenth century emptied it. The survivors left the island and settled on the opposite shore of Lake Tefe, at a location now called Nogueira, and in 1817 a man named F. Andre da Costa moved them again to the current site. In 1759, the commandant Joaquim de Mello da Povoas converted the Carmelite mission into a formal town and gave it the name Ega, a Portuguese imitation of a Celtic word with no obvious Amazonian meaning. The town kept the name Ega officially until 1855, when it was elevated to the status of a city and renamed Tefe, though Tefe had been the common spoken name for much longer.

## Bates Among the Muras

Henry Walter Bates, the English naturalist who spent eleven years in the Amazon in the 1840s and 1850s, arrived in Tefe in the middle of the nineteenth century and stayed long enough to write about it. What struck Bates most was the linguistic landscape. The Mura and other peoples of the lake region learned Tupi quickly, he wrote, and despite the enormous diversity of indigenous languages spoken throughout the Amazon, Tupi was the lingua franca. He claimed it was spoken with little corruption along the banks of the main Amazon for a distance of 2,500 miles. Bates was not an uncritical observer, but he was an attentive one, and his book The Naturalist on the River Amazons gave European readers one of the first detailed accounts of a thriving, polyglot Amazonian town.

## The Bishops of the Holy Spirit

In 1910, the Vatican established an apostolic prefecture centered on Tefe, assigning it to priests of the Congregation of the Holy Spirit, known informally as the Spiritans. The prefecture became the Prelature of Teffe and covers a vast stretch of the upper Amazon. Spiritan priests and nuns have worked in the region for more than a century, and the church has played a complicated role in the lives of the indigenous peoples around the lake: providing education and health care in a region the Brazilian state often did not reach, and also serving as a vector for the kind of religious and cultural pressure that missionaries applied everywhere they went. The cathedral stands in the center of Tefe today, a reminder that the town began as a mission and never entirely stopped being one.

## The Flooded Forest Beyond the Town

Tefe is the administrative home for one of the most important protected areas in the Amazon. The Mamiraua Sustainable Development Reserve, just west and north of the town at the confluence of the Solimoes and Japura, covers more than a million hectares of varzea, the Amazon's seasonally flooded forest. Scientists working at Mamiraua treat Tefe as the end of the road, or rather the end of the river-route, before they push on to the reserve's field stations. The town also holds 46 percent of the Tefe National Forest, established in 1989, and about a third of the Catua-Ipixuna Extractive Reserve, created in 2003 as the first extractive reserve in the state of Amazonas. The small palm Bactris tefensis, named for the town, grows only in the varzea of this region and nowhere else on Earth.

## A Town Without Roads

The city has no road connection to anywhere. Azul Linhas Aereas and Voepass fly to the small Tefe Airport from Manaus, and fast boats take about twelve hours to make the same trip. There are no plans to build a road, and the population, which reached 59,547 at the last full census and has grown modestly since, does not seem to want one. The city is evenly split between urban residents and people living in the rural settlements that line the lake and the surrounding rivers. Most of what the town needs moves on water. The lake freezes nothing, never empties, and connects Tefe to a river system that stretches for thousands of kilometers in every direction. For a place founded on a mission that survived a smallpox outbreak and two relocations, it has settled into its location with a kind of patient finality.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.35 degrees S, 64.71 degrees W on the south bank of the Solimoes River at the mouth of the Tefe River. The nearest airport is Tefe Airport (SBTF), served only by flights from Manaus. Best viewed from 5,000 to 10,000 feet where Lake Tefe, a dark, roughly circular body, sits on the southern edge of the silt-laden Solimoes. The Mamiraua reserve lies to the northwest at the Solimoes-Japura confluence and is easily identifiable from altitude during the May-June high-water peak when the flooded forest canopy shimmers under standing water.
