# Jaú National Park

2.36 million hectares of Amazonian rainforest - one of the largest protected areas in Brazil and the core of a UNESCO World Heritage Site that spans four conservation units.

**Location:** -2.1703, -62.6172

**Categories:** 1980 establishments in Brazil, National parks of Brazil, Protected areas of Amazonas (Brazilian state), World Heritage Sites in Brazil

## Introduction

The name comes from a fish. The jaú, also called the gilded catfish, is one of the largest fish in Brazil - a deep-water giant that can reach more than a meter and a half in length and hunts along the bottoms of black-water rivers. The people who named the river named it for what swam there, and the park created to protect the river basin in 1980 took the river's name in turn. Parque Nacional do Jaú now covers 2,367,333 hectares - nearly 24,000 square kilometers - making it one of the largest protected areas in Brazil. It contains the entire Jaú River basin, from headwaters to mouth, and it sits at the heart of a UNESCO World Heritage Site that has kept growing since it was first inscribed.

## A Basin, Sealed

The park's boundaries were drawn to protect a whole hydrological unit. The Jaú River and its two neighbors - the Unini to the north and the Carabinani to the south - all flow east into the right bank of the Rio Negro. All three basins fall entirely within the park, and that completeness matters. A protected area that only covers part of a watershed is vulnerable to what happens upstream. The Jaú, in contrast, controls its own hydrology from headwater to confluence. It sits about 220 kilometers northwest of Manaus in the state of Amazonas, crossing the municipalities of Barcelos, Codajás, and Novo Airão. On its edges it joins other protected lands - the Rio Unini Extractive Reserve to the north, the Amanã Sustainable Development Reserve to the northwest, the Rio Negro State Park's northern section to the east. It is less an island of conservation than a hinge between connected ones.

## The Interfluvial Plateau

The terrain here is what geographers call the Negro-Solimões interfluvial plateau - the ground between the black-water Rio Negro and the whitewater Solimões, emerging a few million years ago as the soft sediments of the low Amazon basin. Two elevations show up inside the park. The higher ground, called the Trombetas-Negro plateau, rises to flat-topped hills around 150 meters cut by river valleys. The lower terrain sits around 100 meters. Large areas remain seasonally flooded with poor drainage, and scattered permanent lakes hold water year-round. Annual rainfall exceeds 2,500 millimeters, with peaks in March and September when solar radiation is maximum and convectional storms reach their intensity. Temperatures average 26 degrees Celsius. The whole system is shaped by water - when it rises, what it carries, and where it goes when it falls.

## Forests Against Flooding

Seventy-seven percent of the park is dense rainforest. Fourteen percent is open rainforest. Seven percent is transitional country between rainforest and campinarana, and two percent is pure campinarana - the white-sand habitat where leached, acidic soils support only dwarf shrubs and specialized plants that have invested everything in conservation rather than growth. Botanists have catalogued about 400 plant species in the park, several restricted to specific habitats: the uplands, the flooded forest, the campinarana. The number is almost certainly an undercount. Amazonian ecosystems routinely yield new species to systematic survey, and the Jaú has not been exhaustively inventoried. 263 fish species have been recorded, and some of those were new to science when first catalogued. This is what undisturbed Amazonian black water looks like - not more biodiverse than neighboring whitewater ecosystems but different, with its own specialized communities.

## Animals That Need Space

The park's scale makes it suitable for animals that require large ranges to survive. The jaguar hunts across territories of dozens of square kilometers and does not tolerate habitat fragmentation well. The margay, smaller and more arboreal, depends on closed canopy. Giant otters live in family groups that patrol specific river stretches and have been extirpated from most of their old range by fur trade and habitat loss. The Amazonian manatee feeds on aquatic vegetation in slow water and has been hunted to scarcity across much of its historical range. All four are protected species within Jaú, and the park's size gives them room to exist as populations rather than as remnants. Pink river dolphins move through the black-water channels. Harpy eagles nest in the emergent canopy. The fauna is not as dense as along the fertile Solimões - black-water systems cannot support the same biomass - but it is intact, which is rarer and harder to restore.

## World Heritage, Growing

UNESCO inscribed Jaú National Park as a World Heritage Site in 2000. The Central Amazon Ecological Corridor, established in 2002, added management coordination across the wider region. In 2003 the property was expanded. Anavilhanas National Park, Amanã Sustainable Development Reserve, and Mamirauá Sustainable Development Reserve were joined to Jaú to form the Central Amazon Conservation Complex - a single World Heritage property spanning four distinct management units. In 2010 the Lower Rio Negro Mosaic brought the park into a broader regional protection framework. The Chico Mendes Institute for Biodiversity Conservation administers the park itself, classified as IUCN category II. The Amazon Region Protected Areas Program supports operations. The objective, as stated in the founding decree of September 24, 1980, is a mega-reserve for present and future generations - a phrase that sounded aspirational then and reads now as a specific instruction about how long conservation has to last.

## Flight Context

Centered at 2.17 degrees south, 62.62 degrees west, about 220 kilometers northwest of Manaus. Cruising altitudes of 10,000 to 20,000 feet reveal the immense canopy of the Negro-Solimões interfluvial plateau and the dark ribbons of the Jaú, Unini, and Carabinani rivers. Nearest airport is Manaus (SBEG), with small airstrips at Novo Airão and Barcelos serving regional flights. Expect strong afternoon convection year-round with peak storm activity in March and September.
