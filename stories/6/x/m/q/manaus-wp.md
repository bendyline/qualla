# Manaus

Capital of Amazonas state, Brazil - once the rubber boom's Paris of the Tropics, now a 2.3-million-person metropolis at the confluence of the Negro and Amazon rivers.

**Location:** -3.1189, -60.0217

**Categories:** Cities in Amazonas (Brazilian state), Manaus, Populated places on the Amazon

## Introduction

Half the members of an opera troupe once died of yellow fever in a single season performing here - and people kept buying tickets. That sentence captures something essential about Manaus at its height. In the late nineteenth century, rubber barons paid to ship a Parisian opera company across the Atlantic and up the Amazon so that the wives of latex millionaires could hear Verdi in the jungle. The deaths did not cancel the performances. They were part of the cost of doing business in a city that was briefly, improbably, one of the richest in South America. Then the rubber ran out, the opera house closed for eighty years, and Manaus faded into the isolated capital of Brazil's biggest state.

## From Fort to City

The Portuguese built a rock-and-clay fort here in 1669, naming it Sao Jose do Rio Negro, to keep the Dutch from creeping down from their base in Suriname. Four cannons guarded the curtains. Around the fort, indigenous mestizos settled and worked, and eventually the outpost became a village called Lugar da Barra. On November 13, 1832, it was elevated to a town and renamed Manaus - a spelling of the indigenous Manaos people whose territory this had been. The name has stuck through multiple administrative reshufflings. In 1848 the city was briefly renamed Cidade da Barra do Rio Negro under colonial law. In 1856 the governor restored it to Manaus, and Manaus it has remained. The word reportedly means Mother of the Gods in the Manaos language.

## The Cabanagem and Its Aftermath

Before rubber, there was revolution. The Cabanagem, beginning in 1835, was an uprising in which Black Brazilians, indigenous peoples, and mixed-race Amazonians - the cabanos, named for their thatched-roof dwellings - rose against the white political elite and briefly took power in Grao-Para. The rebels twice occupied Manaus. The repression that followed was catastrophic: the population of Grao-Para fell from about 100,000 to 60,000, a 40 percent collapse through killing, disease, and flight. Most of the dead were cabanos themselves - poor Amazonians with grievances that modern Brazilians can still trace in regional politics. The revolt also had a quieter consequence. The involvement of Upper Amazon rebels, later centered on Manaus, helped create a regional identity that eventually justified separating Amazonas from Para to form a new state. Revolution against oppression, paradoxically, helped draw the map.

## Rubber and Ruin

The rubber boom lasted roughly from 1880 to 1912, and in those three decades Manaus became one of the gaudiest cities on Earth. Historian Robin Furneaux wrote that no extravagance deterred the barons: one bought a vast yacht, another installed a tame lion in his villa, a third watered his horse on champagne. The city paid ten million public-funded dollars to build the Teatro Amazonas, with imported Italian marble, Scottish iron, and French glass. European opera companies steamed up the Amazon to perform - the one from which half died of yellow fever being only the most dramatic of many seasons. Then, in a quiet act of biopiracy, Henry Wickham smuggled rubber tree seeds out of Brazil. They were cultivated into plantations in British Malaya. Southeast Asian rubber, grown in rows rather than harvested from scattered wild trees, undercut the Amazon completely. By 1920 the boom had ended. Manaus had generated electricity before many European cities; now it could not afford to run its own generators. The opera house closed. The city waited eighty years for its next act.

## The Free Zone and the Second Boom

In 1957, a federal law created the Manaus Free Trade Port - a customs-exempt zone designed to attract manufacturing to a city the rest of Brazil had mostly forgotten. It took another decade before the structure really worked. In 1967, President Castelo Branco signed Decree-Law 288, creating the Manaus Free Trade Zone with tax incentives radiating outward for kilometers. Factories started arriving. By the late twentieth century, multinationals built television, motorcycle, and mobile phone plants here - LG, Samsung, Nokia, Siemens, and others. The idea was to prevent the Amazon from falling out of Brazil's economy entirely. It worked. Manaus grew from 343,000 residents in 1960 to over 2.2 million today, making it Brazil's seventh-largest city. The growth has not been gentle. Favelas climb the red-earth hillsides. The historic center, once restored for the 2014 World Cup, still hosts beggars and informal vendors alongside colonial architecture. But the city is now undeniably a city - not just a rubber ghost, but a living metropolis.

## The Pandemic City

In 2020 and 2021, Manaus became a grim laboratory for COVID-19. The first wave infected an estimated 76 percent of the city's population - so many that researchers wondered if herd immunity might prevent a second wave. It did not. In January 2021, a variant called P.1 - later renamed Gamma - emerged in Manaus and tore through a population that had already been infected once. Hospitals ran out of oxygen. Patients died gasping while pleas for supplies circulated on social media. The tragedy exposed both the country's public-health failures and the particular vulnerability of a city hours by air from anywhere else. Manaus has since recovered, more or less, and returned to the rhythm it has kept for fifty years: manufacturing, tourism, and slow struggle with a geography that connects it to the world only by air and water.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.1189 S, 60.0217 W, at the confluence of the Rio Negro and Solimoes rivers. Eduardo Gomes International Airport (SBEG/MAO) is 20 km north of downtown. Manaus Air Force Base (SBMN) occupies former Ponta Pelada Airport south of the city. The Meeting of the Waters - two kilometers of black and tan river running unmixed - is visible from 3,000-6,000 feet on clear days, just east of downtown. The city is a red-earth and concrete sprawl surrounded by dense forest; the pink-walled Teatro Amazonas is a downtown landmark. Wet season (December-May) brings severe afternoon storms. Dry season offers beaches along the Rio Negro.
