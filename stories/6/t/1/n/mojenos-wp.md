# Mojeños

The Mojeños are an Arawak-descended people of Bolivia's Beni Department whose ancestors engineered thousands of artificial hills and hydraulic systems across the Amazon lowlands centuries before European contact.

**Location:** -15.6670, -65.9170

## Introduction

Thousands of artificial hills, some rising sixty feet above the floodplain, dot the lowlands of Bolivia's Beni Department. Hundreds of rectangular ponds, each carefully dug to a depth of about three feet, connect to canals that link rivers and catch seasonal floodwater. These are not natural formations. They are the work of the ancestors of the Mojenos -- also known as Moxenos or Moxos -- an Arawak-descended people who engineered one of the most sophisticated hydraulic civilizations in pre-Columbian South America. The great earthworks, dated to around 250 CE, turned a flood-prone wilderness into productive farmland, and their remnants still mark the landscape today.

## Architects from the East

The story begins far from Bolivia. Between the 15th and 8th centuries BCE, agricultural Arawak groups from the lowlands of present-day Suriname abandoned their homeland and migrated west and south, carrying with them a tradition of incised ceramics and a talent for large-scale construction. The Arawak language family would eventually span an enormous range -- from the Caribbean islands of Cuba and Hispaniola to the interior of South America -- making it one of the most geographically widespread indigenous language groups in the Americas. The branch that reached the Bolivian lowlands became the Moxos, and they brought their architectural ambitions with them. Pottery fragments found as far afield as the outskirts of modern Santa Cruz de la Sierra confirm that Arawak-descended peoples once inhabited a vast stretch of the Bolivian lowlands.

## The Kingdom of Grigota

When Spanish chronicler Diego Felipe de Alcaya described the peoples of the region, he found not scattered bands but an organized confederation. Between the final spurs of the Andes and the central arm of the Guapay River, communities stretched along the banks and across the plains, allied under a leader Alcaya called a king. This ruler bore the dynastic name Grigota, wore a vividly colored shirt, and lived in a comfortable dwelling. Beneath him served chiefs -- caciques named Goligoli, Tundi, and Vitupue -- each commanding hundreds of warriors. Whether Alcaya's account exaggerates the hierarchy is debatable, but the physical evidence of centralized planning is not. The canal systems, raised fields, and artificial mounds required coordinated labor on a scale that implies strong political organization.

## Missions and New Identities

Jesuit priests arriving from Santa Cruz de la Sierra began evangelizing the region in the 1670s, establishing a chain of missions near the Mamore River. The first was Loreto, and the principal mission followed at Trinidad in 1686. What emerged from this encounter was not simple conversion but a complex process of ethnogenesis. Distinct Mojeno identities crystallized around different mission towns: Mojeno-Trinitarios at Trinidad, Mojeno-Loretanos at Loreto, Mojeno-Javerianos, and Mojeno-Ignacianos at San Ignacio de Moxos. The Jesuits, for their part, accepted the indigenous names for God in local languages and tried to embrace cultural elements not contrary to Christian practice. By the early twentieth century, the Mojenos numbered around 30,000, their identity shaped as much by the mission experience as by their Arawak heritage.

## Spirits of Jungle and Water

The Mojenos' cosmology divides the world into three realms: the sky above, the earth here, and the underworld below. A father creator governs the earth in collaboration with spirits called duenos -- masters of particular places and things -- and with the ancestors, who help maintain justice and balance. The principal duenos are the spirit of the jungle, connected with the jaguar, and the spirit of the water, connected with the rainbow. When the community drifts from its norms, the result is a spiritual sickness that requires collective diagnosis and treatment through prayer, ritual dance, and natural remedies. These dances are not performances for outsiders; they are acts of renewal, recharging both the community and the universe. Today, many Mojeno communities remain affiliated with the Central de Pueblos Indigenas del Beni, continuing to organize around the same lowland geography their ancestors reshaped with mounds and canals more than seventeen centuries ago.
