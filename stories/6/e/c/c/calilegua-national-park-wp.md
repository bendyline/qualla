# Calilegua National Park

On the eastern slopes of the Andes, a single road climbs through a cloud forest so rich it shelters half of Argentina's birds - and the jaguars that hunt among them.

**Location:** -23.6390, -64.8380

**Categories:** National parks of Argentina, Protected areas of Jujuy Province, Protected areas established in 1979, Southern Andean Yungas

## Introduction

Most of Argentina's far northwest is dry - bleached canyons, cactus, and the bare bright stone of the Quebrada. Then the land tips eastward off the Andes, the clouds catch on the slopes, and everything turns green. This is the yungas, a band of subtropical mountain rainforest that drapes the eastern foothills in fog and dripping vegetation. Calilegua National Park protects the largest stretch of it in the country: 76,306 hectares of cloud forest where jaguars move through the undergrowth and roughly half of Argentina's bird species can be found. It is the biggest national park in the Argentine Northwest, and one of its wildest places.

## Three Forests in One Climb

A single mountain road, Provincial Route 83, threads up through the park, and as it climbs it passes through three distinct worlds stacked one atop another. At the bottom, around 500 meters, sit the steamy jungle foothills. Higher up the slopes give way to dense montane cloud forest, where mist hangs in the canopy for much of the year. Higher still the trees thin into foggy highland grassland, near 3,600 meters. The yungas - also called the Tucumano-Oranense forest - are a transition zone, the green seam stitching the high Andes to the eastern lowlands. To drive Route 83 is to climb through that seam, watching one ecosystem dissolve into the next.

## Where the Jaguar Still Hunts

Calilegua shelters the jaguar, the largest predator in South America, along with a full guild of smaller cats: the puma, the ocelot, the slim long-bodied jaguarundi, and the small spotted pampas cat. Through the lower forest moves the tapir, the largest land mammal of the yungas, a heavy, ancient-looking browser with a flexible prehensile snout. These are not animals you are likely to see - the forest is too thick, the cats too wary - but the park exists in large part to keep enough unbroken habitat that they have somewhere to be. Protecting a top predator like the jaguar means protecting everything beneath it on the food chain too.

## Half a Country's Birds in One Forest

The real spectacle at Calilegua is feathered. The dense, layered yungas is one of the richest bird habitats in Argentina, home to a remarkable share of the nation's species - by one widely cited reckoning, roughly half of all the birds recorded in the entire country. Toucans, parrots, hummingbirds, and dozens of less famous forest dwellers fill the canopy with sound. The vertical stacking of habitats helps: each band of forest, from humid foothill to misty highland, supports its own community of birds, so a single climb up the mountainside passes through one avian neighborhood after another. For birdwatchers, Calilegua is a pilgrimage.

## Rain on Schedule

The yungas are wet because the mountains make them so. Moist air rolling in from the east hits the Andean wall and dumps its water on these slopes - up to 1,800 millimeters a year in the wettest zones, most of it falling in torrential bursts between November and April. In those months the rain comes down like a faucet, feeding the streams and keeping the canopy perpetually damp. Temperatures are mild, averaging around 17°C in winter and 28°C in summer, though high summer can spike past 40°C and the loftier elevations see frost in winter. The result is a forest that feels tropical at its feet and almost alpine at its crown.

## A Park With Neighbors

Calilegua was established on July 19, 1979, in the Ledesma Department on the eastern slopes of the Calilegua hills. It does not stand alone - the region carries a strong human imprint, and the small towns around the park keep alive local customs, languages, and traditions, including those of the Kolla communities of the northwest. Visitors come for ecotourism: forest trails, birdwatching, and the chance to see one of the continent's great transitional ecosystems intact. Not far north lies Baritú National Park, protecting another fragment of the same yungas. Together they guard a green ribbon along the Andes that most travelers, fixed on the painted deserts to the west, never imagine is there.

## Flight Context

Calilegua National Park lies at 23.64°S, 64.84°W, on the eastern Andean slopes in the Ledesma Department of Jujuy Province. From above it appears as a sharp contrast: deep-green forested mountains rising abruptly from the drier lowlands to the east, often capped or veiled by cloud - the defining signature of the yungas. Terrain ranges from about 500 m at the foothills to 3,600 m at the highland grasslands, so expect significant relief and frequent low cloud. Best viewed 9,000-13,000 ft AGL in the dry winter season (May-October); the wet season (November-April) brings heavy rain, low ceilings, and reduced visibility. Nearest major airport: Gobernador Horacio Guzmán International, San Salvador de Jujuy (SASJ), roughly 100 km west-southwest; Martín Miguel de Güemes International at Salta (SASA) lies about 160 km south. The forest canopy and persistent cloud make this terrain to respect, not skim.
