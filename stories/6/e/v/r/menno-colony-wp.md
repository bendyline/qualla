# Menno Colony

The first Mennonite settlement in South America, carved out of the brutal Paraguayan Chaco by Canadian farmers who crossed an ocean to keep teaching their children in German.

**Location:** -22.5100, -60.0100

**Categories:** Populated places in the Boquerón Department, German diaspora in Paraguay, Mennonitism in Paraguay, Populated places established in 1926, Russian Mennonite diaspora in South America

## Introduction

A school lesson sent them across the world. In 1917, Canada made secular education compulsory and English the language of the classroom, and to the most conservative Mennonites of Manitoba and Saskatchewan that ruling felt like an assault on the faith itself. Rather than surrender the German-language schooling at the heart of their community, roughly 1,743 of them sold their prairie farms, boarded ships, and sailed for one of the harshest landscapes in the Americas: the thorn-forest interior of the Paraguayan Chaco. In 1926 they founded Menno Colony, named for Menno Simons, the sixteenth-century Dutch reformer whose nonviolent followers became the Mennonites. It was the first Mennonite settlement in South America, and the first anywhere in the southern hemisphere.

## The Bargain in the Wilderness

Paraguay wanted bodies in the Chaco. The vast, undeveloped scrubland was claimed but barely held, and the government was willing to make extraordinary promises to fill it with industrious settlers. The concessions it granted the Mennonites read like a charter for a country within a country: exemption from military service, the right to run their own German schools, religious freedom, an open door for more immigrants, and a guarantee that they could govern their own affairs without interference. In exchange, the newcomers bought their land at an inflated price from the Argentine firm Casado, one of the largest landholders in the region. They had won the right to live as they wished. What they had not understood was the price the land itself would demand.

## The Green Hell

Nothing in the printed brochures prepared them for the Chaco. The settlers arrived with almost no experience farming under tropical conditions, and the reality was far more extreme than the promoters had promised. Summers seared, winters parched, and the long droughts broke only in torrential rains that turned the flat earth to mud. Markets were impossibly distant. Many of these conservative families also refused, on principle, to use modern agricultural machinery, which made every furrow harder. For decades, life in Menno was defined by deprivation, and most of what they grew they simply ate. In the 1950s, worn down by hardship and chafing against the colony's strict conservatism, a wave of families gave up and sailed back to Canada.

## The People Who Were Already Here

The Mennonites did not arrive in an empty land, whatever the word "undeveloped" implied. The central Chaco was the homeland of the Enlhet, whom the settlers called the Lengua, a people of the Maskoy language family who had hunted, fished, and gathered across these forests long before any ship left Manitoba. Roughly 600 lived in the immediate area at the founding. Over the decades, more indigenous people came, drawn by work, medicine, and schools, and in 1961 the Mennonites founded a cooperative, ASCIM, to support them. Today indigenous residents number around 25,000, outnumbering the Mennonites themselves. The relationship has been close but never equal: the two cultures have rarely intermarried, and labor in the colonies has often fallen hardest, and paid least, on indigenous hands.

## Milk and Money

The turning point came from grass and cattle. The introduction of drought-resistant buffalo grass from North America in 1955 laid the foundation for a cattle industry, and the completion of the Trans-Chaco Highway to Asunción in 1965 finally connected the colony to a market. In the 1980s, the agricultural cooperative borrowed against World Bank credit and bet heavily on dairy, and the bet paid off. The town of Loma Plata, home to roughly 3,500 people, became the prosperous administrative heart of a colony that now sprawls across some 7,500 square kilometers. The same isolation that once nearly broke Menno had, in the end, forged one of Paraguay's most productive agricultural regions, and the descendants of those who fled in the 1950s began to return.

## Flight Context

Menno Colony sits at roughly 22.51°S, 60.01°W in the central Paraguayan Chaco, with the town of Loma Plata as its hub. The terrain is flat and low, rarely above 300 meters, a near-featureless quilt of cleared dairy pasture and dark thorn-forest cut by the dead-straight ribbon of the Trans-Chaco Highway (Route PY09) running southeast toward Asunción. The nearest significant airfield is the Mennonite-built Mariscal Estigarribia airfield (ICAO SGME) to the northwest, with its unusually long Cold War-era runway; Asunción's Silvio Pettirossi International (ICAO SGAS) lies several hundred kilometers southeast. Best viewing is winter (June-August), when the dry-season air is clearest and the contrast between green pasture and gray scrub is sharpest. Summer haze, dust, and towering convective storms can sharply cut visibility.
