# Campo Grande

The capital of Mato Grosso do Sul, a state carved out of Mato Grosso in 1977, where an Okinawan community, a vast urban forest, and a Brazilian Air Force base share the cerrado plateau.

**Location:** -20.4686, -54.6222

**Categories:** State capitals in Brazil, Municipalities in Mato Grosso do Sul, Central-West Brazil, Japanese diaspora

## Introduction

In 1977, the Brazilian government did something unusual: it cut a state in half. Mato Grosso - already enormous, larger than many countries - was split along a line that left the old capital, Cuiaba, governing only the northern half. The southern half got its own state, Mato Grosso do Sul, and a new capital. The chosen city was not the largest, not the oldest, and not the most politically connected. It had simply, years before, outgrown Cuiaba in population - which is rare in Brazil, where capital cities usually are also the biggest. Campo Grande had been the region's economic center for a generation already. Now it became its political one too.

## From Waypoint to Capital

Before the city existed, its location was a waypoint. Travelers leaving Sao Paulo or Minas Gerais to reach northern Mato Grosso stopped here, where the plateau offered clear ground and shallow streams. Jose Antonio Pereira founded the settlement that would become Campo Grande in the nineteenth century. The early twentieth century brought a railway connecting the town to Corumba on the Bolivian border and eastward to Bauru in Sao Paulo state. The Western Brazilian Army Headquarters also came - a choice that stationed permanent military families in town and gave the settlement an administrative weight out of proportion to its size. Between 1970 and 2007, the population climbed from 140,000 to 750,000. Today it crosses 900,000, making it Brazil's seventeenth-largest city and the third-largest in the Central-West region.

## The Okinawan Connection

Campo Grande's cultural profile departs from Brazilian norms in one especially distinctive way: it holds one of the largest Okinawan Japanese communities in Brazil. Immigrants from the Okinawa prefecture - historically distinct from mainland Japan, with their own language and food traditions - settled here in waves throughout the twentieth century. Their grandchildren and great-grandchildren now run restaurants, groceries, and farms that pass as fully local to outside eyes. Soba, the Okinawan noodle soup, is now the city's unofficial dish. Middle Easterners, Armenians, Portuguese, Germans, Italians, Spaniards, and Paraguayans settled in smaller but still influential numbers. Praça da Republica on Afonso Pena Avenue holds a monument to Japanese immigration. Praça Oshiro Takamari hosts the weekly Aboriginal Fair. The actress Aracy Balabanian, born here in 1940 and a fixture of Brazilian television for decades, traces her descent to Armenian immigrants - a reminder that the mixing in Campo Grande runs deeper than tourism brochures suggest.

## An Urban Forest on the Plateau

The city's defining physical fact is trees. Campo Grande is one of the most densely wooded urban areas in Brazil, with 96.3 percent of houses shaded by canopy. The municipality holds Parque das Nacoes Indigenas at 119 hectares, the 135-hectare Prosa State Park created in 2002, and the Parque Estadual Matas do Segredo. Tropical savanna climate - Koppen Aw - drives seasonal extremes. January arrives warm and rainy, with highs averaging 29 degrees Celsius. July turns dry and cool, with lows around 14 degrees and occasional near-freezing nights. Annual rainfall reaches 1,465 millimeters. The altitude - several hundred meters above the surrounding Pantanal swampland - gives Campo Grande a climate measurably more extreme than lowland Brazilian cities, though still moderate by global standards.

## Cattle, Soy, and Airbases

The economy rests on agribusiness. Campo Grande anchors slaughterhouses that supply domestic and international markets, exporting beef alongside soy, rice, manioc, and increasingly sugar cane. IBGE data lists 11,657 commercial enterprises and 1,300 industrial ones. The city's GDP reached R$20.7 billion in 2013, ranking it the richest in Mato Grosso do Sul and the 33rd richest in Brazil. Per capita income that year hit R$24,839. Campo Grande International Airport shares its runways with Campo Grande Air Force Base - ALA5, among the most important bases of the Brazilian Air Force - a dual-use arrangement that sends passenger jets taxiing alongside military transports. Three federal highways converge here: BR-262 to Corumba and Bolivia west and Sao Paulo east, BR-163 to Cuiaba north and Dourados south, and BR-060 toward Brasilia.

## Morenao and the Marathon

The Estadio Universitario Pedro Pedrossian, opened in 1971, seats 45,000 and carries the nickname Morenao - the big brown - after the city's own sobriquet. Campo Grande's main football clubs, Esporte Clube Comercial and Operario Futebol Clube, play their biggest matches here. Auto racing has its venue too: the Orlando Moura International Autodrome runs a 3,433-meter circuit. Every October, the Volta das Nacoes mini-marathon fills Parque das Nacoes Indigenas with 26,400 runners, including professional athletes from Kenya and elsewhere. The Festa Junina arrives in June with bonfires, typical food, and rural-themed dancing. The annual Campo Grande Film Festival, held every January and February since 2004, has become a major showcase for independent Brazilian cinema. For a city built at the intersection of cattle plains and military logistics, Campo Grande has taken on far more cultural life than its geography suggested it would.

## Flight Context

Located at 20.47 degrees S, 54.62 degrees W at roughly 600 meters elevation on the central Brazilian plateau. Campo Grande International Airport (SBCG) shares operations with Campo Grande Air Force Base (ALA5) - expect military and commercial traffic mixed. The airport can receive Boeing 737 and Airbus A320. Best viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 feet to appreciate the heavily wooded urban grid, major parks scattered through the city, and the transition toward the Pantanal wetlands visible to the west.
