# Tupã, São Paulo

Named for a thunder god and founded on ground cleared by a violent frontier, Tupã holds Brazil's great museum of Kaingang memory, a century-old Latvian colony, and the country's biggest peanut harvest.

**Location:** -21.9350, -50.5139

**Categories:** brazil, sao-paulo-state, indigenous-history, museums, immigration, agriculture

## Introduction

The museum at the heart of Tupã is named for a Kaingang woman who, the story told here goes, climbed ten metres into a jequitibá tree and sang from daybreak to sunset. Vanuíre had been brought west from Campos Novos do Paranapanema by Brazil's Indian Protection Service, sent to persuade her own people to come in from the forest ahead of the surveyors and the rails. What she sang were peace songs, and the stakes were not abstract: an estimated nine in ten Kaingang in this region died between 1905 and 1921, killed by violence and by the diseases that travelled with it. On 19 March 1912 ten Kaingang warriors walked unarmed into the camp of the men clearing the line. That day is remembered as the beginning of the end of open fighting in western São Paulo. Vanuíre died in 1918, at Icatu. Eleven years later a businessman from Pernambuco named Luiz de Souza Leão founded a town on the cleared ground and called it Tupã, after the thunder god.

## Keeping the Memory in the Room

In 1966 the state of São Paulo opened the Museu Histórico e Pedagógico Índia Vanuíre in Tupã, and it has since become one of the most important collections of Indigenous material culture in Brazil, holding roughly 38,000 objects from Kaingang, Krenak and many other peoples. What makes it unusual is who it answers to. The museum works directly with Kaingang and Krenak communities from the Vanuíre and Icatu Indigenous Lands, the two reserves where survivors of the frontier period were settled, and it presents their history as a living one rather than a closed case. The word pacification appears in the older literature about 1912. It is worth reading with a hard eye: what was being pacified was the resistance of people whose land was being taken, and the peace that followed cost them nearly everything. The museum does not pretend otherwise.

## A Town Between Two Rivers

Tupã was founded on 12 October 1929 on land that was then tropical forest, and became a municipality by state law in 1938. It sits between the Aguapeí and the Rio do Peixe, on the ridge of high ground that gives the Alta Paulista region its name, some 520 kilometres west-northwest of the city of São Paulo. The municipality covers about 628 square kilometres at an elevation near 524 metres, and its climate is the mild tropical highland variety of the interior: an annual average around 24.5 degrees Celsius and roughly 1,365 millimetres of rain, most of it in summer. Around 65,000 people live here, making Tupã the second-largest city of the Alta Paulista. Beyond the seat, the municipality takes in three districts with three very different histories: Parnaso, Universo and Varpa.

## Varpa, an Ear of Grain on the Rio do Peixe

In November 1922 a group of Latvian Baptists began settling the right bank of the Rio do Peixe, refugees from a Europe reordered by the Russian Revolution, where the new Bolshevik authorities were tightening the screws on religious practice. They called their colony Varpa, a Latvian word meaning ear of grain. More than 2,400 Latvians eventually arrived. They cleared forest, organised themselves as a cooperative, built houses in a plainly European idiom, and raised a Baptist church that was for a time the largest in South America, with room for over a thousand people seated. A century later the district still keeps the thread: Varpa celebrates Ligo, the Latvian midsummer festival, and the surnames on its gravestones are not the surnames of anywhere else in São Paulo.

## Peanuts, and the Business of Looking

Tupã is the leading peanut-producing municipality in Brazil, with something on the order of 20,000 hectares under the crop, alongside corn and cassava. That is the economy you would expect. The other one is stranger. The city has also claimed the title of national capital of photography, on the strength of roughly 130 businesses in the photographic trade concentrated in a town of 65,000 people, a cluster with no obvious geographic logic and a great deal of local pride behind it. Tupã is formally designated an Estância Turística, one of the state's tourism-priority municipalities, which brings state funding for the museum and the town's cultural calendar. Between the peanut harvest, the camera shops and the Kaingang collection, the place is considerably harder to summarise than its size suggests.

## Getting There

Tupã is reached from the capital by the SP-294, the Comandante João Ribeiro de Barros highway that runs the spine of the Alta Paulista. The city grew up on the Tronco Oeste railway line, and the old rail geometry still shows in the street plan. José Vicente Faria Lima State Airport (SDTP) sits at the edge of town and handles general aviation. The Catholic parishes belong to the Diocese of Marília, about 80 kilometres east, which is also the nearest city with a commercial airport. If you make only one stop, make it the Índia Vanuíre museum. It is the reason a town founded in 1929 has a memory that reaches much further back.

## Flight Context

Tupã lies at 21.94 degrees S, 50.51 degrees W in the Alta Paulista region of western São Paulo state, at an elevation of roughly 524 metres, on the divide between the Aguapeí River to the north and the Rio do Peixe to the south. From the air the city reads as a compact grid on high ground, with the two river valleys framing it and the straight line of the old Tronco Oeste railway running through. Surrounding land is peanut, corn and cassava cultivation with pasture, so field colour shifts markedly between the wet and dry seasons. José Vicente Faria Lima State Airport (SDTP) serves the city for general aviation; the nearest commercial field is Marília (SBML), about 80 km east, with Presidente Prudente (SBDN) roughly 130 km west and Araçatuba (SBAU) about 100 km north. Recommended viewing altitude is 4,000 to 8,000 feet AGL. Conditions are generally clear and stable from May to September; expect afternoon thunderstorms from November through March.
