# Araçatuba

For three decades the price of Brazilian beef was set in pencil in a public square in Araçatuba, a railway camp of 1908 that grew into the northwest of São Paulo's largest city and still answers to the name Capital of Fat Cattle.

**Location:** -21.2089, -50.4328

**Categories:** brazil, sao-paulo-state, railways, cattle, cities, agriculture

## Introduction

For more than thirty years, a good part of the price of Brazilian beef was settled in a public square. Ranchers came in from Minas Gerais, Goiás and Mato Grosso and gathered under the trees of what is now Praça Rui Barbosa in central Araçatuba, and there, weekly, they argued out the value of the arroba, the fifteen-kilo unit Brazilian cattle are still traded in. Nothing was electronic and very little was even written down. Figures went into notebooks in pencil or were sealed by word alone, on the understanding that a man who broke his word in that square would not be doing business in it again. The nickname stuck in the 1950s and has never come off: Capital do Boi Gordo, capital of the fat cattle. Araçatuba is a city of over 200,000 people now, with universities and a gas pipeline and an airport, and it is still known nationally for a market that ran on handshakes.

## The Camp at the End of the Rails

Araçatuba began on 2 December 1908 as a work camp on the Estrada de Ferro Noroeste do Brasil, the railway being driven northwest across São Paulo toward the Mato Grosso frontier. Its name is Tupi: arasá, the araçá fruit, plus tyba, an abundance of, so the place was known for its thickets of Psidium cattleianum long before any surveyor arrived. Camps like this usually disappeared once the rail head moved on. This one did not. It became a municipality on 8 December 1921, and by then the settlers were arriving in numbers, Italians and Japanese and Spaniards and Portuguese, taking up land the railway had made reachable. The line also brought sickness. Epidemics of cutaneous leishmaniasis broke out along the Noroeste during its construction in 1907 and 1908, spreading with the deforestation that opened the route.

## Coffee, Cotton, Cattle

Three crops built the city in sequence. Coffee ran from roughly 1910 into the 1930s, on the same speculative wave that pushed plantations across the whole interior of São Paulo. Cotton took over from the 1930s into the 1950s. Then came the cattle, and with them the square. The transition was less a collapse than a reallocation: land that had been worked intensively by hand was turned to pasture, which needs far fewer people and far more space. That is the shape of the northwest today, and it is why the arroba mattered so much here. Araçatuba was the point where the herds of three states met a railhead, and where the men who owned them could look each other in the face.

## What the Herds Paid For

The modern city holds about 208,000 people across 1,167 square kilometres, sitting at roughly 390 metres near the Tietê River and the Ribeirão Baguaçu, some 530 kilometres from the state capital. Cattle no longer dominate the ledger. Services account for just under 80 percent of local GDP, industry for about 19 percent, and primary production for under 2 percent, a distribution that describes a regional hub rather than a farm town. Araçatuba anchors a microregion of eight municipalities spread over 5,365 square kilometres, and it has become the university centre for the northwest of the state. The Brazil-Bolivia gas pipeline crosses here, and the Tietê-Paraná waterway gives the city a barge connection to the interior. Expô Araçatuba, held annually, is among the largest agricultural fairs in the country, which is the old economy showing through the new one.

## When the Sandfly Moved to Town

In 1997 researchers recorded Lutzomyia longipalpis, the sandfly that transmits visceral leishmaniasis, in an urban area of São Paulo state for the first time. The place was Araçatuba. An outbreak followed at the end of the decade, affecting dogs heavily and people too, and it forced a rethink of a disease that had been filed away as rural and remote. The lesson public health researchers drew was blunt: old assumptions do not survive rapid social change. A city that grows outward into pasture and scrub, with dogs in every yard and vegetation at the edge of every new subdivision, builds the conditions the parasite needs. Control programmes in Araçatuba have been studied ever since as a template for what urban leishmaniasis looks like and how hard it is to reverse.

## Two Wheels and the River

One statistic captures how Araçatuba actually moves: motorcycles and scooters make up 37 percent of the city's registered vehicles, well above the Brazilian average of 27 percent. It is flat, warm and spread out, and a bike is the sensible answer. The SP-300, the Marechal Cândido Rondon highway, and the SP-463 carry the road traffic; Dario Guarita Airport (SBAU) handles the flights. On the northern edge, the Tietê draws people out of town on weekends to the yacht club and the jet skis, a river that was a barrier when the railway crews arrived and is now a recreation ground. The football is provided by Associação Esportiva Araçatuba, latest in a line of clubs stretching back to the railway workers' Clube Atlético Ferroviário.

## Flight Context

Araçatuba lies at 21.21 degrees S, 50.43 degrees W in northwestern São Paulo state, at an elevation of roughly 390 metres, on rolling terrain between the Tietê River to the north and the Ribeirão Baguaçu. The municipality covers about 1,167 square kilometres. From the air the city presents as a large, low-rise urban area with a distinct grid and the linear scar of the old Noroeste railway running through it; the Tietê and its reservoir make the strongest navigational reference in the area. Surrounding land is overwhelmingly pasture and sugarcane. Dario Guarita Airport (SBAU) serves the city directly; alternates are Marília (SBML) about 100 km southeast, Presidente Prudente (SBDN) about 175 km southwest, and São José do Rio Preto (SBSR) about 130 km northeast. Recommended viewing altitude is 4,000 to 9,000 feet AGL. Best visibility runs May to September; expect smoke haze during cane harvest and strong afternoon convection in the December-March wet season.
