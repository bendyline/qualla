# Lavras

Founded by a Paulista rebel hunting gold in 1729, Lavras later became known as the 'land of ipês and schools' — a Minas Gerais town that turned its name for mining excavations into an academic legacy.

**Location:** -21.2450, -45.0000

**Categories:** Municipalities in Minas Gerais, Colonial settlements, University towns, 1729 establishments in Brazil

## Introduction

Around 1720, a man wanted by the Portuguese crown rode west into the backcountry of what is now southern Minas Gerais. His name was Francisco Bueno da Fonseca, and he was a Paulista — a restless frontier type from Santana de Parnaíba, one of the old bandeirante stomping grounds of São Paulo. Eight years earlier, in 1712, he had led a revolt against a Portuguese judge sent to collect taxes in São Paulo. Running from colonial authority, he headed for the gold. He and his sons settled in the fertile country where the Capivari and Mortes rivers pour into the Rio Grande, establishing a hamlet they called Sant'Ana das Lavras do Funil around 1729. The name was blunt and specific: lavras meant the open-pit excavations where prospectors dug alluvial gold, and the country around it held plenty of both. Three centuries later, the gold is long gone. The town, somehow, has outlasted its own reason for existing.

## The Bueno Family's Century

The Bueno family did not fade into frontier obscurity. They became local power. In 1737, Governor Martinho de Mendonça de Pina e Proença granted the settlers a sesmaria — a colonial land grant — formalizing their occupation and encouraging the agricultural and livestock activity that would outlast the gold. The family produced Bartolomeu Bueno do Prado, the grandson of the legendary bandeirante Anhanguera (Bartolomeu Bueno da Silva) and son-in-law of Francisco Bueno da Fonseca. On June 18, 1759, Bueno do Prado led 400 armed men out of the hamlet to dismantle the Quilombo do Campo Grande — a confederation of settlements founded by escaped enslaved Africans in the western backlands of Minas Gerais. This is a brutal chapter. The expedition ended in the destruction of free Black communities that had built lives away from the colonial plantation system, and the reward that came back to Lavras included land, status, and the capitão-mor title the Bueno family carried thereafter. By 1760, the town had 1,000 inhabitants, twice its neighbor Carrancas, and the parish seat was transferred here.

## Archaeology Beneath the Gold

Long before any Portuguese arrival, this land was inhabited. Archaeological sites across the region of Lavras document ceramic artifacts from the Aratu tradition — Indigenous pottery dated roughly between 800 and 1,200 years before the present, placing its makers here centuries before Francisco Bueno da Fonseca ever sighted the Rio Grande. The Aratu people and their successors left the ground seeded with evidence of settlements, toolwork, and burial practices that researchers continue to document today. These findings complicate the frontier narrative. The bandeirantes who claimed to be discovering empty territory were, as they often were, walking into land with its own long human memory.

## City Rights and the Liberal Revolt

Lavras achieved town rights in 1831 and formal city status in 1868, when authorities shortened its name from Lavras do Funil to simply Lavras. A fiscal report from 1832 described a modest settlement — 245 buildings, no paved streets, and only three private primary schools serving 62 students. The town's most dramatic political moment came in 1842, during the Liberal Revolution. For more than a month, from June 14 to July 22, conservative and liberal factions maintained rival headquarters across the Praça Dr. Augusto Silva, the square that still anchors the town center. The defeated liberals were scattered — some fled, some were imprisoned — and the episode ended with imperial amnesty. Lavras had experienced, in miniature, the ideological fault lines that would shape nineteenth-century Brazil.

## Land of Ipês and Schools

After the Proclamation of the Republic in 1889, Lavras reinvented itself as a regional center of education. The decades around the turn of the twentieth century saw a wave of new institutions: the Evangelical Institute, founded in 1892 by the American missionary Samuel Rhea Gammon; Our Lady of Lourdes College in 1900, run by the Congregation of the Auxiliary Sisters of Our Lady of Sorrows; the Lavras School Group founded in 1907 by Firmino Costa; and — the most consequential — the Lavras Agricultural School, founded in 1908 by Benjamin Harris Hunnicutt, which became the seed for what is now the Federal University of Lavras. The journalist Jorge Duarte coined the nickname that still sticks: terra dos ipês e das escolas, the land of ipê trees and schools. The ipês bloom spectacularly each year in yellow and purple waves across the Minas plateau. The schools continue producing teachers, agronomists, and researchers.

## The Immigrants Who Came

The 1920 Brazilian census painted a surprisingly international picture of small-town Lavras. Among 42,000 residents, the survey counted 806 foreigners — 1.9 percent of the population, but drawn from across the world. Italians dominated: 380 of them, the largest single immigrant community. There were 189 Portuguese, 166 Lebanese arriving in the wake of Ottoman-era emigration, 28 Spaniards, 20 Americans (mostly, probably, connected to the evangelical mission that founded the institute), 12 Austrians, five French, two Russians, one Uruguayan, and three of undetermined nationality. That demographic mix has continued to shape the town's cultural texture. Today Lavras holds around 105,000 people, anchored by the federal university, sitting at 919 meters of altitude with a mild humid subtropical climate, a Catholic diocese based in São João del-Rei, and the same square where liberals and conservatives once camped — now Praça Dr. Augusto Silva, a working public space surrounded by cafes and commerce.
