# Piranguinho

A railway village in the Sapucaí valley that turned rapadura and roasted peanuts into an identity, and once a year builds a slab of candy longer than a swimming pool.

**Location:** -22.4008, -45.5319

**Categories:** Municipalities in Minas Gerais, 1962 establishments in Minas Gerais, Food and drink, Festivals, Brazil

## Introduction

On a Saturday in June 2026, thirty-two groups of children each took charge of one metre of candy. What they were guarding, laid out in a single unbroken slab through the middle of Piranguinho, was 32.2 metres of pé-de-moleque weighing 480 kilograms — roasted peanuts set in molten rapadura, cooled hard, measured, certified, then cut up and handed out to everyone who had come to watch. It was the nineteenth edition of the Festa do Maior Pé de Moleque do Mundo and it beat the town's own 31-metre mark from the year before, which had beaten the 30-metre mark from the year before that. The record is registered with Ranking Brasil rather than Guinness, which does nothing to dampen the local view of the matter. This town of roughly nine thousand people in southern Minas Gerais has decided what it is, and it is peanut brittle.

## Maria Paulina's Bar

The story starts in 1909, at a bar beside the railway station kept by a woman named Maria Paulina. She sold a sweet to passengers waiting for trains, and the recipe her family used was not the one most of Brazil knows. Elsewhere pé-de-moleque means peanuts bound in caramelised sugar. In Piranguinho it means peanuts bound in rapadura — unrefined whole cane sugar, boiled down and set, darker and more mineral than caramel, tasting faintly of molasses and smoke. That distinction is the whole business. More than a century later, families in the town still make it in open pans, and the state of Minas Gerais formally recognised the artisanal method as intangible cultural heritage in 2009, the same year Piranguinho took the title it now paints on its signs: National Capital of Pé de Moleque.

## The Baroness and the Sleepers

Piranguinho exists because of a railway that needed wood. In the late nineteenth century, under the empire of Dom Pedro II, coffee money was financing track across Minas Gerais under the banner of the Rede Mineira de Viação, and one planned line was to link Itajubá with Santa Rita do Sapucaí. The land it crossed belonged to Baroness Leocádia de Lourenço, and the forest on it held exactly what the engineers needed: jacarandá, jequitibá, taiúva, pereira — hardwoods dense enough to make railway sleepers that would not rot. A deal was struck. The Baroness would cut and supply the timber, recruit the labour, straighten the course of the Ribeirão dos Porcos, and build a water-powered sawmill to process it all. Construction is believed to have begun in the 1880s. Workers came for the wages, put up houses of wood and pau-a-pique around the mill, and stayed. That cluster of sawmill housing is the town. Piranguinho did not become a municipality in its own right until 1963.

## The Sapucaí Valley

The town lies in the valley of the Sapucaí river, in the green folds of southern Minas between the Mantiqueira range and the São Paulo border, where the climate stays mild most of the year and the mornings come up with mist in the low ground. BR-459 and MG-295 cut the town into sectors and carry most of what passes through. Anyone driving in on the MG-295 should note the speed camera at the urban boundary and the 40 km/h limit it enforces, a detail the town considers important enough to publish. It is that kind of place: small, specific, entirely certain about its own rules.

## A Year Measured in Metres

The festival calendar gives Piranguinho its shape. Rodeos run at the Centro Regional de Rodeio Paulo Max in each half of the year. July belongs to Santa Efigênia, the town's patron saint, and to the Catholic festa held in her name. But June is the one that draws the crowds — three days of music, dancing, regional cooking and, at the centre of it, the slow theatrical business of boiling rapadura and pouring it over peanuts while several thousand people watch. Roughly seventeen thousand visitors turned up for the 2025 edition. When the measuring is done and the number is announced, the record is immediately destroyed: the candy is broken into pieces and eaten. Next year the town starts again, and tries to add a metre.

## Flight Context

Piranguinho sits at 22.40°S, 45.53°W in the Sapucaí river valley of southern Minas Gerais, in rolling terrain on the northwestern flank of the Serra da Mantiqueira. Give yourself altitude here — the surrounding ridges rise well above the valley floor, and 6,000–8,000 ft MSL keeps you clear while still showing the pattern of small farms, pasture and gallery forest along the river. Visual references are the Sapucaí itself, the BR-459 corridor running southwest toward Itajubá, and the town of Itajubá a few kilometres away. Nearest paved fields of any size are Varginha (SBVG) to the northwest and Guaratinguetá (SBGW) across the Mantiqueira in the Paraíba valley to the south; Confins at Belo Horizonte (SBCF) is the region's major hub. Morning valley fog is common in the cooler months, and afternoon build-ups over the Mantiqueira are routine in the December-to-March wet season.
