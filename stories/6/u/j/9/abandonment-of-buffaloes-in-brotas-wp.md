# Abandonment of Buffaloes in Brotas

An anonymous tip in November 2021 led police to a farm outside Brotas where more than a thousand buffaloes had been left without pasture or water, in a case Brazilian animal-welfare groups came to describe as one of the worst in the country's history.

**Location:** -22.1833, -48.1957

**Categories:** Environmental crimes in Brazil, Cruelty to animals in Brazil, 21st century in São Paulo, Animal welfare, Brazil

## Introduction

The tip that reached the civil police in Brotas in November 2021 was vague in the way anonymous tips usually are. There were buffaloes on a farm out past the edge of town, the caller said - at least five hundred of them - and they had nothing to eat and no water to drink. Officers drove out to Fazenda São Luiz da Água Sumida, a property straddling the boundary between Brotas and Ribeirão Bonito in the interior of São Paulo state. What they found was worse than the call had suggested. The herd numbered more than a thousand. Many of the animals were female and pregnant. And someone had been running a tractor across the pasture.

## What the Officers Walked Into

The inquiry that followed would account for 1,056 buffaloes and 72 horses and mares on the Água Sumida farm. The animals had been left in a stretch of land stripped of vegetation, with no trough, spring, or river within reach. Douglas Brandão do Amaral, the police chief who took the case, described watching the pasture being deliberately ruined the day before the arrest - a tractor tearing up grass that was still good. His conclusion was blunt. "We saw that his intention, I still don't know for what reason, was to kill these buffaloes," he said. Because he read the destruction as deliberate, he charged the case in material competition: not one count of animal mistreatment, but one count for every animal. Luiz Augusto Pinheiro de Souza, the farm's owner, was arrested on November 11, 2021.

## The Farm Called Vanished Water

Água Sumida means vanished water, and the name turned out to be a grim accident of geography. Investigators later concluded that the pastures had been given over to soybean cultivation, the herd left in place as the fields around it changed use. Animals died. More than a hundred are recorded as having perished during the abandonment, and officers found carcasses on the property - some in the open, others that investigators came to suspect had been buried there. These were not statistics on a page. They were pregnant water buffalo, animals bred for docility and dependence, standing in a field waiting for feed that never came. Souza denied that he had abandoned the herd at all.

## A Case the Country Followed

The story moved fast through Brazilian media. Newspapers, television, and the Sunday newsmagazine *Fantástico* covered it; celebrities amplified the appeals; animal-protection organizations mobilized to keep the surviving animals alive. Souza had already been fined R$2.13 million over the treatment of animals before this case broke, and fines imposed by the Environmental Police in connection with the Brotas herd reached R$3.5 million. Taken together, prosecutors calculated, the charges could theoretically total 500 years in prison. There was an awkward local footnote, too: Souza owned a restaurant in Brotas called Fatto di Bufala, its menu built partly on cheese made from the milk of the species now starving on his land. After the case broke, he sent a message to Brotas mayor Leandro Corrêa, who said he felt intimidated by it and passed the material to the investigating police chief.

## Feeding a Thousand Survivors

Rescue is the dramatic word, but what actually happened afterward was slower and less cinematic. The surviving buffaloes stayed where they were, and an animal-protection organization took over their daily care - hay, water, veterinary attention, hired hands - at a running cost of tens of thousands of reais every month. Ownership of the herd was disputed in court between the farmer and the NGO while the feeding continued. Dry-season drought made it harder still; in the months when the pasture browns and the rivers drop, keeping more than a thousand large animals fed became a logistical problem measured in truckloads. Donations carried much of it. Public attention, which arrives in a rush, has to be converted into something that lasts years.

## The Sentence

In January 2025, more than three years after the tip, a court convicted Souza of mistreating the animals. The sentence came to four years, seven months, and ten days combined - one year of reclusão plus three years, seven months and ten days of detention - both to be served initially in a semi-open regime, along with 128 day-fines set at the minimum wage of the time. Souza said he would appeal and, under the ruling, was permitted to do so while remaining free; custody of the herd, already transferred to an animal-welfare organization back in November 2021, was not returned to him. It is a modest number of years against the arithmetic prosecutors once floated, and animal-welfare advocates said so. But the conviction established something Brazilian courts have rarely stated so plainly: that leaving livestock to die of thirst is not a business decision gone wrong. It is a crime, counted animal by animal.

## Flight Context

Fazenda São Luiz da Água Sumida sits at 22.18 degrees south, 48.20 degrees west, on the Brotas / Ribeirão Bonito municipal line in central São Paulo state, roughly 240 km northwest of the city of São Paulo. This is the Depressão Periférica: gently rolling terrain at 500 to 700 metres elevation, quilted in sugarcane, soy, and pasture, cut by the Jacaré-Pepira river. At 3,000 to 5,000 feet AGL the land reads as a patchwork of rectangular fields with dark riparian forest tracing the watercourses. Nearest field is Mário Pereira Lopes State Airport at São Carlos (SDSC), about 36 km northeast; Bauru-Arealva (SBAE) lies roughly 80 km west, and Araraquara (SBAQ) about 70 km north. Winter, from June to September, brings the clearest air and the driest ground; summer afternoons build heavy convective cloud from October through March.
