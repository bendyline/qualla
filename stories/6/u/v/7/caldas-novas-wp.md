# Caldas Novas

The world's largest hydro-thermal resort, where 86 wells pump water from a 600-meter quartzite column beneath the Serra de Caldas plateau in Goias.

**Location:** -17.7439, -48.6258

**Categories:** Municipalities in Goiás, Hot springs, Cerrado, Tourism in Brazil

## Introduction

Eighty-six wells pump water out of the ground at Caldas Novas. Together they draw 1,200 cubic meters an hour - roughly 320,000 gallons - for fourteen hours a day, every day, and the earth does not run dry. The water comes up between 34 and 57 degrees Celsius, warm enough to swim in year-round, and the source is not volcanic. For a long time people believed it must be. Early surveys imagined an ancient crater under the Serra de Caldas plateau, rainwater seeping into it, heating at depth, returning through cracks. More modern geological studies show the truth is stranger: rainfall stored in a layer of quartzite 600 meters below, pushed upward by the pressure of the rock column itself.

## References From 1545

The first written mentions of these thermal waters appeared not in Brazil but in Spain, in 1545 - decades before the Portuguese knew the region existed in any formal sense. What Spanish colonists had heard, and how, remains unclear. The next documented European encounter came in 1722, when Bartolomeu Bueno da Silva, son of the famous bandeirante Anhanguera, turned off the trail his father had blazed years earlier and found the springs that feed the Rio Quente. He called them Caldas Velhas - the Old Hot Springs - and they lie under what is now the Rio Quente Resorts complex. The newer springs at the current town of Caldas Novas were documented in 1777 by Martinho Coelho and Gustavo-Mauricio Silva de Carvalho Alves, and the town grew around them: a district by 1857, a municipality by 1911.

## The Numbers of a Boom Town

In 1980, Caldas Novas had 11,274 residents. By 2007, the population had reached 62,204 - nearly a sixfold expansion in less than three decades. The city grew at 4.18 percent a year through the late 1990s and early 2000s, driven almost entirely by a single industry. Tourism now employs almost two thousand people directly and supports more than eighty hotels with 12,000 beds between them, most with heated pools fed by the thermal system. High season brings 100,000 visitors. Carnival brings 300,000 - nearly five times the resident population - concentrated into one delirious week when Caldas Novas becomes the largest hydrothermal party on earth. Beyond tourism, the economy rests on soybeans (15,600 hectares in 2006), corn (3,600), sorghum, and dairy. But everything turns on the water.

## Guarding the Aquifer

When the Usina Hidreletrica Corumba I dam was planned on the Corumba River, engineers worried that its cold reservoir could infiltrate the geothermal water table and cool the springs. The utility Furnas Centrais Eletricas ran the most thorough study of the Caldas Novas thermal system to date. Their conclusion: no risk. The reservoir sits at an elevation of 595 meters; the source of the thermal water lies at 644 meters, safely above any possible infiltration path. To protect the recharge zone more directly, the state created the 12,315-hectare Caldas Novas State Park, covering the Serra de Caldas plateau itself - the catchment from which rain ultimately becomes hot spring. The park sits within the municipal boundaries. Without it, the aquifer would still exist, but with less certainty.

## Strange Waters in the Neighborhood

The thermal country around Caldas Novas holds stranger features than the main wells. Lago de Piratininga, a small lake near the city, boils in places - bubbling pockets where gas escapes or where the thermal column comes close enough to surface to heat shallow water. Lago de Corumba, the 64-square-kilometer reservoir formed by damming the Corumba River, reflects the cerrado sky in long cold sheets. The Rio Quente itself runs naturally warm, unusual for any tropical river and the basis of the resort complex downstream. At Rio Quente Resorts, 20 kilometers west in the smaller municipality, seven hotels spread across 500 square kilometers serve more than a million tourists annually - making the two-town system together the largest hot-water destination on the planet.

## A Quality of Life

On the United Nations Human Development Index, Caldas Novas scored 0.802, ranking it tenth out of 242 municipalities in Goias and 533rd out of 5,507 in Brazil. The literacy rate reached 92.5 percent in 2000. Schools enrolled 18,765 students across 52 institutions, and three universities maintained campuses in town. The numbers describe a mid-sized Brazilian city doing well - not Sao Paulo wealth, but far from rural poverty. What the figures do not capture is the strange constancy of the place: a town whose fundamental resource is older than any human settlement, rising from quartzite at a predictable rate, neither diminished by the tourists who come to bathe in it nor exhausted by the wells that carry it to hotel pools. The water that welled up for Bartolomeu Bueno in 1722 is still arriving.

## Flight Context

Located at 17.74 degrees S, 48.63 degrees W, 170 km south of Goiania in central Brazil. Nearest airport is Caldas Novas Airport (SWCN) for regional flights; primary connections through Goiania (SBGO) and Brasilia (SBBR). Best viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 feet to appreciate the Serra de Caldas plateau, Lago de Corumba to the south, and the thermal country extending toward Rio Quente. The 12,315-hectare state park appears as a distinct highland green mass above the town.
