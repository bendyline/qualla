# Palmelo

Goias's smallest municipality grew outward from a single Spiritist meeting hall founded in 1929, and it remains the only town in Brazil where the census finds Spiritists to be the largest religious group.

**Location:** -17.3239, -48.4239

**Categories:** Municipalities in Goiás, Religion, Brazil, Small towns

## Introduction

Fogo selvagem - wild fire - is the Brazilian name for an endemic form of pemphigus, an autoimmune disease that raises blisters across the skin and, before modern treatment, frequently killed. In the 1920s a man named Dorcelino Damasio da Silva had it. In the cerrado of southeast Goias, a group of his neighbors watched him recover, and what they believed they had witnessed rearranged the map. On February 9, 1929, eighteen of them founded a Spiritist center on open ranchland and named it Luz da Verdade, the Light of Truth. There was no town yet. The town came afterward, growing outward from the building the way villages elsewhere grow outward from a mill or a river crossing.

## Eighteen Founders and a Building

Palmelo is the smallest municipality in Goias, a state with more than 240 municipalities, and its dimensions still feel provisional. It broke away from neighboring Pires do Rio in 1953 to govern itself, though Pires do Rio wraps around it on the north, south, and east even now; only the western edge touches anything else, and that is Santa Cruz de Goias. Roughly 2,260 people lived here in 2007, nearly all of them in the small grid of the town itself, at a density of about 38 per square kilometer. Goiania, the state capital, is 128 kilometers away by BR-352 and GO-020. The Ribeirao Caiapo and its two tributaries cross the municipality on their way to the Corumba River. Summers are wet, winters bone-dry, and the thermometer rarely leaves the range between 16 and 26 degrees Celsius. None of that explains why anyone outside Goias has heard of the place.

## What Kardec Taught

Spiritism was codified in France in the 1850s by Allan Kardec, the pen name of the educator Hippolyte Leon Denizard Rivail. It holds that the spirit survives death and reincarnates, that certain people - mediums - can communicate with those who have died, and that charity is not optional. The doctrine found its largest following not in Europe but in Brazil, and in Palmelo it became civic infrastructure. Avenues carry the names of Kardec and of Emmanuel, a spirit guide well known to Brazilian Spiritists. There is an Escola Espirita and a pharmacy called Nosso Lar, after the most famous book in the Brazilian Spiritist canon. In 1937 the Sanatorio Euripedes Barsanulfo opened beside the Luz da Verdade center, a psychiatric sanatorium named for a Spiritist educator from Minas Gerais. It is still in operation.

## The People Who Come

Around three hundred mediums work at the center, unpaid, and visitors arrive from every state in Brazil. They come for what the center calls spiritual examination: sessions of passes, of mediums linking hands in chains, of procedures described as surgeries performed without incision. Tourism in Palmelo means this and almost nothing else. It is worth being plain about who makes the trip. Many arrive after hospitals have run out of answers, or carrying grief that has no clinical name, and they come with the specific hope that something here will help. Whether these treatments work in the way the center describes is not a question a visitor can settle, and the town does not press the argument. It simply keeps the doors open. The 2022 census confirmed what the town has claimed for decades - Palmelo is the only municipality in Brazil where Spiritists outnumber every other religious group.

## Cattle, Clay, and the Ordinary Week

Between sessions, Palmelo does what small Goias towns do. Cattle are raised for beef and for milk, and a dairy operated in town alongside three modest industrial units and twenty-two shops. The soil yields good clay, so bricks are made here. Oranges, manioc, and corn grow on 63 farms squeezed into 1,161 hectares, which is not much land to work with; the herds and the crops are small by regional standards because there is simply nowhere to put more of either. The public numbers are better than the poverty of the setting would suggest. One hospital with fourteen beds and two clinics serve the municipality. In 2000, infant mortality stood at 9.80 per thousand live births against a national figure of 33.0, among the lowest rates in the state, down from 40.97 a decade earlier. The municipal human development index of 0.787 ranked 18th of 242 municipalities in Goias.

## A Town Shaped by an Idea

Most towns are founded because of something in the ground - gold, water, a river crossing, a rail junction. Palmelo was founded because eighteen people changed their minds about the nature of the world and wanted somewhere to act on it. Nearly a century later, the ranchland around it is still ranchland and the brick kilns still burn, but the streets are named for a French educator and a spirit, and the buses keep arriving. Approaching from the air, there is little to distinguish Palmelo from a hundred other tidy grids scattered across the cerrado. The difference is entirely a matter of what people believe happens inside one of the buildings.

## Flight Context

Palmelo sits at 17.3239 degrees south, 48.4239 degrees west, in the rolling cerrado of southeast Goias at roughly 800 meters elevation. From 6,000 to 8,000 feet AGL the whole municipality fits in one window - it is the smallest in the state - with the compact urban grid set among pasture and the thin dark lines of the Ribeirao Caiapo drainage running toward the Corumba River. Pires do Rio, the larger regional center, lies just to the east and makes the easier visual fix; the BR-352 and GO-020 corridors thread the area. Nearest major fields are Goiania / Santa Genoveva (SBGO), about 128 km northwest, and Brasilia (SBBR) farther north. Best light is the dry season, roughly May through September, when haze from agricultural burning is the main visibility concern; the wet summer brings afternoon convection and towering cumulus over the plateau.
