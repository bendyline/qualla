# Yasuni National Park

The most biodiverse place on Earth, where 100,000 insect species and the Waorani people share a park that survived a national referendum on its future.

**Location:** -0.6600, -76.0700

**Categories:** National parks of Ecuador, Biosphere reserves, UNESCO biosphere reserves, Amazon rainforest, Indigenous territories, Biodiversity hotspots

## Introduction

One hectare of Yasuni National Park contains more insect species than the entire continent of North America. That sentence sounds like hyperbole until you check. Scientists keep counting, and they keep finding more. The numbers here are genuinely difficult to process. There are 121 reptile species, over 500 fish species, 596 bird species, 117 bat species. There are more amphibian species per unit area here than anywhere else on the planet. Ten monkey species swing through the canopy. Six caiman species patrol the dark waters. Yasuni does not hold some records. It holds most of them, and in categories you may not have heard of, like woody vines and vascular plants, it holds the world totals.

## How Yasuni Got Here

The Ecuadorian government declared Yasuni a national park in July 1979. UNESCO designated it a Biosphere Reserve in 1989. The park covers 982,300 hectares along the equator where the Andes gives way to the tropical lowland Amazon basin, a geographic transition zone where altitude and climate combine to maximize biological diversity. The territory also holds over 40 percent of Ecuador's proven oil reserves, which made Yasuni the hotspot in a battle between the oil industry and the conservation movement that ran for decades. President Rafael Correa launched the Yasuni-ITT Initiative in 2007, proposing that the international community pay Ecuador to leave the oil in the ground. The world pledged less than $400 million against the $3.6 billion price tag. Correa cancelled the program in 2013 and opened the park to drilling. But in August 2023, Ecuadorian voters approved a national referendum demanding the drilling stop. The state oil company was ordered to dismantle its equipment. For now, the park's biosphere integrity has outlasted petro-dollars.

## Creatures That Defy the Edges of Categories

Yasuni has at least 43 endemic animal species and 270 endemic plant species, organisms found nowhere else on Earth. One tree frog is called Osteocephalus yasuni because it was first described here. A round-eared bat called Lophostoma yasuni holds the same distinction. The Napo saki monkey swings through the canopy alongside the more common howler and spider monkeys. Giant otters, endemic to the area, hunt the rivers. Pink river dolphins, Inia geoffrensis, surface in the brown water. Black caimans can grow up to four meters long and weigh half a ton. The Sunset daggerwing butterfly flashes red, blue, and black across sunlit clearings. The Convict tree frog has stripes like a prisoner's uniform. Even cataloging what lives here would take multiple lifetimes, and researchers keep adding species.

## The People Who Were Already Here

Two indigenous peoples call Yasuni home. The Kichwa, who live in accessible villages and trade with the outside world, and the Huaorani (also spelled Waorani), some of whom live in contact with the Ecuadorian state and some who do not. The Tagaeri and Taromenane are Waorani sub-groups that rejected all outside contact after decades of oil-driven disruption. They remain in voluntary isolation, one of the last such peoples in South America, and their territory marked as the Zona Intangible is legally off-limits to outsiders. The Waorani's century-long struggle to keep oil companies out of their forest is what the 2023 referendum confirmed. Guided day trips visit Kichwa and accessible Huaorani villages, where visitors can see daily life and share meals. Respect protocols apply everywhere.

## Clay Licks and Canopy Towers

One of the park's signature spectacles is the clay lick, a section of exposed riverbank clay where thousands of macaws and other parrots gather. Scientists think the birds eat the clay for mineral content, though another theory holds that the clay's higher pH helps balance acids from their tropical fruit diet. Watching the clay licks at dawn is like watching the forest itself erupt. Several lodges operate canopy observation towers that reach above the rainforest ceiling, offering platform views of birds that otherwise stay hidden in foliage. Night walks reveal species that daylight cannot. Kayak trips on the murky rivers drift past monkeys drinking at the bank and caimans holding position like logs. Nothing here moves fast. It does not have to.

## Getting to the End of the Road

The nearest city with air service is Coca, where LATAM runs a daily 30-minute flight from Quito for about US$100. From Coca, you take a motorized river canoe about four hours to reach the park boundary. Visitors need a permit, which tour operators handle, and all treks require certified guides. Hikes through cleared trails run several miles a day. Canoe trips from comfortable motorized boats are the other option, and kayak treks combine both. The park's wettest months are April through August. The driest are December through February, though daily showers continue even then. Yellow fever and typhoid vaccinations are advised. Carry water, use DEET, wear long sleeves, and do not touch wildlife. The park holds world records for venomous as well as nonvenomous snakes. A trip here demands preparation but returns something that almost no other trip can: you will see, over and over, things you have never seen before.

## Flight Context

Centered around 0.66 degrees S, 76.07 degrees W, in Ecuador's Orellana and Pastaza provinces, along the equator. Park covers 982,300 hectares from the foothills of the Andes east to the Peruvian border. Nearest airport is Francisco de Orellana (SECO) in Coca. Access is then via Napo and Tiputini river systems using motorized canoes. Best aerial viewing at 5,000-12,000 feet under clear early morning light; cloud cover is frequent through the afternoon. The park lies within the Ishpingo-Tambococha-Tiputini oil field area; following the August 2023 referendum ordering drilling to stop, Petroecuador began the process but compliance has been extremely slow as of 2025.
