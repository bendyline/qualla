# Iquitos

In the Peruvian Amazon, an isolated city built by rubber money became the birthplace of Amazonian pop art, Werner Herzog's Fitzcarraldo, and a community of Moroccan Jewish descendants who eventually migrated to Israel.

**Location:** -3.7333, -73.2500

**Categories:** Cities in Peru, Upper Amazon, Loreto, rubber boom history

## Introduction

Werner Herzog arrived in Iquitos in the early 1980s with a movie script about a man obsessed with building an opera house in the jungle. To film it, he pulled a 320-ton steamship over a mountain using ropes, pulleys, and hundreds of indigenous laborers. The cast nearly mutinied. The lead actor was replaced partway through. The ship actually went over the hill. The film was Fitzcarraldo, and its hero was loosely based on Carlos Fitzcarrald, a Peruvian rubber baron who, in the 1890s, had done a similar feat for real - dragging a boat between watersheds to access new rubber territory. The movie cemented Iquitos in the global imagination as the place where the Amazon's isolation creates either genius or madness, sometimes both at once.

## Amazonian Pop Art

Cut off from Peru's coastal and Andean cultural centers, Iquitos developed its own visual language. Amazonian pop art - sometimes called wild naive - is a self-taught, mural-focused style known for its electric color and its references to ayahuasca visions. It blends indigenous Amazonian motifs with European iconography and commercial characters borrowed from television, particularly MTV. Painters like Christian Bendayan, Roldan Pinedo, Elena Valera, Rember Yahuarcani, Brus Rubio, and Victor Churay have carried the style into contemporary gallery spaces. Amazonian graffiti followed, with Pukuna 8990 becoming the most influential movement in the city. The sound of Iquitos is equally unusual - local musicians blend traditional Amazonian rhythms with electronica, hip hop, punk, heavy metal, and psytrance, and the Children's and Youthful Symphonic Orchestra is the main symphonic group.

## Vargas Llosa's City

Mario Vargas Llosa - the Peruvian Nobel laureate - returned to Iquitos again and again in his fiction. His 1973 novel Captain Pantoja and the Special Service, which satirizes a Peruvian army officer assigned to set up a mobile brothel service for jungle garrisons, is set in and around the city. Francisco Lombardi filmed the adaptation in Iquitos in 2000. Parts of The Green House, the 1965 novel that won the Romulo Gallegos Prize, take place here. The Dream of the Celt (2010) - Vargas Llosa's novel about Roger Casement, the Irish diplomat who exposed the Peruvian Amazon Company's atrocities against indigenous rubber workers in the Putumayo region - returns to the territory Casement investigated. The Peruvian Amazon Company was headquartered in Iquitos. Its owner, Julio Cesar Arana, served as mayor of Iquitos from 1902 to 1903 and later as senator of Loreto.

## The Moroccan Jews of Iquitos

In the late nineteenth century, during the rubber boom, young Jewish men from Morocco traveled to the Peruvian Amazon looking for work and opportunity. Many settled in Iquitos, married local Peruvian women, and raised families. By the mid-twentieth century, their descendants - the Iquitos Jewish community, numbering in the hundreds - had largely drifted from Jewish practice, assimilating into Peruvian Catholic society. Starting in the late 1990s, a segment of the community began studying Judaism seriously, seeking formal conversion under Israeli rabbinic authorities. Many eventually emigrated to Israel, traveling thousands of miles from a river city they had never left to a country most had never seen. The 2008 documentary The Fire Within: Jews in the Amazonian Rainforest tells the story. A small community remains in Iquitos today.

## One of the Seven Wonders

On August 13, 2012, Iquitos received a 120-kilogram bronze plaque from the New7Wonders Foundation, marking the Amazon River basin as one of the Seven Natural Wonders of the World. The distinction is shared with Bolivia, Brazil, Ecuador, Suriname, Colombia, Venezuela, and French Guiana, but Peru hosted the ceremony. A massive parade ran down Avenida Quinonez. An afternoon session was held at the confluence of the Itaya and Nanay rivers. A night session took place in the 28 July Square. President Ollanta Humala accepted the award alongside First Lady Nadine Heredia and Loreto Regional President Ivan Vasquez. Reaction was mixed. Many residents worried that neither the city's urban planning nor its infrastructure was ready for the tourism influx the award was supposed to bring. Iquitos received 150,000 tourists in 2010. Numbers dropped to 46,000 foreign tourists in 2011, then rose again as international flights resumed.

## Carnaval and the Day of San Juan

Iquitos celebrates two major festivals, both shaped by its particular geography and cultural mix. Carnaval, held in February, is heavily pagan and thoroughly wet. Revelers throw water balloons - called cabacinas - at anyone they can reach, along with paint, flour, and any other substance that makes the celebration more chaotic. The Day of San Juan on June 24 honors Saint John the Baptist, patron of the Peruvian Amazon. The central food is juane - rice, meat, olives, and spices wrapped in bijao leaves and steamed, a dish that travels well in a region where portable food matters. The shunto jump dance accompanies the feast. Even the festivals reflect the city: cut off from the coast, built on the river, blending indigenous practice with imported Catholicism with the particular humor of a city that has to entertain itself.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.73 degrees S, 73.25 degrees W on the left bank of the Amazon River in Peru's Loreto region. Iquitos sits at approximately 120 m (394 ft) elevation. Coronel FAP Francisco Secada Vignetta International Airport (SPQT) is the primary access point - Iquitos is famously the largest continental city unreachable by road. The confluence of the Itaya, Nanay, and Amazon rivers creates distinctive visual landmarks from the air. Viewing altitude of 4,000-8,000 ft reveals the city's pattern along the Amazon shoreline and the extensive floodplain. Heavy rainfall year-round, with peak flooding March-May. Best morning visibility before afternoon thunderstorms build.
