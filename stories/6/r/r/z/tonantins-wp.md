# Tonantins

Born from a violent collision between missionaries and a river tribe in the 1770s, this Amazonas municipality became a reality TV footnote 243 years later.

**Location:** -2.8728, -67.8019

**Categories:** Municipalities in Amazonas (Brazilian state), Populated places on the Amazon, Brazil history, Portuguese Empire

## Introduction

In the 1770s, a Spanish Franciscan and a Portuguese fort jockeyed for position at the mouth of the Putumayo River. The Treaty of Madrid in 1750 had given Spain the river, but the Portuguese wanted it back. Fort São Fernando do Ica was established in 1768. And somewhere in that shifting frontier, a Carmelite missionary named Frei Matias Diniz founded a village of Caiuvicenas people on the Solimoes. A rival indigenous group killed him. Their name, Tonantins, survived on the landscape long after everyone involved was gone. Today Tonantins is a Brazilian municipality of 18,897 souls, 867 kilometers west of Manaus by river, reachable only by boat.

## Colonial Chess on the Solimoes

Around 1754, Spanish expeditions began pushing harder into the western Amazon, troubling the Portuguese colonial government. Franciscan missionaries helped Spain set up villages on the Putumayo River and attempted a fort at the river's mouth. They abandoned the fort in 1766, retreating to the Napo river, hobbled by communication failures with the Papal Post Office and by tropical conditions. The Portuguese moved in two years later. Under orders from Fernando da Costa de Ataide Teive, Governor of Grao-Para and Rio Negro, soldiers of a Portuguese captain occupied the abandoned Spanish post and renamed it Fort Sao Fernando do Ica. Further expeditions followed under successive colonial officials. On October 1, 1777, the Treaty of Saint Ildefonso restored restrictions on the South American colonies and fixed the Amazonian boundary along the Javari River, settling at least the paperwork.

## A Village That Kept Dying

The first Tonantins failed. Frei Matias Diniz's settlement was killed off with him by the rival tribe whose name it now carries, and the site where that happened became the district of Sao Francisco. Between 1774 and 1775, a man referred to only as Lord Sampaio gathered Caiuvicenas, Passes, and Tikuna people from the surrounding forest and tried again. Priests moved through on expeditions and catechized the new residents, building churches and a school. The second village held. By 1814, a permanent church had been built on the riverbank, and the settlement began its bureaucratic life as a district of Tefe. December 1, 1938 transferred it to Sao Paulo de Olivenca. December 19, 1955 moved it to Santo Antonio do Ica. December 10, 1981 separated it to form its own jurisdiction. July 2, 1985 brought official municipal incorporation.

## Cassava and Rain

The economy runs on subsistence agriculture. Cassava, or yuca, is the primary crop, supplemented by beans, rice, and corn. The temperature barely moves, holding between 23 degrees Celsius and 31 degrees Celsius year-round. The climate is classified as rainforest humid, and the rainy season lasts eight and a half months, from mid-October through June. The municipality borders and partly overlaps the Jutai-Solimoes Ecological Station, which protects adjacent forests from development. With no roads reaching Tonantins, the forest around the town stays largely intact. River boats arrive at the main port from both upstream and downstream, bringing supplies and tourists heading for the Pirarucu Festival in late November, when the town briefly doubles in population.

## 90 Days on the Amazon

In 2017, Tonantins appeared in an unlikely global venue. The television show 90 Day Fiance featured Karine Martins, a young woman from Tonantins who was dating and then marrying an American man named Paul Staehle. The show documented his travels to her hometown, the extreme remoteness of the place, the week-long river voyage, the language and cultural differences. The series returned to Tonantins across multiple seasons. For a municipality of fewer than 19,000 people, the appearance on American reality television was the most international media attention the town had ever received. Before that, most outsiders had heard of Tonantins only as a dot on an Amazon river map.

## The Paperwork Outlasts Everything

What Tonantins shows, more than many more visited places, is how colonial history and indigenous history overlap without resolving. The Portuguese and Spanish fought over rivers that were never theirs. Missionaries tried and failed and tried again. A Carmelite was murdered by people whose descendants might still be in the area. The Tikuna, whose language is spoken by 50,000 people across the triple frontier, contributed founding families to the second attempt. Through all of that, the Brazilian administrative state ticked through its reorganizations, changing which larger jurisdiction Tonantins belonged to every few decades. The municipal boundary set in 1985 is the current arrangement. The river does not care. The fish do not care. And yet Tonantins is, on paper, a discrete place with a mayor and a population of 18,897 as recorded in 2020, and the paperwork is what keeps the place legible to anyone far enough away to need it.

## Flight Context

Located at 2.87 degrees S, 67.80 degrees W, on the north bank of the Solimoes River in western Amazonas state, Brazil. No airport; access by river only. Nearest airports are Tefe (SBTF) downstream and Tabatinga (SBTT) upstream near the triple border with Peru and Colombia. The Jutai-Solimoes Ecological Station overlaps part of the municipality. Best aerial viewing at 5,000-15,000 feet over flat rainforest with the meandering Solimoes the dominant visual landmark.
