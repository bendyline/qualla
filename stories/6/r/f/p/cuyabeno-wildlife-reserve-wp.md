# Cuyabeno Wildlife Reserve

An Ecuadorian Amazon reserve of flooded forests and black-water rivers - a rare swamp-ecosystem at the foothills of the Andes, spanning nine distinct ecosystems in one park.

**Location:** -0.1167, -75.8333

**Categories:** Protected areas established in 1979, Nature reserves in Ecuador, Upper Amazon, Geography of Sucumbios Province, Ramsar sites

## Introduction

The drainage is what makes Cuyabeno strange. Other Amazon parks sit on well-drained ground near the Andes, where the runoff from the mountains cuts fast channels and leaves little time for standing water. Cuyabeno does the opposite. Its 5,900 square kilometers lie on a poorly drained plain, and the plain fills up. Seasonally flooded forests, semi-permanent lakes, and black-water rivers combine into an ecosystem that is almost un-Amazonian in the typical sense - a swamp against the mountains. The reserve was decreed on July 26, 1979 as part of Ecuador's first national protected-areas system, sitting between the Putumayo Canton in Sucumbios Province and the Aguarico Canton in Orellana Province.

## How It Was Drawn

The reserve's original 1979 footprint covered only about 150,000 hectares - the Cuyabeno River watershed alone. Then human settlement pushed into the upper watershed during the early 1980s. Rather than try to evict the new settlers, the reserve's first director Flavio Coello and ecologist Daan Vreugdenhil proposed in 1983 to redraw the boundaries: exclude the invaded zones, extend east to the Peruvian border. The boundary changes grew the reserve to 590,112 hectares. Much of Ecuador's protected-areas planning rests on 1970s fieldwork. Alan Putney's team surveyed the Andean and coastal areas that would become 30 of Ecuador's national protected areas; Vreugdenhil's team surveyed the nine Amazon zones that would eventually include Cuyabeno. The FAO report "National Strategy on the Conservation of Outstanding Wild Areas of Ecuador" turned those surveys into policy.

## Nine Ecosystems

Ecologists have broken Cuyabeno into nine major ecosystems, each shaped by water. On the higher hills of the upper watershed lie well-drained forests - the richest per-hectare tree diversity in the reserve, with as many as 307 species in a single hectare recorded in neighboring Yasuni. Below that, varzea forests flood seasonally with sediment-rich white water. Black-water swamps full of Mauritia flexuosa palms rise in other zones. The igapo - forests semi-permanently flooded by sediment-poor black water - grow dense with macrolobium trees, epiphytes, herons, blue-and-yellow macaws, and hoatzins. Submerged herbal vegetation fringes lakes and rivers. Then the water bodies themselves: the cafe-au-lait Rio Aguarico, the tannin-black Rio Cuyabeno, permanent lakes like Zancudo Coche, and semi-permanent lakes like the Cuyabeno Lake that dry at least partly in most years.

## What Lives Inside

All the characteristic large Amazon mammals inhabit the reserve: lowland tapir, two deer species, jaguar and puma, capybara, both South American otter species - the giant river otter Pteronura brasiliensis and the smaller neotropical otter. Two dolphin species work the rivers; Amazonian manatees haunt the quieter backwaters. Ten monkey species swing through the canopy, joined by dozens of rodent and bat species. The bird count is under debate - some ornithologists claim 530 species, others over 580, and no single authoritative list is maintained. During peak wet season, thousands of hectares of forest flood simultaneously, creating a seasonal El Dorado for an estimated 350 fish species, two cayman species, boa constrictors, and anacondas. Dolphins have been observed swimming deep inside the inundated forest, following the fish into what is normally dry ground.

## People of the Rivers

Three indigenous peoples remain tied to the reserve, each along a distinct river system. The Siona live around the upper Cuyabeno lakes and along the Tarapuy River. The Cofan inhabit the banks of the Aguarico. The Secoya - whose ancestors lived inside what became the reserve - were pushed just outside the modern boundaries. A small number of elders in all three communities retain knowledge of medicinal plants, but that knowledge is being lost generation by generation. New arrivals since the 1980s have added complexity: smallholder colonists, and Quichua speakers from elsewhere in Ecuador. For most of these communities, traditional fishing, farming, and hunting persisted as the economic base until oil-road construction and ecotourism began reshaping the local economy in the 1980s and 1990s.

## Two Lake Systems

Practically, Cuyabeno divides into two zones separated by travel time. The western lake network, anchored by the Laguna Grande, sits close enough to Lago Agrio that it can be reached by paved road and a short canoe trip - this is where most tourism concentrates. The eastern lake network, against the Peruvian border, requires serious river travel, and is reached largely through the Cofan-run Cofan Lodge. The two zones feel like different parks. The flooded-forest wetlands hold fewer species overall but vastly more individuals of each; the higher ground between and above them holds the astonishing tree diversity for which the region is famous. Rainfall averages about 3,000 millimeters per year, humidity stays between 85 and 95 percent, and the annual temperature oscillates around 25 degrees Celsius. The dry season, such as it is, lasts from mid-December through mid-March.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 0.12 deg S, 75.83 deg W, in Ecuador's northern Amazon near the Putumayo and Aguarico river systems. The reserve's terrain is low and flat - elevation slopes gently from about 326 m in the west to under 177 m in the east. Nearest commercial airport: Lago Agrio (SELA). Expect near-continuous low ceilings, frequent showers, and restricted ground-level visibility year-round. The reserve borders Peru along the Lagartococha River on its eastern edge.
