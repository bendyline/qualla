# Ecuador

The first country to grant legal rights to nature in its constitution - a megadiverse republic whose four regions squeeze Andean peaks, Amazon rainforest, Pacific coast, and the Galapagos Islands into a single small country.

**Location:** -1.4000, -78.4000

**Categories:** Countries in South America, Equatorial countries, Andean countries, Member states of the United Nations, Megadiverse countries

## Introduction

When the Ecuadorian Constituent Assembly drafted the 2008 constitution, it did something no country had done before: it gave legal standing to nature itself. Mountains, rivers, forests - pachamama, in Kichwa - could now bring suit to defend their own integrity. The move was radical, pragmatic, and characteristic. Ecuador is one of seventeen countries UN Environment designates as megadiverse, and it has the highest biodiversity per square kilometer of any nation on Earth. The constitution followed from that geography. When your small country contains more bird species than all of North America, you reconsider what rights mean.

## Four Worlds, One Country

The country's area is 283,571 square kilometers, about the size of Nevada, yet its geography divides into four distinct regions. La Costa, the Pacific lowlands, runs from Esmeraldas in the north through Guayaquil to the Peruvian border - fertile, tropical, the engine of banana exports for Dole and Chiquita and the heart of Ecuador's rice crop. La Sierra, the Andean highlands, holds ten provinces, most of the country's 30-plus volcanoes including Cotopaxi and Chimborazo, and the predominantly Kichwa indigenous population in traditional farming villages. La Amazonia, the eastern jungle provinces - Morona Santiago, Napo, Orellana, Pastaza, Sucumbios, Zamora-Chinchipe - contains most of Ecuador's oil reserves and stretches of forest set aside as indigenous untouchable zones for the Waorani, Shuar, and Kichwa. La Region Insular: the Galapagos Islands, roughly a thousand kilometers offshore, where Darwin worked out natural selection in 1835.

## The Conquest and the Criollos

The Incas reached the highland valleys only in the 1460s, absorbing confederations that had taken two generations of Inca rulers to subdue. They never fully conquered the Amazon or the coast. In 1532 Francisco Pizarro trapped the Inca emperor Atahualpa at Cajamarca, extracted a room of gold as ransom, and then executed him anyway. Smallpox had arrived first. By the time Quito became the seat of a Spanish Real Audiencia in 1563, the native population was already collapsing from epidemic disease. After nearly 300 years of Spanish rule Quito still held only about 10,000 people. On August 10, 1809, the city's criollos declared the first independence movement in Spanish America - an uprising that lasted less than two months but earned Quito the nickname Luz de America, the Light of America. Full independence came after Antonio Jose de Sucre defeated the Spanish Royalists at the Battle of Pichincha in 1822. Ecuador separated from Bolivar's Gran Colombia in 1830.

## A Century of Lost Territory

Modern Ecuador is smaller than the country that declared independence. Border disputes with Colombia lasted until 1934; the 1916 Munoz Vernaza-Suarez Treaty ceded much of the northern Amazon. Disputes with Peru were bloodier. The 1941 Ecuadorian-Peruvian War went badly for Ecuador - 11,681 Peruvian troops faced a poorly armed Ecuadorian force of 2,300. The Rio Protocol of 1942 formalized the losses. The unresolved border in the remote Cordillera del Condor erupted in fighting in 1981 (the Paquisha Incident) and again in 1995 (the Cenepa War). Only the 1998 Brasilia Presidential Act finally drew the line. Between those two treaties, Ecuador lost substantial Amazon basin territory it had claimed since colonial times. The border demarcation came into effect on May 13, 1999 - ending the longest-running territorial dispute in the Western Hemisphere.

## Oil, Dollars, and Upheaval

The 1972 completion of the Andean pipeline, carrying crude from the eastern slopes of the Andes to the Pacific coast, turned Ecuador into South America's second-largest oil exporter. The oil economy brought instability. Between 1964 and 1992 Texaco drilled 339 wells in the Ecuadorian Amazon and abandoned 627 toxic wastewater pits - the pollution became the basis for the Texaco-Chevron case, documented in the film Crude. Economic crisis in 1998-1999 collapsed the sucre; Ecuador adopted the U.S. dollar as its national currency on March 13, 2000, eliminating the sucre entirely by September that year. Rafael Correa's three terms (2007-2017) reduced poverty from 36.7 percent in 2006 to 22.5 percent by 2016 - the Gini index of inequality improved from 0.55 to 0.47. His successor Lenin Moreno reversed course on economic policy. In October 2019, protests against the end of fuel subsidies drove the government from Quito to Guayaquil temporarily. In January 2024, President Daniel Noboa declared an internal armed conflict against organized crime after the Los Choneros cartel leader Fito escaped prison and gunmen seized a television station on live broadcast.

## The Rights of Nature

For all the political turbulence, the country's ecological legal regime has held. Ecuador's 2008 constitution recognized legally enforceable rights of nature - a doctrine since invoked by courts to stop industrial projects that would have generated, in the supreme court's 2022 phrasing, "excessive sacrifices to the collective rights of communities and nature." The country hosts 1,600 bird species in its continental area - about 15 percent of the world's known birds - plus 38 endemic Galapagos species, more than 16,000 plant species, 106 endemic reptiles, 138 endemic amphibians, and 6,000 butterfly species. The Sociobosque program, begun in 2008, pays private and community landowners to preserve native ecosystems; it now covers roughly 6,295 square kilometers. The Galapagos remains on the UNESCO list even as introduced species and tourism pressure strain its fragile ecosystems. The country's 2018 Forest Landscape Integrity Index score was 7.66/10, placing Ecuador 35th of 172 countries globally. Ecuador won its first Olympic gold in Atlanta 1996 when Jefferson Perez took the 20 kilometer walk. Small country, tall numbers.

## Flight Context

Ecuador spans latitudes 2 deg N to 5 deg S, longitudes 75 to 91 deg W (including the Galapagos). Total area 283,571 km sq; 2,337 km of Pacific coastline. Major airports: Quito's Mariscal Sucre (SEQM / UIO) at 2,400 m elevation east of the city, Guayaquil's Jose Joaquin de Olmedo (SEGU / GYE) at sea level. Galapagos hubs: Baltra (SEGS / GPS) and San Cristobal (SEST / SCY), both reachable only via the mainland. Mountain terrain in the Sierra reaches 6,268 m at Chimborazo - expect strong orographic weather, persistent cloud, and density-altitude considerations for high-country operations.
