# Cotopaxi

An active equatorial stratovolcano crowned with one of the world's rarest glaciers, sacred to the Andes long before the Spanish arrived.

**Location:** -0.6806, -78.4378

**Categories:** Stratovolcanoes of Ecuador, Active volcanoes, Andean Volcanic Belt, Glaciers of Ecuador, Sacred mountains

## Introduction

The name translates roughly as 'neck of the moon,' and when you see the crater from the right angle you understand why - a 250-meter-deep depression ringed by ice cornices, shaped like a crescent laid against the sky. Cotopaxi rises from the Ecuadorian highlands fifty kilometers south of Quito, an active stratovolcano that has erupted eighty-seven times in recorded history and is erupting still. Between October 2022 and February 2023 alone, Ecuador's Geophysical Institute counted roughly 8,000 earthquakes beneath its summit, about 1,600 tremors each month. The mountain is not resting. It is breathing.

## Ice on the Equator

Cotopaxi sits almost directly on the equator, and yet a glacier begins at its five-thousand-meter shoulder and climbs to the crater rim. Equatorial glaciers are vanishingly rare on Earth - a quirk of altitude beating latitude - and Cotopaxi wears one of the best of them. From Latacunga and from Quito, on a clear morning before the afternoon clouds gather, the cone appears as a nearly perfect white pyramid. The symmetry is deceptive. Two concentric crater rims scar the summit, the outer one partly free of snow, irregular in profile. Inside, the crater floor is flat and ice-veiled. The highest point, on the outer rim's north side, is where climbers stand when the weather allows.

## Sacred Before the Conquest

Long before the Inca pushed north into these valleys in the fifteenth century, the Andean peoples who lived in the shadow of Cotopaxi treated it as a living presence. They worshipped the mountain as the sender of rain - the guarantor of the land's fertility - and they believed its summit was where the gods lived. The Quechua and Aymara roots of the name itself hint at older meaning: q'oto for throat or neck, phakhsi for moon. The crescent crater gave the mountain a face that could be read from the valley floor. When the Inca arrived and then the Spanish after them, both found a peak already woven deep into sacred geography. Indigenous artists from the town of Tigua still paint Cotopaxi in bright, flattened panels that carry that reverence forward.

## The 1877 Catastrophe

Cotopaxi's most violent historical eruptions clustered in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries - 1742, 1744, 1768, and then the great event of June 26, 1877. In 1744 and again in 1768, lahars and ashfall destroyed the colonial town of Latacunga, which had been rebuilt at the same place each time. The 1877 eruption was something else entirely. Pyroclastic flows boiled down every side of the mountain, melting the ice cap in a matter of minutes. The meltwater mixed with ash and debris into lahars that raced west toward the Pacific and east into the Amazon basin, traveling hundreds of kilometers. Whole drainages were scoured clean. The painter Frederic Edwin Church had already made Cotopaxi famous in North America with his 1862 canvas, but after 1877 the mountain acquired a second reputation - as a destroyer capable of reaching the sea.

## Climbers and Ghosts

Alexander von Humboldt tried in 1802 and got only to 4,500 meters. Moritz Wagner failed in 1858. The summit went to the German geologist Wilhelm Reiss and his Colombian partner Angel Escobar on November 28, 1872 - the first confirmed ascent. Eight years later the celebrated British alpinist Edward Whymper made the third ascent with the Carrel cousins and actually slept on the summit. A refuge hut went up in 1971 at 4,864 meters, named for the Venezuelan independence fighter Jose Felix Ribas. Then on Easter Sunday 1996, after days of earthquakes had weakened the ice wall above, a huge section of glacier broke loose in the warm midday sun. Holidaymakers were on the mountain. Those trapped inside the refuge smashed windows on the downhill side and scrambled to safety. Thirteen people died on the slope above. The refuge still sits in that same valley, still vulnerable.

## A City in the Path

The latest eruption cycle began on October 21, 2022, and has continued since. Vulcanologists watch the numbers with unease. Quito's suburban valleys lie directly downslope to the north; more than two million people live within lahar-reach. Latacunga sits in the southern valley, destroyed at least twice already, rebuilt each time. Cotopaxi has become both a national icon and a civil defense problem, the subject of painted landscapes by Church and Rafael Troya, a song by the rock band The Mars Volta, a cameo in Close Encounters of the Third Kind - where the name belongs not to the mountain but to a ship that vanished in 1925. From cruise altitude the cone still looks serene. The people who live below it know better.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 0.68 deg S, 78.44 deg W, summit elevation 5,897 m (19,347 ft). Maintain generous clearance above the summit - local winds and plume activity make the airspace hazardous during eruptive periods. Nearest major airport: Mariscal Sucre International in Quito (SEQM / UIO), about 50 km north; Cotopaxi International in Latacunga (SELT) lies 31 km southwest. On clear mornings the cone is visible from over 100 km away; afternoon clouds typically obscure the summit.
