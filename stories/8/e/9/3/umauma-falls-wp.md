# Umauma Falls

Three waterfalls stacked in close succession on the flank of Mauna Kea, each one slowly migrating uphill as the volcanic rock erodes beneath it.

**Location:** 19.9051, -155.4747

**Categories:** Waterfalls of Hawaii (island)

## Introduction

Most waterfalls are singular spectacles. Umauma Falls is a three-act play. Located on the Umauma River about 16 miles north of Hilo on Hawaiʻi Island, these three cascades tumble in close succession down a gorge on the eastern flank of Mauna Kea, each dropping into its own plunge pool before the water gathers itself and falls again. They are unique in Hawaiʻi — not for their height or volume, but for this stacked arrangement, a geological accident that reveals how waterfalls are born, how they move, and how they will eventually disappear.

## Built by a Volcano, Shaped by Rain

The Umauma River has been carving its channel since Mauna Kea first rose from the sea millions of years ago. As the volcano grew, it laid down alternating layers of lava and volcanic ash, each with different hardness. Water exploits these differences ruthlessly. Where a soft ash layer sits beneath hard basalt, the river undercuts the rock face, and a waterfall forms. The three tiers of Umauma Falls exist because the river encountered three such weak layers in close succession. The Umauma sits at a climatic boundary on Mauna Kea's eastern flank: to the south, trade winds deliver heavy, persistent rain; to the north, conditions are noticeably drier. The Umauma River is the most northerly stream on this slope that flows year-round without drying up in summer — a distinction that says as much about rainfall gradients as it does about the river itself.

## Waterfalls That Walk

Every waterfall is temporary. Heavy rains send boulders tumbling downstream, and when those boulders plunge over a falls, they hammer the rock at the base, deepening the plunge pool. Over time, the pool undermines the lip of the falls until the rock face collapses. The debris fills the pool, gets washed away by the next flood, and the cycle begins again — but now the waterfall has moved a few feet uphill. This process, repeated over geological time, means that Umauma Falls is slowly migrating up the slope of Mauna Kea. If one tier erodes into a resistant layer of rock and stalls, a lower tier can catch up and merge with it, creating a single, taller falls. To see what Umauma might look like in the distant future, you need only visit the neighboring Kolekole Stream to the south, where ʻAkaka Falls — a single 442-foot cascade — formed through exactly this process of merger over deep time.

## Hidden in Plain Sight

For most of modern history, the only way to see Umauma Falls was to hike up the riverbed. The surrounding terrain, overgrown with wild guava and abandoned sugarcane, concealed the gorge from anyone who did not know exactly where to look. In 1995, Walter L. Wagner, then developing the World Botanical Gardens, explored the gulch edge searching for a viewing point and discovered a natural overlook hidden beneath decades of overgrowth. He cleared the agricultural berm, removed the guava trees, and built a concrete platform with retaining walls and steel railings. The overlook changed hands around 2008, and visitors now access it through the Umauma Experience, a botanical garden property that charges an entry fee. It is a small irony that one of Hawaiʻi's most distinctive waterfalls spent most of the 20th century invisible to the very tourists who flocked to the island's more famous cascades.

## Three Pools, Three Rhythms

During normal flows, each plunge pool at the base of the three tiers becomes a natural swimming hole — a feature common to waterfalls on Mauna Kea's flanks, where the erosion process reliably scoops out basins deep enough to hold still water. But when the rain intensifies, these gentle pools vanish. Two feet of rain in a few hours has been recorded in the Umauma watershed, and storms on the upper slopes can be even more extreme. The river transforms, swelling from a murmur to a roar, carrying rocks and debris downstream with enough force to reshape the channel. It is this violence, repeated over millennia, that created the falls in the first place. The plunge pools are not permanent features but momentary pauses in an ongoing conversation between water and stone, each flood erasing what the last one carved and beginning the sculpture anew.

## Flight Context

Located at 19.91°N, 155.47°W on the northeast flank of Mauna Kea, approximately 16 miles north of Hilo. The falls are set in a narrow gorge that is difficult to spot from high altitude, but the Umauma River gorge is identifiable as a dark cut in the green slopes when flying at 1,500–3,000 feet AGL along the Hamakua Coast. The three-tiered falls are best seen from the overlook on the south rim of the gorge. Nearest airport: Hilo International Airport (PHTO) approximately 16 nm south-southeast. The area is frequently cloud-covered by afternoon trade wind buildup; morning approaches offer clearer views.
