# Norilsk

The world's northernmost major city, built by gulag prisoners on top of vast nickel deposits, where 176,000 people endure polar night, extreme pollution, and total isolation from Russia's road network.

**Location:** 69.3333, 88.2167

**Categories:** closed cities, Arctic, mining, Russia, Soviet history, gulag, pollution, permafrost

## Introduction

The name probably comes from a word for a fishing pole. Norilo, the long thin stick that Siberian fishermen threaded through holes in river ice to stretch their nets, gave its name to the Norilka River, and the river gave its name to the city that should not exist. Norilsk sits 300 kilometers north of the Arctic Circle on permafrost, with no road or rail connection to the rest of Russia, temperatures that plunge past minus 50 in winter, and air so polluted that the surrounding tundra has been stripped of vegetation for kilometers. Yet 176,000 people live here, making it the largest city in the world's continuous permafrost zone after Yakutsk, and the second-largest inside the Arctic Circle after Murmansk.

## Prisoners and Permafrost

Indigenous peoples knew about the copper in these hills during the Bronze Age. Archaeologists have found primitive smelting equipment and raw copper near Lake Pyasino. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, copper from the Norilsk deposits reached Mangazeya, the Arctic trading post on the Taz River that served as a hub for furs and metals. But the modern city owes its existence to geologist Nikolay Urvantsev, who between 1919 and 1926 confirmed the presence of rich polymetallic ores in the western spurs of the Putorana Plateau, and to Stalin, who in 1935 established the Norillag labor camp to exploit them. Prisoners built everything: the mines, the smelters, the railway to Dudinka. According to camp archives, 16,806 prisoners died during the years Norillag operated, from 1935 to 1956, killed by forced labor, starvation, and cold. Many more died in the mines that continued operating with prisoner labor until around 1979.

## The Metal Heart of Russia

Beneath Norilsk and the nearby Talnakh Mountains lie the largest known nickel-copper-palladium deposits in the world, formed 250 million years ago during the eruption of the Siberian Traps. Nornickel, the mining giant headquartered here, produces 35 percent of the world's palladium, 25 percent of its platinum, 20 percent of its nickel, and 20 percent of its rhodium. In Russia, 96 percent of nickel and 95 percent of cobalt come from these operations. The company's net profit in 2021 reached $6.974 billion. Enriched ore travels by rail to the port of Dudinka, then by sea to Murmansk for further processing at the Monchegorsk refinery on the Kola Peninsula. The port at Dudinka dismantles its facilities every spring when the Yenisei floods, water levels rising as much as 20 meters, a seasonal rhythm that governs the entire logistics chain.

## A City Designed by Prisoners

The architects who planned Norilsk were themselves inmates of Norillag. Gevorg Kochar, Mikael Mazmanyan, and Olgerd Trushinsh designed the city center under the supervision of Vitold Nepokoichitsky, who arrived from Leningrad in 1939 and brought neoclassical sensibilities with him. The result is that Norilsk's central avenue, with its grand facades and formal squares, unexpectedly resembles St. Petersburg. An early design mistake oriented streets along the axes of prevailing winds, assuming snow would blow clear. The winds proved too strong and the snow too deep, so later blocks adopted compact perimeter layouts to create sheltered courtyards. Gas and water pipes run above ground, mounted on stilts, because the seasonal freeze-thaw cycle of permafrost makes underground utilities impossible. Refrigerant circulates through networks of pipes beneath buildings to keep their foundations frozen solid.

## Smog, Acid Rain, and a River Turned Red

Russia's Federal State Statistics Service ranks Norilsk as the country's most polluted city. In 2017, the city produced 1.798 million tons of carbon pollutants, nearly six times more than Russia's second-most polluted city. The Blacksmith Institute has placed Norilsk among the ten most polluted places on Earth, citing emissions of heavy metals, radioactive isotopes, and sulfur dioxide. In September 2016, the Daldykan River turned red, with preliminary evidence pointing to wastewater from a Nornickel smelting plant. In May 2020, a fuel tank ruptured at a Nornickel power plant, spilling 21,000 tons of diesel into the Ambarnaya and Daldykan rivers, one of the largest oil spills in Russian history. Vladimir Putin declared a state of emergency. Nornickel was fined 146 billion rubles. The closure of the old nickel smelter in 2016 cut annual emissions by about 400,000 tons, and the company has committed $14 billion to reducing sulfur dioxide emissions by 75 percent.

## Life at the Edge of the World

The midnight sun shines from May 20 to July 24. Polar night lasts from roughly November 30 to January 13. Winter extends eight months, with average January temperatures around minus 28 degrees Celsius and snowstorms on more than 50 days per year. When the sun returns after polar night, indigenous Nenets and Dolgans celebrate the festival of Heiro. The Norilsk Polar Drama Theater, founded in 1941 with a troupe composed largely of gulag prisoners, is one of the northernmost theaters in the world. Legendary Soviet actors Innokenty Smoktunovsky and Georgiy Zhzhonov performed here during and after their imprisonments. A mosque built in 1998, the northernmost in the world, serves the local Tatar community. Fiber optic internet arrived only in 2017. Norilsk remains closed to foreign visitors without special permits, an Arctic industrial fortress where life expectancy is ten years below the Russian average and the residents still call everywhere else "the mainland."

## Flight Context

Located at 69.33N, 88.22E, approximately 300 km north of the Arctic Circle. Served by Alykel Airport (UOOO), 35 km west of the city center, with a modernized runway. The city sits at the foot of the 1,700 m Putorana Mountains. Visible from altitude as a dense urban cluster with prominent industrial smokestacks and pollution haze surrounded by treeless tundra. The Norilsk railway runs west to the port of Dudinka on the Yenisei River. No road connections to any other city. Valek Airport also serves the area. Weather severe: polar night Nov-Jan, blizzards frequent, temperatures to -50C.
