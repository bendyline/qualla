# Zambia

A squashed-peanut-shaped country where the Zambezi plunges over Victoria Falls, safari camps outnumber cities, and 73 ethnic groups have somehow always managed to get along.

**Location:** -14.0000, 28.0000

**Categories:** country, africa, safari, travel, victoria-falls

## Introduction

Look at Zambia on a map and the shape makes no sense - a squashed peanut with long ears poking east and west, the kind of border only a colonial draftsman in a faraway office could have drawn. Somehow the country inside that peculiar outline ended up as one of Africa's calmer corners, holding 73 ethnic groups, 20 major languages, one of the Seven Natural Wonders of the World, and some of the least commercialised safari country on the continent. Travelers come for the thunder of Victoria Falls and stay for what is not there: no striped tourist vans, no packaged crowds, no fences between you and the elephants.

## The Smoke That Thunders

The Kololo people called it Mosi-oa-Tunya - the smoke that thunders - and from a distance that is exactly what you see: a column of mist rising hundreds of meters into the sky, visible long before the river itself appears. At the gorge, the Zambezi falls about 108 meters across a 1.7-kilometer-wide cliff, one of the largest sheets of falling water on Earth. The Zambian side gets you closer. Trails wind right to the edge of the lip, and if you time it for late October or November - after the wet season has ebbed but before the river rises again - you can wade to Devil's Pool, a basin of calm water perched on the very edge of the drop, with nothing between you and the spray but a ledge of basalt worn smooth by millennia of water.

## Safari Country Without the Stripes

Zambian safaris are the ones guides dream of leading. South Luangwa National Park, east of Lusaka, is where the walking safari was invented in the 1950s by Norman Carr - you are on foot, your guide reads spoor in the dust, and the lions are close enough that you lower your voice without being told. North Luangwa is wilder still, known for enormous buffalo herds and the large prides that follow them. Lower Zambezi was once the president's private hunting ground, which kept the roads rough and the wildlife thick. And in a single grove of Kasanka National Park each October, an estimated eight million straw-colored fruit bats arrive for the fig harvest - the largest mammal migration on the planet, happening quietly under most travelers' radar.

## The Kuomboka and Other Festivals

Every spring, usually around Easter, the Lozi king of Western Province moves house - and he does it with theatre. The Kuomboka is the ceremonial migration of the litunga from his dry-season palace at Lealui to his wet-season palace at Limulunga, performed on a flotilla of royal barges down the flooding Zambezi. The king wears an elaborate Victorian ambassador's uniform, a relic of a colonial-era treaty that became tradition. Drummers keep time as the boats move. Elsewhere on the calendar: the Kazanga of the Nkoya people, celebrated for over 500 years; the Ncwala near Chipata, where the Ngoni chief tastes the first fruit and spears a bull; the Umutomboko in Luapula, where Mwata Kazembe dances the story of his people's arrival from the Congo. Bring patience for long speeches and tolerance for dust. Foreigners tend to get waved into the VIP stands.

## Nsima and Mosi

Zambian home cooking orbits one staple: nsima, a thick maize porridge rolled into balls with the right hand and dipped into a relish of beans, vegetables, peanuts, or - if the household can afford it - chicken, beef, or tiny dried kapenta fish. A plate of nsima and relish costs a few kwacha in a local restaurant. Rice grown in Chama, in the eastern hills, offers a welcome alternative to maize fatigue. The national beer is Mosi, a clear 4 percent lager named for the falls, available in every bar and shop. Near the borders you can find Carlsberg from Malawi, Simba from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kilimanjaro from Tanzania. Rural homebrews range from honey beers in the south to tea-leaf wine in the east. Avoid kachasu - a distilled spirit that, by reputation, sometimes contains ingredients no drink should contain.

## Getting Around a Country the Size of France

Zambia is big - about the size of France - and the distances feel it. The main tarred roads run a spine from Livingstone in the south through Lusaka to the Copperbelt in the north, with branches east to Chipata and Malawi, and north to Tanzania. Everything else is gravel or dirt, and in the December-to-April wet season a good number of those routes become impassable mud. A 4x4 is not a luxury here if you plan to see the parks. Minibuses - vans stuffed to creative capacity - are the rural workhorse, uncomfortable but a fast way to meet people and practice your greetings. Luxury coaches on the main routes are genuinely luxurious by regional standards. And if you want a slower journey, the TAZARA train to Dar es Salaam takes 38 hours through some of the most beautiful country in southern Africa - built in the 1970s with Chinese labor, still running, still the cheapest way to watch the Great Rift Valley roll by.

## The Flyover Country That Isn't

From the cockpit at 35,000 feet, Zambia looks like a land of quiet green plateaus cut by the thin silver of the Zambezi and the darker ribbon of the Luangwa. The Copperbelt shows as a cluster of mining scars in the north. Victoria Falls, at the southwestern corner, announces itself with a permanent smudge of mist even on cloudless days - pilots on the Johannesburg-Nairobi route sometimes bank a few degrees just to give passengers the view. What you cannot see from the air is how empty most of it is: 20 million people in a country bigger than France, most of them clustered along the single rail line south from the mines to Livingstone.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 14°S, 28°E - roughly centered on Zambia's Central Province. Recommended viewing altitude FL350 or higher for the full country silhouette; FL200 reveals the Zambezi gorge and Vic Falls mist plume. Major airports: Kenneth Kaunda International (FLKK/LUN) at Lusaka, Harry Mwanga Nkumbula (FLLI/LVI) at Livingstone near the falls, Simon Mwansa Kapwepwe (FLSK/NLA) at Ndola in the Copperbelt. Dry-season visibility is excellent May-August; September-November brings dust haze, and the wet season December-April produces towering cumulonimbus along the ITCZ - plan accordingly.
