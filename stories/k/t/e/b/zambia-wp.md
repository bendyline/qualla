# Zambia

A country whose borders were drawn by Cecil Rhodes's mining ambitions, whose rivers drain half a continent, and whose name was taken from the Zambezi on the day of independence in 1964.

**Location:** -14.0000, 28.0000

**Categories:** country, africa, southern-africa, history, geography, landlocked

## Introduction

On 24 October 1964, the Union Jack came down at Government House in Lusaka and the new flag of Zambia went up - a green field with an orange eagle, broken by bands of red, black, and copper. The country chose its name that night: Zambia, from the Zambezi, the great river whose falls had once been named Victoria by a Scottish missionary and whose shores had been divided by Cecil Rhodes's British South Africa Company seventy years earlier. Kenneth Kaunda stepped out to speak as the first president. Behind him lay a colonial territory stitched together from a patchwork of 73 ethnic groups. Ahead lay the hard work of becoming a nation.

## Before the Europeans Came

The land that would become Zambia was never empty. By the 12th century, Bantu-speaking peoples had moved into the region from the Congo Basin and from the northeast, bringing iron tools, millet agriculture, and political organization. The Lunda Empire rose to the northwest around 1600, built on a Luba model of statecraft after a hunter named Chibinda Ilunga married the Lunda princess Lueji and inherited her kingdom. Further east, the Maravi Confederacy expanded from Lake Malawi in the 1400s under the Phiri clan, its ivory feeding Swahili traders on the Indian Ocean coast. The Maravi brought the Nyau secret society with them - ritual dance and mask traditions that persist in Zambia today. Most Zambians now trace their ancestry to these Luba-Lunda and Maravi states, and to the peoples who scattered across the plateau when those empires eventually fragmented.

## The Company That Owned a Country

Cecil Rhodes wanted copper, and in 1890 his British South Africa Company persuaded or coerced local chiefs into signing mineral concessions that Rhodes then parlayed into a Royal Charter. For the next 33 years, Northern Rhodesia was governed not by Britain but by a private corporation with a flag and a police force, answerable mainly to its shareholders. The territory was handed over to the Colonial Office in 1923, but the mining companies that Rhodes had seeded kept their power. When industrial copper extraction began in the Copperbelt in the late 1920s, the mines pulled tens of thousands of African workers from across the region into new company towns - Ndola, Kitwe, Chingola, Luanshya - that grew faster than any indigenous settlement ever had. The wealth flowed south to London. The wages stayed low.

## The Federation and the Breaking Away

In 1953, Britain fused Northern Rhodesia with Southern Rhodesia and Nyasaland into the Central African Federation - a settler-dominated union that Africans north of the Zambezi correctly saw as a mechanism to entrench white rule. Kenneth Kaunda, a former teacher from Chinsali, became the face of the resistance. His United National Independence Party pushed for dissolution, and by 1963 the federation had collapsed. Independence came on 24 October 1964, and Kaunda became president of a country that owned the Copperbelt but had almost no trained managers, engineers, or doctors - the colonial education system had prepared Africans for clerking, not leadership. Zambia nationalized the mines, built universities, and took in refugees and guerrilla fighters from neighbors still under white rule: Rhodesia, Angola, Mozambique, South West Africa. The bills came due in the 1970s when copper prices collapsed.

## The Land Itself

At 752,614 square kilometers, Zambia is the 39th-largest country on Earth - roughly the size of Texas, and significantly larger than France. Most of it sits between 1,000 and 1,400 meters above sea level, a high plateau that keeps the tropical heat manageable. Two great river basins drain it. The Zambezi basin covers roughly three-quarters of the country, gathering water from the Kafue, the Luangwa, and the Zambezi itself - the river that forms the southern border with Namibia, Botswana, and Zimbabwe before plunging over Victoria Falls. The Congo basin drains the northern quarter through the Chambeshi and the Luapula. In the east, the Luangwa Valley splits the plateau in a dramatic rift-valley curve, its steep sides home to Zambia's most famous national parks. In the west, the Barotse Floodplain of the upper Zambezi floods annually from December to June - an event so central to Lozi culture that the royal Kuomboka migration is built around it.

## A Patchwork That Holds

Zambia's more than 21 million people represent about 73 officially recognized ethnic groups speaking 20 major languages plus dozens of dialects. The Bemba are the largest group at roughly a third of the population, mainly in the north and the Copperbelt. The Nyanja-speaking peoples of the east - Chewa, Ngoni, Nsenga - form another 18 percent. The Tonga of the Zambezi Valley make up about 17 percent. The Lozi of Barotseland, the North-Western peoples, the Tumbuka, the Lamba, and 60-odd smaller groups fill out the rest. What Zambia has largely avoided, by the standards of its neighbors, is ethnic violence. Kaunda's founding slogan - 'One Zambia, One Nation' - was more than a motto. English serves as a neutral lingua franca. Intermarriage is common. The country that colonial cartographers cobbled together has, against the odds, become a place its people recognize as home.

## Aircraft Overhead

From cruise altitude, Zambia reveals its geography plainly. The Zambezi traces a silver line along the southern edge, its falls marked by a permanent column of mist. The Luangwa Valley shows as a dark green gash cutting northeast. The Copperbelt glows at night with smelter flares, the only substantial light on an otherwise dark plateau. Lake Kariba, created by the 1959 damming of the Zambezi, is a vast irregular smear on the southern border - still one of the largest artificial lakes on Earth. North of the capital, the plateau is nearly featureless: miombo woodland stretching toward the horizon, broken only by the rail line and the single tarred highway running from Lusaka to the mines.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 14°S, 28°E - central Zambian plateau, typical cruise altitude FL350-FL400 for regional traffic. Major airports: Kenneth Kaunda International (FLKK/LUN) at Lusaka, Harry Mwanga Nkumbula (FLLI/LVI) at Livingstone, Simon Mwansa Kapwepwe (FLSK/NLA) at Ndola. Victoria Falls is visible from the air as a permanent mist column at the Zambezi-Zimbabwe border; Lake Kariba forms a distinctive elongated shape along the southern boundary. Dry-season flying May-August offers excellent visibility; the wet season December-April produces ITCZ convective activity with tops commonly above FL450.
