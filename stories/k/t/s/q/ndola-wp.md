# Ndola

Zambia's third-largest city is a mosaic of limestone, copper, railways, and memory - founded in 1904 by a trader nicknamed Chiripula, anchored by a slave-market fig tree that has now fallen to termites, and shaped by the railhead that still runs through its center.

## Introduction

The Mukuyu tree stood for centuries, and for a stretch of the nineteenth century, Arab slave traders held markets beneath its branches, buying and selling human beings in this corner of central Africa before the rails or the boma or the mine shafts ever arrived. The fig tree is a kind of mukuyu, heavy-limbed, wide-canopied. This particular mukuyu eventually fell - termites, the explanation goes - but the memory of what happened beneath it is kept as a national monument. Ndola sits on a layer of history that cannot be flattered into a single narrative. Founded in 1904 as a colonial trading post by John Edward Chiripula Stephenson, it has been, in turn, a boma, a railhead, a refining town, a cement capital, the site of one of the most consequential plane crashes of the twentieth century, and the third-largest city in independent Zambia.

## Before the Boma

The Lamba people were here long before colonial mapmakers drew the first lines around the Copperbelt. They had migrated south from the Luba-Lunda kingdom around 1600, and by the time Stephenson arrived in 1904, the area around what would become Ndola was under Chief Mushili, with Senior Chief Chiwala holding broader authority in the Lambaland. Chief Chiwala himself had come from Malawi during the slave trade, a migration that tells its own layered story about the region's nineteenth century. The name Ndola comes from a small river that rises in the Kaloko Hills and drains into the Kafubu - the two waterways that still frame the city on its south side. When Stephenson planted his trading post, he was not founding a town on empty land. He was founding a colonial administrative point on ground that had already supported communities, chiefdoms, and commerce for generations. The Lamba chiefdom of Chiwala continues today - Chief Chiwala is still a significant interested party in the mining rights held on land adjacent to his traditional authority, a continuity that stretches across everything that has been built on top of it.

## The Railway That Made the Town

In 1907, Rhodesia Railways reached Ndola, and the town's future was set. From here you could take the train south to Bulawayo and on to Cape Town - a single iron line connecting central Africa to the southern ocean. Later the line was extended north into the Congo, eventually linking to the Benguela Railway that ran all the way across Angola to the Atlantic port of Lobito. Copper traveled east, south, and west on those tracks. Before the road network developed in the 1930s, northern Zambia's trade flowed through Ndola by a different route - a track north to Kapalala on the Luapula River, and boat transport from there into the Chambeshi system, making the whole Northern Province effectively Ndola's hinterland. The railhead defined the city. It is still defining it: the operating sections of the old Cape-to-Cairo line still carry freight through Ndola today, connecting Kitwe, Kabwe, Lusaka, Livingstone, and - across the Congo border - Lubumbashi.

## Limestone, Cement, and the Second Act

When copper crashed in the 1970s, Ndola needed another economy, and the ground beneath it offered one. The limestone reserves around the city are among the most homogeneous in the world - exactly the kind of consistent, pure deposit that makes cement production efficient. Between 1974 and 2009, more than half of all cement produced in Zambia came from a single plant five kilometers southeast of downtown: Chilanga Cement, Ndola Works, which drew its limestone from local quarries. In 2008, parent company Lafarge Cement Zambia completed a larger replacement plant at Chilanga itself, and Ndola's share of national production dropped. But the limestone beneath the city is still there, and Ndola Lime - wholly owned by ZCCM Investment Holdings - remains the country's sole producer of agricultural and industrial lime. The four processing plants on Ndola's southern and eastern edges sit on land close to Chief Chiwala's traditional chiefdom, which makes Chiwala himself an ongoing participant in the city's industrial economy, not a historical footnote.

## The Plane Crash and the Stadium

On September 18, 1961, an aircraft carrying United Nations Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjold crashed on the outskirts of Ndola during the Congo Crisis. Fifteen others died with him. The crash site, 15 kilometers west-northwest of downtown, is now the Dag Hammarskjold Crash Site Memorial - a national monument and a spot that continues to draw both pilgrims and investigators. The original Dag Hammarskjold Stadium, on the banks of the Kafubu River south of the city, was razed in the 1980s. Its replacement, the Levy Mwanawasa Stadium, rose in the northwest with 50,000 seats - the first modern stadium in Zambia and still home to Ndola's top-flight football club, Zesco United, nicknamed team ya ziko, the national team. Between the crash memorial and the stadium, the rail yards and the lime plants, the Copperbelt Museum's collection of regional gems and the thermal power station that stopped running when Kariba Dam came online in the 1960s, Ndola assembles itself the way most African cities do: from every layer it has ever had, all still visible at once.

## Flight Context

Located at 12.97°S, 28.63°E, elevation roughly 1,269 m (4,163 ft). Simon Mwansa Kapwepwe International Airport (FLSK, IATA: NLA) is 15 km west of downtown, just north of the Dag Hammarskjold Crash Site Memorial. The city is served by the surviving sections of the historic Cape-to-Cairo Railway, with freight lines running to Kitwe northwest, Kabwe and Lusaka south, and Lubumbashi in DR Congo via Sakania. Visible landmarks: Levy Mwanawasa Stadium (northwest), the Indeni oil refinery, Bwana Mkubwa open-cast mine 10 km southeast. The Kafubu River runs south of the city center. Lake Chilengwa, a limestone sinkhole, lies 14 km east at approximately 12°58'S 28°45'E. Recommended viewing altitude 6,000-10,000 ft AGL. Clearest visibility in the dry season (May-October).
