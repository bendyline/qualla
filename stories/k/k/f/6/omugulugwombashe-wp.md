# Omugulugwombashe

In a quiet forest in northern Namibia, the first shots of the war for independence were fired in 1966 - and the small settlement where they rang out became sacred ground for a nation.

**Location:** -17.7678, 14.7031

**Categories:** Populated places in the Omusati Region, Conflicts in 1966, 1966 in Africa, August 1966 in Africa

## Introduction

On a public holiday each August, Namibians look north to a patch of forest most of them will never visit, on the western edge of the Cuvelai floodplain. Nothing about Omugulugwombashe announces its importance. It is a small settlement with a clinic and a primary school, reached by a gravel road 22 kilometres west of Tsandi. Yet this is where a country began. On 26 August 1966, the first battle of the long war for Namibian independence was fought here, and the men who stood their ground that morning are now buried as national heroes on the very spot where they fell into history.

## A Mandate Revoked, a War Begun

The fight had been a long time coming. For decades South Africa had ruled the territory then called South West Africa, and in 1966 the International Court of Justice dismissed a case challenging that authority on procedural grounds, closing off a decade of legal effort. With peaceful avenues exhausted, the South West African People's Organization - SWAPO - turned to armed struggle, having founded its military wing years earlier. Its fighters, many returning from exile and training abroad, slipped into the north of the country to build the first camps of a liberation army on home soil. Omugulugwombashe was one of them, established in June 1966 under the command of John Ya Otto Nankudhu. His group had barely begun raising defensive structures, intending to train as many as ninety fighters, when the war found them first. Two months after the battle, in October 1966, the United Nations General Assembly formally stripped South Africa of its mandate and placed the territory under direct UN administration - but South Africa refused to comply with that resolution too.

## The Morning of 26 August

They never got the chance to prepare. At dawn on 26 August 1966, eight helicopters clattered in low over the trees, carrying troops of the South African Defence Force and South African Police. Only seventeen fighters were in the camp - outnumbered, half-armed, caught with their defences still half-built. The engagement was brief and one-sided. When it ended, two of the SWAPO fighters lay dead and one was wounded; eight more were captured, while the South African forces suffered no casualties at all. Measured by the cold arithmetic of war, it was a minor skirmish. Measured by what it began, it was the opening shot of a conflict that would grind on for more than two decades and end, at last, in an independent Namibia.

## The Price the Captured Paid

For the men taken alive, the battle was only the beginning of their ordeal. Many were shipped to Robben Island, the notorious prison off Cape Town where South Africa caged its political enemies, and there they served sentences that swallowed the better part of their lives. John Ya Otto Nankudhu and Julius Shaambeni Shilongo Mnyika each served seventeen years before their release in 1985. Immanuel Shifidi endured eighteen. Gaus Shikomba, sentenced to life in 1969, was not freed until 1984. Others who escaped the camp that day were hunted down in later years - Lameck Ithete was not arrested until 1969, and served three years in a Pretoria jail. These were not abstractions in a liberation narrative. They were young men who traded decades of their freedom for the idea of their country's, and most lived to see it realised.

## Hallowed Ground

A nation does not forget where it was born. After independence, Omugulugwombashe became one of only two state cemeteries in Namibia, a resting place for national heroes, and a memorial shrine was raised here and inaugurated by the country's founding president, Sam Nujoma. Later, Namibia's second president, Hifikepunye Pohamba, unveiled a statue of the six men who led the camp - John Nankudhu, his deputy Simeon Shixungileni, Patrick Iyambo, Victor Mensah, James Hamukwaya and Nelson Kavela. The government had already marked the site with a monument on the battle's thirtieth anniversary in 1996. What was once a hidden bush camp is now a place of pilgrimage, where the names of the founders are carved in stone for schoolchildren and dignitaries alike to read.

## A Symbol Larger Than the Fight

The strange power of Omugulugwombashe is that it matters far beyond what happened there. As a military action it changed nothing on the day - a camp overrun, a handful of men killed or jailed. But it was first, and being first made it everything. The date became Heroes' Day, a national holiday recognised by the United Nations, and the battle gave its name to symbols of the new state: the Omugulugwombashe Medal, the country's prestigious decoration for liberation-war veterans, and even the Omugulugwombashe Star, the name given to Namibia's first luxury passenger train in 2006. That train was a short-lived disappointment, its imported locomotives soon failing on Namibian rails. But the name endures, attached to a forgotten forest clearing that a country chose to remember as the place where it first stood up and fought back.

## Flight Context

Omugulugwombashe lies at roughly 17.77 degrees south, 14.70 degrees east in the Omusati Region of far northern Namibia, on the D3633 gravel road about 22 km west of Tsandi. The surrounding country is the flat, wooded fringe of the Cuvelai Basin - a maze of seasonal watercourses and scattered homesteads that floods into a vast shallow wetland after the rains and dries to dusty bush in winter. From the air it reads as low green-brown woodland threaded with pale sand tracks, with little high relief. The regional hub of Oshakati lies to the east. The nearest significant airport is Ondangwa (FYOA) to the east-northeast; Hosea Kutako International (FYWH) at Windhoek lies far to the south. Best viewed in the dry season for clear conditions; the wet season can leave much of the basin under sheet water and haze.
