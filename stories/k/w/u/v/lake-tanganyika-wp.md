# Lake Tanganyika

The world's longest freshwater lake and one of its oldest, home to 250 endemic cichlid species and the stage for a strange World War I naval campaign.

**Location:** -6.1000, 29.5000

**Categories:** lake, rift valley, ancient lake, Tanzania, DRC, Burundi, Zambia, cichlids

## Introduction

At depths below 200 meters, Lake Tanganyika has no oxygen and no life. Just toxic hydrogen sulphide fossilized in layers of water that haven't mixed with the surface in thousands of years. Above that line, in the oxygenated upper reaches, biologists have catalogued at least 250 endemic species of cichlid fish, almost all found nowhere else on Earth. The shallowest and deepest portions of the same lake are two nearly separate worlds. Tanganyika is the world's longest freshwater lake - 676 kilometers on a north-south axis - the second deepest after only Lake Baikal, and between 9 and 12 million years old. In East Africa it is simply called an ocean.

## Three Lakes That Became One

Tanganyika sits in the Albertine Rift, the western branch of the East African Rift system, where the continent is literally pulling apart. It is an ancient lake - one of only about twenty on Earth older than a million years - and its three basins have different ages. The central basin started forming 9 to 12 million years ago, the northern 7 to 8 million, the southern 2 to 4 million. During long dry periods those three basins were separate lakes, which is part of why the life inside Tanganyika is so extraordinary. The lake is shared by four countries: Tanzania holds 46 percent of the shoreline and the DRC 40 percent, with Burundi at the north tip and Zambia at the southern end. It drains west through the Lukuga River into the Congo system, and ultimately into the Atlantic at Banana, thousands of kilometers downstream.

## The Cichlids Of Tanganyika

Tanganyika's cichlids are so important to evolutionary biology that the lake has been called a natural laboratory of speciation. At least 250 species are endemic - roughly 98 percent of its cichlids are found nowhere else. They include Boulengerochromis microlepis, one of the largest cichlids in the world, and Neolamprologus multifasciatus, among the smallest. N. multifasciatus lives its entire life inside the discarded shells of a single snail species. Perissodus microlepis has evolved asymmetric jaws so it can sneak up on larger fish and bite scales off their sides. Some Trematocara species have been caught at depths greater than 200 meters - deeper than any other cichlid known. How a few deepwater genera survive in nearly oxygen-free water is not well understood. The lake also holds more than 200 crustacean species, 17 endemic snail genera, and Storm's water cobra, a fish-eating snake found only here.

## Burton, Speke, And A German Ferry

The first known Westerners to reach Tanganyika were the British explorers Richard Burton and John Speke, searching for the source of the Nile in 1858. Speke went north and found the real source, Lake Victoria. Later David Livingstone visited the lake and recorded the name 'Liemba' for its southern part, probably from the Fipa language. The name Tanganyika itself means 'stars' in Luvale. And in the First World War the lake became an improbable naval theatre. The Germans had complete early control via the gunboat Graf Goetzen. The British response was operatic: Lieutenant Commander Geoffrey Spicer-Simson shipped two armed motor boats, HMS Mimi and HMS Toutou, from England to the lake by rail, road, and river, arriving at Albertville - now Kalemie - and waiting until December 1915 to attack. The Germans' Kingani was captured, the Hedwig sunk in February 1916. In July 1916 Commander Zimmer scuttled the Graf Goetzen rather than surrender her. She was raised by British salvage crews in 1924, refurbished, and returned to service in 1927 as the MV Liemba. That same ship, now undergoing major renovation, has carried passengers up and down the lake for a century.

## The Protein Of A Region

Depending on whose numbers you trust, Tanganyika's fishery supplies somewhere between a quarter and three-fifths of the animal protein in the diets of the people who live along its shores. That is millions of people across Tanzania, Burundi, the DRC, and Zambia. Commercial fishing began in earnest in the mid-1950s, and has, together with warming water, put significant pressure on the stocks. Since the 1950s the lake's surface temperature has risen noticeably. Local fishing technique has evolved over centuries. The Lusenga is a wide net worked by one person from a canoe. Lift-netting involves two parallel canoes dropping a net deep, then hauling up together. Chiromila fishing uses three canoes - one anchored with a lantern, the other two working a net that begins encircling the lit one. On a moonless night from shore, the lantern fleet looks like a dropped necklace of stars.

## Flight Context

Located along the axis from about 3.4°S to 8.8°S at roughly 29.5°E. Principal airports along the shore: Bujumbura (ICAO: HBBA) at the north, Kigoma (HTKA) on the Tanzanian east coast, Kalemie (FZRF) on the Congolese mid-west, and Mbala (FLMP) near the southern end. Lake surface elevation 773 m; maximum depth 1,470 m. In clear weather the lake appears from altitude as a deep indigo ribbon bordered by forested rift escarpments. Expect significant afternoon convection in the wet season (November to April).
