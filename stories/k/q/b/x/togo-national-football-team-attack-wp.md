# Togo National Football Team Attack

A 2010 ambush on the Togolese football team in Angola's Cabinda enclave killed three people and exposed the long-simmering separatist conflict that the Africa Cup of Nations had inadvertently walked into.

**Location:** -5.7200, 12.2800

**Categories:** conflict, sports, terrorism, angola, football

## Introduction

The bus had just crossed from the Republic of the Congo into Cabinda when the shooting started. It was January 8, 2010, and the Togo national football team was traveling to its first match of the Africa Cup of Nations, hosted that year by Angola. Machine gun fire raked the convoy for thirty minutes. When it stopped, bus driver Mario Adjoua was dead. Assistant coach Amelete Abalo and sports journalist Stanislas Ocloo would die the following day. Nine others were wounded, including goalkeeper Kodjovi Obilale, who took a bullet in the lower back that fragmented into his abdomen. The attackers were not after the footballers at all. A separatist group called FLEC-PM -- the Front for the Liberation of the Enclave of Cabinda, Military Position -- said they had targeted the Angolan military escort at the head of the convoy. The Togolese players were collateral damage in a conflict most of the world had never heard of.

## An Enclave's Long War

Cabinda is an oddity on the map of Africa: a sliver of Angolan territory separated from the rest of the country by a strip of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Rich in offshore oil, Cabinda has long been at odds with Luanda. The Front for the Liberation of the Enclave of Cabinda had waged a low-intensity insurgency for decades, seeking independence for a province that many Cabindans felt had been absorbed against its will. By 2010, the movement had splintered into factions. The attack on the Togolese team was carried out by one of its more obscure offshoots -- fifteen fighters under a commander known as Sametonne, according to FLEC-PM secretary general Rodrigues Mingas, who was living in exile in France. Angola's decision to hold Africa Cup matches in Cabinda had placed an international sporting event squarely in a conflict zone, and the separatists seized the opportunity to make their cause visible.

## Thirty Minutes of Terror

The players' accounts are harrowing. Emmanuel Adebayor, Togo's captain and star striker, called it "one of the worst things I've ever been through in my life." He was among the least injured and found himself carrying screaming teammates into a hospital. Thomas Dossevi described "twenty minutes of shots, of blood and fear." Richmond Forson recalled how the luggage bus was riddled with bullets first -- the attackers apparently mistaking it for the team bus -- before gunfire turned on the coaches themselves. Obilale's injuries were so severe that South African doctors ultimately recommended leaving the bullet fragments in his abdomen rather than risk further damage by operating. The attack left psychological scars that proved even harder to treat. In April 2010, Adebayor announced his retirement from international football, saying he was "still haunted by the events I witnessed on that horrible afternoon." He would not return to the national team until November 2011.

## Withdrawal and Disqualification

In the immediate aftermath, the Togolese squad was torn. Some players wanted to withdraw entirely. Others, like Dossevi, argued for staying to compete "in memory of the dead" and to "show our national colours, our values, and that we are men." The Togolese government settled the debate by ordering the team home on security grounds. The Confederation Africaine de Football then disqualified Togo from the tournament. When Togo's sports minister requested readmission so the team could mourn its fallen members while honoring its commitment, CAF refused. The group stage proceeded with three teams instead of four. It was a bitter outcome for a squad that had done nothing wrong -- punished, in effect, for being attacked.

## Justice and Its Limits

Angola arrested nine suspects in the days following the attack, including two FLEC operatives captured near the ambush site. But the most prominent prosecutions drew sharp criticism. Four men -- a Catholic priest named Raul Tati, a lawyer, an economist, and a former police officer -- were convicted of crimes against state security for possessing FLEC documents and having traveled to Paris to meet exiled leaders. Their sentences ranged from three to five years. The court never established a direct link between the four and the attack itself. Human Rights Watch called them "activists" and described the trial as "a lost opportunity to restore justice in Angola, and particularly in Cabinda." The Angolan government, critics charged, had used the shooting as a pretext to crack down on anyone who had ever spoken publicly about Cabindan independence.

## What the Bullets Left Behind

The 2010 Africa Cup of Nations continued without Togo. Angola stepped up security, and organizers of the upcoming 2010 FIFA World Cup in South Africa insisted the attack had no bearing on their own preparations. But the shooting in Cabinda had raised uncomfortable questions about the intersection of sport and politics in Africa -- about what it means to host an international tournament in a nation where armed conflicts simmer just beyond the stadiums. For Togo, the legacy was personal. Three families lost loved ones. Obilale's professional career ended. An entire squad carried trauma that no amount of time could fully erase. The Cabindan separatist cause, briefly illuminated by worldwide headlines, receded again into obscurity. The football world moved on. The people caught in the crossfire did not have that luxury.
