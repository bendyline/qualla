# Piet Retief

Voortrekker leader Piet Retief wrote the manifesto that launched the Great Trek, negotiated a land treaty with the Zulu King Dingane, and was murdered -- along with his entire delegation -- at the moment of its ratification.

**Location:** -28.4269, 31.2700

**Categories:** history, great-trek, south-africa, voortrekker

## Introduction

The treaty was signed on 6 February 1838. By that afternoon, everyone who had signed it on the Boer side was dead. Piet Retief had led roughly a hundred men and servants to King Dingane's royal kraal to ratify a land cession that would open Natal to Voortrekker settlement. After the signing, Dingane invited the delegation to watch a performance by his soldiers. Then he ordered them seized. Retief was forced to watch as his son Pieter Cornelis and every member of his party were clubbed to death on the ridge called kwaMatiwane. He was killed last. The massacre would trigger the Battle of Blood River, reshape the political geography of southern Africa, and become one of the most contested events in the country's history -- claimed by some as martyrdom, understood by others as one act of violence within a much larger pattern of dispossession.

## A Huguenot Grandson on the Frontier

Retief was born on 12 November 1780 in the Wagenmakersvallei of the Cape Colony, today the town of Wellington in the Western Cape. His family were Boers of French Huguenot descent -- his great-grandfather, Francois Retif, had arrived as a refugee from Mer near Blois in 1689. Retief grew up on the family vineyard Welvanpas, working there until he was 27 before moving to the vicinity of Grahamstown. He acquired wealth through livestock but suffered repeated losses from Xhosa raids along the frontier, and he had a persistent talent for financial trouble -- going bankrupt at least twice through land speculation. These losses, combined with broader frustrations among the Dutch-speaking frontier farmers, fed the movement that would become the Great Trek. Retief became its voice. On 22 January 1837, he wrote the Voortrekker manifesto, published in the Grahamstown Journal, cataloguing the grievances that impelled the Boers to leave: no protection from armed raids, no redress against British policies, and inadequate compensation following the Slavery Abolition Act of 1833, which freed their enslaved laborers.

## Forward Into the Unknown

Retief's household departed in two wagons from his Winterberg farm in early February 1837, joining a party of 30 other wagons. They crossed the Orange River into territory beyond British control. When several trekking parties converged at the Vet River, Retief was elected "Governor of the United Laagers" -- a grand title for a loose coalition that quickly dissolved. He became the lone leader of the group moving east, toward the coast. On 5 October 1837, he established a camp of 54 wagons at Kerkenberg near the Drakensberg ridge, then rode forward with Jan Gerritze Bantjes and fourteen men to explore the land between the mountains and Port Natal. Retief was struck by the bay's potential as a free-trade port. He sent word back to the laager: the trekkers could now enter Natal. It was an invitation into a territory already governed by the Zulu kingdom, and the question of who held legitimate authority over this land -- the Zulu king, the Boer migrants, or the distant British crown -- would soon be answered in blood.

## The Treaty and the Trap

Retief began negotiations with Zulu king Dingane kaSenzangakhona in November 1837. Dingane agreed to Boer settlement in Natal on one condition: the Boers must recover cattle stolen by the rival Tlokwa people. Retief complied, using the reputation of Boer rifles to cow the Tlokwa into surrendering some 700 head of cattle. At Retief's request, Bantjes drew up the Retief-Dingane Treaty, outlining the areas of Natal to be ceded for Boer settlement. Despite warnings from others at Port Natal, Retief returned to Dingane's kraal on 25 January 1838 with a delegation of roughly a hundred people -- men and their servants -- to finalize the agreement. The deed of cession was signed on 6 February. What happened next has been interpreted through many lenses: as treachery, as a calculated act of self-defense by a king who saw an armed migration threatening his sovereignty, as a collision between two peoples whose ideas of land, treaties, and power were fundamentally incompatible. Dingane ordered the delegation killed. Their bodies were left on the kwaMatiwane hillside.

## Blood River and Beyond

The massacre did not end Boer settlement in Natal -- it accelerated it, and it hardened it. Dingane's forces attacked Voortrekker camps in the region, killing approximately 534 people in the area around present-day Weenen -- around 282 Voortrekker men, women, and children alongside 252 Khoikhoi and Basuto servants -- a settlement whose name comes from the Dutch for "weeping." On 16 December 1838, a Boer commando of roughly 470 men under Andries Pretorius met a Zulu force at the Ncome River. The Boers, positioned behind a laager of wagons with cannons and rifles, inflicted devastating losses. The Zulu dead reportedly numbered in the thousands; no Boers were killed. The battle became known as Blood River, and its anniversary was commemorated for generations -- first as a day of Boer triumph, later reinterpreted and contested as South Africa reckoned with its history. The short-lived Boer republic of Natalia followed, but it suffered from weak governance and was eventually annexed by the British Cape Colony.

## A Legacy in Contested Bronze

Retief remains one of the most complex figures in South African history. A town in Mpumalanga bears his name. Pietermaritzburg, the capital of KwaZulu-Natal, was named partly in his honor -- Erasmus Smit, the Voortrekker minister, recorded in his diary that the settlement's name combined Retief's first name with that of Gerrit Maritz, president of the Voortrekker council. (Originally it was Pietermauritzburg, incorporating both of Retief's names, before the spelling was simplified.) His manifesto articulated genuine grievances -- the frontier Boers lived in genuine insecurity and felt genuinely abandoned by the British government. But that manifesto also listed the abolition of slavery as a grievance, and the Great Trek he championed was, at its core, a movement of settlers claiming land already inhabited by other peoples. To tell Retief's story honestly requires acknowledging all of this: the courage of the trek, the horror of his death, and the dispossession that the movement he led inflicted on others.

## Flight Context

The town of Piet Retief lies at approximately 27.00S, 30.81E in southern Mpumalanga, near the Eswatini border. Key geographic features include the Drakensberg escarpment to the west and the rolling grasslands of the highveld. The Ncome River (Blood River) battlefield is approximately 60 km to the southeast at 28.37S, 30.53E. Piet Retief Airport (FAPI) serves the town. The broader story spans from Wellington in the Western Cape (33.64S, 19.00E) through Port Natal (Durban, FALE) to the Zulu heartland. The Drakensberg passes through which the Voortrekkers crossed are dramatically visible from altitude.
