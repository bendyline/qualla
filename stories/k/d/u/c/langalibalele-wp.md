# Langalibalele

King of the amaHlubi, Langalibalele refused to surrender his people's guns to colonial authorities in 1873 -- a stand that cost him decades on Robben Island and split the Colony of Natal down the middle.

**Location:** -29.2558, 29.4203

**Categories:** history, resistance, south-africa, colonial-era

## Introduction

His name meant "the blazing sun," and for the colonial authorities of Natal in the 1870s, that is exactly what Langalibalele became -- a problem too bright to look at directly, too dangerous to ignore. King of the amaHlubi, a Bantu-speaking people who had settled between the Buffalo and Blood rivers in what is now KwaZulu-Natal, Langalibalele found himself at the center of a crisis over guns, sovereignty, and the limits of colonial power. His refusal to register firearms that his people had legally acquired while working the diamond mines at Kimberley set off a chain of events that would send him to Robben Island, fracture the politics of two British colonies, and reverberate all the way to Nelson Mandela's own imprisonment on that same island a century later.

## A People Scattered and Regathered

The amaHlubi had already survived one catastrophe before Langalibalele was born around 1814. His father, King Mthimkhulu II, died in battle when both Langalibalele and his elder brother were still children. The regent, Prince Mahwanqa, fled northward across the Pongola River with the boys to seek sanctuary in the Wakkerstroom area of Mpumalanga. Other amaHlubi scattered -- some south to Pondoland, others west to the Orange Free State, still others into Basutoland under the protection of Chief Molapo. After King Shaka's assassination in 1828, Mahwanqa returned to the amaHlubi's traditional lands and began rebuilding. But the region was transforming rapidly around them. Voortrekkers were crossing the Drakensberg. The Zulu kingdom was fracturing. The British were establishing the Colony of Natal. By 1848, conflict with Zulu king Mpande forced Langalibalele and his people to flee south into the colony, where they were settled under British oversight and subject to "native law" -- a parallel legal system administered by chiefs but reviewable by colonial magistrates.

## Diamonds, Guns, and a Line in the Dust

The diamond rush of the early 1870s pulled thousands of young men from across southern Africa to the mines at Kimberley, in the British Colony of Griqualand West. Among them were many amaHlubi, who toiled underground and were sometimes paid not in money but in firearms -- a legal practice in Griqualand West, though one that made colonial authorities in neighboring Natal deeply uneasy. When the Hlubi laborers carried their guns home, Natal's magistrate at Estcourt, John Macfarlane, ordered Langalibalele to hand in his people's weapons for registration. The king did not know who among his people held firearms and refused to enforce the order. He sent five of the eight men the government specifically named, but the colonial authorities wanted total compliance. Sir Benjamin Pine, who arrived as lieutenant governor in July 1873, ordered Langalibalele's arrest. It was a demand loaded with menace -- in traditional African society, a chief summoned to the seat of power in this manner was often killed or had his cattle and wives confiscated.

## Flight Across the Drakensberg

Rather than submit, Langalibalele made plans to flee with his people across the Drakensberg into Basutoland via the Bushman's River Pass. The colonial government responded with a three-pronged military operation. Major Anthony Durnford -- who would later die at Isandlwana -- led one column to intercept the fleeing amaHlubi at the pass. A skirmish at the summit left British soldiers dead, an outcome that colonial Natal characterized as rebellion rather than what it was: a desperate people trying to escape arrest. Martial law was declared on 11 November 1873. Two flying columns pursued Langalibalele into Basutoland, entering through the Orange Free State and reaching the Maluti Mountains by early December. Langalibalele had thrown himself on the mercy of the Basuto chief Molapo, with whom his people had historical ties. But Molapo betrayed him, handing the king and five of his sons over to a local force who delivered them to Captain Allison on 13 December.

## The Trial That Divided a Colony

Langalibalele was tried and banished to Robben Island, but the sentence immediately drew condemnation. John Colenso, the first Bishop of Natal, traveled to England to plead the king's case, arguing that Langalibalele had never intended rebellion and that the trial was a miscarriage of justice. The Cape Colony's liberal government agreed. Minister John X. Merriman publicly declared the trial illegitimate, insisting that "no white man would have been sentenced so severely" and that Langalibalele "had been victimised because of his colour." The debate cut to the heart of colonial governance -- could a man be tried under one legal system and punished under another? The Cape's Prime Minister had to threaten resignation before a bill to move Langalibalele off Robben Island could pass. In August 1875, the Colonial Secretary Lord Carnarvon referred the case back to the Cape courts. Langalibalele was eventually transferred to a farm near Cape Town, but he remained under life arrest.

## The Blazing Sun's Long Shadow

Langalibalele died in 1889, still a prisoner. In keeping with amaHlubi tradition, his burial place was kept secret for over sixty years, until his grandson revealed the site to the Native Commissioner in Estcourt in October 1950. But his story refused to stay buried. The crisis over his imprisonment exposed the fundamental contradiction of colonial justice -- that "native law" could be invoked to control African populations but abandoned the moment its outcomes inconvenienced the colonial project. The inconsistent gun policies across British colonies that triggered the crisis contributed directly to Lord Carnarvon's ill-fated confederation scheme, which in turn led to the Anglo-Zulu War and the First Anglo-Boer War. In 1990, shortly after his own release from Robben Island, Nelson Mandela laid a wreath on Langalibalele's grave -- one prisoner of that island honoring another, separated by more than a century but connected by the same refusal to accept that defiance could be legislated away.
