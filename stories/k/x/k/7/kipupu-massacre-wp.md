# Kipupu Massacre

A 2020 militia attack on a remote South Kivu village that exposed the deepening cycle of ethnic violence in eastern Congo, where contested death tolls and competing narratives obscure the suffering of civilians caught between armed groups.

**Location:** -3.6400, 28.7000

**Categories:** conflict, human-rights, democratic-republic-of-the-congo, massacres

## Introduction

On the morning of July 16, 2020, the village of Kipupu in eastern Congo's Mwenga Territory was not on any international news ticker. It was a settlement in South Kivu, one of the most volatile provinces in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where armed groups had been killing civilians for decades with little consequence. By nightfall, Kipupu had joined the long, terrible list of Congolese villages where the word "massacre" became the only adequate description of what had happened. Gunmen from the Ngumino and Twiganeho militias, both affiliated with the Banyamulenge community, attacked the village with firearms and machetes, set homes ablaze, and stole livestock. What followed was a dispute not over whether people died, but over how many -- a dispute that, in its own way, revealed as much about the conflict as the attack itself.

## Roots in a Deeper Wound

The violence at Kipupu did not erupt from nowhere. Since 2019, clashes between militias loyal to the National Coalition of the People for the Sovereignty of Congo (CNPSC), a Mai-Mai coalition, and Banyamulenge armed groups had been intensifying across the highlands of South Kivu. The CNPSC viewed the Banyamulenge -- a Congolese Tutsi community with cultural ties to Rwanda -- as foreign interlopers whose armed groups represented another Rwandan-backed rebellion, much like the M23 movement that had destabilized the region years earlier. The Banyamulenge militias, for their part, maintained they were self-defense forces protecting a community that faced extermination. Both sides had committed atrocities. Civilians on all sides paid the highest price, trapped between groups whose grievances, however rooted in real history, were prosecuted through indiscriminate killing.

## The Attack and Its Contested Toll

Provincial deputies from South Kivu reported that 220 people were killed as the militias swept through Kipupu and surrounding areas. Houses burned. Machetes and gunfire cut through a population with no meaningful protection. But the Kivu Security Tracker, an independent monitoring group that tracks armed conflict in eastern Congo, put the death toll at 18 -- a figure more than ten times lower than the provincial claim. The gap between these numbers was never conclusively resolved. What remained beyond dispute was the nature of the attack: armed men targeting a civilian population that had done nothing to provoke the assault. Whether 18 families or 220 families buried their dead, each person killed was someone's parent, child, or neighbor, lost in a conflict whose logic had long since ceased to serve the people it claimed to protect.

## Voices Raised, and Silenced

The attack provoked outrage that briefly punctured the world's indifference to eastern Congo's suffering. Around thirty elected officials from South Kivu called on the national government and the Armed Forces of the DRC (FARDC) to strengthen civilian protection in the region. They also appealed to MONUSCO, the United Nations peacekeeping mission, to do more. Opposition politician Martin Fayulu demanded an international investigation. Nobel laureate Denis Mukwege, himself from South Kivu, condemned the violence. Yet the response also exposed the fractures that make accountability impossible in eastern Congo. Some Banyamulenge activists denied the massacre entirely. The Mahoro Peace Association, a Banyamulenge group, alternately claimed the attack never happened and attempted to redirect blame toward CNPSC attacks on Banyamulenge civilians. One official from South Kivu reported receiving death threats after publicly condemning the killings.

## A Pattern Without an End

Kipupu was not the first massacre in Mwenga Territory, and it was not the last. Eastern Congo has endured armed conflict almost continuously since the mid-1990s, a legacy of the Rwandan genocide's spillover, the exploitation of mineral wealth by armed groups, and the persistent weakness of state authority in remote provinces. The DRC's list of massacres stretches across decades and fills pages. For the people of Kipupu, the aftermath looked like the aftermath everywhere in South Kivu: displacement, grief, and the knowledge that no investigation would bring meaningful justice. The village sits in a landscape of extraordinary beauty -- green hills rolling toward the shores of Lake Tanganyika -- that has been scarred by extraordinary cruelty. The gap between what the land promises and what its people endure is the central tragedy of eastern Congo.
