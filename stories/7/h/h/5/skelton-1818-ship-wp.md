# Skelton (1818 ship)

The first vessel to sail direct from Scotland to Australia, her captain's published account opening the South Atlantic trade routes to British commerce.

**Location:** -21.8167, -39.3333

**Categories:** 1818 ships, Ships built in Whitby, Age of Sail merchant ships of England, Maritime incidents in July 1828, Australian emigration history

## Introduction

Captain James Dixon kept notes. When he sailed from Leith on 20 June 1820 with fifty-seven passengers and seventeen crew, he carried with him not just emigrants bound for the Cape and Australia but the intention to document everything - ports, prices, politics, the practical geography of a route no Scottish vessel had ever attempted. The ship was Skelton, built two years earlier at Whitby. She would be the first to sail direct from Scotland to Australia. Her captain's later book, crammed with economic and commercial observations, would help open the route for those who followed.

## A Ship Built for Hard Economics

Skelton first appeared in Lloyd's Register in 1819 under Dixon, who was both master and owner - a hint at the era's financial pressures that pushed captains to take on ownership risk. Her trade was initially the London-Quebec run, a workmanlike timber and passenger route across the North Atlantic. By 1820 the listing had changed to London-New South Wales. The British economy was weak that year, vessel demand was weak, and enterprising shipowners began looking south for emigrants willing to pay passage to the Cape and Australia. Dixon spotted the opportunity and became one of those pioneers, turning his Whitby-built merchantman into a transport for people willing to risk everything for a new world.

## Measles at Table Bay

Skelton reached Table Bay on 28 September 1820 after fourteen weeks at sea. The passage had carried disease. The colony's medical officer, meeting the vessel at anchor, limited how many could land because of an outbreak of measles aboard. Three of four passengers who had booked passage to the Cape still managed to disembark. The ship then sailed on for Hobart Town, which she reached on 27 November, and Port Jackson - modern Sydney - on 17 January 1821. Every arrival was a small victory against a very long logistical chain, and Dixon was recording it all in the notes that would become his published account.

## Kangaroo Skins and Ox Horns

The return manifest tells its own story of early colonial economics. Skelton left Sydney on 10 June 1821 for Hull via Rio de Janeiro, carrying 181 bales of wool, 40 tons of timber and plank, 1,680 kangaroo skins, a little wattle bark, and 2,800 ox horns. The wool and timber would be unsurprising to anyone who knows Australian export history; the kangaroo skins and ox horns reveal the more opportunistic side of the trade. She rounded Cape Horn on 3 August and reached Sugarloaf Mountain and Rio de Janeiro on 23 August - the first vessel in five years to arrive at Rio direct from Port Jackson. Three days after departing Rio, two unidentified vessels approached in suspicious formation. Dixon prepared to resist. They eventually left.

## A Route That Held

Skelton arrived at the Isle of Wight on 24 November 1821, the Downs the following day, the Humber on the 27th, and finally Leith on 5 December - a round trip of nearly eighteen months and Dixon's voyage, as the record drily notes, was a commercial success. That mattered more than the individual journey. Between 1820 and 1824, ten vessels sailed from Leith to Australia, following the trail Dixon had blazed. The route he had proved economically viable carried Scottish emigrants south for decades afterward, Skelton among them on later runs. Her voyage helped write a piece of the nineteenth-century British Empire into being.

## Wrecked West of Anguilla

By 1828, Skelton's master had changed from Tait to Percy, and her trade had shifted again - this time London to Trinidad, the West Indies run that had absorbed so many aging merchantmen. On 30 July 1828, sailing from Trinidad toward London, she struck a submerged rock approximately ten miles west of Anguilla. The rock lay thirteen feet below the water - invisible, unmapped, fatal. Skelton foundered. Her crew survived, reaching Tortola by boat and being reported at safety by Lloyd's List on 23 September. The ship that had opened a Scottish route to Australia ended her working life on a Caribbean reef, a small item in the shipping columns, the final entry in a career that mattered more than the wreck suggests.

## A Record From Altitude

The coordinates mark the approximate stretch of Atlantic where Skelton would have tracked northward from Rio de Janeiro toward Europe in September 1821. From cruising altitude the Brazilian coast curves west and south of this position; offshore, the waters above the Vitória-Trindade chain of seamounts stretch deep blue toward the horizon. This is the maritime corridor that carried Dixon home, and the invisible road that his published account would later help others follow.
