# Skelton (1818 ship)

The first vessel to sail direct from Scotland to Australia, her captain's published account a detailed field guide to the Cape, Van Diemen's Land, New South Wales and Rio de Janeiro.

**Location:** -21.8167, -39.3333

**Categories:** 1818 ships, Ships built in Whitby, Age of Sail merchant ships of England, Maritime incidents in July 1828, Australian emigration history

## Introduction

Captain James Dixon kept notes. When he sailed from Leith on 20 June 1820 with fifty-seven passengers and seventeen crew, he carried with him not just emigrants bound for the Cape and Australia but the intention to document what he found - ports, prices, politics, the practical geography of a route no vessel from Scotland had yet sailed direct. The ship was Skelton, built two years earlier at Whitby. She would be the first to sail direct from Scotland to Australia. Her captain's book, published in Edinburgh in 1822 and crammed with economic and commercial observations, was a guide for those who followed.

## A Ship Built for Hard Economics

Skelton first appeared in Lloyd's Register in 1819 under Dixon, who was both master and owner. Her trade was initially the London-Quebec run across the North Atlantic. By 1820 the listing had changed to London-New South Wales. The British economy was weak that year, vessel demand was weak, and enterprising shipowners began looking south for emigrants willing to pay passage to the Cape and Australia. Dixon spotted the opportunity and became one of those pioneers, turning his Whitby-built, three-masted merchantman into a transport for emigrants.

## Measles at Table Bay

Skelton reached Table Bay on 28 September 1820 after fourteen weeks at sea. The passage had carried disease. The colony's medical officer limited how many could land because of an outbreak of measles aboard. Three of four passengers who had booked passage to the Cape still managed to disembark. The ship left the Cape on 14 October and sailed on for Hobart Town, which she reached on 27 November, and Port Jackson - modern Sydney - on 17 January 1821. Every arrival was a small victory against a very long logistical chain, and Dixon was recording it all in the notes that would become his published account.

## Kangaroo Skins and Ox Horns

The return manifest tells its own story of early colonial economics. Skelton left Sydney on 10 June 1821 for Hull via Rio de Janeiro, carrying 181 bales of wool, 40 tons of timber and plank, 1,680 kangaroo skins, a little wattle bark, and 2,800 ox horns. The wool and timber would be unsurprising to anyone who knows Australian export history; the kangaroo skins and ox horns reveal the more opportunistic side of the trade. She rounded Cape Horn on 3 August and reached Sugarloaf Mountain and Rio de Janeiro on 23 August - the first vessel in five years to arrive at Rio direct from Port Jackson. Dixon sailed from Rio on 24 September. Three days later, a sloop and a schooner approached in a suspicious manner and would not identify themselves. Dixon prepared to resist. They eventually left.

## A Route That Held

Skelton arrived at the Isle of Wight on 24 November 1821, the Downs the following day, the Humber on the 27th, and finally Leith on 5 December - a round trip of nearly eighteen months, and a commercial success. That mattered more than the individual journey. Between 1820 and 1824, ten vessels sailed from Leith to Australia, and within two years a Leith joint-stock venture, the Australian Company, was running the first planned regular service to the colony. Skelton herself made the run again, sailing from Leith in 1822. Her voyage helped write a piece of the nineteenth-century British Empire into being.

## Wrecked West of Anguilla

By 1828, Skelton's master had changed from Tait to Percy, and her trade had shifted again - this time London to Trinidad, the West Indies run that had absorbed so many aging merchantmen. On 30 July 1828, sailing from Trinidad toward London, she struck a submerged rock approximately ten miles west of Anguilla. The rock lay thirteen feet below the water - invisible, and fatal. Skelton foundered. Her crew survived, reaching Tortola by boat and being reported at safety by Lloyd's List on 23 September. The ship that had opened a Scottish route to Australia ended her working life on a Caribbean rock, a small item in the shipping columns, the final entry in a career that mattered more than the wreck suggests.

## A Record From Altitude

The coordinates are the position Dixon recorded, three days out of Rio on 27 September 1821, when the sloop and schooner closed on Skelton. From cruising altitude the Brazilian coast lies some 200 kilometers to the west; to the north and east, the Vitória-Trindade chain of seamounts runs out into deep blue water. This is the maritime corridor that carried Dixon home, and the invisible road that his published account would later help others follow.
