# Buritizeiro

On the left bank of the Sao Francisco River, in a town named for a palm tree, the great river of Brazil churns into rapids before a 1920 bridge that was supposed to reach the Amazon.

**Location:** -17.3508, -44.9619

**Categories:** Municipalities in Minas Gerais, Sao Francisco River, Brazilian cerrado

## Introduction

The name comes from a palm tree. Buriti - Mauritia vinifera - a tall, slender palm with fan-shaped leaves that grows in soggy patches across the Brazilian cerrado, marking where water sits just below the surface. A grove of them is a buritizal. Enough of them together, on a particular stretch of the Sao Francisco River in northern Minas Gerais, and eventually a town will take their name. Buritizeiro sits on the left bank, 836 meters above sea level, looking across at its older sibling, Pirapora. Between the two towns, the Sao Francisco - the river Brazilians call Velho Chico, Old Francis - spills over a six-hundred-meter run of rapids. The rapids begin beneath the Marechal Hermes bridge, built in 1920 to carry a railroad that was supposed to reach Belem, in the Amazon, and never did. The tracks stopped here. So did the train that was going to stitch Brazil together end to end.

## The River That Would Not Be Tamed

The Sao Francisco is a working river. It has been for centuries. Steamboats once pushed upriver from Pirapora toward Montes Claros, carrying salt and cattle and news. Fishermen still pole their canoes through the morning mist, throwing nets for surubim and dourado. But at the Marechal Hermes bridge, the river remembers that it is not a road. It accelerates, breaks into white water, tumbles through a 600-meter stretch of rapids. Fishermen travel from across Brazil to stand in the spray and cast into the pockets of slack water between the rocks. It is a ritual as much as a sport. You show up at dawn, you pay your respects to Old Francis, and if you are lucky the river gives you a fish in return.

## The Railroad That Stopped

The plan was audacious. Build a railroad from the center of Brazil all the way to Belem, the Amazon port where the continent opens to the Atlantic. Lay the tracks north from Minas Gerais, cross the Sao Francisco at Pirapora, keep going through the cerrado and the caatinga and into the rainforest. In 1920 the bridge went up. Steel trusses, a proud piece of early republican engineering named after Marshal Hermes da Fonseca, a recent Brazilian president. And then the tracks stopped just north of the river. Money ran out. Political attention drifted. The frontier was harder than anyone had allowed for. Pirapora became the end of the line instead of a waypoint. The name of the settlement on the south bank, Sao Goncalo das Tabocas, was changed to Pirapora in 1923. Three decades later, in 1953, the villages that had grown up around it became municipalities of their own. Buritizeiro had to wait until 1962.

## A Town of Waterfalls and Cattle

Step away from the river and Buritizeiro spreads out into the Paracatu microregion - humid subtropical, hot summers, dry winters, the land folded just gently enough to make streams run and cattle graze. The farms are big. Soybeans dominate the planted area, 24,000 hectares in the 2006 count, with corn, beans, and rice following behind. Cotton still gets planted. Coffee grows on eight hundred hectares. Pastures cover more than 200,000 hectares, grazing 118,000 head of cattle. Hidden in the folds of the land are waterfalls with names like Cachoeira Grande, Cachoeira das Andorinhas, Cachoeira do Riacho Doce. The Fazenda dos Cristais holds two of them. These are the quiet destinations of weekend drives - places where a local family parks the truck, unpacks lunch, and spends an afternoon in the cool spray while the cerrado hums around them.

## A Place Named for a Tree

The buriti palm is one of those species that means more than itself. Its fruit is pressed into an orange-yellow juice sold at bus stations across central Brazil. Its fibers are woven into mats and bags. Its presence in the landscape is a promise of water below ground, which in a region of dry winters is a useful promise to be able to read. The Cerrado depends on buriti palms to hold moisture and feed wildlife. When people settled this stretch of the Sao Francisco, they did not bother with a grander name. They pointed at the groves and called it Buritizeiro - the place of the buriti. That the town exists at all says something about the logic of the Brazilian interior. People go where the water is, where the land will grow crops and graze animals, where a river provides both drinking water and a road. Buritizeiro, population 28,121 in 2020, has managed to do this for a long time.

## Flight Context

Located at 17.35 S, 44.96 W, on the left bank of the Sao Francisco River across from Pirapora in northern Minas Gerais. The Marechal Hermes bridge and the stretch of rapids below it are the defining landmarks - best viewed from 2,000-4,000 ft. Paracatu-Vianopolis Airport (SNVB) is the nearest small field, though Montes Claros Airport (SBMK / MOC), about 170 km northeast, has better service.
