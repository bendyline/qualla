# Biribiri State Park

A ghost village of 1,200 workers sits inside a 16,999-hectare park of waterfalls, maned wolves, and a bell cast in the factory that fed them.

**Location:** -18.1454, -43.6189

**Categories:** State parks of Brazil, Cerrado, Industrial heritage, Espinhaco Mosaic

## Introduction

The clock on the village church came from the Portuguese royal family. The bell was cast in the factory itself, made by the same workers who lived in the small valley town where 1,200 people spun textiles for a century. When Estamparia SA closed its doors in 1976, the village emptied. The church stayed. The clock still tells time. The houses still stand, and today visitors rent them from the company that once employed their occupants' parents. Vila de Biribiri is not a ruin. It is a village still owned, still maintained, simply unoccupied by the work that built it. Walking its stone paths, you are crossing an abandoned industrial town surrounded by 170 square kilometers of cerrado wilderness in one of the least-visited corners of Minas Gerais.

## The Bishop's Factory

Joao Antonio dos Santos, Bishop of Diamantina, founded the Estamparia textile mill in 1876 in what was then a fazenda in the Serra do Espinhaco. It was one of the earliest and most important textile factories in Minas Gerais. The Biribiri River powered the hydroelectric turbines that ran the looms and lit the village. Workers lived in small stone houses clustered around a warehouse, a school, and a church. For a hundred years the mill ran, the river turned, the looms clacked. Then the Brazilian textile industry contracted, the factory closed in 1976, and the village emptied out almost overnight. Estamparia SA still owns it. The buildings remain in remarkably good condition. Two restaurants now serve the day-trippers who hike in from Diamantina 13 kilometers away.

## Cerrado on the Espinhaco

Biribiri State Park covers 16,999 hectares in the Serra do Espinhaco, the quartzite mountain range that gives Diamantina its diamonds and its name. Altitudes range from 720 meters in the north to 1,480 meters in the south - enough vertical relief to hold alpine meadows, gallery forest, and the tough shrub-and-grass cerrado that is South America's most threatened biome outside the Amazon. Vegetation here includes Vellozia squamata, which flowers in white rings across the rocky fields, the native pequi tree called Caryocar brasiliense whose fruit shows up in mineiro cuisine, jacaranda, pau-santo, mangaba - the region's dry endemic flora. The wettest month is December. Temperatures average 18 Celsius through the year. It is cerrado at its most rugged and accessible.

## Wolves and Caracaras

The maned wolf - Chrysocyon brachyurus, a tall russet predator that looks like a fox on stilts - lives in the park alongside cougars and crested caracaras. The giant anteater walks here, probing termite mounds with its long tongue. The rock cavy, a small rodent shaped like a guinea pig, scrambles over quartzite ledges. Three bird species draw specialists to the park: the lesser nothura, a partridge-like ground bird; the helmeted manakin with its red crown; and the hyacinth visorbearer, a hummingbird endemic to this exact slice of the Espinhaco that flashes iridescent blue and violet over the rocky fields. Biribiri is one of the best places left to see it. The park protects habitat that has largely disappeared elsewhere in Minas Gerais.

## Waterfalls and the Slave Trail

Six kilometers from the entrance, the Cachoeira do Sentinela drops into a pool visitors can swim in. Six kilometers further on is the Cachoeira dos Cristais, reachable by car. The Caminho dos Escravos - the Slave Trail - runs twenty kilometers through the interior of the park, a path carved and walked by enslaved Africans who worked the diamond fields of Diamantina in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The name itself is a marker of who built the infrastructure that made colonial Minas Gerais rich - people forced into the work, moving gold and diamonds on their backs through these same hills. Other pools called Agua Limpa and Estudante provide swimming spots. The park is open every day. Admission as of recent years has been free. May through July are cold and dry, the best hiking months.

## The Park That Exists Because It Does

Biribiri State Park was created by decree 39.909 of September 22, 1998, named after the Fazenda Biribiri that covered most of its area. It joined the Espinhaco Mosaic of conservation units in 2010. And like many Brazilian state parks, it has struggled with enforcement - in April 2013 the Minas Gerais Public Ministry charged that the State Forestry Institute was failing to staff, plan, or protect Biribiri along with five other units. There is one caretaker in a shack at the entrance. A lookout house sits at a high point for fire season. Infrastructure is minimal. The park survives mostly because the topography is too rugged for casual development and because a handful of locals care about it. Go in the dry months. Hire a guide. The hyacinth visorbearer does not advertise itself.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 18.15 S, 43.62 W in the Serra do Espinhaco of northern Minas Gerais, near the historic diamond city of Diamantina 300 km north of Belo Horizonte. Elevations range from 720 to 1,480 meters. Nearest airports are Diamantina (SBDN) 15 km south and Confins (SBCF) 290 km south near Belo Horizonte. Visible landmarks include the Jequitinhonha River basin, the quartzite ridges of the Espinhaco range, and the abandoned village of Vila de Biribiri with its church spire.
