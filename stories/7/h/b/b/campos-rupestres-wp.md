# Campos Rupestres

On quartzite ridges rising out of central Brazil, in soil so thin and so poor that nearly a third of the plants grow nowhere else on Earth, the rupestrian grasslands keep their strange and stubborn inventory.

**Location:** -18.2448, -43.6927

**Categories:** Ecoregions of Brazil, Grasslands of Brazil, Espinhaco Range, Tropical and subtropical grasslands, Flora of Minas Gerais

## Introduction

The word is rupestrian - rock-dwelling. Where the Espinhaco Range and other old ranges push up above 900 meters across Minas Gerais, Bahia, and Goias, the forest thins and something stranger appears. Rocky outcrops scatter the landscape like broken teeth. Grasses run between the rocks. Bromeliads cling to crevices. Orchids flower from ledges. This is campo rupestre - a discontinuous montane ecoregion that covers less than one percent of Brazil, about 66,447 square kilometers in its broadest definition, and yet holds about 15 percent of the country's plant diversity. Around 30 percent of the species found here grow nowhere else on Earth. The soils are terrible - shallow, acidic, starved of phosphorus, lying on rock that has weathered for hundreds of millions of years. The wind is strong. The winters are dry. The summers are wet. And somehow, on this impoverished stone, life has found reasons to keep inventing itself.

## Old Land, Strange Plants

Campo rupestre grows on quartzite, granite, migmatite, gneiss, metarenite, and itabirite - a suite of ancient rocks that have been exposed and eroded for hundreds of millions of years. The landscape is, in geologic terms, impossibly stable. It has not been glaciated. It has not been submerged. It has not been buried under younger sediments. It has simply sat there, slowly weathering, slowly shedding nutrients, slowly forcing its plants to figure out how to live on almost nothing. Ecologists compare it with three other places in the world: the Pantepui Highlands of Venezuela, the Greater Cape of South Africa, and Southwest Australia. All four share the same pattern - ancient stable rock, terrible soil, and an explosion of endemic species that have had geological ages to specialize. The Espinhaco Range alone holds 7 percent of all Brazilian plant species. When scientists sampled three different ironstone sites within campo rupestre, they shared less than 5 percent of their plant lineages with each other. Each outcrop was, in effect, its own ecological island.

## Grasses and Rocks, Different Neighborhoods

Walk through campo rupestre and the vegetation changes abruptly with the substrate. On the sandy patches between rocks, grasses and grass-like plants dominate - members of the families Poaceae, Cyperaceae, and Eriocaulaceae. Among them bloom the paper-like inflorescences of sempre-vivas, the everlasting flowers harvested and dried for sale across Brazil. On the rocky outcrops themselves, orchids and bromeliads take over. The Velloziaceae, a strange family of rosette-forming monocots, produce white or violet flowers from cushions of dead leaves. The Clusiaceae - related to mangosteens - push resinous stems out of crevices. Shrubs of Lychnophora, known locally as arnica do campo or candeia, bring silver-leafed resistance to the fire-prone slopes. Almost every rock seems to have its own assemblage, shaped by microclimate, drainage, and the depth of whatever thin soil has accumulated in a crack.

## Who Else Lives Here

Up close, the grasslands can look empty. From a distance, they hide more than you expect. Tapirs browse the transitional forests along streams. Capybaras gather in the riparian zones where water pools. Maned wolves trot through on their long legs, looking for small mammals and fruit. Bush dogs, rare and mostly nocturnal, use the dense gallery forests as cover. Armadillos dig. Lizards and tortoises populate the warmer zones. Brazilian mergansers, now among the world's rarest waterbirds, fish in the fast-running streams that drain off the plateau. The giant otter appears on its list of threatened mammals. The yellow-bellied seedeater and the reticulate leaf frog cling to small, scattered habitats. The fossorial giant rat and the orange-brown Atlantic tree-rat are names most people will never hear spoken aloud, because the animals themselves are rarely seen and the habitats that hold them are small and shrinking.

## Fire, Mining, and the Cost of Being Overlooked

Campo rupestre fires are ancient. Some of the plants depend on them - species that flower after a burn, rootstocks that resprout. But fire at the wrong frequency turns destructive. Too often, and even the adapted species start to fail. Too rare, and woody shrubs take over grassy patches. The main threats come from somewhere less forgiving than fire. Mining is first. The same itabirite that hosts some of the richest plant communities is also iron ore - and Brazil, the world's second-largest iron ore producer, has pulled down hills across Minas Gerais to feed global steel. Eucalyptus plantations, cattle ranching, plant harvesting, tourism, urban expansion, climate change, invasive species - each chips away. And yet campo rupestre has been under-studied compared to Brazil's forests. The general assumption - that grasslands and rocky outcrops are less interesting than trees - has carried its usual cost. Conservation areas were drawn with forest in mind. Campo rupestre, sitting on the wrong kind of land, often sits outside the lines on the map. The species that depend on it do not always have time to wait for the lines to be redrawn.

## Flight Context

Located at 18.24 S, 43.69 W, across the Espinhaco Range of central-eastern Brazil, mostly in Minas Gerais with additional areas in Bahia and Goias. Elevations from 900 to 2,000+ m; dramatic rocky outcrops best viewed from 6,000-10,000 ft. Nearest airports: Diamantina (SNDT), about 5 km east-northeast of these coordinates on the Diamantina plateau; Belo Horizonte's Tancredo Neves (SBCF / CNF), about 155 km south, for broader access to the Espinhaco. Clearest visibility in the dry season (June-August). Watch for strong afternoon updrafts over the quartzite ridges.
