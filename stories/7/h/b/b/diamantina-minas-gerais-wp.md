# Diamantina, Minas Gerais

A UNESCO-listed colonial town where diamonds built Brazil's richest mining district and a freed enslaved woman became the lifelong partner of one of the colony's wealthiest men.

**Location:** -18.2489, -43.6000

**Categories:** Municipalities in Minas Gerais, 1831 establishments in Brazil, World Heritage Sites in Brazil

## Introduction

For nine years, the Portuguese Crown had no idea its colony was sitting on a diamond deposit so rich it would become the western world's great source of diamonds. The governor of Minas Gerais, D. Lourenço de Almeida, confirmed the discovery in 1729, and Portugal's response was swift and absolute: full imperial control. In the mountains 292 kilometers by road north of Belo Horizonte, at 1,114 meters above sea level, a small chapel town called Arraial do Tejuco became the beating heart of the great diamond rush of the eighteenth century. The Baroque churches and cobblestone streets still standing here have watched three centuries pass without losing their colonial bones.

## Rotten Water and Cold Mountains

The place's early recorded names in these Espinhaco highlands are Tupi: tyiuka, meaning rotten water, and Ybyty-ro'y, cold mountain. The first survived, in modified form, through the colonial period. When Portuguese settlers founded a chapel honoring Saint Anthony of Padua here in 1713, they called the settlement Arraial do Tejuco. It sat in territory occupied by Indigenous peoples of the Macro-Je language family. The first documented reports of outside contact trace back to 16th-century expeditions up the Jequitinhonha and Sao Francisco rivers. Everything that happened afterward flowed from one discovery sixteen years after the chapel was raised.

## The Intendency of Diamonds

When diamonds surfaced in 1729, Portugal moved quickly and harshly. In 1734, the Crown established the Intendency of Diamonds, a regime so controlling that residents needed permits to enter, trade, or travel within the district. Smuggling carried severe penalties. The Crown's monopoly over these deposits held until 1845. Tejuco was one of the largest diamond mining operations in the western world during the 18th century, and by century's end it had the third-largest population in the Captaincy of Minas, behind Vila Rica (modern Ouro Preto). The Estrada Real, the royal road established in 1697 to push colonial development inland, eventually reached up from the coast through Ouro Preto and terminated here, funneling diamonds down to Portuguese ships and hauling flour, salt, and manufactured goods back up into the mountains.

## Chica da Silva

The most extraordinary figure of colonial Tejuco was Francisca da Silva de Oliveira, known to history as Chica da Silva. Born around 1730 into slavery, she was purchased and then freed by Joao Fernandes de Oliveira, the contractor who held the royal diamond concession and who was, by virtue of that concession, one of the richest men in colonial Brazil. They never formally married, but they lived together for about fifteen years and had thirteen children. In a society structured by rigid racial hierarchy, Chica da Silva moved through the upper reaches of Tejuco's society, wearing the finest fabrics and attending religious processions in a position that would have been unimaginable for nearly any other woman of African descent in the colony. Her story has been adapted into a celebrated 1976 film, songs and a television drama shown abroad, though the historical Chica has often been obscured beneath the legend.

## A Girl's Diary and a President's Hometown

In 1893, a thirteen-year-old girl named Alice Dayrell Caldeira Brant began keeping a diary of her life in late-19th-century Diamantina. Published decades later under the pen name Helena Morley as Minha Vida de Menina, the book captured a mining town in decline with such clarity that the American poet Elizabeth Bishop translated it into English as The Diary of Helena Morley, giving it a second life abroad. A few years after Alice began her diary, another Diamantina child was born who would leave an even larger mark: Juscelino Kubitschek, born 1902, who became President of Brazil from 1956 to 1961 and oversaw the construction of Brasilia. When Kubitschek rose to power, first as governor of Minas Gerais and then as president, he brought improvements home: the Federal School of Dentistry, the Hotel Tijuco, the state school named for his mother Julia. The town that had shaped his boyhood never forgot the favor.

## Heritage of Humanity

In 1831, the town finally emancipated itself from the neighboring municipality of Serro and took the name Diamantina, formalizing what everyone already knew; it became a city in 1838. In 1938, on its centennial as a city, the National Institute of Historic and Artistic Heritage designated the historic center a National Historic Heritage site. UNESCO elevated it to World Heritage status in 1999. The 2022 census counted 47,702 people in the municipality, which spans about 3,890 square kilometers. Tourism, services, and small industry support the economy. Beyond the cobblestone streets and Baroque churches, the 16,999-hectare Biribiri State Park contains the historic village of Biribiri and preserves stretches of the cerrado highlands where the diamond streams still run. The Jequitinhonha River, one of the major rivers of eastern Brazil, flows east of town.

## Flight Context

Diamantina sits at 18.25 degrees S, 43.60 degrees W, in the Espinhaco highlands of northern Minas Gerais at 1,114 meters elevation. The best visual approach comes from the south, following the ridgeline from Belo Horizonte. Diamantina Airport (SNDT) lies about 6 km west-northwest of town; in August 2026 Azul Conecta began scheduled Cessna Grand Caravan flights from Belo Horizonte-Confins (SBCF), about 160 km south-southwest. The town occupies a saddle between rugged quartzite ridges; look for the white bell towers of its Baroque churches rising above red-tile rooftops.
