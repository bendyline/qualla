# Diamantina, Minas Gerais

A UNESCO-listed colonial town where diamonds built Brazil's richest mining district and a freed enslaved woman became the wife of the colony's wealthiest man.

**Location:** -18.2489, -43.6000

**Categories:** Municipalities in Minas Gerais, 1831 establishments in Brazil, World Heritage Sites in Brazil

## Introduction

For nine years, the Portuguese Crown had no idea its colony was sitting on a diamond deposit so rich it would dwarf every other known source in the western world. The governor of Minas Gerais, D. Lourenço de Almeida, confirmed the discovery in 1729, and Portugal's response was swift and absolute: full imperial control. In the mountains 292 kilometers north of Belo Horizonte, at 1,114 meters above sea level, a small chapel town called Arraial do Tejuco became the beating heart of the greatest diamond rush the world had ever seen. The Baroque churches and cobblestone streets still standing here have watched three centuries pass without losing their colonial bones.

## Rotten Water and Cold Mountains

The Tupi-speaking peoples who moved through these Espinhaco highlands before the Portuguese arrived gave the place two names: tyiuka, meaning rotten water, and Ybyty-ro'y, cold mountain. Both stuck, in modified form, through the early colonial period. When Portuguese settlers founded a chapel honoring Saint Anthony of Padua here in 1713, they called the settlement Arraial do Tejuco. It sat in territory occupied for thousands of years by indigenous peoples of the Macro-Je language family, whose expeditions up the Jequitinhonha and Sao Francisco rivers had long predated any European arrival. The first documented reports of outside contact trace back to the 16th century. Everything that happened afterward flowed from one discovery sixteen years after the chapel was raised.

## The Intendency of Diamonds

When diamonds surfaced in 1729, Portugal moved quickly and harshly. In 1734, the Crown established the Intendency of Diamonds, a regime so controlling that residents needed permits to enter, trade, or travel within the district. Smuggling carried severe penalties. The Crown's monopoly over these deposits held for 111 years, only ending in 1845. Tejuco represented the largest diamond mining operation in the western world during the 18th century, and by century's end it had grown into the third-largest town in the Captaincy of Minas, behind only Vila Rica (modern Ouro Preto) and rivaling prosperous Sao Joao del-Rei. The Estrada Real, the royal road established in 1697 to push colonial development inland, eventually reached up from the coast through Ouro Preto and terminated here, funneling diamonds down to Portuguese ships and hauling flour, salt, and manufactured goods back up into the mountains.

## Chica da Silva

The most extraordinary figure of colonial Tejuco was Francisca da Silva de Oliveira, known to history as Chica da Silva. Born around 1730 into slavery, she was purchased and then freed by Joao Fernandes de Oliveira, the contractor who held the royal diamond concession and who was, by virtue of that concession, the richest man in colonial Brazil. They lived together for fifteen years and had thirteen children. In a society structured by rigid racial hierarchy, Chica da Silva moved through the upper reaches of Tejuco's society, wearing the finest fabrics and attending religious processions in a position that would have been unimaginable for nearly any other Black woman in the colony. Her story has been adapted into songs and television dramas translated into multiple languages, though the historical Chica has often been obscured beneath the legend.

## A Girl's Diary and a President's Hometown

In 1893, a thirteen-year-old girl named Alice Dayrell Caldeira Brant began keeping a diary of her life in late-19th-century Diamantina. Published decades later under the pen name Helena Morley as Minha Vida de Menina, the book captured a mining town in decline with such clarity that the American poet Elizabeth Bishop translated it into English as The Diary of Helena Morley, giving it a second life abroad. A few years after Alice began her diary, another Diamantina child was born who would leave an even larger mark: Juscelino Kubitschek, born 1902, who became President of Brazil from 1956 to 1961 and oversaw the construction of Brasilia. When Kubitschek rose to national power, he brought improvements home: the Federal School of Dentistry, the Hotel Tijuco, the state school named for his mother Julia. The town that had shaped his boyhood never forgot the favor.

## Heritage of Humanity

In 1831, the town finally emancipated itself from the neighboring municipality of Serro and took the name Diamantina, formalizing what everyone already knew. In 1938, on its centennial as a city, the National Institute of Historic and Artistic Heritage designated the historic center a National Historic Heritage site. UNESCO elevated it to World Heritage status in 1999. Today about 47,825 people live in the municipality, which spans 3,870 square kilometers. Tourism, services, and small industry support the economy. Beyond the cobblestone streets and Baroque churches, the 16,999-hectare Biribiri State Park contains the historic village of Biribiri and preserves stretches of the cerrado highlands where the diamond streams still run. The Jequitinhonha River flows east of town, one of the great rivers of Brazil.

## Flight Context

Diamantina sits at 18.25 degrees S, 43.60 degrees W, in the Espinhaco highlands of northern Minas Gerais at 1,114 meters elevation. The best visual approach comes from the south, following the ridgeline from Belo Horizonte. Diamantina Airport (SBDN) serves regular flights from Belo Horizonte's Pampulha Airport (SBBH) and Confins International (SBCF). The town occupies a saddle between rugged quartzite ridges; look for the white bell towers of its Baroque churches rising above red-tile rooftops.
