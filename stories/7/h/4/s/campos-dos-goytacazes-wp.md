# Campos dos Goytacazes

On flat floodplain land where the Goytacazes once lived, Campos became the largest city in Rio de Janeiro state outside the capital - a city built first on sugar, then on oil.

**Location:** -21.7539, -41.3239

**Categories:** Populated coastal places in Rio de Janeiro (state), Municipalities in Rio de Janeiro (state), Populated places established in 1677, History of Rio de Janeiro state

## Introduction

The name is a compound. Campos means fields - these very particular low, flat, often-flooded fields that spread inland from the Brazilian coast when the land is new and the rivers still wander. Goytacazes was the name the Portuguese gave to the Indigenous people who lived here, fishermen and hunters of the coastal lowlands who fought off colonization longer than almost any group in southeastern Brazil. Both names have stuck, even though the Goytacazes are long gone as a recognizable people. Today Campos dos Goytacazes is the largest municipality in Rio de Janeiro state by area - 4,032 square kilometers, most of it still flat - and the largest city outside the Greater Rio metropolitan region, with 483,540 residents. It is also the easternmost municipality in the state. The land is 14 meters above sea level, which is to say nearly at sea level, and in November storms can dump more than 140 millimeters of rain in a single day.

## Fields, Cane, and a Town Becomes a City

The Portuguese came to colonize this stretch of coast in the 16th century. The village of Sao Salvador de Campos de Goytacazes was officially founded on May 29, 1677. For more than a century it was a village. Then on March 28, 1835, it was elevated to city status. The land was made for sugarcane - flat, warm, well-watered. Plantations spread. The enslaved Africans who worked them made Campos one of the most important sugar economies in imperial Brazil. In the 20th century, as sugar mills modernized and consolidated, the town diversified. It grew into the second-largest city in Rio de Janeiro state by the 1950s. Then came the crash. Sugar prices collapsed in 1954 - a Brazilian echo of what happened to Detroit's auto industry - and the population slid. Other places rose to replace it. Campos spent the rest of the 20th century looking for a new foundation.

## Oil Under the Sea

The foundation turned out to lie offshore. The Campos Basin, beginning a few dozen kilometers east of the city, holds billions of barrels of oil. When Petrobras began drilling in the basin in the 1970s and 1980s, Campos dos Goytacazes became a service town, a place where the money flowed in from rigs and platforms visible only as lights on the horizon. The royalty payments that Brazilian federal law directs to coastal oil-producing municipalities turned Campos into one of the wealthiest cities per capita in the country for a while. The GDP reached 17.3 billion reais in 2016 - a staggering figure for a regional city. Per capita income sat around 35,000 reais. The money paid for universities, hospitals, new roads, and at least twelve colleges and institutes, including the Universidade Estadual do Norte Fluminense. It also paid for municipal tensions and controversies that have never quite settled, because oil money is notoriously hard to spend well.

## The Lion of Campos

Campos has a theological story that few Brazilian cities can match. Bishop Antonio de Castro Mayer led the Roman Catholic Diocese of Campos for decades and earned the nickname The Lion of Campos for his resistance to the reforms of the Second Vatican Council. When Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre of the Society of Saint Pius X consecrated four bishops without papal approval in Econe, Switzerland, in 1988 - an act the Vatican considered schismatic - Castro Mayer was at his side, participating in the consecrations. The ripple has never quite gone away. Today two Catholic jurisdictions coexist in the same city: the regular diocese, led by Bishop Roberto Gomes Guimaraes, and the Personal Apostolic Administration of Saint John Mary Vianney, led by Monsignor Fernando Areas Rifan. This second body, now reconciled with Rome, celebrates the traditional Latin Mass and exists in a kind of canonical niche created specifically for Campos. It is one of the few places in the world where a traveler can see the post-Vatican-II tensions still worked out in parallel parishes.

## A City That Carries Its Violence

The oil money has not made Campos safe. In 2018 the city recorded 233 homicides, a murder rate of 46 per 100,000 residents - one of the highest in the southern half of Brazil. The reasons are the familiar ones in Brazilian cities of this size: drug trafficking routes along the coast, unequal distribution of the oil wealth, militias that emerged from the security vacuum. The climate carries another kind of weight. The lowest temperature ever recorded in the city was 9.5 degrees Celsius on July 6, 1942. The highest was 41.6 degrees on October 31, 2012. Rain comes in bursts: 159.3 millimeters on a single day in December 1955, and at least a dozen other days since 1933 when more than 100 millimeters fell in twenty-four hours. Beyond the city, Campos contains part of the 21,444-hectare Desengano State Park, established in 1970, where the Atlantic Forest survives on steep slopes above the floodplain. The football clubs Americano, Campos, Goytacaz, and Rio Branco carry the old names forward. The derby between Americano and Goytacaz - the Goyta-cano - still fills a stadium on a Sunday afternoon.

## Flight Context

Located at 21.75 S, 41.32 W, on the flat coastal plain of northern Rio de Janeiro state, about 286 km northeast of Rio city. Elevation 14 m; largest municipality in the state by area. Bartolomeu Lysandro Airport (SBCP / CAW) serves regional flights; longer runways at Rio-Galeao (SBGL / GIG). From 4,000-8,000 ft the flat sugarcane and oil-service landscape is distinctive - you can see offshore platform lights at night over the Campos Basin to the east. Tropical climate, hot year-round, heaviest rain in November. Watch for fast-building afternoon storms in summer.
