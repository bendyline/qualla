# Gouveia, Minas Gerais

A high-plateau Brazilian town where an 18th-century church of Chica da Silva still stands, and where Latin America's first wind turbines briefly spun before going silent.

**Location:** -18.4539, -43.7408

**Categories:** brazilian municipalities, minas gerais, colonial brazilian history, renewable energy history, espinhaço range

## Introduction

At 1,113 meters above sea level, the air at Gouveia is thin enough to make winter mornings sharp and summer evenings cool, which is unusual for Brazil. This is highland country, the southern edge of the Jequitinhonha River valley, where Minas Gerais climbs into the Serra do Espinhaço and the horizon goes flat-topped and rocky. A town of about 11,800 people sits on this plateau, 34 kilometers southwest of Diamantina, one bank and eleven primary schools in its own accounting of what it is. But the reason to look twice at Gouveia has to do with two hills and the relationships between them: one carrying an 18th-century church built by the enslaved, the other carrying the ruins of Latin America's first wind farm.

## The Church on the Granite

At the highest point within the urban perimeter, on top of a large block of granite, stands the Church of Nossa Senhora das Dores, built in the 18th century entirely from stone, by enslaved laborers. Inside is an image of Our Lady of Sorrows that, according to local tradition and municipal record, once belonged to Chica da Silva — the enslaved-born woman of Diamantina whose relationship with the Portuguese crown diamond contractor João Fernandes de Oliveira made her one of the most famous figures in colonial Brazil. Chica da Silva lived about 34 kilometers north in Diamantina, where she became a woman of property and status, bearing thirteen children by Fernandes and eventually gaining her freedom. The image at Gouveia is one of the tangible links between her life and the surrounding towns. The church that holds it was built by people whose names the parish records did not preserve.

## Morro do Camilinho and the Wind

On another hill outside town stands the ruined Morro do Camilinho wind station, documented in Gouveia's own cultural heritage inventory as the first wind power station built in Latin America. It does not work anymore. The turbines that once turned above the Espinhaço no longer turn, and the installation has become a ruin alongside the churches and waterfalls the municipality catalogs. What the local record does not dwell on is the larger story: Brazilian wind energy only began to scale up in the 2000s, after decades of being considered marginal, and early experiments like the one on Morro do Camilinho were the proof-of-concept that failed slowly and taught quietly. The plateau is windy. The plateau is still windy. The first people to put turbines on it were right about the physics and wrong about the timing.

## Waterfalls of the Upper Jequitinhonha

Gouveia's tourist literature lists six waterfalls, which for a municipality of this size is a lot: Melo, Capivara, Barro Preto, Santo Antônio, São Roberto, and Barão. They drop through the rocky terrain of the upper Jequitinhonha watershed, the great river that carries much of northeastern Minas Gerais eastward toward the Atlantic. The São Roberto waterfall shares its name with a neighborhood, a church, and an old factory, suggesting it was once the center of gravity for this part of the municipality. The Capivara waterfall takes its name from the South American rodent that lives near water across Brazil and that has, for centuries, been both hunted and tolerated by the people living alongside them. These falls do not carry Iguazú's drama. They carry the more ordinary beauty of a highland where water has been finding the same routes down for thousands of years.

## Four Churches, Three Centuries

Besides Nossa Senhora das Dores, Gouveia has three other churches that map its growth through time. Nossa Senhora de Lourdes, built in the 1930s in the São Roberto neighborhood near the old factory, is architecturally simple but locally beloved. São Geraldo's, built in the 1990s, honors the Italian saint Gerard Majella and stands in the São Lucas neighborhood. São Sebastião, completed in 2009 next to the São Miguel Cemetery in Serrinha, is the newest. A town this size with this many churches is telling you something about itself — that the Portuguese Catholic infrastructure installed in the 18th century has continued to layer outward, parish by parish, generation by generation, into the second decade of the 21st.

## A Small Economy, a Long Horizon

The municipality became a municipality in 1953, which is relatively recent — it was a district of a larger unit before that. Its 2005 GDP was about R$51 million, most of it services, a bit from small industry, a small share from agriculture. Coffee, sugarcane, beans, corn. About 9,400 head of cattle on 323 rural properties. The 2000 HDI of Minas Gerais municipalities ranged from Poços de Caldas at 0.841 down to Setubinha at 0.568. Gouveia sits somewhere in the middle, one of hundreds of small Brazilian towns trying to provide a hospital with 41 beds and public schools for the children of farmers. Patients with serious conditions are transferred to Diamantina. The buses run on BR-367. The plateau holds.

## Flight Context

Located at 18.45°S, 43.74°W at an elevation of 1,113 m in the Serra do Espinhaço highlands of central Minas Gerais. Nearest airport is Diamantina (no commercial service); the closest commercial airport is Belo Horizonte's Confins International (SBCF), about 250 km south. The broader region is recognizable from cruising altitude by the flat-topped quartzite plateaus of the Espinhaço and the silver threads of the upper Jequitinhonha River. The old Morro do Camilinho wind site sits on one of the highest points near town.
