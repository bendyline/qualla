# Gouveia, Minas Gerais

A high-plateau Brazilian town where an 18th-century church of Chica da Silva still stands, and where Brazil's first grid-connected wind plant once spun before going silent.

**Location:** -18.4539, -43.7408

**Categories:** brazilian municipalities, minas gerais, colonial brazilian history, renewable energy history, espinhaço range

## Introduction

At 1,113 meters above sea level, the air at Gouveia is thin enough to make winter mornings sharp and summer evenings cool, which is unusual for Brazil. This is highland country, in the statistical Jequitinhonha region, where Minas Gerais climbs into the Serra do Espinhaço and the horizon goes flat-topped and rocky. A municipality of about 11,300 people (11,331 at the 2022 census) sits on this plateau, 34 kilometers by road southwest of Diamantina. But the reason to look twice at Gouveia has to do with two hills and the relationships between them: one carrying an 18th-century church built by enslaved people, the other carrying the remains of Brazil's first grid-connected wind plant.

## The Church on the Granite

At the highest point within the urban perimeter, on top of a large block of granite, stands the Church of Nossa Senhora das Dores, built in the 18th century entirely from stone, by enslaved laborers. Inside is an image of Our Lady of Sorrows that, according to local tradition and municipal record, once belonged to Chica da Silva — the enslaved-born woman of Diamantina whose relationship with the Portuguese crown diamond contractor João Fernandes de Oliveira made her one of the most famous figures in colonial Brazil. Chica da Silva lived about 34 kilometers north in Diamantina, where, freed around the time Fernandes bought her in 1753, she became a woman of property and status and bore him thirteen children. The image at Gouveia is one of the tangible links between her life and the surrounding towns. The church that holds it was built by enslaved people whose names have largely been lost.

## Morro do Camilinho and the Wind

On another hill outside town stands the old Morro do Camilinho (Camelinho) wind station, listed in Gouveia's cultural heritage inventory as the first wind power station in Latin America. The state utility CEMIG built it in 1994 as a 1-megawatt experimental plant, and it became the first wind plant in Brazil connected to the power grid. It has since been decommissioned. The turbines that once turned above the Espinhaço no longer turn, and the site now sits alongside the churches and waterfalls the municipality catalogs. What the local record does not dwell on is the larger story: Brazilian wind energy only began to scale up in the 2000s, after decades of being considered marginal, and experiments like the one on Morro do Camilinho were early proofs of concept. The plateau is windy. The plateau is still windy. The first people to put turbines on it were right about the physics and simply early.

## Waterfalls of the Espinhaço

Gouveia's tourist literature lists six waterfalls, which for a municipality of this size is a lot: Melo, Capivara, Barro Preto, Santo Antônio, São Roberto, and Barão. They drop through the rocky terrain of the Paraúna basin, whose waters run west to the Rio das Velhas and on to the São Francisco. The São Roberto waterfall shares its name with a neighborhood and an old factory, suggesting it was once the center of gravity for this part of the municipality. The Capivara waterfall takes its name from the South American rodent that lives near water across Brazil and that has, for centuries, been both hunted and tolerated by the people living alongside them. These falls do not carry Iguazú's drama. They carry the more ordinary beauty of a highland where water has been finding the same routes down for thousands of years.

## Four Churches, Three Centuries

Besides Nossa Senhora das Dores, Gouveia has three other churches that map its growth through time. Nossa Senhora de Lourdes, built in the 1930s in the São Roberto neighborhood near the old factory, is architecturally simple but locally beloved. São Geraldo's, built in the 1990s, honors the Italian saint Gerard Majella and stands in the São Lucas neighborhood. São Sebastião, completed in 2009 next to the São Miguel Cemetery in Serrinha, is the newest. A town this size with this many churches is telling you something about itself — that the Portuguese Catholic infrastructure installed in the 18th century has continued to layer outward, parish by parish, generation by generation, into the 21st.

## A Small Economy, a Long Horizon

The municipality became a municipality in 1953, which is relatively recent — it was a district of a larger unit before that. Its 2005 GDP was about R$51 million, most of it services, a bit from small industry, a small share from agriculture. Coffee, sugarcane, beans, corn. In 2006, about 9,400 head of cattle on 323 rural properties. The 2000 HDI of Minas Gerais municipalities ranged from Poços de Caldas at 0.841 down to Setubinha at 0.568. Gouveia sits somewhere in the middle, one of hundreds of small Brazilian towns trying to provide a small hospital (41 beds in 2005) and public schools for the children of farmers. Patients with serious conditions are transferred to Diamantina. The buses run on BR-367. The plateau holds.

## Flight Context

Located at 18.45°S, 43.74°W at an elevation of 1,113 m in the Serra do Espinhaço highlands of central Minas Gerais. Nearest airfield is Diamantina; the closest major commercial airport is Belo Horizonte's Confins International (SBCF), about 135 km south-southwest in a straight line (roughly 250 km by road to Belo Horizonte). The broader region is recognizable from cruising altitude by the flat-topped quartzite plateaus of the Espinhaço and the Gouveia depression drained by the Paraúna's tributaries. The old Morro do Camilinho wind site sits on one of the highest points near town.
