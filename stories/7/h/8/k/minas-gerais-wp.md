# Minas Gerais

Brazil's fourth-largest state by area, second-largest by population, and first in mines. Home to 20 million mineiros, more baroque churches than anyone has counted, and a state flag whose Latin motto means liberty though it be delayed.

**Location:** -18.9500, -44.5667

**Categories:** Minas Gerais, States of Brazil, Southeast Region, Brazil

## Introduction

In 1974, in a cave between Lagoa Santa and Pedro Leopoldo, archaeologists found a skull they named Luzia. She was 11,400 years old, making her the oldest human fossil found anywhere in the Americas. Her bones sit - or did, until the 2018 museum fire that nearly destroyed them - in the National Museum of Brazil, and her presence anchors Minas Gerais to a staggering depth of human occupation. Twenty million people live in this Brazilian state today, across 853 municipalities, in an area larger than France. It holds the sources of some of the country's longest rivers, the largest road network in Brazil, and a 18th-century cultural heritage so dense that whole towns have been declared UNESCO World Heritage sites. Minas means mines. The name does not lie.

## The Gold Rush That Built an Empire

In 1692, the bandeirante Antonio Rodrigues Arzao found the first gold in what would become Minas Gerais. By 1696, Salvador Fernandes Furtado had found more on the banks of the Carmo River, and a camp there became the town of Nossa Senhora do Carmo - today's Mariana. Two years later, Antonio Dias de Oliveira found gold at the foot of Itacolomi Peak and founded what became Vila Rica, now Ouro Preto. The news traveled. In the following decades, hundreds of thousands of people - mostly Portuguese, including New Christians escaping the Inquisition, plus colonials from Sao Paulo, Bahia, and Pernambuco - flooded into the mountains. Epidemics followed. Famine followed. So did enslaved Africans, brought in unprecedented numbers to work the mines. The wealth extracted was immense. It funded the Portuguese Crown's European wars, rebuilt Lisbon after the 1755 earthquake, and shifted Brazil's economic center of gravity permanently from the northeast to the southeast.

## The Conspiracy of the Inconfidentes

By the late 18th century, Portugal was taxing Minas Gerais at one-fifth of all gold extracted - the quinto - and the gold was running out. Rebellion brewed. In 1789, a group of middle-class colonists, inspired by the American and French Revolutions, began planning an uprising. They were intellectuals, junior officers, a few priests. Their most famous member was Joaquim Jose da Silva Xavier, a dentist who went by the nickname Tiradentes, the tooth-puller. The plot was betrayed. In 1792 Tiradentes was publicly hanged in Rio de Janeiro by order of Queen Maria I of Portugal, and his body was quartered and displayed along the road back to Minas as a warning. His companions were exiled to Africa. But their proposed flag - a red triangle on white with the motto Libertas quae sera tamen, liberty though it be delayed - became the flag of Minas Gerais, and Tiradentes himself became a national martyr. April 21 is a national holiday in his honor.

## Aleijadinho and the Baroque Miracle

Antonio Francisco Lisboa was born around 1738 to a Portuguese architect and an enslaved African mother. He worked as a sculptor in the gold towns and developed, in middle age, a degenerative disease that crippled his hands and feet - probably leprosy - earning him the nickname Aleijadinho, the little cripple. He could not walk without help. His assistants tied his chisels to his remaining fingers. With those bound hands he produced the twelve soapstone prophets that still stand on the terrace of the Sanctuary of Bom Jesus de Matosinhos in Congonhas - figures so alive they seem about to speak - and hundreds of carvings across the baroque churches of Ouro Preto, Sabara, and Sao Joao del-Rei. The work survived because the gold ran out before the towns could be modernized. Mineiran colonial architecture is now more intact than any comparable colonial heritage on the continent, and much of what the eye finds most remarkable was made by a man who could barely hold a tool.

## The Milk Half of Cafe com Leite

After independence in 1822, Minas Gerais produced president after president - so many, alongside neighboring Sao Paulo, that the politics of the First Republic became known as cafe com leite, coffee with milk. Sao Paulo brought the coffee. Minas brought the milk, from the enormous dairy industry that still defines its central plateau. In 1956 the mineiro Juscelino Kubitschek became president and, in five unreasonably ambitious years, built Brasilia as the new national capital - an act of political will that remains the most audacious single accomplishment of Brazilian statecraft. Tancredo Neves, also a mineiro, was elected in 1984 as the first civilian president after the military regime; he died before taking office. Minas has produced jurists, writers, composers, and - most famously - a reserved, careful political temperament that Brazilians still invoke when discussing the state's character.

## What You Can See From the Air

Minas Gerais does not have a coastline, but it has everything else. The Serra da Mantiqueira and Serra da Canastra rise along the southern borders to peaks near 2,800 meters. The Espinhaco range cuts north through the center, a dramatic spine of ancient rock. The Sao Francisco River, born here, runs 3,000 kilometers north to the Atlantic. The Iron Quadrangle near Belo Horizonte is visible from altitude as a patchwork of open-pit mines - enormous ochre terraces stepped into the mountains where iron ore is still extracted. The cerrado savanna dominates the west. The dry northern sertao merges into Bahia. Colonial towns appear from the air as clusters of red tile roofs against green hillsides, their church towers still the tallest structures. Fly across on a clear day and the state unspools for hours: mountain, valley, mining pit, farmhouse, soybean field, mountain again. A geography shaped by minerals, still producing them.
