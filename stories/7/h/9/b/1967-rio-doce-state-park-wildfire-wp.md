# 1967 Rio Doce State Park Wildfire

In September 1967, a fire of unknown origin burned through one-third of the largest Atlantic Forest reserve in Minas Gerais for more than a month, killing twelve of the men fighting it, including the sergeant who led them.

**Location:** -19.6500, -42.5339

**Categories:** Wildfires in Brazil, Atlantic Forest, 1967 in Brazil, 1960s wildfires, 1967 disasters in South America

## Introduction

Sergeant Agenor Almeida Costa and the crew he led were surrounded by a line of fire near the Dom Helvecio Lagoon on October 18, 1967, and only one of them walked out. They had been fighting a blaze that had already burned for five weeks through the Rio Doce State Park, in eastern Minas Gerais, and they were not winning. By the time the main fire was brought under control six days later - only after crews hacked thirty kilometers of firebreak through century-old Atlantic Forest - 9,000 hectares of the reserve were ash. Only one Brazilian wildfire had killed more people: a 1963 fire in Parana that took 110 lives. But the Rio Doce fire took something else too. It took a third of what was then, and still is, the largest remnant of Atlantic Forest in Minas Gerais.

## A Forest Worth Saving

The park had been protected for less than a quarter century when it burned. Dom Helvecio Gomes de Oliveira, archbishop of Mariana, had pushed for its creation alongside Governor Benedito Valadares and his agriculture secretary Israel Pinheiro, and their decree - No. 1,119, signed July 14, 1944 - carved 30,000 hectares out of country that was otherwise being stripped for charcoal and eucalyptus. Eastern Minas Gerais in the middle twentieth century was furnace country. Ironworks and steel plants needed wood, and Atlantic Forest trees went into the ovens by the truckload. The Rio Doce State Park survived as the last big intact piece in its part of the state, a refuge of peroba and jacaranda and yellow-flowering ipe, some specimens reaching thirty meters tall and a century old. It was the thing worth saving, and in September 1967 it began to burn.

## The Conditions That Fed It

The wet season should have arrived in October. It did not. At the rainfall station in Coronel Fabriciano, the only meaningful precipitation between April 21 and November 17 was 21 millimeters on May 18 and 11 millimeters on September 14. Almost nothing else fell. The forest dried, the understory cracked, and temperatures climbed. When the first flames appeared in mid-September, no one was certain where they came from. A discarded cigarette in a neighboring pasture was the early suspicion, then dismissed when investigators found the fire had started in the middle of the forest. Suspicion turned to sparks from the charcoal ovens near the park boundary, where local industry baked wood into fuel; sabotage and a tourist's abandoned campfire were also blamed, and the cause was never established. The park's surveillance team that year consisted of ten soldiers, one corporal, and Sergeant Agenor Almeida Costa - twelve men, total, for 30,000 hectares of forest.

## The Day the Line Closed

October 18 was the day it turned fatal. Fire behavior in the Atlantic Forest is not what Americans think of from Western wildfires - the fuel is different, the weather is different, and the terrain folds and folds again - but the result can be the same. A crew surrounded by flames on multiple sides, with no way out, has few options and most of them are bad. Sergeant Agenor and the crew he had gathered - forestry company workers and a forest ranger among them - were caught near the Dom Helvecio Lagoon, a body of water within the park named for the archbishop who had helped create it. Eleven men, the sergeant among them, died there; a twelfth died of his burns two days later. Only Jose Lino, a forestry worker, survived, wounded, by reaching a boat on the lagoon. Three separate inquiries followed - a military police inquiry into the sergeant's death, a military inquiry into his men's deaths, and a third into the causes of the fire itself - but none of them could change what had already happened. The fire kept burning. Thirty kilometers of firebreak were cut through the forest by hand and machine. The main fire was finally contained on October 24, though flames stayed active into the last days of the month; by November it was out.

## What Grew Back

The burned third of the park is still there, and so is the unburned two-thirds. The forest regenerated. Atlantic Forest systems, given half a chance, are resilient - the seed bank in the soil is rich, the rainfall returns, the trees that survived on the margins throw their seeds back into the cleared ground. In the nearly sixty years since, the park has not suffered another comparable fire. Two hiking trails - Angico Vermelho and Vinhatico - now cross ground that burned in 1967, and guides will point out the evidence if you know to ask, but most visitors walk through it without noticing. The tree canopy has closed. A smaller fire in September 2019 burned 484 hectares between Marlieria and Timoteo, a reminder that what happened in 1967 was not a final event, only a scale of event the park has so far avoided repeating.

## The Sergeant and the Lake

The Dom Helvecio Lagoon is still there, a dark mirror in the forest. The story of Agenor Almeida Costa and the men who died with him lives in the archives and the regional newspapers from that October - and in the name of the park's environmental police barracks in Marlieria, renamed for Sergeant Agenor in 2011, when his widow and the families of the other men were honored. The Rio Doce State Park, 35,976 hectares by the 2019 measurement, remains the largest Atlantic Forest reserve in the state and one of the most important fragments in the country. It exists partly because three men in the 1940s pushed for a decree. It came close to being lost in 1967, when twelve men died trying to save it.

## Flight Context

Located at 19.65 S, 42.53 W in eastern Minas Gerais, Brazil. The park covers 35,976 hectares between the municipalities of Dionisio, Marlieria, and Timoteo, roughly 150 km east-northeast of Belo Horizonte in a straight line. The park's lake system - Dom Helvecio and some 40 natural lakes in all - provides a distinctive visual signature from the air. Nearest airports: Usiminas/Ipatinga (SBIP) about 20 km north and Confins/Belo Horizonte (SBCF) to the west. Best viewed at 6,000-10,000 ft to appreciate the forest patch against the surrounding landscape of cleared pasture and eucalyptus plantations. Dry season (June-September) greatly increases fire risk; smoke plumes in the region are not uncommon in those months.
