# Calado Station

Once one of the three largest stations on the Vitoria-Minas Railway, Calado Station gave birth to the city of Coronel Fabriciano before the bulldozers came for it in 1979.

**Location:** -19.5287, -42.6230

**Categories:** Railway stations in Brazil, Coronel Fabriciano, Minas Gerais, History of Minas Gerais, Vitoria-Minas Railway

## Introduction

There is a bus terminal in Coronel Fabriciano where a railway station used to be. The terminal was built in the 1980s, functional and unloved, on the spot where for fifty-five years a different building had stood - handsome, proportioned, the kind of train station that gave a small Brazilian town a reason to believe it was somewhere. The Calado Station opened on June 9, 1924. When it did, it was one of the three largest stations on the Vitoria-Minas Railway, the great iron-ore line that still winds across Minas Gerais to the Atlantic port of Vitoria. The city of Coronel Fabriciano did not exist yet. It would grow up around this station, street by street, until the station itself seemed small and inconvenient. In 1979, the railroad rerouted itself outside of town, the station closed, and the building came down. No hearing, no fight, no preservation order - just the quiet demolition of a place everyone had known.

## A Station Before a City

The land had been a camp. Workers laying the track up the Rio Doce Valley in the early 1920s pitched their tents at Barra do Calado, where the Caladao stream meets the Piracicaba River. The station went up first, in 1922, and then sat idle for almost two years while the rails caught up. It was briefly named Raul Soares, after the governor, before settling on Calado - the name of the little stream that had shaped the camp. Around the station, houses began to rise. A clerk named Jose Zacarias da Silva Roque grew tired of walking the long distance between the notary's office and the terminal, and his complaint helped push the local seat of Melo Viana district to the spot in 1933. Before it was Coronel Fabriciano, this was simply the place where the train stopped.

## Iron, Wood, and Charcoal

What made the station matter was what it carried. When the Companhia Siderurgica Belgo-Mineira set up in the area, the Calado Station became the hub where machinery was unloaded and where wood and charcoal were loaded for the blast furnaces of Joao Monlevade, further up the line. Trucks rolled in with logs cut from the Doce River valley. Workers stacked the wood in yards around the station. In the 1940s, the first machinery and workers for the Acesita steelworks, built on the opposite bank of the Piracicaba River, stepped off trains at Calado. The Companhia Vale do Rio Doce - what is now simply Vale, one of the world's largest mining companies - took over the railway in 1942 and eventually built 102 houses near the tracks for its employees. The hierarchy of the company was written into the houses themselves: the higher your rank, the finer the dwelling. This was a company town built around a station that was in turn built around a railway that existed to move iron to the coast.

## The Peak and the Demolition

The busiest years came in the 1960s. The station sat at the middle of a commercial center that had grown loud and crowded around it, and it served two purposes at once - timber yards out back, passenger platforms out front. The building was expanded in the late 1960s, carefully, keeping the original architecture. Then the logic of the city turned against it. Cities do not like freight trains running through their centers. In the early 1970s, Vale announced that it would move the tracks away from downtown Fabriciano. The Calado Station, no longer connected to anything, would be closed and handed to the city government. The transfer of railway operations dragged on until 2008, by which point the station itself had long since been flattened to make way for the new Estacao Square and the bus terminal. A replica was proposed in the 1990s. The project never moved. A surface subway to Cenibra in Belo Oriente was proposed using the old tracks. It never moved either.

## What Stays When a Building Goes

The Vitoria-Minas Railway still crosses Coronel Fabriciano, but the station that serves the city now sits inside the neighboring municipality of Timoteo and answers to a different name - Mario Carvalho. For passengers from Fabriciano, the Timoteo station is actually farther than the old Calado site. The river divides them. What remains of the real station lives in fragments. A painting called O ultimo trem - The Last Train - hangs in town, donated in 1999 by Jose Anastacio Franco and declared municipal heritage. The painter Mirian Franco worked from a photograph by Argemiro Ribeiro, and so the station survives twice over, as an image of an image. Outside the Sao Sebastiao Cathedral stands a miniature of the Calado Station, a small architectural memorial to the building the town did not keep. A few objects are held in the Jose Avelino Barbosa Museum, which opened in October 2014. The lesson Coronel Fabriciano learned is one that many Brazilian cities learned the same way: once a building is gone, you can commission paintings and miniatures forever, but you will not get it back.

## Flight Context

Located at 19.53 S, 42.62 W, in the Steel Valley of eastern Minas Gerais, on the Piracicaba River where it divides Coronel Fabriciano from Timoteo. The Vitoria-Minas Railway still runs through the region carrying iron ore to the Atlantic. Nearest airport: Usiminas Airport (SBIP / IPN) at Ipatinga, about 15 km east, serving the Vale do Aco metropolitan area. Regional best viewed from 3,000-6,000 ft to see the old rail corridor threading through the industrial landscape. The Piracicaba River valley is often cloudy; mornings tend to be clearest.
