# Calado Station

Once one of the three largest stations on the Vitoria-Minas Railway, Calado Station gave birth to the city of Coronel Fabriciano before it closed in 1979 and the tractors came for it in 1982.

**Location:** -19.5287, -42.6230

**Categories:** Railway stations in Brazil, Coronel Fabriciano, Minas Gerais, History of Minas Gerais, Vitoria-Minas Railway

## Introduction

There is a square in Coronel Fabriciano where a railway station used to be. Before the square came a bus terminal, built in the 1980s on the spot where for some sixty years a different building had stood - handsome, proportioned, the kind of train station that gave a small Brazilian town a reason to believe it was somewhere. The Calado Station opened on June 9, 1924. When it did, it was one of the three largest stations on the Vitoria-Minas Railway, the great iron-ore line that still winds across Minas Gerais to the Atlantic port of Vitoria. The city of Coronel Fabriciano did not exist yet. It would grow up around this station, street by street, until the station itself seemed small and inconvenient. In 1979, the railroad rerouted itself outside of town and the station closed; in 1982 the building came down. City advisers suggested declaring it a monument, but no preservation order came - just two days of tractors and dynamite at a place everyone had known.

## A Station Before a City

The land had been a camp. Workers laying the track up the Rio Doce Valley in the early 1920s pitched their tents at Barra do Calado, where the Caladao stream meets the Piracicaba River. The station went up first, in 1922, and then sat idle for almost two years while the rails caught up. It was briefly named Raul Soares, after the governor, before settling on Calado - the name of the little stream that had shaped the camp. Around the station, houses began to rise. A clerk named Jose Zacarias da Silva Roque grew tired of walking the long distance between the notary's office and the terminal, and his complaint helped push the local seat of Melo Viana district to the spot in 1933. Before it was Coronel Fabriciano, this was simply the place where the train stopped.

## Iron, Wood, and Charcoal

What made the station matter was what it carried. When the Companhia Siderurgica Belgo-Mineira set up in the area, the Calado Station became the hub where machinery was unloaded and where wood and charcoal were loaded for the blast furnaces of Joao Monlevade, further up the line. Trucks rolled in with logs cut from the Doce River valley. Workers stacked the wood in yards around the station. In the 1940s, the first machinery and workers for the Acesita steelworks, built on the opposite bank of the Piracicaba River, stepped off trains at Calado. The Companhia Vale do Rio Doce - what is now simply Vale, one of the world's largest mining companies - took over the railway in 1942 and eventually built 102 houses near the tracks for its employees. The hierarchy of the company was written into the houses themselves: the higher your rank, the finer the dwelling. This was a company town built around a station that was in turn built around a railway that existed to move iron to the coast.

## The Peak and the Demolition

The station's peak came in the 1960s. The station sat at the middle of a commercial center that had grown loud and crowded around it, and it served two purposes at once - timber yards out back, passenger platforms out front. The building was expanded in the late 1960s, carefully, keeping the original architecture. Then the logic of the city turned against it. Cities do not like freight trains running through their centers. In the early 1970s, Vale announced that it would move the tracks away from downtown Fabriciano. The Calado Station, no longer connected to anything, would be closed and handed to the city government. The station closed on January 29, 1979, and was demolished on March 15, 1982, after the city hall rejected its advisers' proposal to declare it a monument. A bus terminal took its place in the 1980s, and in 2008 that too moved away to make room for the Estacao Square, named for the lost station. A replica was proposed in the 1990s. The project never moved. A surface subway to Cenibra in Belo Oriente was proposed using the old tracks. It never moved either.

## What Stays When a Building Goes

The Vitoria-Minas Railway still crosses Coronel Fabriciano, but the station that serves the city now sits inside the neighboring municipality of Timoteo and answers to a different name - Mario Carvalho. Even so, it sits on the bank of the Piracicaba, the river that divides the two towns, closer to central Fabriciano than to any of Timoteo's residential neighborhoods. What remains of the real station lives in fragments. A painting called O ultimo trem - The Last Train - was commissioned by Jose Anastacio Franco, later donated to the city, and declared municipal heritage in 1999. The painter Mirian Franco worked from a photograph by Argemiro Ribeiro, and so the station survives twice over, as an image of an image. Outside the Sao Sebastiao Cathedral stands a miniature of the Calado Station, a small architectural memorial to the building the town did not keep. A few objects are held in the Jose Avelino Barbosa Museum, which opened in October 2014. The lesson Coronel Fabriciano learned is one that many Brazilian cities learned the same way: once a building is gone, you can commission paintings and miniatures forever, but you will not get it back.
