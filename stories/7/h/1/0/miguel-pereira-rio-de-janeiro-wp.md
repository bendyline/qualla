# Miguel Pereira, Rio de Janeiro

A mountain town named for the doctor who prescribed it, where Rio's water originates and the drovers once wove bamboo mats to drag their mules through the mud.

**Location:** -22.4539, -43.4689

**Categories:** Municipalities in Rio de Janeiro (state)

## Introduction

The town was named after the doctor who told everyone it was a good place to live. Miguel Pereira de Souza Silva was a Brazilian physician and professor who, in the 1930s, began publicly describing the healing properties of a mountain station on the rail line between Japeri and Paraiba do Sul - the cool climate, the mineral water, the way the fog burned off by mid-morning and the afternoons stayed mild. He wrote papers. He drew patients. The town that grew up around the Estiva railway stop eventually took his name in gratitude. Today Miguel Pereira sits at 618 meters in the green interior of Rio de Janeiro state, and its residents still pour a glass of tap water and remind you that Rio itself drinks what flows from these hills.

## Estiva Was the First Name

Before it was a spa town, and before there was a railway, this valley was a bog. Mule drovers coming up the Rio das Mortes toward Sacra Familia do Tingua fixed a river crossing in the small floodplain that is now the town center. They called the place Barreiros, then Tejuco, because their mule trains bogged down there like donkeys in quicksand. The solution was practical: they wove mats of bamboo slats - estivas - and laid them down on the mud so the animals could pass. The crossing took the name of the fix. Estiva it was for two centuries, and Estiva the railway stop was called when the line through Japeri opened in 1903. The town under that name was tiny, muddy, and entirely functional. The elegant name Miguel Pereira would come later, in the 1930s, after the doctor's climate campaign began to pay off.

## Coffee, Crown, and Collapse

The lands around Miguel Pereira were part of the 18th- and 19th-century coffee frontier that climbed the Serra do Mar from the port at Rio. Small fazendas produced food crops for the capital in the 1700s, and by 1770 the Fazenda da Piedade da Cruz was established as a serious coffee operation. The early 19th century brought expansion: coffee plantations multiplied, powered - the sources acknowledge plainly - by enslaved labor, the brutal engine of Brazilian plantation agriculture. When abolition came in 1888, the estates collapsed. The soil had been exhausted by decades of monoculture. Many freed laborers had nowhere to go and no land to inherit. The region entered a decades-long decline that only the railway, the summer-tourism boom, and eventually Dr. Pereira's climate sermons would reverse.

## Lake Javary and the Pedal Boats

The town's postcard image is a lake that did not exist two hundred years ago. Lake Javary was formed by damming the Corrego do Saco stream in the village of Barao de Javary, and it is not large, but it is picturesque. A rustic wooden bridge spans its narrow waist. Pedal boats drift across the surface on Sunday afternoons. One bank runs along RJ-125, the main road into town, so the lake is the first thing most visitors see. On a mild winter morning with fog burning off the surface and the bluish mountains of the Tingua Biological Reserve rising behind, it is possible to understand exactly what Dr. Pereira was talking about.

## The Water That Feeds Rio

This is a town built on water. The Rio Santana rises within the Tingua Biological Reserve and feeds the Rio Guandu system, which in turn supplies the Baixada Fluminense and a substantial portion of the city of Rio de Janeiro itself with drinking water. A water-treatment plant dating from the imperial era still functions on the property - a piece of 19th-century hydraulic engineering that has, with modifications, kept working. The municipality is shaped like a fish on the map, its body practically inside the Tingua reserve boundaries, and the reserve's protection is what keeps the water clean. Every Cariocan who drinks from the tap in Copacabana is drinking Miguel Pereira.

## The Climate They Still Talk About

Summer averages a high of 24.6 Celsius and a low of 17.6. Winter averages a high of 18.7 and a low of 10.3. Rain falls year-round but nothing is ever extreme. In a country where summer routinely breaks 40 degrees and winter brings dangerous flash floods, that kind of moderation is genuinely rare, and Miguel Pereira built an identity around it. It holds the official title of Climatic Resort - a Brazilian designation for places medically certified as restorative. The farms around town supply Rio's kitchens with dairy products, bay leaves, flowers, preserves, sausages, and cachaca. The vacation cabins scattered up the hills belong to policemen, teachers, railway workers - union getaways for categories of Rio professionals who cannot afford beach houses. On weekends the roads fill. On Monday morning, the fog rolls back in and the lake goes quiet again.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 22.45S, 43.47W, approximately 150 km northwest of Rio de Janeiro city in the Centro-Sul Fluminense region. Elevation 618 meters. Nearest major airport: Rio de Janeiro-Galeao (SBGL); closer general aviation options include Santos Dumont (SBRJ) and Campo dos Afonsos (SBAF). Tingua Biological Reserve dominates local airspace southwest of town - protected wildlife area with terrain rising to 1,600 meters. Afternoon convection over the Serra do Mar ridgelines is routine in summer.
