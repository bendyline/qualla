# Mariano Procópio Museum

A Renaissance villa built for an emperor who arrived before the paint dried, now home to 50,000 objects from imperial Brazil and a garden a Swiss naturalist called the paradise of the tropics.

**Location:** -21.7461, -43.3597

**Categories:** History museums in Brazil, Museums in Minas Gerais, Art museums and galleries in Brazil, Juiz de Fora

## Introduction

The contractor had a problem. The villa meant to host Emperor Dom Pedro II was not ready. The macadam highway between Juiz de Fora and Petropolis - the first of its kind in South America - was about to be inaugurated in 1861, and the engineer responsible, Mariano Procopio, had ordered an elaborate Renaissance-style residence built to shelter the Emperor for the ceremony. The stonework was rising too slowly. The royal retinue was arriving anyway. So Procopio simply installed the imperial family in his own house next door, and the Emperor would not see the finished Villa Ferreira Lage until his second visit eight years later. That villa still stands, and today it holds one of Brazil's oldest museum collections - roughly 50,000 objects that survived an empire and outlived the family that built it.

## The Villa That Kept Its Face

The architect was German - Carlos Augusto Gambs, who led the engineering team building the Estrada Uniao e Industria between Juiz de Fora and Petropolis. He chose a raised plateau for the villa, built it of large bricks, and designed it in a heavy Renaissance idiom intended to impress arriving nobility. Stucco walls. Hardwood paneling. Paintings set into every available surface. The villa was sited in an 78,000-square-meter park of native Brazilian flora - ipe and jacaranda and the remnant Atlantic Forest that Jean Louis Rodolphe Agassiz, the Swiss naturalist who visited in the 1860s, called the paradise of the tropics. That park, and its lake where pedal boats still drift, remains the approach every visitor takes. The villa itself has kept its original exterior and interior, a rarity in a country where 19th-century buildings are usually gutted or demolished.

## Alfredo's Obsession

Mariano Procopio died in 1872. His property went to his sons, Frederico and Alfredo Ferreira Lage, who split the land between them. Frederico built his own elaborate mansion of imported European materials and then died suddenly in 1901 at age 39; his property went to the Ministry of War, which turned it into a military barracks. Alfredo inherited the villa, and he had already been accumulating things since childhood. Paintings, sculptures, porcelains, armor, rare books, coins, historical documents - whatever he could acquire at auctions in Rio or Paris or London. Benefactors added to the hoard. The Duke of Caxias sent pieces. Afonso Arinos donated. The sculptor Rodolfo Bernardelli contributed his own works. Amelia Machado Cavalcanti de Albuquerque, wife of the Viscount of Cavalcanti, gave whole rooms' worth. By the time Alfredo was ready to open his collection to the public, it had outgrown the villa.

## A Museum for a Dead Empire

On 23 June 1921, the centennial of Mariano Procopio's birth, Alfredo opened the museum to select visitors. Among them were Princess Isabel - daughter of Dom Pedro II and the woman who had signed the 1888 law abolishing slavery - and her husband, the Count of Eu. They had just returned from exile. The republican government that deposed their family in 1889 had finally repealed the banishment, and one of their first stops was this improbable Renaissance villa in Juiz de Fora, where so much of the imperial period they had lived through was being preserved. Alfredo used that visit to announce his intention to donate everything to the municipality. On 13 May 1922 - the anniversary of Princess Isabel's abolition decree - the museum officially opened to the public, including a newly built annex to house the overflow.

## What the Collection Holds

A large share of the objects came originally from the Palacio Sao Cristovao, the old residence of Dom Pedro II in Rio de Janeiro. When the imperial palace was dispersed after the fall of the monarchy, pieces drifted into private hands and eventually into Alfredo's. The museum now holds paintings by French masters Charles-Francois Daubigny and Jean-Honore Fragonard, the Dutch Willem Roelofs, and major Brazilians including Pedro Americo, Rodolfo Amoedo, and Belmiro de Almeida. Pedro Americo's Tiradentes Esquartejado - a haunting rendering of the national martyr's dismembered body - hangs here. Plaster casts by Clodion and Marius Mercie occupy niches alongside sculptures by Modestino Kanto. There are 50,000 pieces in total: silver, porcelain, armor, coins, postcards, clothing, rare books, natural history specimens. It is the first museum ever built in Minas Gerais and the third in Brazil.

## A Slow Reopening

Much of the collection and a large part of the gardens have spent recent years closed for restoration. A 19th-century villa and its contents do not survive 160 tropical summers without intervention, and the work has been long. The park remains accessible - families still feed the carp in the lake, children still ride the rustic bridge paths, and the Renaissance facade still rises above the slope exactly as Gambs designed it. From above, Juiz de Fora sits in a narrow valley between the Serra da Mantiqueira foothills and the Paraiba do Sul watershed, and the museum's 78,000 square meters of green show up as one of the larger unbroken canopies near the city center - a pocket of 19th-century Brazil embedded in a 21st-century industrial city.
