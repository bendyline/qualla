# Juiz de Fora

A Minas Gerais city that was once Latin America's industrial frontier, built the first hydroelectric plant on the continent, and reinvented itself three times across 175 years.

**Location:** -21.7500, -43.3500

**Categories:** city, industrial-heritage, historical

## Introduction

In 1889, on the Paraibuna river just outside Juiz de Fora, engineers finished the first hydroelectric power plant in Latin America. Marmelos Zero, they called it. By the same year the city had rails, a macadamized road connecting it to Rio de Janeiro, and textile mills whose smoke made it wealthy enough to be second only to São Paulo and Rio itself. For a brief, dizzying window at the turn of the twentieth century, the valley town in southern Minas Gerais was one of the most important industrial centers in South America. Then the coffee moved west, and the boom ended, and the city spent the next fifty years watching itself fade.

## The Forest Zone

Juiz de Fora's story begins with a road and a forest too thick to see through. In the early 18th century, the Portuguese crown opened the Caminho Novo, the New Way, to move gold more efficiently from the mines at Vila Rica - now Ouro Preto - down to the port at Rio de Janeiro. The route cut through dense Atlantic rainforest so unbroken the region earned its name: Zona da Mata, the Forest Zone. At first, travelers saw only the trees. The first settlers were the merchants and farmers who supplied the mule trains moving gold north and food south, and the village they built along the Paraibuna river carried an earlier name: Santo Antônio do Paraibuna.

## From Gold to Coffee to Steam

When the gold ran out, the coffee moved in. Capital freed from exhausted mines flowed into plantations in the Zona da Mata, and the village at the river crossing grew wealthy enough to be promoted to city status in 1850. Progress accelerated. In 1861, the Estrada União e Indústria - the Union and Industry Road - opened, the first macadamized highway in Latin America. In 1866 the railroad arrived. In 1889 came Marmelos Zero, the hydroelectric plant on the Paraibuna whose turbines first lit Latin American cities. By the first decade of the 20th century, foreign and domestic capital had turned Juiz de Fora into one of the leading textile and industrial producers on the continent, rivaled only by São Paulo and Rio.

## The Capital That Left

Then came the slow reversal. Coffee's frontier rolled southwest onto the fertile plateaus of São Paulo state, and the industrial energy followed. Worse for Juiz de Fora, much of the wealth still generated in Minas Gerais was being redirected to build the brand-new state capital, Belo Horizonte, founded at the end of the 19th century to replace Ouro Preto. The Great Depression of the 1930s deepened the city's decline. By the 1940s, Juiz de Fora's national influence had evaporated, and its population barely moved for thirty years. It was a city that had been the future, and was now just another place that used to matter.

## The Second Rise

Recovery came from two decisions no one could have predicted. In the 1960s, the military government that ruled Brazil chose Juiz de Fora as a major military center, bringing soldiers, investment, and infrastructure. Then the Federal University of Juiz de Fora, UFJF, was founded, attracting students from across Minas Gerais and beyond. Through the 1970s a rare phenomenon began: the industrial rebirth of a post-industrial town. Steel and engineering returned. Later, Mercedes-Benz opened an automotive factory here, and the city became the third-most-important industrial center in the state despite being fourth in population. A call-center economy and private colleges followed. The city had found a new way to be essential.

## The Art Nouveau That Stayed

Walk down Rio Branco Avenue today and the city's layers are all on view. Art Nouveau buildings from the first decade of the 20th century, their iron and glass still intact, stand beside Art Deco apartments from the 1940s and concrete modernist blocks, including one designed by Oscar Niemeyer. The Mariano Procópio Museum holds a nationally important collection. The Pró-Música Philharmonic Orchestra plays year-round. Immigrants from Portugal, Italy, Germany, Syria, Lebanon, and more recently China have each left a restaurant or a festival in their wake. Every two years the Deutsches Fest commemorates German immigration to the city, which began in 1858. The rock festival Festival de Bandas Novas has promoted local bands since 1999, and the Primeiro Plano cinema festival draws filmmakers every November. The city that once powered a continent now mostly powers its own cultural life - and does so with the confidence of a place that has already been, lost, and regained its place in history.

## Flight Context

Located at 21.75°S, 43.35°W in the Paraibuna valley of southern Minas Gerais, Brazil, at approximately 700 m elevation. Best viewed from 4,000-6,000 ft AGL. The Paraibuna river threads through the urban area west to east; Marmelos Zero, the historic hydroelectric plant, sits on the river just outside the city. Nearest commercial airport is Presidente Itamar Franco Airport (SBZM), 35 km north in Goianá. Francisco Álvares de Assis Airport (SBJF) within city limits serves no commercial flights. The BR-040 highway runs through the city connecting it to Rio de Janeiro (south) and Belo Horizonte (north).
