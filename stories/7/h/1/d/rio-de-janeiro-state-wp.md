# Rio de Janeiro (state)

The Brazilian state whose capital was a capital, whose beaches draw the world, and whose mountains sometimes see snow.

**Location:** -22.2500, -42.6600

**Categories:** States of Brazil, Southeast Region, Brazil, Atlantic coast of Brazil

## Introduction

For nearly two centuries, the city of Rio de Janeiro was Brazil's capital. Colonial, imperial, republican, it held the title through all three eras. When the federal government moved to Brasília in 1960, Rio lost its political primacy but kept its reputation. The state around it, one of Brazil's smallest and less than half the size of Portugal, contains nearly everything: coastline and mountain country, petroleum and coffee, favelas and French-style imperial palaces, carnival and oil rigs. Sixteen million people live here. It is the third most populous state in Brazil and the most densely populated.

## Fluminense and Carioca

The demonyms for Rio de Janeiro are older and more complicated than outsiders usually realize. Residents of the state are fluminenses, from the Latin flumen, meaning 'river.' Residents of the capital city are cariocas, a term first recorded in 1502. For centuries the two words competed, official and unofficial, rural and urban. When Rio lost its status as federal district in 1960 and became the short-lived State of Guanabara, carioca became official for the city. In 1975, Guanabara was reabsorbed into Rio de Janeiro state, and the two demonyms finally sorted themselves out: carioca for the city, fluminense for the state as a whole. The fine-grained distinction matters to people here, even if foreigners tend to collapse it all into 'Rio.' On the Brazilian flag, Rio is represented by Mimosa, the beta star in the Southern Cross constellation.

## From French Colony to Imperial Capital

European presence here began in 1502 with Portuguese ships. Between 1555 and 1567, the French attempted to establish a colony called France Antarctique on the shores of Guanabara Bay. To dislodge them, Estácio de Sá founded the city of Rio de Janeiro in March 1565 as a Portuguese stronghold. The city's prosperity came with the 18th-century gold rush in Minas Gerais, when ore flowing down to the coast for export turned the port into the empire's economic heart. In 1763, Rio de Janeiro became the capital of colonial Brazil. In 1808, the Portuguese royal family fled Napoleon and arrived in Rio, turning the colonial capital into the only city outside Europe that had ever served as the seat of a European monarchy. The Bank of Brazil, the Royal Press, the Botanical Garden, and the Royal Military Academy all date from this extraordinary decade.

## Coastline, Mountains, Weather

The state has a 635-kilometer Atlantic coastline studded with bays, Guanabara being the most famous, Sepetiba and Ilha Grande the others. Inland, the terrain rises fast. The Serra do Mar separates Rio from São Paulo to the southwest. The Serra da Mantiqueira forms the physical border with Minas Gerais to the north, and within it stands Pico das Agulhas Negras at 2,791 meters, the state's highest point. Much of the state is highland, often above a thousand meters. The result is a climate that surprises travelers expecting nothing but beaches. On the coast, annual means run around 23 degrees Celsius, with summer temperatures near 26. In the mountains, winters can drop to freezing. Frost is not uncommon in Teresópolis, Nova Friburgo, and Petrópolis. Snow has fallen, occasionally, in Itatiaia National Park. The principal rivers are the Paraíba do Sul, the Guandu, the Piraí, the Macaé, and the Muriaé.

## Oil, Coffee, Tourism

The state economy is dominated by services and industry, with agriculture providing less than one percent of GDP. Rio de Janeiro produces nearly 90 percent of Brazil's crude oil, extracted from offshore fields in the deepwater pre-salt basin. Royalties from that oil have supported state finances through decades of political turbulence; when international prices fell from $93 per barrel to under $50 between 2014 and 2016, state revenues collapsed with them. Coffee and sugarcane once dominated the interior: the area around Campos dos Goytacazes once ran 27 sugar mills, though only a handful still operate. Today, the state produces pineapples, oranges, tangerines, lemons, cassava, and bananas in modest quantities. But tourism is where the state's real gravity lies. Rio de Janeiro is Brazil's second-busiest gateway for foreign visitors after São Paulo, with about 1.5 million international arrivals in 2024, and the city is the most internationally recognized Brazilian destination.

## Places Beyond the City

The 92 municipalities of the state include places the capital's fame can overshadow. Paraty, in the south, preserves Portuguese colonial architecture and cobbled streets where motor vehicles are still largely banned. Armação dos Búzios, on a rocky peninsula in the lakes region, draws high-end tourism to beaches that French actress Brigitte Bardot made famous in the 1960s. Angra dos Reis faces a bay dotted with hundreds of tropical islands, including Ilha Grande. Cabo Frio and Arraial do Cabo, farther east, offer dune beaches and exceptionally clear water. In the mountains, Petrópolis served as the imperial summer capital and still holds the former imperial palace. Teresópolis, Nova Friburgo (colonized by Swiss immigrants in the 19th century), and Penedo (settled by Finns) preserve an unexpected European character in the Brazilian highlands. Resende, in the far west, holds the country's first national park, the largest truck factory, and the military academy at Agulhas Negras. The state contains, in other words, something for every imaginable visitor. What it cannot contain is its own reputation, which the capital extends around the world.
