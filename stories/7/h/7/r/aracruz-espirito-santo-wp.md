# Aracruz, Espirito Santo

A central-coast Espirito Santo municipality founded by two Jesuits in 1556, later reshaped by jacaranda sawmills, Italian immigrants, a paper industry, and an Indigenous land struggle that has never really ended.

**Location:** -19.8200, -40.2728

**Categories:** Populated coastal places in Espirito Santo, 1556 establishments in the Portuguese Empire, Municipalities in Espirito Santo, Populated places established in 1556

## Introduction

The ship Sofia arrived in 1874 carrying about 386 emigrants from Trentino, in the Alps. They had been recruited by Pietro Tabacchi, an Italian landowner in Santa Cruz, to settle his Nova Trento estate a few kilometers up the Piraque-acu River. Three centuries earlier, in 1556, two Jesuit priests named Braz Lourenco and Diogo Jacomo had planted a town called Aldeia Nova, the New Village, on the same coast. The Jesuits baptized the Tupiniquim who lived there. Neither the Jesuits nor the Italians were the first people in Aracruz. They had, in the language of colonial Brazil, arrived among people who had always been there.

## A Place Named Twice

The municipality's identity has shifted with the economy. The original village of 1556 was called Aldeia Nova until it was overtaken by a neighbor that took the name first. The old one became Aldeia Velha, the Old Village. In 1848 Aldeia Velha was emancipated as a municipality and renamed Santa Cruz. Ninety-five years later, in 1943, both the municipality and its capital were renamed Aracruz. Then came the jacaranda problem. The most profitable tree in the region was the fragrant hardwood jacaranda, and the mills that processed it - serrarias - were mostly in a different town called Sauacu. In 1948 the state moved the municipal capital there. Sauacu was renamed Aracruz. The original Aracruz went back to being Santa Cruz. The names now sit exactly opposite where they started.

## The People Who Were Here First

Several thousand Indigenous people live today in Aracruz Municipality, in a cluster of villages (counts run from nine to twelve) - among them Caieiras Velha, Comboios, Iraja, Pau Brasil, Tekoa, and Tres Palmeiras - on three demarcated Indigenous territories. They belong to two peoples - the Tupiniquim, who were here when the Jesuits arrived in 1556, and the Guarani, who arrived later. Their ancestors lost most of this land over four and a half centuries of colonization, immigration, and industrial development. In the twentieth century, a massive paper and pulp industry - Aracruz Celulose, for decades one of the largest bleached eucalyptus pulp producers in the world, whose mill is now run by Suzano - took further ground. The Tupiniquim and Guarani people spent decades in legal struggles to secure the territories they now live on, and only in 2007 were their expanded lands recognized. The Tupiniquim language is gone, though the Guarani still speak their own. What remains, held carefully, are subsistence farming practices, handcraft traditions, and the memory of who was here first.

## Italians Up the River

The Italians who came on the Sofia in 1874 moved inland from Santa Cruz along the Piraque-acu River to Fazenda Nova Trento, the New Trento Farm. The colony failed within months; many of its settlers left for Santa Leopoldina, Rio Grande do Sul, or the mountains where Santa Teresa grew. Other groups established the Palmas Farm. Coffee and cassava plantations took root on the cleared land. In a region where the Tupiniquim and the Jesuits had both shaped the early landscape, the Italian fazendas added another layer - farms, churches in the hills, and Italian surnames that still appear from Aracruz up to the interior mountains. Espirito Santo state as a whole absorbed a large Italian migration in the late nineteenth century.

## Coasts Under Protection

What runs along the coast itself is harder to develop. In 2010, two federal conservation units were created offshore of Aracruz: the 17,741-hectare Santa Cruz Wildlife Refuge and the 112,545-hectare Costa das Algas Environmental Protection Area. The Costa das Algas - Coast of Algae - protects a stretch of continental shelf named for its beds of marine algae, along with reefs and the marine life they support; the nearby Comboios Biological Reserve guards a sea turtle nesting beach. From the beach, you might not notice anything different. From underwater or from a survey boat, you would see a coastline that has been through 470 years of human use and still holds an ecological richness worth legal defense.

## A Day in November

On November 25, 2022, a sixteen-year-old former student of the Primo Bitti State Elementary and Middle School entered that public school in Aracruz and opened fire. He then attacked the Praia de Coqueiral Educational Center, a nearby private school. Four people were killed - three teachers and a twelve-year-old student - and twelve others were wounded. He was arrested about four hours later. The attack, part of a national conversation about school violence in Brazil that had been intensifying through the early 2020s, marked Aracruz in a way no previous event had. The families of the victims became the center of a municipal grief that rippled out to the entire country. Walking Aracruz now, you move through a town with four and a half centuries of layered history - Tupiniquim, Jesuit, Italian, pulp industry, conservation, and November 25 - and the newest layer is the one that hurts the most.

## Flight Context

Located at 19.82 S, 40.27 W on the central coast of Espirito Santo state, roughly 55 km north of Vitoria in a straight line. The municipality covers 1,423 km2 at sea level to moderate coastal elevation. The Piraque-acu and Piraque-mirim rivers drain into the Atlantic together at Santa Cruz, the old coastal seat east of today's inland town. Offshore conservation units - Santa Cruz Wildlife Refuge and Costa das Algas EPA - create distinctive green and blue boundaries on marine charts. Nearest airport: Vitoria/Eurico de Aguiar Salles (SBVT / VIX), about 50 km to the south. Best viewed at 2,000-5,000 ft to appreciate the coastline, inland farmlands, and Atlantic Forest fragments. Expect stable onshore winds and scattered cumulus through the afternoon; visibility generally excellent outside wet-season convection (November-March).
