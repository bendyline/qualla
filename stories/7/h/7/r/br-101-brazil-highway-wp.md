# BR-101 (Brazil highway)

The transcoastal highway that follows nearly the entire Atlantic edge of Brazil, linking twelve state capitals and one of the longest bridges in the Americas.

**Location:** -19.7509, -40.3799

**Categories:** Federal highways in Brazil, Transportation infrastructure, Atlantic coast of Brazil

## Introduction

Drive the BR-101 and you never really leave the coast. The highway shadows the Atlantic for almost 4,800 kilometers - from the tropical dunes of Touros in Rio Grande do Norte, past twelve state capitals, over a 13-kilometer bridge across Guanabara Bay, through the pampas of the south, to the sandy southern terminus at Sao Jose do Norte in Rio Grande do Sul. It is the second-longest highway in Brazil. It is also, arguably, the most scenic - a single ribbon of asphalt that touches more ocean, more cities, and more of the country's population than any other road. Except that it is not quite single. Two gaps remain in its length. Both gaps exist because someone decided the Atlantic Forest was worth protecting more than the highway was worth completing.

## Twelve Capitals on One Road

The numbers do the talking. The BR-101 crosses twelve Brazilian states: Rio Grande do Norte, Paraiba, Pernambuco, Alagoas, Sergipe, Bahia, Espirito Santo, Rio de Janeiro, Sao Paulo, Parana, Santa Catarina, and Rio Grande do Sul. It connects twelve state capitals directly - more than any other rodovia in Brazil. Its formal name is Rodovia Governador Mario Covas, honoring the Sao Paulo governor who pushed for its improvement. Locally it carries other names - Translitoranea, transcoastal, or in some regions the nickname Brioi. Only BR-116, which runs inland from south to north, exceeds it in length. For anyone moving goods or people along Brazil's populated eastern corridor, BR-101 is the principal option. The Rio-Niteroi Bridge, 13 kilometers long across Guanabara Bay, is part of it.

## The Two Gaps

The highway has two deliberate gaps on the paper map. One runs between Peruibe and Iguape in southern Sao Paulo state. Another stretches from Cananeia in Sao Paulo to Garuva in Santa Catarina. Both gaps exist because the Atlantic Forest - Mata Atlantica, the coastal biome that once covered most of eastern Brazil and now survives in roughly seven percent of its original range - was judged more valuable intact than bisected. The highway could have been punched through. It was not. Instead, between Curitiba and Garuva in the south, the BR-101 was replaced by a combination of BR-101 and BR-376, widened by the Parana state government rather than the federal one. Drivers going the full length must detour inland through Curitiba. The forest stays. Whether that was the right call is a question Brazil has answered by leaving the gaps in place.

## Duplication, Slowly

Brazilian highway construction happens on a long timeline. The 312-kilometer section between Curitiba and Florianopolis was duplicated - meaning a second carriageway added, converting the road from two-lane to four-lane - around the year 2000. The 350-kilometer section between Palhoca in Santa Catarina and Osorio in Rio Grande do Sul started duplication in 2005. Rio Grande do Sul's 88.5 kilometers were delivered in February 2011. Santa Catarina delivered its final 14.5 kilometers only in 2019. The northeastern stretch between Natal and Salvador began duplication in 2005 and was still being completed in the early 2020s. A 10-kilometer section crossing the indigenous reserve at Joaquim Gomes in Alagoas remained contested into 2024 - work there cannot proceed without the consent of the Xucuru-Kariri people who live along the road. The timeline teaches patience.

## The Rio-Niteroi Bridge

The 13-kilometer bridge across Guanabara Bay between Rio de Janeiro and Niteroi was the longest prestressed-concrete bridge in the world when it opened in 1974. It is still among the longest. Driving across it is the closest BR-101 gets to the water - for eight minutes of bay, surrounded by ships, islands, Pao de Acucar rising in the western distance, the Atlantic opening east. The bridge replaced a ferry crossing that had linked the two cities since the colonial era. It made the metropolitan area of Rio de Janeiro possible in its modern form. And it turned BR-101 from a broken route into a continuous one along that stretch of coast, carrying hundreds of thousands of vehicles a day between the two anchor cities of Guanabara Bay.

## Road as Nation

Brazil is a country whose geography resists easy travel. The Andes it does not have, but it has the Amazon, the Pantanal, the Atlantic Forest, a coastline longer than the Mediterranean, and a distance from Oiapoque in the north to Chui in the south that exceeds the length of the continental United States east to west. The BR-101 does not cover all of that. It covers the part where most of the people live - the populated coastal belt where roughly three-quarters of Brazil's 215 million people have their homes. From Natal to Niteroi to Florianopolis to the gauchos of Rio Grande do Sul, the highway stitches together the country's oldest cities, its beach towns, its port economies. It is slow in places, patchy in others, forever under expansion. But it remains, kilometer for kilometer, the most Brazilian road in Brazil.

## Flight Context

The highway extends from Touros at 5 S, 35 W in Rio Grande do Norte to Sao Jose do Norte at 32 S, 52 W in Rio Grande do Sul, tracing Brazil's Atlantic coast for nearly 4,800 kilometers. Visible landmarks from altitude include the Rio-Niteroi Bridge across Guanabara Bay, the coastal cities of Natal, Recife, Salvador, Vitoria, Rio de Janeiro, and Florianopolis, and the gaps where the road yields to the protected Atlantic Forest in southern Sao Paulo and northern Santa Catarina.
