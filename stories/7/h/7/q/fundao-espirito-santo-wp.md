# Fundão, Espírito Santo

A small coastal Brazilian municipality where Italian immigrants, congo drums, and a Carnival beach of a hundred thousand all share the same eighteen thousand-person town.

**Location:** -19.9328, -40.4069

**Categories:** brazilian municipalities, coastal brazil, italian immigration, espirito santo

## Introduction

A stretch of BR-101 highway can define a Brazilian municipality, and in Fundão it does. The highway slices through the town center, with an intersection and traffic lights, and on holidays the traffic jams can stretch ten kilometers. At Carnival, as many as a hundred thousand revelers pack Praia Grande, a beach whose district counted 5,349 residents in 2010. Fundão is small. Its total population in 2022 was 18,014. But on the right weekend the beach alone holds more than five times the town, and then the tide recedes and Fundão goes back to being what it mostly is: a coffee-and-banana municipality on the Espírito Santo coast, dealing quietly with the consequences of being this close to Vitória.

## Italians and Congo Drums

Fundão's cultural signature is an unusual collision. Italian immigrants arrived in the late nineteenth century, settling the interior and planting coffee, and their descendants still make the local polenta, jabuticaba wine, and milk liqueur. But the congo bands are older and louder. These are percussion ensembles with African roots, carried through the Catholic calendar by processions that raise masts to Saint Benedict and Saint Sebastian. The mast-raising happens at the Chrysantho de Jesus Rocha Staircase, a neo-Baroque flight of steps built in the shape of a chalice and host, fronting the Parish Church. The drummers file up; the masts go up with them. In 2013 the municipality went further and officially recognized gospel music as a cultural manifestation of Fundão. Catholic processions, congo percussion, Italian cuisine, and state-sanctioned gospel all at once. Small towns can hold more than their size suggests.

## The Forest That Was

Most of Fundão's original Atlantic Forest is gone, replaced by pasture and farmland. What survives does so in protected pockets. The Goiapaba-Açu Environmental Protection Area, created in 1994 and spanning Fundão and neighboring Santa Teresa, shelters 133 bird species, including the robust woodpecker and the bare-throated bellbird whose ringing call carries farther than almost any other sound in the forest. Mangroves still line the Reis Magos River. The restinga, Brazil's coastal scrub ecosystem of salt-tolerant trees and vines, still holds parts of the shore at Praia Grande. The Augusto Ruschi Forest Garden, presented as a model at the 1992 Earth Summit in Rio, is a 4.5-hectare reforestation project where Atlantic Forest species like jequitibá, peroba, and sucupira grow alongside fruit trees — cashew, jackfruit, mango, tangerine. A reconstruction rather than a preservation, but reconstruction here is itself the point.

## Praia Grande at Low Tide

The ordinary Praia Grande is one of Espírito Santo's best-kept beaches, which is another way of saying that municipal rules have restricted beachfront construction here in ways that rules have not restricted Vila Velha or Guarapari further south. The natural features hold. Calm waters suit families and small boats. Guesthouses and kiosks and the occasional pousada stretch back from the sand without towers. Then Carnival arrives and the demographics of Fundão invert completely: a town of 18,000 hosts a beach of 100,000, with music, heat, and the slow geometry of bodies on sand under the southeast Brazilian sun. Outside Carnival, fishermen come back with their hauls; the estuary of the Reis Magos River sits at the southern end, toward Nova Almeida; out of season the beach is quiet, a condition Carnival makes hard to imagine.

## The Railway That Saves the Trip

The Vitória-Minas Railway runs daily passenger trains between Cariacica in Greater Vitória and Belo Horizonte in the highlands, and Fundão Station opened on that line on 15 May 1905. For locals, the train is not nostalgia. The BR-101 highway through Fundão is genuinely dangerous. In 2011, a hilly rural section near town was ranked among the two deadliest stretches in Espírito Santo, with eight deaths that year. In 2012, the road between Fundão and Ibiraçu had 112 traffic accidents and ranked twelfth worst on the entire BR-101. The passenger train runs at sensible speeds, arrives where it's supposed to, and does not hinge on highway police coverage. For residents making longer trips inland or to the state capital, it is the adult option.

## What the Census Doesn't Capture

On paper, Fundão is a middle-income Brazilian municipality: Human Development Index 0.718, per capita GDP just under R$23,000 in 2018, a mixed-farming economy producing thousands of tons of coffee and bananas plus hundreds of tons of tangerines - the ponkan is a municipal symbol. The statistics flatten a place that is actually three or four different places overlapping. Praia Grande is a beach town. The seat is an administrative center. Timbuí and Irundi are farming districts, Irundi first settled by Italian immigrant families in 1875. Along BR-101 since the 1960s, Dona Jandyra's homemade preserves have been sold — cocadas, liqueurs infused with genipap, fig, mallow and flower petals, crystallized sweets of guava, jackfruit, and tomato — the kind of local economy that no census code contains but that tells you what the municipality actually tastes like.

## Flight Context

Located at 19.93°S, 40.41°W on the Espírito Santo coast roughly 45 km north of central Vitória in a straight line (53 km by road). Nearest major airport is Vitória/Eurico de Aguiar Salles (SBVT / VIX), about 37 km south. The Reis Magos River estuary and Praia Grande mark the coastline; Goiapaba-Açu peak (about 850 m) and the green slopes of the protected area rise inland. BR-101 runs through the municipality parallel to the coast. At cruising altitude, the Vitória-Minas Railway traces a clean line against the green.
