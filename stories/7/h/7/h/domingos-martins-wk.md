# Domingos Martins

A Brazilian mountain town where Pomeranian immigrants preserved a language now thought extinct in its European homeland, and winter thermometers drop to zero.

**Location:** -20.3628, -40.6589

## Introduction

The language is called East Pomeranian, and in the region it came from, part of what is now Germany and Poland, it is believed extinct. In the mountainous interior of Espirito Santo state, Brazil, it is still spoken. That improbable survival is the strangest thing about Domingos Martins, a town 542 meters above sea level where winter temperatures drop as low as zero Celsius and the restaurants serve schnitzel, strudel, and pork knuckle. Capixabas, as Espirito Santo natives are called, drive up from Vitoria fifty kilometers east for the cold, the Germanic architecture, the blue-green wall of Atlantic forest that earned the town its nickname: Cidade do Verde, the City of the Green.

## Pomeranians in the Atlantic Forest

Pomeranian immigrants settled this mountain region in the middle of the 19th century, leaving a homeland that has since been divided, rewritten, and largely absorbed by history. In Brazil they formed tight communities in the hills, often in places that were hard to reach, and kept speaking the language they had brought with them. Generations later, that language persisted. The architecture persisted too: steep-roofed houses with carved wooden trim, small bell-towered churches, beer gardens behind restaurants, bakeries selling streusel and poppy seed cakes. Sommerfest in January and February celebrates the original German immigration. Pommerfest in September specifically marks the Pomeranian contribution. Blumenfest in October fills the squares with flowers, and Festa do Vinho in July raises glasses to the region's wines.

## Pedra Azul

The defining natural feature of the municipality is Pedra Azul, the Blue Rock, a massive granite dome rising 1,822 meters above sea level in the 12-square-kilometer Pedra Azul State Park. The stone acquires a blue tonality at certain times of day, depending on the angle of sunlight on the lichens that coat it, hence the name. Hiking trails lead part of the way up the dome, and the park protects a stretch of high-altitude Atlantic forest that once blanketed these mountains before coffee and cattle thinned it. The rock is visible from much of the surrounding countryside, a landmark that orients drivers and hikers alike. Within the broader municipality, mountains rise to 2,000 meters and the watersheds feed down into the rivers of Espirito Santo.

## The First Evangelical Bell Tower

Beside the main square, known as Praca Arthur Gerhardt, stands a Lutheran church that is more than a church. Built in 1866 and given its tower in 1887, it is a listed heritage building, and it is considered the first Lutheran church in Brazil built with a tower. During the period of the Imperial Government, non-Catholic churches were legally prohibited from having towers. Domingos Martins, with its concentration of German Lutheran settlers, built one anyway. The structure became a quiet act of religious assertion that foreshadowed broader changes in Brazilian civic life. In June each year, the Erntedankfest, a harvest thanksgiving festival, still takes place in the square facing the church, the name deliberately preserved in German.

## Forests, Waterfalls, and the Jucu

The mountainous setting of Domingos Martins has made it a year-round base for outdoor activity, though the cold winters draw the biggest crowds. Hiking trails loop through fragments of Atlantic forest preserved around the town, following streams to waterfalls where bathing is possible in the warmer months. The Jucu River descends through the mountains with enough gradient for rafting on its rapids, another draw for visitors based in town. The infrastructure to support all of this is substantial for a mountain community: the town operates as a tourism hub with hotels, restaurants, guide services, and festival venues, and a sizable portion of the economy turns on these visitors.

## German Cuisine on a Capixaba Plate

Most restaurants in Domingos Martins specialize in German cuisine, a fact that surprises visitors who expect Brazilian standards. Schnitzels, roasted pork, potato salads, and heavy breads share menus with regional Capixaba dishes like moqueca (the Espirito Santo version is lighter than the Bahian counterpart and does not use dende oil) and the locally made soft drinks from Refrigerantes Coroa. Biscuit factories borrow recipes from great-grandparents. The result is a hybrid food culture: German by heritage, Brazilian by ingredient and climate. BR-262 is the highway most people use to reach town, running from Vitoria to Belo Horizonte; the alternative is the BR-101 from the coast. Vitoria Airport (Eurico Salles Airport) in the state capital is the nearest air connection, with daily flights from Sao Paulo, Rio, Brasilia, Belo Horizonte and other major cities.

## Flight Context

Domingos Martins sits at 20.36 degrees S, 40.66 degrees W in the mountains of central Espirito Santo at 542 meters elevation, with surrounding peaks reaching 2,000 meters. Pedra Azul rises to 1,822 meters (its neighbor Pedra das Flores to 1,909) and serves as the most prominent landmark for visual navigation. The closest commercial airport is Vitoria/Eurico Salles (SBVT / VIX), about 40 kilometers east-northeast in a straight line (about 50 km by road). From altitude, look for the combination of dense Atlantic forest patches on the steep slopes and the distinctive bare granite dome of Pedra Azul. The BR-262 highway corridor runs east-west through the municipality.
