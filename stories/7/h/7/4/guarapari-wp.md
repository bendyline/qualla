# Guarapari

A Brazilian coastal town whose monazite sand is among the most radioactive in the world, marketed briefly in the 1960s as a health spa.

**Location:** -20.6578, -40.5108

**Categories:** radioactive beaches, natural background radiation, atlantic forest, espirito santo, jesuit missions

## Introduction

The beaches of Guarapari emit more natural radiation than almost any other public place on Earth where people routinely sunbathe. Sensors placed on certain sand patches have registered background dose rates up to 55 microsieverts per hour, compared to roughly 0.34 across the United States, more than a hundred times the baseline. A full year of direct exposure on the worst spots would be equivalent to dozens of chest X-rays or several CT scans. And yet for several decades in the mid-twentieth century, Guarapari was marketed as a health destination, tourists came specifically to lie on the radioactive sand, and the city's population swelled during every tourist season. Brazil's coast holds contradictions.

## The Monazite Coast

Guarapari's radioactivity is geological. A 500-mile stretch of Brazil's Atlantic coast, running from north of Rio de Janeiro up toward southern Bahia, contains beach sands enriched with monazite — a phosphate mineral carrying rare earth elements along with the heavier radioactive elements thorium and uranium. Sea waves have been pounding coastal mountains rich in monazite for millennia, concentrating the black-and-reddish heavy sand along the shoreline. The uranium and thorium decay slowly, emitting gamma radiation. On most of the coast the concentrations are modest. On specific patches at Guarapari, monazite reaches levels that make the beach one of the most studied sites in the world for research into high natural background radiation and its effects on humans.

## What the Numbers Mean

At the extremes, sensors on Guarapari's sand have recorded up to 175 millisieverts per year — a dose 500 times what most Americans receive from all sources of background radiation, and higher than international workplace limits for nuclear workers. A single abdominal CT scan delivers 20 to 30 millisieverts. A chest X-ray, 0.1. Residents of Guarapari city, living inland from the worst spots, receive an average of about 5.2 millisieverts per year — elevated but within a range found in some mountainous regions worldwide. Epidemiological studies of Guarapari residents have not shown dramatically higher cancer rates, which has made the city a valuable natural laboratory for studying low-dose radiation. It has also made it an uncomfortable tourist brand.

## The 1960s Health Craze

In the mid-1960s and 1970s, a theory circulated in Brazilian popular health writing that the monazite sands of Guarapari had medicinal properties, particularly for rheumatic conditions. The reasoning drew on the same European tradition that built radium spas a generation earlier, and on a Brazilian tourist industry looking for a distinctive feature. The city grew in the boom that followed. Hotels went up along the beach. The Brazilian middle class from Minas Gerais and São Paulo adopted Guarapari as a vacation standard. Mainstream medicine did not support the health claims, and the enthusiasm has since cooled, but the tourism pattern it established remains. People still come. They still lie on the sand. Some of them know about the thorium. Most do not.

## Before the Sand Became Famous

Guarapari's written history begins around the year 1000, when Indigenous peoples who had long occupied the southern coast of what is now Espírito Santo were driven inland by the advance of Tupi peoples moving south from the Amazon region. The Temiminós, one of these Tupi groups, controlled the area when Portuguese explorers arrived in the 16th century. In 1585, the Jesuit priest José de Anchieta — a foundational figure in colonial Brazilian Catholicism, missionary, and playwright — founded a Jesuit mission on the site to catechize the Temiminós, naming the settlement after Saint Anne. For the inauguration, Anchieta wrote the Auto Tupi, a religious play performed in the Tupi language. The village became a town in 1679, a district in 1835, a municipality in 1878. Emperor Dom Pedro II visited in 1860. At the end of the 19th century, Italian immigrants landed on the Benevente River and settled the interior, planting coffee. All of this predates, by centuries, the moment someone noticed the sand was unusual.

## Protected Ecosystems Next Door

The municipality of Guarapari also contains two significant protected areas. The Concha d'Ostra Sustainable Development Reserve, 953 hectares established in 2003, protects the mangroves around the Bay of Guarapari — a nursery for fish and crustaceans and a stopover for migratory waterbirds. The 1,500-hectare Paulo César Vinha State Park, formerly called the Setiba nature reserve, protects an Atlantic stretch of dunes, lagoons, and coastal marshes where sea turtles nest and where the coastal ecosystem retains enough of its pre-development character to function as a reference landscape. The contrast between these preserves and the hotel-lined urban beaches is stark. A few kilometers separate them. One is a global anomaly of radioactive beach tourism. The other is a piece of the Atlantic coastal ecosystem as it looked before Portuguese arrival. Both are Guarapari.

## Flight Context

Located at 20.66°S, 40.51°W on the Espírito Santo coast, 47 km south of Vitória. Closest major airport is Vitória's Eurico de Aguiar Salles International (SBVT). The town's own airport (SNGA) has no scheduled commercial service. From cruising altitude, recognize Guarapari by its arc-shaped bay with the white of Praia do Morro and the darker patches of monazite sand on specific beach sections. The Paulo César Vinha State Park, 1,500 ha of coastal ecosystem, extends south along the coast. Elevation near sea level; hills rise rapidly inland toward the 800-meter coastal range.
