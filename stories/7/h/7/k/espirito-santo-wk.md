# Espirito Santo

A narrow coastal state squeezed between the Atlantic and the mountains, where Capixabas trace their heritage to a mix of Indigenous, African, German, and Italian roots.

**Location:** -20.2500, -40.2500

## Introduction

In 1534, Portuguese colonizers landed on what is now the Espirito Santo coast and founded Vila Velha. Their tenure there was brief. The hostility of the Goitaca people, who had lived in that region long before any European ship arrived, forced the settlers across a narrow strait to an island where they established a new settlement they named Vitoria, a declaration of victory that the name made permanent. Vila Velha survived as the oldest town in the state; Vitoria became its capital. Today a 4.8-kilometer bridge links the two, and the people of both call themselves Capixabas, a word that draws on Indigenous, African, and European roots to describe something uniquely born of this coast.

## A Coast Between Two Mountains

Espirito Santo is one of Brazil's smaller states by area, forming a long narrow band between the Atlantic Ocean and the mountains of Minas Gerais. The geography creates sharp contrasts over short distances. On the coast, beach towns like Guarapari draw summer crowds with their nightlife and clubs, and Itaunas is famous for its dunes and for forro, the Northeastern Brazilian dance that echoes through its dance halls on weekend nights. Inland, cool mountain towns like Santa Teresa and Domingos Martins were settled by Italian and German immigrants respectively, preserving architectures, cuisines, and in some cases languages that have become Capixaba in their own way. The state is also home to a lake district whose Juparana Lagoon is the second-largest lake in Brazil.

## Capixaba Identity

The origin of the Capixaba people, as Espirito Santo natives call themselves, is a Brazilian story compressed onto a small stretch of coast. Indigenous peoples, African descendants of enslaved laborers, and European immigrants of various origins intermarried over centuries to produce the modern mix. The word Capixaba itself is believed to come from an Indigenous Tupi term for the cleared land where plants were cultivated. The cuisine reflects the blend. Moqueca Capixaba, a seafood stew cooked in a traditional clay pot, is milder than its Bahian cousin because it does not use dende palm oil. Caranguejada, stuffed crab, appears on menus up and down the coast. Torta Capixaba, a layered seafood dish traditional to Lent, represents one of the more baroque achievements of Brazilian regional cooking.

## Rocks and Ruins

Near Cachoeiro de Itapemirim stands an unusual rock formation called O Frade e a Freira, the Monk and the Nun, two pillars of weathered stone that lean toward each other as if in quiet conversation. Inland, the Pedra Azul State Park preserves a 1,909-meter quartzite peak whose blue tint varies with the angle of sunlight on the lichen covering its surface. The state contains two components of the Discovery Coast Atlantic Forest Reserves, a UNESCO World Heritage site that preserves some of the last remaining fragments of Brazilian Atlantic Forest outside Bahia. And just north of Serra, in the municipality of Ibiracu, sits the Zu Lai monastery, described as the largest Buddhist monastery in Latin America, with reclining and upright Buddha statues and meditative gardens open to visitors.

## Ports, Industry, and Sports Fishing

Espirito Santo punches above its weight economically. The state has seen some of the largest economic development gains in Brazil in recent decades, built largely on its port complex. Tubarão, near Vitoria, is one of the most efficient ports in Latin America and has been for decades, a core link in Brazil's iron ore exports to China, Europe, and beyond. The state's industry is diversified, and its role in international trade outsizes its geographic footprint. Vitoria itself, the second-oldest city in the state (Vila Velha beats it by a decade), sits on an island, offers one of Brazil's best sport fishing grounds in the waters beyond its bay, and anchors a metropolitan area whose beaches, nightlife, and food have made it an underrated Brazilian destination.

## Summer, Winter, and the Atlantic

The coastline defines the tourist calendar. Beach towns fill up in the southern hemisphere summer of December through February, when coastal temperatures run hot and the water stays warm. The mountain interior draws the opposite season: Capixabas and visitors alike head inland during the cool months to find temperatures that genuinely feel like winter, including the occasional near-zero night in towns like Domingos Martins. Vitoria Airport, formally Eurico Salles Airport, receives daily flights from most Brazilian state capitals on carriers including LATAM, Gol, Azul, TEAM, and TRIP. The highway corridor connecting coast to interior passes through some of the most dramatic elevation changes in southeastern Brazil, from sea level to 2,000 meters in under 60 kilometers.

## Flight Context

Espirito Santo stretches along the Atlantic coast of southeastern Brazil between roughly 18 and 21 degrees S and 39 to 41 degrees W. The principal airport is Vitoria/Eurico Salles (SBVT), which serves the capital and has regular connections to Rio (SBGL, SBRJ), Sao Paulo (SBGR, SBSP), Brasilia (SBBR), Belo Horizonte (SBCF), and other capitals. From altitude, the state's defining feature is the contrast between the Atlantic shoreline and the abrupt rise of the mountainous interior, with peaks climbing to 2,000 meters within a short inland distance. Look for the Pedra Azul quartzite dome inland from Vitoria, and for the port complex of Tubarão south of the capital.
