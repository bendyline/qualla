# Espirito Santo

A narrow coastal state squeezed between the Atlantic and the mountains, where Capixabas trace their heritage to a mix of Indigenous, African, German, and Italian roots.

**Location:** -20.2500, -40.2500

## Introduction

In 1535, Portuguese colonizers landed on what is now the Espirito Santo coast and founded Vila Velha. Their hold there was precarious. The hostility of the Goitaca people, who had lived in that region long before any European ship arrived, forced the settlers across a narrow strait to an island where they established a new settlement they named Vitoria, a declaration of victory that the name made permanent. Vila Velha survived as the oldest town in the state; Vitoria became its capital. Today bridges link the two, among them the 3.3-kilometer Terceira Ponte, and the people of both call themselves Capixabas, a Tupi-derived word for people whose Indigenous, African, and European roots are uniquely born of this coast.

## A Coast Between Two Mountains

Espirito Santo is one of Brazil's smaller states by area, forming a long narrow band between the Atlantic Ocean and the mountains of Minas Gerais. The geography creates sharp contrasts over short distances. On the coast, beach towns like Guarapari draw summer crowds with their nightlife and clubs, and Itaunas is famous for its dunes and for forro, the Northeastern Brazilian dance that echoes through its dance halls on weekend nights. Inland, cool mountain towns like Santa Teresa and Domingos Martins were settled by Italian and German immigrants respectively, preserving architectures, cuisines, and in some cases languages that have become Capixaba in their own way. The state is also home to a lake district whose Juparana Lagoon is often described as the second-largest lake in Brazil.

## Capixaba Identity

The origin of the Capixaba people, as Espirito Santo natives call themselves, is a Brazilian story compressed onto a small stretch of coast. Indigenous peoples, descendants of enslaved Africans, and European immigrants of various origins intermarried over centuries to produce the modern mix. The word Capixaba itself is believed to come from an Indigenous Tupi term for the cleared land where plants were cultivated. The cuisine reflects the blend. Moqueca Capixaba, a seafood stew cooked in a traditional clay pot, is milder than its Bahian cousin because it does not use dende palm oil. Caranguejada, a local crab dish, appears on menus up and down the coast. Torta Capixaba, a seafood pie traditional to Holy Week, represents one of the more baroque achievements of Brazilian regional cooking.

## Rocks and Ruins

Near Cachoeiro de Itapemirim stands an unusual rock formation called O Frade e a Freira, the Monk and the Nun, two pillars of weathered stone that lean toward each other as if in quiet conversation. Inland, the Pedra Azul State Park preserves a 1,822-meter granite peak whose blue tint varies with the angle of sunlight on the lichen covering its surface. The state contains two components of the Discovery Coast Atlantic Forest Reserves, a UNESCO World Heritage site shared with Bahia: the Linhares forest reserve and the Sooretama Biological Reserve. And just north of Serra, in the municipality of Ibiracu, sits the Morro da Vargem Zen Monastery, a Soto Zen house founded in 1974 that claims to be the first Buddhist monastery in South America, where a 35-meter Buddha statue was unveiled in 2021.

## Ports, Industry, and Sports Fishing

Espirito Santo punches above its weight economically. The state has seen some of the largest economic development gains in Brazil in recent decades, built largely on its port complex. Tubarão, in Vitoria, is counted among the most efficient ports in Latin America, a core link in Brazil's iron ore exports to China, Europe, and beyond. The state's industry is diversified, and its role in international trade outsizes its geographic footprint. Vitoria itself, the second-oldest city in the state (Vila Velha beats it by sixteen years), sits on an island, offers one of Brazil's best sport fishing grounds in the waters beyond its bay, and anchors a metropolitan area whose beaches, nightlife, and food have made it an underrated Brazilian destination.

## Summer, Winter, and the Atlantic

The coastline defines the tourist calendar. Beach towns fill up in the southern hemisphere summer of December through February, when coastal temperatures run hot and the water stays warm. The mountain interior draws the opposite season: Capixabas and visitors alike head inland during the cool months to find temperatures that genuinely feel like winter, including the occasional near-zero night in towns like Domingos Martins. Vitoria Airport, formally Eurico Salles Airport, receives daily flights from Sao Paulo, Rio, Brasilia, Belo Horizonte and other cities on LATAM, Gol, and Azul. The highway corridor connecting coast to interior climbs steeply, from sea level to well over 1,000 meters within about 100 kilometers.

## Flight Context

Espirito Santo stretches along the Atlantic coast of southeastern Brazil between roughly 18 and 21.3 degrees S and 39.7 to 41.9 degrees W. The principal airport is Vitoria/Eurico Salles (SBVT), which serves the capital and has regular connections to Rio (SBGL, SBRJ), Sao Paulo (SBGR, SBSP), Brasilia (SBBR), Belo Horizonte (SBCF), and other capitals. From altitude, the state's defining feature is the contrast between the Atlantic shoreline and the abrupt rise of the mountainous interior, with peaks climbing to 2,000 meters within a short inland distance. Look for the Pedra Azul granite dome inland from Vitoria, and for the Tubarão port complex on the coast at the northern edge of the capital, beside Camburi beach.
