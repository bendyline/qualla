# Anchieta Palace

A Jesuit schoolhouse from 1551, the oldest structure serving as a seat of government in Brazil, now the executive palace of Espirito Santo state in Vitoria.

**Location:** -20.3213, -40.3396

**Categories:** Espirito Santo, Architecture in Brazil, Palaces in Brazil, Jesuit sites, Colonial architecture

## Introduction

The building started as a chapel covered in straw, without walls. Father Afonso Bras wrote about it in August 1551, describing his arrival in the captaincy of Espirito Santo: residents welcoming him with pleasure and joy, a poor home built quickly so the Jesuits could retreat into it, and every day preaching doctrine to the enslaved Africans and Indigenous people forced into that village. Out of those thatched beginnings grew a stone college. Out of the college grew a palace. For more than two hundred years, the House of St. James was the center of education in Espirito Santo. It has been the seat of the state government since the late eighteenth century, which makes Anchieta Palace one of the oldest government buildings still in use anywhere in Brazil.

## Fire and Stone

The first stone church burned in 1570. Survivors of a shipwreck at the mouth of the Rio Doce had washed up grateful to the Jesuits, and Father Inacio de Tolosa marshaled them into rebuilding, drilling an eight-meter well behind the new church to supply it. In 1666 the structure threatened collapse and was remodeled so thoroughly that it came out looking new. A second college wing went up in 1707 facing Vitoria's bay; a third closed the inner courtyard in 1734, beside the church's second tower. Fire returned in 1796, destroying the interior and the image of St. James that had stood on the altar. By then, the Jesuits had already been expelled from Brazil for nearly forty years - Portugal had turned on the order in 1759, and the dean of the Espirito Santo college and five of his priests had been arrested that December. When the building was rebuilt after the 1796 fire, it was no longer a Jesuit house. It was renamed the Government Palace.

## Joseph of Anchieta

The man the building is named for passed through these rooms more than once. Joseph of Anchieta - Jose de Anchieta in Portuguese - was a Canary Islander by birth and a Jesuit by vocation, one of the first Europeans to seriously study the Tupi language, a writer of grammars and poems who lived among Indigenous people for decades and helped establish what would become Sao Paulo. In 1587 he was responsible for directing the first wing of the St. James College facing John Climacus Square, and for the church of Our Lady of Conception in the nearby town that now bears his name. From 1592 to 1594 he served as Visitor of the Jesuit houses of the South, and in 1596 he became governor of Vitoria's house. He retired to Reritiba, where he died on June 9, 1597. He was buried in front of the main altar of the old church of St. James. In 1610 his remains were moved to Bahia. A fragment of his tibia remains in the palace today.

## An Emperor in the Guest Room

In 1860, Emperor Dom Pedro II and Empress Dona Teresa Cristina Maria stayed at the palace for fifteen days. The visit marked the beginning of a renovation campaign that would transform the old Jesuit compound into something more suitable for imperial protocol. The first of the church's two bell towers came down during that renovation. The second was demolished in 1919, under Governor Bernardino Monteiro. In 1945, Governor Jones dos Santos Neves signed decree number 15,888, officially naming the building Anchieta in honor of the Jesuit who had walked its corridors four centuries earlier. The State Council of Culture listed the building as heritage in 1983, and a major restoration - the first real one, given how many fires and renovations the place had absorbed without ceremony - ran from 2004 to 2009.

## The Two Towers That Were

Anchieta Palace holds one of those historical distinctions that only mean something to architects and archivists: it was the only Jesuit structure in colonial Brazil to have two bell towers and a clock. Both towers are gone now, demolished in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The building is also one of only four in Brazil that use the Sgraffito technique, an Italian wall-decoration method in which layers of tinted plaster are scratched to reveal patterns in contrasting colors. The palace sits directly opposite the Port of Vitoria, at the entrance to Cidade Alta, one of the city's oldest neighborhoods. From the water, its long facade looks more like a colonial convent than a modern government seat - which, of course, is what it was, and in some structural sense still is. You can walk up to it from the port, climb the hill into the old town, and stand in front of a building where Joseph of Anchieta corrected the grammar of a Tupi catechism in the 1590s and where, the next morning, a governor will hold a cabinet meeting in a room that has been holding cabinet meetings, in one form or another, since the 1790s.

## What the Walls Still Hold

When the palace was a church, bodies were buried beneath its floor. That was colonial Catholic practice - the dead sleeping in consecrated ground under the pews, priests beneath the altar, congregation in the side chapels. Excavations during later renovations turned up human remains on the grounds, a reminder that the building has been occupied far longer than any modern institution in it. Today the palace holds works of art described only as priceless. Among the relics is that fragment of Anchieta's tibia, kept in the building named for the man it came from. For a structure whose original wall material was straw, the accumulation is remarkable: stone, fire, renovation, demotion, rebirth, restoration, and now four and a half centuries of continuous institutional memory, all looking down on the freighters in the harbor below.

## Flight Context

Located at 20.32 S, 40.34 W in downtown Vitoria, capital of Espirito Santo state in southeastern Brazil. The palace sits in Cidade Alta, directly opposite the Port of Vitoria, which provides the clearest visual landmark - the port cranes and container terminal are visible from significant altitude. Nearest airport: Vitoria/Eurico de Aguiar Salles (SBVT), approximately 10 km east of the city center. Best viewed at 1,500-3,000 ft AGL to appreciate the colonial quarter's relationship to the modern port and the surrounding bay. Afternoon sea breezes typical year-round; expect moderate turbulence near the ridgeline east of downtown.
