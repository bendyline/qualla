# Caparao National Park

On the border between Minas Gerais and Espirito Santo, Caparao protects Pico da Bandeira - Brazil's third-highest peak - and a staircase of Atlantic Forest climbing from 997 meters to 2,892.

**Location:** -20.4502, -41.7838

**Categories:** National parks of Brazil, Protected areas of Minas Gerais, Protected areas of Espirito Santo, Protected areas of the Atlantic Forest, Mountain ranges of Brazil

## Introduction

The climb starts in the rainforest. At the entrance in Alto Caparao, Minas Gerais, the air is wet and the canopy is dense. Epiphytes coat the trees. Bromeliads hold pools of rainwater in their centers. About thirteen kilometers - six by park road to the Tronqueira trailhead, seven on foot - and more than a vertical kilometer later, the forest thins, the wind picks up, and the traveler steps out onto rocky open fields strewn with tussocks and low shrubs. In winter - June and July here - there can be frost on the ground and ice crusts on the leaves. At 2,892 meters stands the summit of Pico da Bandeira, the third-highest peak in Brazil. From the top, the view runs east toward the Atlantic coast, about 120 kilometers away. The border between Minas Gerais and Espirito Santo runs invisibly through the rocks at your feet. This is Caparao National Park - 31,800 hectares of ancient mountain scooped out of the Serra do Caparao, created in 1961 by presidential decree, and protected ever since as one of the most vertical cross-sections of Atlantic Forest anywhere in Brazil.

## The Third Summit

Brazil is not famous for its mountains. The country is mostly low plateau and coastal lowland, and the peaks it does have - unlike the Andes to the west - rarely look like mountains the way a European or North American visitor expects. Pico da Bandeira is one of the exceptions. Its 2,892-meter summit is the third-highest point in the country. Only the peaks on the Venezuela border, far to the north, rise higher. Ten other summits within Caparao exceed 2,000 meters, creating a small but genuinely alpine landscape in a country more often described by its jungles. The altitude range inside the park runs from 997 meters at its lowest to 2,892 at the top. That nearly two-thousand-meter vertical span does more ecological work than almost any other in Brazil. A single path can take you through tropical rainforest, high-altitude forest, brushwood, and open alpine meadow - four vegetation zones compressed into one climb.

## Two States, Two Sides of the Mountain

Eighty percent of the park lies in Espirito Santo. The east side, facing the Atlantic, is wetter. Moist air from the coast piles up against the slopes, drops its rain, and leaves the forests dense and dripping. Tropical rainforest climbs high here before giving way to the alpine zone. The west side, in Minas Gerais, is drier. The tropical rainforest goes up to about 1,800 meters and is replaced by high-altitude forest and brushwood to 2,400, and then by the open rocky fields. The park was created on May 24, 1961, by decree of President Janio Quadros, with an original area of about 33,000 hectares. It now covers 31,800. Municipalities on the Minas side include Alto Caparao, Alto Jequitiba, Caparao, and Espera Feliz. On the Espirito Santo side: Dores do Rio Preto, Divino de Sao Lourenco, Ibitirama, Iuna, and Irupi. In 2002 it joined the Central Atlantic Forest Ecological Corridor, linking its protection to a larger regional plan.

## What Lives Above the Clouds

The park is one of the last refuges of the northern muriqui, Brachyteles hypoxanthus, one of the largest primates in the Americas and one of the most endangered. Muriquis travel in troops through the canopy, peaceful by primate standards - they do not hierarchy-fight the way many species do, preferring a loose cooperative society. The maned wolf, Chrysocyon brachyurus, lopes through the transitional zones on its long stilt-like legs. Pampas deer, Ozotocerus bezoarticus, keep to the more open slopes. The park's species lists also include the jaguar, the tapir, and the harpy eagle - the enormous gray-and-white raptor that hunts monkeys and sloths straight out of the canopy. Among the birds are the black-fronted piping guan and the solitary tinamou. Alpine meadows, a rarity in Brazil, host plants adapted to stony soil, frost, and ice. Some of these species occur only here.

## Walking In, Quietly

The Minas Gerais entrance sits in Alto Caparao. Visitors can drive to the park gate, walk to one of four camping areas, and set off on a network of hiking trails that climb through the biomes toward the summit. The Espirito Santo entrance is at Dores do Rio Preto. The rules are simple and uncompromising. No domestic animals. No hunting or fishing equipment. No collecting of plants, fruit, animals, or stones. No motorcycles or bikes. The park visitor center includes a small auditorium with 64 seats, bathrooms, a souvenir shop, a snack bar. The Minas Gerais entrance, inaugurated in September 1998, feels functional rather than grand - a place meant for people who are passing through on their way somewhere more important. The park protects the headwaters of three rivers: the Itabapoana, the Itapemirim, and the Doce. What falls as rain on Caparao eventually reaches the Atlantic, and along the way supplies water to the towns downstream. The mountain holds more than its own story.

## Flight Context

Located at 20.45 S, 41.78 W, on the border between Minas Gerais and Espirito Santo states. Pico da Bandeira at 2,892 m is the third-highest peak in Brazil and a dominant VFR landmark on clear days. Altitude range 997-2,892 m across the park. Nearest airports: Usiminas (SBIP / IPN) at Ipatinga, about 130 km northwest, for Minas-side access; Eurico de Aguiar Salles (SBVT / VIX) at Vitoria for the Espirito Santo side, about 155 km east. Best viewed from 10,000-14,000 ft to see the full summit ridge. Dry season (June-August) brings cold but clear mornings; afternoons can build cumulus against the ridgeline. Frost and subfreezing temperatures possible at higher elevations in winter.
