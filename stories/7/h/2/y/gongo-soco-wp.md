# Gongo Soco

A Cornish gold mine deep in the Minas Gerais forest, where British engineers, enslaved Brazilians, and emperors all converged in the 19th century before iron ore and abandonment took over.

**Location:** -19.9642, -43.5981

**Categories:** mining history, cornish diaspora, brazilian slavery, industrial archaeology, minas gerais

## Introduction

An arch still stands in the Brazilian forest east of Belo Horizonte, covered now by a fig tree that has worked its way into the stonework. The arch was built to mark the visits of two emperors: Pedro I in 1831 and Pedro II in 1881. The gold mine it belonged to has been closed for 170 years. The iron mine that replaced it closed in 2016. But the arch remains, and so does the English cemetery up the hill, where ten gravestones still carry inscriptions in a language most residents of the nearest village cannot read. Gongo Soco was, for a brief stretch of the nineteenth century, the largest gold mine in Brazil. Its workers were Cornish and Brazilian and enslaved African. Its ledgers were kept in pounds sterling. Its ruins are now a forest.

## A Bitencourt at the Stream

The story begins with a Bitencourt prospector in 1745, wading up a stream that cut through the region, noticing the glint in the gravel. The alluvial gold ran out eventually, as it always did in colonial Brazil, and the land passed through inheritance to João Baptista Ferreira de Souza Coutinho, Baron of Catas Altas. In 1825 he sold it for £79,000 to the Imperial Brazilian Mining Association, a Cornish company that had looked at Brazil's gold reserves and decided the problem was not the gold but the technology. They brought the technology. Steam engines. Water wheels. Iron rails. Grinding mills with twenty-four wooden hammers tipped in iron, pounding gravel into dust day and night. Between 1826 and 1856, Gongo Soco produced more than 12,000 kilograms of gold, peaking in 1839 with a single-year haul of 1,900 kilograms. The machinery never quite reached the deepest veins.

## The Cornish and the Enslaved

The labor was stratified by origin. The Imperial Brazilian Mining Association hired Cornish miners from places like Gwennap and Redruth, on three-to-five-year contracts at £80 a year, paid monthly. The mine director received £3,500 annually. Under him worked four captains, eight officers, and roughly eighty British miners. Beneath them, doing the dangerous and unskilled labor, were 650 enslaved people owned by the company itself, according to the German traveler Ernst Hasenclever who visited in 1839 — though later historians estimate that some of the Black workers were free laborers paid for the same labor. A second count in 1838 recorded 413 enslaved people, 148 Europeans, and 190 free Brazilians, totaling 751. The enslaved women worked above ground, washing the gold-bearing sand in wooden troughs. The enslaved men worked the galleries below, pushing wagons on iron rails, propping the ceilings with tree trunks hauled six miles from the forest. A doctor and a priest served everyone. Their conditions were not equal.

## A Village for Two Worlds

About half a mile from the mine entrance, in the wooded valley of the Gongo Soco River, a village rose around a church among banana trees. The enslaved community lived apart from the Cornish, housed in separate quarters. The mine captain William Jory Henwood, a Cornishman who had brought his own family, opened a school for the children of the enslaved workers. The Casa Grande, a mansion originally built by the Baron of Catas Altas, housed the mine director and the first commissioner and their families; on Saturday evenings the commissioner hosted parties there with concerts and dances. There was a company store, a weekly market where miners could buy chickens and fruit, a library. Two churches served different communities: a Catholic one for the Brazilians and the enslaved, an Anglican one for the Protestants. The Cornish who were Wesleyan Methodists prayed in fields and parlors. They planted seeds from Cornwall in their gardens, trying to grow flowers they remembered.

## The Ruins

The mine's easy gold ran out. By 1856 the annual yield had fallen to 29 kilograms and the operation closed. Some Cornish miners went home; others moved to the Saint John d'El Rey Mining Company nearby. The enslaved people who had built and worked the mine faced a different set of futures, mostly unrecorded. The village decayed slowly. A 1931 census found thirty stone houses still standing along a 1.5-kilometer road. Today there are two archaeological sectors. Sector 1 holds the mine shaft and industrial structures, including a chimney 5.2 meters tall. Sector 2 holds what remains of the village — traces of a hospital, foundations of the two churches, the Cemitério dos Ingleses where those ten English gravestones stand, and the stone wall sixty meters long that ends in the fig-tree-covered imperial arch. The traveler Richard Francis Burton described parts of the complex in his own Brazilian writings.

## Iron and Vale and Ending

In 1986 Mineração Socoimex acquired the site, taking over the remains of the old gold works. In 2000, CVRD — soon to be rebranded as Vale, one of the largest mining companies in the world — acquired full control of Socoimex and reopened Gongo Soco as an iron mine. The hematite reserves were about 75 million tons, and at peak the operation shipped 7 million tons of iron ore a year. But iron prices fell, extraction costs climbed, and by April 2016 Vale announced the mine was closing, with 90 employees laid off. Two centuries of extraction, the first half of it visible in ruins and the second half in machinery still being disassembled. The forest is taking both back. The fig tree on the arch has the patience for it.

## Flight Context

Located at 19.96°S, 43.60°W in the mountains east of Belo Horizonte, in the Iron Quadrangle (Quadrilátero Ferrífero). The nearest major airport is Belo Horizonte's Confins International (SBCF), about 60 km northwest. The region is recognizable from cruising altitude by the red-brown scars of iron mining operations across the otherwise green Mata Atlântica hills. Catas Altas, a nearby historic town, sits at the foot of the Caraça range. Elevations around 900 to 1,200 m.
