# Belo Horizonte

Brazil's sixth-largest city: a planned grid of bars, hills, and the wildest appetite for neighborhood tavern life in the country.

**Location:** -19.9281, -43.9419

**Categories:** Cities in Minas Gerais, Brazilian state capitals, Planned cities

## Introduction

Locals call it BH, pronounced beh-ah-GAH, and they talk about their city the way other Brazilians talk about the beach. The slogan goes, If there is no sea, let's go to the bar. There are a lot of bars. Belo Horizonte claims - without much evidence but with complete conviction - to be the city with the most bars per capita on the planet. Arriving in BH feels like arriving at a reunion where the party started without you but nobody minds if you join late. The grid is orderly. The avenues are broad. The bar on the corner will still be serving cold beer and deep-fried pastel at midnight.

## A City on Graph Paper

Brazil's first planned modern city was inaugurated in 1897 on paper that Aarao Reis had drawn in imitation of Washington, D.C. The urbanist from Para designed a symmetrical grid with streets named after Brazilian states and indigenous tribes, a core surrounded by the Avenida do Contorno that was supposed to keep workers outside the planned zone. It did not work that way. The metropolis grew past its boundaries almost immediately, pouring out into the 34 surrounding municipalities that now form Greater Belo Horizonte - population around six million. But the core still reads on a map. Square blocks intersected by diagonals. Broad avenues lined with trees. The founder's optimism still visible in the shape of the streets, 130 years after the shovels went in.

## Mountains All Around

The name means Beautiful Horizon and refers to the Serra do Curral, the Curral Ridge that walls off the southern edge of the city. Belo Horizonte sits at 852 meters elevation inside a bowl of mountains, which moderates its weather - summer tops out around 29 Celsius, winter rarely drops below 15 at night - and keeps strong winds out. The Parque das Mangabeiras climbs the ridge to give views that locals use for sunset gatherings. Praca do Papa nearby offers the same view at night, when the city becomes a grid of lights pressed against dark hills. The Parque Municipal downtown is closed on Mondays, like most museums. Plan accordingly. The city rewards visitors who know which day to see what.

## Savassi and the Bar Gospel

The central Savassi neighborhood is where BH's bar reputation gets tested and confirmed nightly. Dozens of botecos - small Brazilian taverns - crowd a few blocks of Avenida Prudente de Morais, Rua Marilia de Dirceu, and the intersection of Rua Pium-i with Rua Passatempo. A boteco is not a bar in the North American sense. It is a place where tables spill onto the sidewalk, where you order a chopp of draft beer and pick at fried cassava or salted linguica, where conversation moves louder as the evening progresses and nobody is watching the clock. Comida di Buteco, the gastronomic festival held between April and May, turns the whole city into a competition - bars creating signature dishes, judges circulating, locals voting. The city lives for this.

## Food Worth Leaving the Coast For

Minas Gerais has the most famous regional cuisine in Brazil. Pao de queijo - small, chewy cheese rolls made with cassava flour - originated here and are now consumed nationwide for breakfast. Feijoada, the black-bean-and-meat stew, reaches its richest expression in the mineiro kitchens that spend hours over it. Tutu de feijao, frango com quiabo, torresmo, doce de leite - the state has given the country more of its comfort food than any other. Buffets are the usual format, billed by the kilo, letting diners stack plates with whatever looks good. And cachaca, the sugarcane spirit used in caipirinhas, is considered a state product in Minas. The Expocachaca fair every spring draws producers from across the region to pour samples for anyone who asks.

## Niemeyer's Pampulha

In the 1940s a young mayor named Juscelino Kubitschek commissioned an even younger architect named Oscar Niemeyer to design a neighborhood around an artificial lake on the city's northern side. The resulting Pampulha Complex is now a UNESCO World Heritage site - the Church of Sao Francisco de Assis with its curving concrete roof, the casino turned art museum, the yacht club, the ballroom. Niemeyer's curves would later define Brasilia. They were tested here first. Kubitschek went on to become president and build that new capital. Both men are buried in that city of theirs, but the buildings they made together on the shore of Pampulha Lake are still used, still visited, still photographed in the golden Minas light.

## Basecamp for Colonial Minas

Belo Horizonte makes most sense as a gateway. The colonial towns of Minas Gerais - Ouro Preto 100 kilometers southeast, Tiradentes 194 kilometers away, Sao Joao del Rei 190 kilometers, Diamantina 290 kilometers north, Mariana next to Ouro Preto - are day trips or weekends from the capital. Ouro Preto was Brazil's first UNESCO World Heritage site, the richest ensemble of baroque architecture in the Americas. Tiradentes has its steam locomotive to Sao Joao del Rei. Congonhas has Aleijadinho's twelve soapstone prophets. Serra do Cipo National Park, 100 kilometers out on the MG-010 highway, offers waterfalls and caves. BH itself is where you sleep, eat, drink, and catch the bus. It is the planned grid from which the colonial past becomes accessible, which may be the modern city's truest purpose.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 19.93 S, 43.94 W, city elevation 852 meters in central Minas Gerais. Served by Confins International Airport (SBCF) 44 km north and Pampulha Airport (SBBH) in the city. Visible landmarks include the Serra do Curral to the south, Pampulha Lake with Niemeyer's complex to the north, and the Mineirao football stadium. The mountain bowl holds the city's microclimate - summer rain October to March, dry sunny winters.
