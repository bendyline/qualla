# Belo Horizonte

Often called Brazil's first planned modern city, laid out on graph paper in 1897 to replace a baroque capital that had run out of gold.

**Location:** -19.9211, -43.9389

**Categories:** Cities in Minas Gerais, Brazilian state capitals, Planned cities, UNESCO heritage

## Introduction

Ouro Preto ran out of gold. That was the fundamental problem Belo Horizonte was built to solve. By the 1890s the old capital of Minas Gerais was a baroque masterpiece whose economic moment had passed, its churches and cobblestones still beautiful but its mines exhausted and its republican intellectuals angry. When Brazil became a republic in 1889, the young government decided to start over. In 1893 Governor Afonso Pena selected a small village called Curral del Rey - the King's Corral, an old bandeirante farm - as the site. The urbanist Aarao Reis drew up a grid on paper that drew on Washington, D.C. On December 12, 1897, the new capital was inaugurated. Often called the first planned modern city in Brazil, it opened half-finished, waiting to see if anyone would come.

## The Grid That Broke Its Walls

Aarao Reis laid out perpendicular streets in the center, cut diagonally by avenues named after Brazilian states and indigenous tribes, and encircled the whole plan with the Avenida do Contorno. Workers were to live outside this ring - the trendy neighborhood called Funcionarios was reserved for government employees, and the plan assumed class would be legible on the map. It held for about a decade. By the 1920s the city had spilled past the Contorno, and by the end of the twentieth century it anchored the 34 municipalities of what is now Greater Belo Horizonte, the third-largest metropolitan area in Brazil with six million people. The grid still structures the core. The rest of the city improvised around it, building favelas on the hillsides and industrial districts to the west in Contagem and Betim.

## Niemeyer's Pampulha

In the 1940s a young mayor named Juscelino Kubitschek met a young architect named Oscar Niemeyer and handed him a lake. The Pampulha Complex - a Niemeyer-designed neighborhood around a large artificial lake to the city's north - became one of the first full-scale expressions of Brazilian modernist architecture. The Church of Sao Francisco de Assis, with its concrete parabolic vaults, Candido Portinari's tile mural of Saint Francis and his painted Stations of the Cross, was so radical the Catholic Church refused to consecrate it for some fourteen years. The casino closed in 1946 when gambling was banned nationally, reopening as the Pampulha Art Museum. The yacht club and ballroom joined the complex, which became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2016. Kubitschek became president and commissioned Niemeyer to design the buildings of Brasilia. The working relationship began on the shore of Pampulha.

## Right to Food

In 1993, under mayor Patrus Ananias de Souza, Belo Horizonte launched a public policy that quietly made it famous among urban planners worldwide. The city declared that citizens had a right to food - not charity, not welfare, but a right. Farmers' markets were set up across neighborhoods for direct sales, bypassing middlemen. Market prices were surveyed and posted publicly so consumers could compare. Participatory budgeting drew ordinary residents into municipal decisions. Child mortality fell by more than half. In 2009 the program received the first Future Policy Award from the World Future Council, presented in Hamburg. The Vila Viva program urbanized favelas while rehousing families within their own neighborhoods, using 80 percent local labor. Little of this made headlines at the time. Cities quietly learned from BH instead.

## Metal, Music, Modernism

The Clube da Esquina - the Corner Club - started on a BH street corner in the mid-1960s and became one of the most influential movements in Brazilian music. Milton Nascimento emerged from it. So did Lo Borges, Beto Guedes, Toninho Horta, and the songwriter Fernando Brant, whose lyrics Nascimento set to music that crossed borders. The city also became Brazil's capital of heavy metal. Sepultura, the world's best-known Brazilian metal band, formed here. Sarcofago, often credited as one of the founders of modern black metal, came out of the BH scene in the 1980s. The contrasts keep going: the Palacio das Artes, begun to a Niemeyer design, houses theaters and galleries, the Circuito Cultural Praca da Liberdade ties together eleven museums and cultural spaces around the former governor's palace, and the puppet theater group Giramundo has operated since 1970 with its own dedicated museum. Music, metal, marionettes, modernism - BH holds them all.

## The Pampulha Question

The city is still built on contradictions. It has the architectural ambition of Niemeyer's modernism and the daily reality of traffic-choked avenues. It has one of Brazil's best urban food security records and persistent favela poverty on its hillsides. It has a metro that opened in 1986 and, four decades later, still runs only one line - work on Line 2 finally began in 2024 after decades of promises, and Line 3 remains a plan. It has a tropical savanna climate cooled by its 852-meter elevation and a rainy season that floods parts of the low-lying city in many years. It is Brazil's sixth-largest city and often its most overlooked, shadowed by Sao Paulo and Rio to the south. The local answer, as always, is to go to the bar and argue about it. Belo Horizonte is used to being underestimated.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 19.93 S, 43.94 W at 852 meters elevation in central Minas Gerais. Served by Confins International Airport (SBCF), about 34 km north, for airline flights; Pampulha Airport (SBBH) in the city has handled general aviation only since scheduled flights ended in 2019. The municipality's highest point, about 1,507 m, is in the Serra do Rola-Moca; Pico Belo Horizonte on the Serra do Curral reaches 1,390 m. Visible landmarks include Pampulha Lake with Niemeyer's modernist complex to the north, the Curral Ridge to the south, and the Mineirao football stadium.
