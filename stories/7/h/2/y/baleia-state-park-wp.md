# Baleia State Park

A 102-hectare park on paper, protected since 1932, with 188 species of birds and, for decades, no rangers.

**Location:** -19.9287, -43.8946

**Categories:** State parks of Brazil, Cerrado, Protected areas of Minas Gerais

## Introduction

The park exists and does not exist at the same time. In 1932 decree 10.232 set this rugged patch of cerrado at the foot of the Serra do Curral aside as a botanical garden. It would be the capital's first conservation unit, and one of the first in Minas Gerais. Nothing followed. In 1988 a new decree - 28.162, dated July 6 - created the Baleia State Park formally. Nothing followed that, either. By 2014 the park had been legally protected for more than eighty years while remaining, in every practical sense, land squatters moved onto and fires burned through. That October, the Public Ministry of Minas Gerais filed suit demanding what decades of paperwork had failed to produce - a manager, four rangers, actual enforcement.

## Birds That Should Not Be Here

Somehow, despite everything, the birds stayed. The park catalogs 188 species, of which eleven are endangered. Three prized residents - the white-rumped tanager, the blue finch, and the swallow-tailed cotinga - are specialties of Brazil's cerrado and highlands that have grown scarce in much of their range but persist in the pocket of cerrado and gallery forest within the park's theoretical boundaries. The fauna reads like a survey of small Brazilian wildlife: possums, armadillos, monkeys, coatis, grisons. High-altitude grasslands hold their place on the rocky slopes. Six springs still flow, still supply the population of the Serra neighborhood below. What the park protects, in the absence of rangers, is mostly the topography itself - land too rugged, too vertical, too close to city limits to be fully claimed by development.

## The Solidarity Trail

The Hospital da Baleia owns the adjacent forest, called Mata das Baleias, and in 2010 announced a partnership with private sponsors to develop adventure tourism. A two-kilometer path was laid out from the hospital's headquarters to Pico Belo Horizonte, the 1,390-meter peak also known as Antenna Peak, from which the entire city is visible. They call it the Solidarity Trail. Revenue from visitors was meant to help fund the hospital. The arrangement fills part of the void left by the absent state. Universities send researchers. Weekend hikers climb for the view. The park proper remains understaffed, but the forest beside it has become, by necessity and improvisation, what the park was supposed to be all along.

## The View from Antenna Peak

From the summit, Belo Horizonte spreads out in the shape its planners never intended it to take. The grid of broad avenues laid down in the 1890s is still there in the core, crossed by diagonals that were supposed to keep the city legible. Around that core, over the course of a century, the unplanned city grew: favelas climbing the hillsides, industrial districts to the west, the mass of contemporary BH stretching as far as the metropolitan fringe. From nearly 1,400 meters the city looks nearly continuous. The park you are standing in sits at its edge like a green comma in a sentence that has otherwise run on. That such a comma still exists, despite eighty years of bureaucratic paralysis, is the small miracle the lawsuit was trying to protect.

## Protection by Neglect

There is an irony in how the park survived. Fires came through. Squatters built shelters. Pollution tainted the water. And yet the rare birds are still here. The springs still flow. The three-character story - protected, unimplemented, endangered - has been the park's condition for so long that its continued existence as ecological habitat begins to seem accidental. The 2014 lawsuit demanded change in thirty days: a manager, four rangers, equipment, vehicles. Implementation has moved in fits and starts since then: in April 2025 a state decree renamed it Parque Estadual da Baleia, folded it formally into Brazil's protected-area system and charged the State Forestry Institute with managing it and forming an advisory council, though it still had no approved management plan. The cerrado waits. The white-rumped tanager flits between branches. The Taquaril neighborhood below keeps growing toward the park's edge. Whether the law ever catches up with the land remains an open question.

## 102 Hectares on Paper

The park covers 102 hectares - about four-tenths of a square mile, small enough that it should have been easy to fence, staff, and manage. That it has not been is a comment on priorities rather than possibility. The rugged topography that kept developers out also made the park itself difficult to occupy. Its six springs still serve the people living below, a quiet ecological service the city has been receiving without paying for. The Pico Belo Horizonte, the rare birds, the cerrado's scraggly trees - they remain, for now. Anyone flying over the southeastern edge of Belo Horizonte can see the green interruption in the urban sprawl. That interruption is the Baleia State Park. It has been protected since 1932. It is still waiting for a plan to manage it.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 19.93 S, 43.89 W at the southeastern edge of Belo Horizonte, Minas Gerais. The park sits at the foot of the Serra do Curral, about 5 km east-southeast of the city center in a straight line, ranging in altitude from city level to the 1,390-meter Pico Belo Horizonte. Nearest airports are Pampulha (SBBH) about 10 km northwest and Confins (SBCF) about 35 km north-northwest. Visible landmarks include the Serra do Curral mountains and the antenna array at Pico Belo Horizonte.
