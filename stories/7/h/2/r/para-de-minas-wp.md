# Para de Minas

Born as a rest stop on the road between the Pitangui gold mines and Sao Paulo, this central Minas Gerais town now raises millions of chickens a month.

**Location:** -19.8600, -44.6078

**Categories:** brazilian-cities, minas-gerais, agricultural-history, colonial-roads, poultry-farming

## Introduction

It started as a place to stop. In the 18th century, when Pitangui gold was pulling people deeper into the Minas Gerais backcountry, a road opened between those mines and Sao Paulo. Somewhere along the Paciencia Stream - the Stream of Patience, a name that tells you something about the journey - travelers began pulling up to rest. The surrounding land turned out to be fertile. Some of the travelers stopped resting and started farming, feeding the endless expeditions grinding past toward the gold. That roadside rest stop became a settlement. The settlement became a parish in 1846. The parish eventually became a city called Para de Minas, and today it sits at the center of central Minas Gerais raising millions of chickens a month, processing pigs and cattle, and exerting a quiet regional pull on the smaller towns around it. The through-traffic of the 1700s never really stopped. It just learned to drop off trucks of grain on the way.

## Three Churches on the Same Spot

The chapel that anchored the settlement was built by Manuel Batista - known locally as Pato Fofo, 'Fluffy Duck' - and dedicated to Our Lady of Mercy; it was inaugurated on 2 July 1772. A second Mother Church was inaugurated on 1 January 1901, but that one was torn down on 21 April 1971 to make room for the expansion of Padre Jose Pereira Coelho Square, locally called Matriz Square. On 9 April 1972, a new Our Lady of Mercy Mother Church opened on the reconfigured site. Three churches in two centuries, each dedicated to the same Virgin, the older ones swept away as the town outgrew them. The current church is one of the main architectural landmarks of Para de Minas and anchors the central square where generations have gathered for Sunday services, Holy Week processions, and municipal gatherings. The majority of residents still identify as Catholic - over 83 percent according to the 2010 census - though growing Protestant, Spiritist, and Afro-Brazilian religious communities now share the town.

## Hills That Hold the Water

The terrain is predominantly undulating. About 60 percent of the municipal territory rolls as hills, 20 percent rises into mountainous terrain anchored by quartzite ridges including the Andaime and Piteira formations, and the remaining 20 percent lies flat along stream courses. The highest point, on the Serra da Piteira, reaches 1,196 meters, on ridges of quartzite that have resisted weathering through long geological time. Around them, on flattened hill crests, the land has been capped by consolidated laterite crusts laid down during the Miocene and Pliocene epochs - surfaces millions of years old. The drainage forms a distinctive rectangular pattern visible from above, and the city sits in a region with limited water resources, so the town rations water during prolonged dry spells. The climate is monsoon-influenced humid subtropical - hot rainy summers and mild dry winters with an average annual temperature of 21.8 degrees Celsius. February is the warmest month, averaging about 24 degrees; June is the coolest, averaging about 18.5.

## A Poultry Capital

Livestock farming has been part of the local economy since the 19th century. Pig farming came in the 1990s. Poultry came in the 1970s, and it is poultry that now defines Para de Minas. The Western Minas Farmers Cooperative - known locally as COGRAN - had 240 members by March 2014, when the cooperative was producing an average of three million poultry birds per month in Para de Minas alone. Including neighboring municipalities, that number rose to twelve million. The Francisco Olive Diniz Exhibition Park hosts the Fest Frango - the State Chicken and Pig Fair - drawing regional producers to celebrate the industry that has transformed these hills. Sugarcane, maize, and rice have been cultivated since the 1800s, mostly for local consumption. Cotton was the first crop raised on an industrial scale, serving Brazilian textile factories until polyester from petroleum made those factories obsolete. Coffee came in the 1970s with a French farmer named George Collin, who found he could raise successive harvests here before eventually shifting much of his land to rubber.

## The Town Today

Para de Minas is classified by the IBGE as a regional A zone center - meaning it exerts significant influence over the smaller municipalities around it, including Igaratinga, Maravilhas, Onca de Pitangui, Pequi, and Sao Jose da Varginha. The Intermediate Geographic Region of Divinopolis contains it. The city center has become a major commercial hub, with national chain stores alongside small and medium-sized businesses clustered around the historic square. Fabrika Mall, the city's first shopping center, opened in 2020. The Human Development Index of 0.725 ranks the city 1,154th in Brazil - meaning it sits comfortably in the upper portion of the country, with indicators at or above the national average. Poverty has decreased substantially. From 2000 to 2010, the proportion of residents earning under half the minimum wage dropped by 70.6 percent. Between those statistics and the three million chickens a month, Para de Minas has done the thing that gold-rush rest stops were never supposed to do. It built a lasting economy out of feeding people who were just passing through.

## Flight Context

Located at 19.86 degrees south, 44.61 degrees west in central Minas Gerais, at approximately 2,600 feet (about 790 m) elevation. About 70 km west of Belo Horizonte in a straight line (roughly 85-90 km by road). Nearest major airport: Belo Horizonte Confins (SBCF), roughly 72 km east-northeast. Regional airport at Divinopolis (SNDV), about 45 km southwest, serves the area. Cruise altitudes of 6,000 to 10,000 feet give good views of the distinctive rolling hills, the quartzite ridges of the Andaime and Piteira formations, and the agricultural patchwork of poultry operations, cattle pastures, and cropland. The BR-262 highway runs past the city on its way west from Belo Horizonte.
