# Brumadinho Dam Disaster

At 12:28 p.m. on a Friday in January 2019, a mountain of mining waste broke loose and buried 270 people alive, most of them workers on their lunch break.

**Location:** -20.1197, -44.1214

**Categories:** Vale S.A., 2019 disasters in Brazil, Dam failures in Brazil, Mining disasters in Brazil, Tailings dam failures, History of Minas Gerais

## Introduction

It was lunchtime on a Friday. In the cafeteria of the Córrego do Feijão iron mine, workers were eating. Some had just sat down with their trays. Others were finishing up, thinking about the afternoon shift. Nine kilometers east of the town of Brumadinho, in the hills of Minas Gerais where Brazil's iron ore wealth has been dug from the earth for over a century, nothing seemed out of place. Then, at 12:28 p.m. on January 25, 2019, the tailings dam above them failed. Twelve million cubic meters of toxic mud - the slurry of waste left over from washing iron ore - came down the valley at the speed of a freight train. The cafeteria was buried with almost everyone inside it. So was the mine's administrative building. So were houses, farms, a railway bridge, and a small bed and breakfast in the village of Vila Ferteco, a kilometer down the valley. Two hundred and seventy people died. Most of them had been at work.

## The People Who Did Not Come Home

The dam had been inactive since 2014. It no longer received fresh tailings. To the National Mining Agency, it was classified as a small structure, low risk, low potential for damage. On paper, it was almost forgotten - a closed chapter in the long history of iron extraction in Minas Gerais. But paper does not hold back a mountain of saturated mud. Inside the cafeteria were mine employees, contractors, cooks, cleaners. Outside, in the administrative buildings, engineers and clerks. Down the valley, families in homes that had been there longer than the mine. Train crews working on the rail bridge. Guests at the bed and breakfast. Of the 270 who died, 259 bodies were recovered in the months that followed. Eleven remained missing after the first year of searching. Three years later, six were still missing. The mud was so thick and so deep that searchers sometimes gave up hope of ever finding the rest.

## Warnings That Went Unheeded

The union treasurer called it premeditated. That is a strong word, but it reflected a feeling widespread among the workers: the dam had been trouble, and everyone knew it. Sensors designed to monitor the structure's integrity were known to be faulty. Complaints about stability had circulated for years. And the German inspection firm TUV Sud had certified the dam as safe only months before it gave way. The disaster also carried the weight of a recent parallel. In November 2015, a similar dam owned by the same company, Vale, had failed near the town of Mariana, killing 19 people and obliterating the village of Bento Rodrigues. After Mariana, Vale had vowed never again. Now, three years and two months later, it had happened again. Only this time the death toll was more than ten times higher, and the cafeteria full of workers made the loss feel especially cruel - people buried not on a remote hillside but at the center of their own workplace.

## The River Downstream

By 3:50 p.m. that afternoon, the mudflow had traveled more than five kilometers and reached the Paraopeba River. The Paraopeba supplies water to one-third of Greater Belo Horizonte, a metropolitan region of more than five million people. Cadmium and other metals soaked into the sediments. Water collection was suspended in the downstream communities of Brumadinho, Juatuba, and Pará de Minas. Twenty other municipalities registered some effect. The mud also rolled through the Serra dos Dois Irmaos, a conservation area of the endangered Atlantic Forest. Biologists knew what that meant without having to guess - endemic animals killed, native vegetation smothered, a long recovery, if recovery at all. Two days after the main collapse, sirens wailed again. A second dam above the same site was showing signs of instability. Twenty-four thousand residents of Brumadinho were evacuated in the predawn hours, downtown included. Rescue teams were pulled back. For several hours, the living had to wait while the rescuers saved themselves.

## After the Mud

Vale's stock lost 24 percent in a single day - the largest one-day loss in the history of the Brazilian market. Investigators froze three billion dollars of the company's assets. Brazilian prosecutors eventually charged Vale, its auditor TUV Sud, and sixteen individuals, including the company's former president Fabio Schvartsman, with intentional homicide and environmental crimes. In February 2021, Vale reached an agreement with the state of Minas Gerais to pay reparations initially estimated at seven billion dollars. Israeli search and rescue teams had arrived within days of the collapse, 130 specialists working alongside Brazilian firefighters and naval divers. The Inhotim Institute, one of Latin America's largest open-air art centers, sat in Brumadinho too. It was evacuated but not reached by the mud, and later reopened. For the families of the 270, reparations and agreements and stock prices meant less than the simple fact that people who had gone to work that morning had not come home. In the hills above the town, the earth still remembers.

## Flight Context

Located at 20.12 S, 44.12 W, approximately 60 km southwest of Belo Horizonte. The disaster site lies 9 km east of Brumadinho town in the iron-rich hills of Minas Gerais. Best viewed from 3,000-5,000 ft to see the scale of the tailings complex and the scar of the 2019 mudflow path toward the Paraopeba River. Nearest airport: Tancredo Neves International (SBCF / CNF) at Confins, about 65 km northeast. Pampulha-Carlos Drummond de Andrade Airport (SBBH / PLU) is closer for general aviation. Visibility typically good in the dry season (May-September).
