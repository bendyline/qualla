# Brumadinho Dam Disaster

At 12:28 p.m. on a Friday in January 2019, a mountain of mining waste broke loose and killed 270 people, most of them working at the mine.

**Location:** -20.1197, -44.1214

**Categories:** Vale S.A., 2019 disasters in Brazil, Dam failures in Brazil, Mining disasters in Brazil, Tailings dam failures, History of Minas Gerais

## Introduction

It was lunchtime on a Friday. In the cafeteria of the Córrego do Feijão iron mine, workers were at lunch. Nine kilometers east of the town of Brumadinho, in the hills of Minas Gerais where Brazil's iron ore wealth has been dug from the earth for over a century, nothing seemed out of place. Then, at 12:28 p.m. on January 25, 2019, the tailings dam above them failed. Twelve million cubic meters of mud - the slurry of waste left over from washing iron ore - came down the valley at the speed of a freight train. The cafeteria was buried with people inside it. So was the mine's administrative building. So were houses, farms, a railway bridge, and a small bed and breakfast in the village of Vila Ferteco, a kilometer down the valley. Two hundred and seventy people died - 272, as their families count them, including two unborn children. Most of them had been at work.

## The People Who Did Not Come Home

The dam had been inactive for several years. It no longer received fresh tailings. In the National Mining Agency's registry, it was classified as a small structure with low risk but high potential for damage. On paper, it was almost forgotten - a closed chapter in the long history of iron extraction in Minas Gerais. But paper does not hold back a mountain of saturated mud. Inside the cafeteria were mine employees, contractors, cooks, cleaners. Outside, in the administrative buildings, engineers and clerks. Down the valley, families in their homes. Guests and staff at the bed and breakfast, where at least seven people died. Of the 270 who died, 259 had been found and identified by the first anniversary; eleven were still missing. Firefighters kept digging through the mud for years. When they ended the search seven years after the collapse, two people had never been found.

## Warnings That Went Unheeded

The union treasurer called it premeditated. That is a strong word, but it reflected a feeling widespread among the workers: the dam had been trouble, and many had said so. Sensors designed to monitor the structure's integrity were known to be faulty. Complaints about stability had circulated for years. And the German inspection firm TUV Sud had certified the dam as safe only months before it gave way. The disaster also carried the weight of a recent parallel. In November 2015, a similar dam owned by Samarco - a joint venture of Vale and the Anglo-Australian BHP - had failed near the town of Mariana, killing 19 people and obliterating the village of Bento Rodrigues. After Mariana, Vale had vowed never again. Now, three years and two months later, it had happened again. Only this time the death toll was more than ten times higher, and the cafeteria full of workers made the loss feel especially cruel - people buried not on a remote hillside but at the center of their own workplace.

## The River Downstream

By 3:50 p.m. that afternoon, the mudflow had traveled more than five kilometers and reached the Paraopeba River. The Paraopeba supplies water to one-third of Greater Belo Horizonte, a metropolitan region of more than five million people. Metals carried by the tailings turned up in the river and its sediments. Water collection was suspended in the downstream communities of Brumadinho, Juatuba, and Pará de Minas. Twenty other municipalities registered some effect. The mud also ran through the valley of the Serra dos Dois Irmaos, an area of the endangered Atlantic Forest. Environmental groups warned of native vegetation smothered and wildlife killed, and of a long recovery. Two days after the main collapse, sirens wailed again. A second dam above the same site was showing signs of instability. About 24,000 residents of Brumadinho were evacuated in the early morning. Rescue teams were pulled back, and for several hours the search stood still.

## After the Mud

Vale's stock lost 24 percent in a single day - about US$19 billion of market value, the biggest single-day loss in the history of the Brazilian stock market. Brazilian courts froze three billion dollars of the company's assets. Brazilian prosecutors eventually charged Vale, its auditor TUV Sud, and sixteen individuals, including the company's former president Fabio Schvartsman, with intentional homicide and environmental crimes. The case moved to the federal courts. Schvartsman, who has said he was unaware of problems with the dam, had the charges against him halted by an appeals court, but in April 2026 Brazil's Superior Court of Justice reinstated him as a defendant; trial hearings in Belo Horizonte began in February 2026 and are scheduled to run into 2027, with no verdict yet. In February 2021, Vale reached an agreement with the state of Minas Gerais to pay reparations initially estimated at seven billion dollars. Israeli search and rescue teams had arrived within days of the collapse, 130 specialists working alongside Brazilian firefighters and naval divers. The Inhotim Institute, one of Latin America's largest open-air art centers, is in Brumadinho too. It was evacuated but not reached by the mud, and later reopened. For the families of the 270, reparations and agreements and stock prices meant less than the simple fact that people who had gone to work that morning had not come home. In the hills above the town, the earth still remembers.

## Flight Context

Located at 20.12 S, 44.12 W, approximately 30 km southwest of central Belo Horizonte in a straight line (about 60 km by road). The disaster site lies 9 km east of Brumadinho town in the iron-rich hills of Minas Gerais. Best viewed from 3,000-5,000 ft to see the scale of the tailings complex and the scar of the 2019 mudflow path toward the Paraopeba River. Nearest airport: Pampulha-Carlos Drummond de Andrade Airport (SBBH / PLU), about 35 km north-northeast. Tancredo Neves International (SBCF / CNF) at Confins lies about 57 km north-northeast.
