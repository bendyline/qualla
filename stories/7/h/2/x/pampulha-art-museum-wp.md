# Pampulha Art Museum

Oscar Niemeyer designed it as a casino in 1940 at age 33, launching one of the century's great architectural careers. Today the building holds contemporary art by a lakeshore in Belo Horizonte.

**Location:** -19.8518, -43.9737

**Categories:** modernist-architecture, art-museums, oscar-niemeyer, unesco-heritage, brazilian-art

## Introduction

Before Brasilia, before the UN headquarters in New York, before the curves that would define modernist architecture for half a century, a young Brazilian architect named Oscar Niemeyer was sketching a casino for a lake. The year was the early 1940s. The client was Juscelino Kubitschek, then mayor of Belo Horizonte, who had commissioned four buildings to ring the shore of a new artificial lake in the Pampulha neighborhood. The casino was the first one built - and it was the first major work of Niemeyer's life. He was in his thirties, collaborating with engineer Joaquim Cardoso on the structure, with landscape architect Roberto Burle Marx on the gardens. The partnership between Niemeyer and Kubitschek that launched here would eventually produce the planned capital of Brazil. But here, on the bank of Lake Pampulha, it produced a gambling hall. Today that building is the Museu de Arte da Pampulha, and the casino floor where bands once played for Rio's traveling gamblers now holds one of the country's most distinctive contemporary art residencies.

## The Mayor Who Would Be President

Juscelino Kubitschek was not yet the man who would build Brasilia. That came later. In the early 1940s he was the mayor of Belo Horizonte with an ambition the size of a lake, and the lake was the one he was constructing just north of the city. He wanted a leisure district around it - a place where Belo Horizonte could shed its mining-boomtown edges and become something more sophisticated. Four buildings, a single vision: casino, ballroom, yacht club, and church. For all of it he turned to Niemeyer. The collaboration set the two men on linked trajectories. Kubitschek would become governor of Minas Gerais, then president of Brazil, and in 1956 would commission Niemeyer to design a new capital city carved out of the empty central plateau. Brasilia was the outcome of a relationship that began on the shore of a lake in Belo Horizonte, with a casino.

## A Casino That Could Not Last

When the Cassino da Pampulha opened, it pulled players from across Brazil. The man running it was Joaquim Rolla, who also managed the Urca casino in Rio and the one at Quitandinha Palace in Petropolis. He brought international music shows to a city that had never seen anything like them, and the nightlife of Belo Horizonte was transformed overnight. The run was short. In 1946 Brazil banned gambling nationwide, and the whole Pampulha casino vision collapsed along with every other gaming hall in the country. The building sat waiting for a second purpose. Architects Sylvio de Vasconcellos and Celso Pinheiro, both from Minas Gerais, proposed converting it into an art museum. Vasconcellos became the first director. Pinheiro stayed on as chief conservator until 1965. The conversion was completed in 1957, when the building was briefly known as the Crystal Palace.

## Gardens, Sculptures, and What Survived

Roberto Burle Marx designed the external gardens - the same Burle Marx whose tropical modernist landscapes defined the look of modern Brazilian public spaces. Three sculptures still stand in those gardens: works by Alfredo Ceschiatti, August Zamoyski, and Jose Pedrosa. The Pampulha dam broke in 1955, a disaster that sent water into the museum's foundations and began long-term infiltration problems that would plague the building for decades. For seven decades the museum operated without major renovations - an extraordinary span for a structure designed as a casino and converted in a hurry for another purpose. In 2016, the Municipal Foundation of Culture announced the museum would close for a major overhaul, with an initial budget of R$4.2 million and a two-year timeline. The project has since stretched. An updated version was approved in May 2023, with no fixed reopening date. The building is part of the Pampulha Modern Ensemble, inscribed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site in 2016 - joining three other Niemeyer buildings that remained on the shore.

## The Collection and the Residency

The museum holds around 1,400 works. The highlight of the collection is a body of paintings by Alberto da Veiga Guignard, the German-born Brazilian modernist who spent his career in Minas Gerais and whose dreamlike landscapes anchor the permanent holdings. Alongside Guignard are works by Oswaldo Goeldi, Fayga Ostrower, Anna Letycia, and modernists including Di Cavalcanti, Livio Abramo, Bruno Giorgi, and Ceschiatti. Contemporary holdings extend to Antonio Dias, Frans Krajcberg, Ado Malagoli, Ibere Camargo, Tomie Ohtake, Ivan Serpa, Milton Dacosta, Alfredo Volpi, and Franz Weissmann. Since 2003, the museum has hosted Bolsa Pampulha, an artist residency program launched by curator Adriano Pedrosa - now one of the longest-running and most influential residencies in Brazil. Over two decades, Bolsa Pampulha has supported more than a hundred artists. Alumni include Cinthia Marcelle, Paulo Nazareth, Clara Ianni, Guerreiro do Divino Amor, Sallisa Rosa, Luana Vitra, and Wisrah C. V. da R. Celestino - names that now appear in international biennales and major museum exhibitions. The casino that launched one architect's career kept launching artists long after anyone was rolling dice.

## Flight Context

Located at 19.85 degrees south, 43.97 degrees west, on the southeast shore of Lake Pampulha in the northern part of Belo Horizonte, at approximately 2,800 feet elevation. Nearest airport: Belo Horizonte Pampulha (SBBH), essentially adjacent - a regional field only a few km from the museum. Confins International (SBCF) is the major field, about 40 km further north. Cruise altitudes of 5,000 to 8,000 feet offer excellent views of Lake Pampulha, the curves of Niemeyer's buildings clustered around its shore, and the sprawl of Belo Horizonte to the south.
