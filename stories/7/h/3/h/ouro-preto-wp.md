# Ouro Preto

Officially, 800 tons of gold left this Minas Gerais town for Portugal in the 18th century. Today it is frozen in baroque stone - Brazil's first UNESCO World Heritage Site.

**Location:** -20.3853, -43.5036

**Categories:** colonial-cities, unesco-heritage, minas-gerais, gold-mining, baroque-architecture, brazilian-history

## Introduction

Eight hundred tons. That was the official tally of gold shipped from this one Brazilian mining town to Portugal during the 18th century - and the word official is doing a lot of work, because it does not count the gold smuggled out, nor the gold that stayed in Brazil, nor the gold that was beaten into the leaf still gleaming inside the churches. It does not count what the enslaved African workers extracted at the cost of their lives. When the discovery of a gold-bearing stream at Itacolomi in 1695-96 set off Brazil's Gold Rush, this settlement - then called Vila Rica, 'Rich Village' - became the center of the largest gold boom the Americas had seen. By 1750 its population had climbed near 80,000 while New York held fewer than half that number. Then the gold ran out. Ouro Preto, now meaning Black Gold for the iron-oxide-coated ore found in its hills, became what happens when the world moves on and forgets to knock down your colonial buildings. Brazil's first UNESCO World Heritage Site is essentially a preserved crime scene of extraction, transformed by time into art.

## The Settlement That Became a State Capital

Manuel Borba Gato discovered gold at Rio das Velhas in 1695, and a year later another gold stream was found at Itacolomi. In 1697 the Portuguese crown established the Estrada Real - the Royal Road - reaching from the coast through what would become Ouro Preto onward to Diamantina. A network of small settlements consolidated into the municipality of Villa Rica in 1711, which the crown made the capital of Minas Gerais from 1720 onward. The name Ouro Preto was formally adopted on 20 May 1823, the year after Brazilian independence. By then the town had already been the capital for over a century, would remain so until 1897 - when the state government finally moved to the newly planned city of Belo Horizonte, a location better suited to administration than this steep valley pinned between the Serra do Espinhaco ridges.

## The Art That Gold Made

Extraordinary wealth attracted extraordinary ambition. Vila Rica became a city where the intelligentsia of Europe came looking for patronage, and where a distinct Brazilian baroque flowered - the Barroco Mineiro. The sculptor Antonio Francisco Lisboa, known as Aleijadinho, worked here producing some of the most striking religious art in the Americas despite the degenerative disease that eventually cost him the use of his hands and feet. The painter Manoel da Costa Ataide decorated ceilings. The composer Lobo de Mesquita wrote sacred music. The poet Tomas Antonio Gonzaga made Vila Rica a literary center as well. At its peak, with 100,000 inhabitants, Vila Rica was the largest city in Brazil. The Escola de Minas - the Mining School - was founded here in 1876 and remains a foundation of Brazilian mineral science. The School's Mineralogy Museum on Tiradentes Square holds crystals and gemstones of such value that no cameras are allowed inside.

## The Revolt and What Happened to Tiradentes

In 1789, Ouro Preto became the birthplace of the Inconfidencia Mineira - the Minas Gerais Conspiracy - a failed attempt to break Brazil free of Portuguese rule. The leading figure was Joaquim Jose da Silva Xavier, known as Tiradentes, a junior officer and sometimes dentist who had watched the region's gold flow past toward Lisbon and decided the arrangement was unjust. The revolt was betrayed before it could begin. Tiradentes was arrested, tried, and hanged in 1792. Portuguese authorities then dismembered his body and displayed the pieces along the road between Rio de Janeiro and Ouro Preto as a warning to other would-be revolutionaries. His head ended up in what is now Praca Tiradentes, the central square. The warning did not hold. Brazil declared independence in 1822 anyway. In 1823 the former Vila Rica received the title of Imperial City from Emperor Dom Pedro I, and Tiradentes has since been celebrated as one of Brazil's founding martyrs. The Museu da Inconfidencia, in what was once the municipal palace on Tiradentes Square, traces the movement's history.

## Mines, Minerals, and What Still Works

Former gold mines now offer tours. The Mina do Chico Rei is one of the best known. The Mina da Passagem - given to the United Kingdom by Portugal in the early 19th century for 100 years to help pay down sovereign debt - is the world's largest mine open to the public. The municipality contains about 10% of the 31,270-hectare Serra do Gandarela National Park, created in 2014. Mining is still a factor in the modern economy: Novelis, formerly Alcan, runs an important aluminum operation here. Vale S.A. has a presence. The hills yield iron, bauxite, manganese, talc, marble, and gemstones including imperial topaz - a stone found nowhere else on Earth. Soapstone handicraft items fill the shops in town. The street Carnival attracts thousands every year; the block Ze Pereira dos Lacaios, founded in 1867, is the oldest carnival block still active in Brazil.

## Living in a UNESCO Site

The resident population was 70,227 at the 2010 Census, with 56,293 in the urban area and just under 10,000 rural. Average elevation is 1,116 meters, and the highest point - Pico de Itacolomi - reaches 1,722 meters. The climate is tropical highland with hot humid summers and mild dry winters. Snow fell here in 1843, at least according to one report, and light frosts still happen in June and July. Modern urban development has to keep with the town's historical aesthetic - new construction must match the look of what is already there, or it cannot be built. The town hosts the Universidade Federal de Ouro Preto, with roughly 10,000 students spread across the historic center and its outlying districts. The republicas student housing system, 316 houses in all, gives Ouro Preto its unique rhythm: families who have lived here for generations, students who rotate through, tourists who come for the churches, and the ghost of the gold rush settling deeper into the valley every year.

## Flight Context

Located at 20.39 degrees south, 43.50 degrees west, at approximately 3,800 feet elevation. Pico de Itacolomi stands at 5,650 feet nearby - a useful landmark visible across the Iron Quadrangle. Nearest major airport: Belo Horizonte Confins (SBCF), about 100 km northwest. Cruise altitudes 8,000 to 12,000 feet offer views of the rugged Serra do Espinhaco ridges and the scattered colonial towns of Minas Gerais. The Estrada Real roads still trace historic routes visible from altitude in clear weather.
