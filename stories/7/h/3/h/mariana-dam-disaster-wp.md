# Mariana dam disaster

On 5 November 2015, a mining dam in Minas Gerais collapsed and sent a wave of toxic sludge through Bento Rodrigues toward the Atlantic. Nineteen people died, a river was killed, and the lawsuits continue a decade on.

**Location:** -20.2324, -43.4184

**Categories:** Vale S.A., Dam failures in Brazil, Mining disasters in Brazil, Tailings dam failures, 2015 disasters in Brazil, Minas Gerais

## Introduction

At 3:45 in the afternoon on 5 November 2015, a wall of iron-ore waste taller than a ten-story building gave way above Bento Rodrigues. The village of about 600 people sat downstream from the Fundao tailings dam and had no warning, no siren, no evacuation plan. The wave that came down the valley was the color of rust and moved like wet concrete. It killed nineteen people, erased the village, and began a 668-kilometer journey toward the Atlantic Ocean that would, seventeen days later, stain the sea brown at the mouth of the Doce River. It is still considered Brazil's worst environmental disaster, and a decade later the lawyers are still arguing about who owes what.

## A Village Called Bento Rodrigues

Before the mud came, Bento Rodrigues was an old Minas Gerais village, the kind with a whitewashed church and a soccer field and families who had lived on those hillsides for generations. It had a school. It had a bar. It had a cemetery where ancestors were remembered by name. When the dam above the village failed, the teachers at the school were the first heroes of that afternoon - they got the children out and onto higher ground before the building itself went under. Roads vanished. The only way in or out of what remained of Bento Rodrigues was by helicopter. About 600 people were evacuated to the nearby city of Mariana, where they slept on gymnasium floors and tried to understand that their homes - the concrete of them, the furniture, the family photographs - were now a layer in the sediment 40 kilometers down the Gualaxo River.

## The Volume You Cannot Picture

The dam released 43.7 million cubic meters of mine tailings. The number means little until you try to imagine it. Fifty million tons of iron ore waste, a United Nations report later calculated - the equivalent of 20,000 Olympic swimming pools of toxic mud. The sludge contained arsenic, lead, and mercury in concentrations the World Health Organization considers unsafe for any exposure. When it reached the Doce River at 6:30 that same evening, it turned a waterway that served 230 municipalities into a chemistry experiment no one wanted. Within four days the city of Governador Valadares had to shut its water intakes entirely. People stood in lines for bottled water delivered by the Brazilian Army. The river, locals said, had been killed.

## The Warnings That Were Ignored

What made the disaster a scandal was what came out afterward. In 2013, an internal report had flagged structural cracks in the Fundao dam. Joaquim Pimenta de Avila, one of Brazil's most respected tailings-dam engineers, had written in September 2014 that the dam needed a buttress - a reinforcement to prevent exactly the kind of liquefaction failure that would eventually occur. He had been designing that dam since 2006. His warnings were documented. In November 2025, a decade after the disaster, High Court judge Finola O'Farrell ruled in London that BHP Group was strictly liable for the collapse. 'The risk of collapse of the dam was foreseeable,' she wrote. 'It is inconceivable that a decision would have been taken to continue raising the height of the dam in those circumstances.' The dam had been raised anyway.

## The Mud Reached the Sea

Seventeen days after the collapse, the mudflow reached the Atlantic. Satellite images showed a brown plume spreading offshore at Regencia, staining water that had been blue for as long as anyone had been looking. By January 2016, fine particles had drifted south to the Abrolhos Marine National Park - the richest marine biodiversity in the entire South Atlantic, home to humpback whales that calve in those waters every winter. Scientists described staurozoan jellies dying on the seafloor. Fishermen from Espirito Santo hauled up nets filled with coated, suffocated catch. The Doce River's fishing cooperatives - generations of families who had made their living on the water - were effectively destroyed. Many of them are still trying to get compensation.

## The Long Aftermath

Samarco was owned fifty-fifty by Vale and BHP Billiton, the world's third-largest and largest mining companies respectively. Twenty-one executives were charged with manslaughter in 2016. Fines were issued in the millions, then billions. In October 2024, BHP and Vale signed an agreement with the Brazilian government to pay approximately $30 billion in total compensation - funding for resettlement, environmental restoration, and long-term support for affected communities. The Supreme Federal Court validated the deal in November 2024. But in a British court, the litigation continued. Pogust Goodhead filed on behalf of 235,000 Brazilian claimants in 2018; by 2023 that number had tripled. In November 2025, O'Farrell's ruling meant damages would be determined in a separate trial scheduled for October 2026. In 2019, in Brumadinho not far away, another Vale tailings dam would fail and kill 270 people. The pattern had not been broken.
