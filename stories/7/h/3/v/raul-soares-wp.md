# Raul Soares

A coffee-country municipality in Minas Gerais named for a state governor, but first populated by the Boachás who gave the local peak its name.

**Location:** -20.1019, -42.4528

**Categories:** Municipalities in Minas Gerais, Zona da Mata, Coffee producing regions of Brazil

## Introduction

Before the town was renamed for a governor, it was called São Sebastião do Entre Rios, 'Saint Sebastian Between the Rivers.' Before that, the land between the Matipó and Santana rivers belonged to a people called the Boachás, whose name survives on the nearby peak, Pico do Boachá. That is the order of erasure. A people first, a saint second, a politician third. Minas Gerais towns often carry this stratigraphy in their name histories, and Raul Soares wears its layers plainly.

## The Boachás and the Pioneers

The Boachás, the original inhabitants of these rich mountains between the Matipó and Santana rivers, are named in the local histories without elaboration. In January 1837, two brothers from Mariana, Domingos and Cassimiro de Lana, arrived from the west. The account in the town's records is blunt: they expelled the native population and took possession of the mountains. In 1841, the territory was sold to Francisco Alves do Vale, who moved in with his family. The Boachás had no place in this transaction. Their name survives only on the landscape, attached to a peak that still watches over the town their people once lived beneath. This pattern repeated across the interior of Minas Gerais in the 19th century, as waves of bandeirante-descended mineiros extended their reach into lands the state had never formally controlled.

## A Village Between the Rivers

On October 29, 1873, a wealthy man named João Pinto de Oliveira donated land. The village that grew from the donation was named São Sebastião do Entre Rios, for its location in the fork of two waterways. The town grew slowly, the way towns grew in the interior: a church first, houses around the church, a few shops, a market day. In 1902 São Sebastião became a district, later counted under the municipality of Rio Casca. Then on September 7, 1923, State Law No. 843 split it from Rio Casca as the seat of a new municipality, initially called Matipó. A consolidation council governed until a mayor and city council could be properly elected.

## Renaming for a Governor

In 1924 a state law gave the new municipality another name, Raul Soares, after Raul Soares de Moura, then governor of Minas Gerais. The municipality was formally installed on January 20, 1924, under seven elected councilmen: Joaquim Milagres Sobrinho, João Domingos da Silva, Raphael Raymundo Coelho, José Maria de Souza, Francisco Costa Abrantes, Joaquim José da Silveira, and Carlos Gomes Brandão. Coelho, as the council's chairman, performed both executive and legislative duties, because the office of mayor did not yet exist locally. January 20 is celebrated as the town's emancipation anniversary. It also happens to be the feast day of São Sebastião, the patron saint for whom the original settlement was named. The name Matipó did not disappear; it lives on in a nearby town that is now a municipality of its own. In 1925 the town was raised to city status as Raul Soares.

## Coffee Country

Raul Soares sits in the Zona da Mata, the forested eastern region of Minas Gerais that is coffee country, and that is what the municipality does. Coffee from these hills is exported, along with whatever industrial output the town's small manufacturing base produces. The city has been the headquarters of a manufacturing company named for its patron saint. The population of the municipality, 23,423 at the 2022 census, is distributed across the main town and a ring of villages: Bicuíba, Cornélio Alves, Granada, Santana do Tabuleiro, São Vicente da Estrela, São Sebastião do Óculo, and Vermelho Velho. The terrain is hilly, the roads are winding, and the villages hold onto a rural character that this corner of Minas Gerais has largely preserved despite the century's industrialization.

## Waterfalls and Old Stations

For visitors, the landscape offers ample compensation. Unstructured waterfalls, meaning ones without tourist infrastructure, fall at Emboque, Tarza, and Prefac. Lagoa do Emboque, a small lake, sits amid the hills. Morro do Tico Tico and Pico do Boachá rise above the surrounding country; the latter preserves the indigenous name the colonists almost but not quite erased. The Cultura Square holds the former train station, a monument to the decades when rail connected these mountain towns before the highway arrived. The municipal heritage includes the Santuário São Sebastião, the shrine to the town's namesake saint, and the Praça do Coreto with its bandstand. Sítio Dona Inácia, a preserved farmstead, and the commercial fishing ponds of Fundaça round out the options. It is a town where tourism is local rather than international, where the visitors are mostly people from neighboring municipalities looking for a weekend by a waterfall.

## Flight Context
