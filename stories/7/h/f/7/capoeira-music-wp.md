# Capoeira Music

Born from the survival strategies of enslaved Africans in Brazil, capoeira music turned a combat game into a circle of song, drum, and steel-stringed bow.

**Location:** -17.6319, -41.7378

**Categories:** Afro-Brazilian culture, Brazilian music, Capoeira, Martial arts music, Intangible cultural heritage

## Introduction

On December 5, 1820, a man named Mathias Benguela received 200 lashes. His offense was playing a drum. He was enslaved in Brazil, where drumming could bring severe punishment. Two years before that, in 1818, another man - Joao Angola - had been arrested merely for possessing a small drum at a similar gathering. In 1833, the playing of African drums in Rio de Janeiro was outlawed altogether. These are the facts that open the story of capoeira music, because capoeira music began as an act of resistance by people whose music was policed, punished, and criminalized by the state that held them in bondage. The music and the martial art are inseparable. Capoeira is an Afro-Brazilian practice - a fight often described as disguised as a dance, developed among enslaved Africans and their descendants in colonial and imperial Brazil, played as a game in a circle called a roda, accompanied by singing and percussion. What we now hear at rodas around the world - the twang of the berimbau, the snap of the pandeiro, the slap of the atabaque - is the sound of something that survived.

## Roots in Africa and the Ngoma Drum

The Bantu peoples of Central Africa, among whom capoeira appears to have its roots, believed that music could communicate between worlds - between the living and the ancestors, between the material and the spiritual. The big conga drum, called ngoma in Kikongo, played a central role. In the Congo it transmitted messages to warriors in a language they understood. Carried across the Atlantic by enslaved Africans, the ngoma tradition lives on in the atabaques of Brazilian Candomble. Its religious weight stayed intact: the atabaque was not just an instrument but a way of calling the orixas, the deities of West African origin, into the room. When capoeira players struck up a rhythm, the spiritual memory traveled with them. Early nineteenth-century travelers described capoeira as a war dance, accompanied by drumbeats and hand-clapping. An 1824 illustration by the German artist Rugendas shows a player in the roda beating a drum. Before the authorities clamped down, this was the heart of the music: a big drum, a circle of bodies, a song.

## The Berimbau Arrives Late

The instrument most people now associate with capoeira - the single-string bow with a gourd resonator called the berimbau - arrived in the game relatively late. The berimbau was already widespread in Brazil as a solo folk instrument, used for song accompaniment and for worship in Candomble. But it was not used in capoeira rodas until the early twentieth century, when it began to appear in Bahia. The transition may have had a practical logic as well as a musical one. According to one theory, berimbaus could be fitted with metal blades at the top - turning them into defensive weapons in a time when capoeira was outlawed and playing it openly was dangerous. Brazil's 1890 penal code made capoeira a crime, and it was not decriminalized until the late 1930s. Before metal wire was widely available, berimbaus were strung with plant fibers and could not project much sound. Once brass and steel wire became common - the modern berimbau is often strung with wire culled from used car and bicycle tires - the instrument could carry above a crowd. It took over the roda. Today the berimbau presides. It sets the tempo, calls the games to start and stop, suggests variations in the movement styles of the players. The lead berimbau player is effectively the director of the roda.

## The Shape of a Song

A traditional capoeira roda unfolds in a specific order. It opens with the ladainha, a litany - a solo song sung by the most senior player present, usually the one holding the lead berimbau. Ladainhas teach moral lessons, tell stories, invoke mythology, and range from two lines to twenty or more. The singer calls out Ieeeeeee - a drawn-out cry pronounced YaaaaaY - to bring the roda to order. The ladainha ends with Camara or E hora, hora - It is the hour. This is the cue for the louvacao, a call-and-response section praising God, the mestres, and capoeira itself. Then the corridos begin - shorter songs with overlapping call-and-response, the form African music is known for, borrowed in Bahia from the samba de roda tradition. Two players crouch at the pe-de-berimbau, the foot of the bow, and when the corrido starts, they spring up and begin to play - the movements half-dance, half-combat, always conversing. Mestre Bimba, who in the 1930s created the Regional style and helped bring capoeira above ground, replaced the ladainha in some schools with quadras - solo verses built from four-line stanzas. The full standardized instrument configuration - three berimbaus, two pandeiros, one agogo, one reco-reco, and one atabaque - probably did not settle until the 1960s. Mestre Pastinha, the great keeper of the older Angola tradition, formalized the orchestra and experimented with guitars and even Spanish castanets in the roda.

## Why It Still Matters

Capoeira music is rhythmically complex in ways that reward long listening. The atabaque keeps the pulse - open tone, bass, open tone, with an anticipation leading back into the bar. The agogo, a double bell tuned to a fourth or fifth, lays down a repeating pattern of low and high strikes. The reco-reco scrapes. The pandeiro has more freedom, ornamenting the beat. Above all this, the berimbau plays toques - rhythmic patterns with names like Angola, Sao Bento Pequeno, and Sao Bento Grande. Angola leaves a silence on the third beat that gives the music its characteristic lilt. Sao Bento Grande pushes forward like a driving march. The language of the lyrics mixes Portuguese with surviving words of Kikongo and Yoruba. The syncopation is subtle by Brazilian standards - more a shifting conversation between the lyrics and the isorhythmic cells than the exuberant cross-rhythms of samba. In today's capoeira outside Brazil, where students often do not speak Portuguese, newer students often hesitate to sing or play and favor flashy kicks and acrobatics, letting the music drift into the background. Many capoeira teachers from the tradition will tell you this is the wrong trade. Without the song, the game loses its center. Without the circle of voices, the roda is only exercise. The music is what makes the art what it is. It is also what made it survive.

## Flight Context

The coordinates (17.63 S, 41.74 W) mark the small town of Ladainha in northeastern Minas Gerais, which shares its name with the capoeira litany; capoeira music itself is practiced across Brazil, with long-established traditions in Bahia (Salvador), Pernambuco (Recife) and Rio de Janeiro. For flight over the cultural heartland: Salvador-Deputado Luis Eduardo Magalhaes (SBSV / SSA) puts you in Bahia, where Mestre Bimba and Mestre Pastinha formalized the modern styles. Rio-Galeao (SBGL / GIG) serves the Rio tradition. Ladainha is nearest Teofilo Otoni (SNTO / TFL), about 37 km southeast, and Governador Valadares (SBGV / GVR), about 145 km south. Capoeira is now practiced worldwide, and UNESCO inscribed the capoeira circle on its list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2014.
