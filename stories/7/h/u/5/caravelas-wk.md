# Caravelas

A quiet coastal town on the southern Bahia coast where mangroves meet the Atlantic and boats depart for the Abrolhos archipelago, a humpback whale nursery.

**Location:** -17.7319, -39.2658

**Categories:** Coastal places in Bahia, Abrolhos archipelago, Mangroves of Brazil, Whale watching destinations

## Introduction

Caravelas is a place you go through on your way somewhere else. The town itself sits on the southern Bahia coast, small and unassuming, a few streets around a bus station, a river threading out through mangroves to the sea. Most travelers arrive with a different destination in mind. About seventy kilometers offshore, rising from the warm South Atlantic, lies the Abrolhos archipelago - five islands of volcanic and sedimentary rock and the coral-studded shallows around them, where humpback whales come every winter from Antarctica to breed and calve. Between July and November the water fills with thirty-ton animals and their young. Caravelas is the port you depart from, the town where you sleep the night before the boat leaves at dawn. But those who linger discover something else: a mangrove estuary intact enough that you can step into a small boat and travel through a forest of roots, past crab burrows and oyster clusters, into a silence that even the cicadas respect.

## Getting Here Is Half the Story

The roads tell you what to expect. Come from the north on BR-101 and you turn off at Teixeira de Freitas for the last stretch to the coast. Come from the south and the choice depends on your vehicle. Drivers with a sturdy four-wheel drive have long taken the shortcut via Juerana village - seventy to eighty kilometers that maps showed as asphalt but that travelers reported as mostly rough dirt, so slow that it felt several times longer. In a regular car, you stay on BR-101 to Teixeira de Freitas and put up with the potholes. Either way, you watch for pedestrians. People cycle between towns here, not always with attention to the road. Farm animals cross whenever they please. Blind bends are genuinely dangerous, and at night the risk doubles. Small-town Brazil demands patience from its visitors. Caravelas rewards the patient.

## The Town and Its River

Once you arrive, your car becomes decoration. The town is small enough that five minutes of walking takes you most places you need to go. The bus station sits in the heart of it. Small pousadas and hotels cluster in a few blocks. Local bars serve cold beer to locals who are perfectly happy to make room for a visitor. The Caravelas River runs salt water past the town - an estuary rather than a freshwater river, which is why it is full of shrimp, crab, oyster, and fish that can be caught in the morning and eaten by afternoon.

## Into the Mangroves

This is the reason to stay. Local guides will take you out in a small fishing boat for the day. The estuary and its arm, the Caribe, wind through a forest of mangrove trees, their roots arching out of the water like frozen tentacles. Oysters cluster on the roots at low tide. Crabs emerge from holes. Tiny fish swim around the boat in clouds. If you swim - and you should, but wear a T-shirt because the little fish nibble - never set your feet on the bottom. After low tide the oysters hide under shallow water, and stepping on them will cut you badly. You float. You drift. You let the current take you through the trees. Along the banks live people known as ribeirinhos, river people, who have worked out a way of living on these waters that has changed slowly over generations. They are welcoming. If a guide takes you to visit, take the visit seriously.

## The Whales at Abrolhos

The Abrolhos Marine National Park, established in 1983 as Brazil's first marine national park, sits about seventy kilometers offshore. The name, traditionally said to come from abra os olhos - open your eyes - warns sailors of the reefs that have been wrecking ships here for 500 years. Every Southern Hemisphere winter, between July and November, humpback whales migrate north from their Antarctic feeding grounds to breed and give birth in these warm shallow waters. The babies are born and taught to swim and breathe before the long journey south. You can see them from the boats - fluke slaps, breaches, whole whales clearing the water and crashing back down, the mothers nudging their calves up for air. Visitors may land only on Siriba, one of the five islands, and the reefs are protected. Day boats leave from Caravelas, head out at dawn, return in the late afternoon with everyone sun-tired and quieter than when they left. Trips to Parcel das Paredes, the largest reef bank of the wider Abrolhos, northwest of the islands toward the coast, can mean a night on a rocking boat - not for everyone, but for divers the shallow coral is exceptional. South of town the road dissolves again. North, past Porto Seguro, Ilheus and Morro de Sao Paulo, Bahia stretches toward Salvador.

## Flight Context

Located at 17.73 S, 39.27 W, on the southern Bahia coast of northeastern Brazil. Small coastal town at sea level. Caravelas Airport (SSCV / CRQ, formerly SBCV), about 9 km north, has had no scheduled airline service in years and its operating license was revoked in 2022. Teixeira de Freitas (SNTF / TXF), about 50 km west-northwest, and Porto Seguro (SBPS / BPS), about 145 km north, have commercial flights. For visits to the Abrolhos archipelago about 70 km offshore, boats depart from Caravelas. From 3,000-5,000 ft the mangrove system of the Caravelas estuary and the Caribe is strikingly visible, fingering inland from the coast. Best visibility in the dry season (April-September); humpback whale season July-November. Watch for scattered low clouds off the warm coastal waters.
