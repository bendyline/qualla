# Caravelas

A quiet coastal town on the southern Bahia coast where mangroves meet the Atlantic and boats depart for the Abrolhos archipelago, a humpback whale nursery.

**Location:** -17.7319, -39.2658

**Categories:** Coastal places in Bahia, Abrolhos archipelago, Mangroves of Brazil, Whale watching destinations

## Introduction

Caravelas is a place you go through on your way somewhere else. The town itself sits on the southern Bahia coast, small and unassuming, a few streets around a bus station, a river threading out through mangroves to the sea. Most travelers arrive with a different destination in mind. Seventy kilometers offshore, rising from the warm South Atlantic, lies the Abrolhos archipelago - five volcanic islands and the coral-studded shallows around them, where humpback whales come every winter from Antarctica to breed and calve. Between July and November the water fills with thirty-ton animals and their young. Caravelas is the port you depart from, the town where you sleep the night before the boat leaves at dawn. But those who linger discover something else: a mangrove ecosystem so intact that you can step into a canoe and travel through a forest of roots, past crab burrows and oyster clusters, into a silence that even the cicadas respect.

## Getting Here Is Half the Story

The roads tell you what to expect. Come from the north on BR-101 and you pass through Teixeira de Freitas before settling in for ninety kilometers more to Caravelas. Come from the south and the choice depends on your vehicle. A four-wheel drive can turn off and head toward Juerana village - eighty kilometers of genuinely bad road, the kind that maps describe as asphalt but that is asphalt for perhaps three of those kilometers. The rest is an off-road adventure that stretches seventy kilometers into three hundred of actual driving. In a regular car, you stay on BR-101 and put up with the potholes. Either way, you watch for pedestrians. People cycle between towns here, not always with attention to the road. Farm animals cross whenever they please. Blind bends are genuinely dangerous, and at night the risk doubles. Small-town Brazil demands patience from its visitors. Caravelas rewards the patient.

## The Town and Its River

Once you arrive, your car becomes decoration. The town is small enough that five minutes of walking takes you most places you need to go. The bus station sits in the heart of it. Hotels cluster in a few blocks. At Pousada dos Navegantes on Rua das Palmeiras, a friendly couple named Josanar and Ana run a cosy bed and breakfast and dispense local advice with the generosity of people who actually want their guests to have a good time. The Carenagem restaurant at the end of the main street, next to the gas station, is the place to eat. Local bars serve cold beer to locals who are perfectly happy to make room for a visitor. The Vicosa River runs salt water past the town - an estuarine river rather than a freshwater one, which is why it is full of shrimp, crab, oyster, and fish that can be caught in the morning and eaten by afternoon.

## Into the Mangroves

This is the reason to stay. A local guide named Sergio Freire - known to everyone as Serginho, who also runs a restaurant where he is the cook - will take you out in a small fishing boat for the day. The Vicosa and its tributary Caribe wind through a forest of mangrove trees, their roots arching out of the water like frozen tentacles. Oysters cluster on the roots at low tide. Crabs emerge from holes. Tiny fish swim around the boat in clouds. If you swim - and you should, but wear a T-shirt because the little fish nibble - never set your feet on the bottom. After low tide the oysters hide under shallow water, and stepping on them will cut you badly. You float. You drift. You let the current take you past trees older than the town. Along the banks live people known as ribeirinhas, river people, who have worked out a way of living on these waters that has changed slowly over generations. They are welcoming. If Serginho takes you to visit, take the visit seriously.

## The Whales at Abrolhos

The Abrolhos Marine National Park, established by Brazil in 1983, sits sixty to seventy kilometers offshore. The name, contracted from abra os olhos - open your eyes - warns sailors of the reefs that have been wrecking ships here for 500 years. Every Southern Hemisphere winter, between July and November, humpback whales migrate north from their Antarctic feeding grounds to breed and give birth in these warm shallow waters. The babies are born and taught to swim and breathe before the long journey south. You can see them from the boats - fluke slaps, breaches, whole whales clearing the water and crashing back down, the mothers nudging their calves up for air. The archipelago itself is closed to overnight visitors and most of the reef system is protected. Day boats leave from Caravelas, head out at dawn, return in the late afternoon with everyone sun-tired and quieter than when they left. Parcel das Paredes, a submerged reef complex further out, requires an overnight stay on a rocking boat - not for everyone, but for divers the shallow coral is exceptional. South of town the road dissolves again. North, past Ilheus and Porto Seguro and Morro de Sao Paulo, Bahia stretches toward Salvador.

## Flight Context

Located at 17.73 S, 39.27 W, on the southern Bahia coast of northeastern Brazil. Small coastal town at sea level. Caravelas Airport (SBCV / CRQ) is the direct gateway, with irregular service. Porto Seguro (SBPS / BPS) about 180 km north offers more reliable connections. For visits to the Abrolhos archipelago about 70 km offshore, boats depart from Caravelas. From 3,000-5,000 ft the mangrove system of the Vicosa and Caribe rivers is strikingly visible, fingering inland from the coast. Best visibility in the dry season (April-September); humpback whale season July-November. Watch for scattered low clouds off the warm coastal waters.
