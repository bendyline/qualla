# Battle of Abrolhos

On September 12, 1631, a fire in the open ocean 200 miles off Brazil ended an admiral's life and bought Iberian sugar another passage home.

**Location:** -18.0333, -38.6667

**Categories:** Naval battles, Dutch-Portuguese War, Eighty Years' War, Brazilian history

## Introduction

The name was a warning. Abre olhos, eyes open - Portuguese sailors had been saying it for a century before the battle, calling to each other as they passed the half-submerged rocks that stuck out of the sea two hundred miles off Brazil's coast. On September 12, 1631, eyes were more than open. They were watching fire leap across the deck of a Dutch flagship as a Spanish admiral directed musketeers to shoot at anyone trying to put it out. By nightfall, Admiral Adrian Pater was in the water, drowning. The Prins Willem was gone. A convoy of sugar merchantmen was on its way to Lisbon, and the Dutch West India Company had lost one of the most capable commanders it would ever send to Brazil.

## Sugar at Stake

The Eighty Years' War had been grinding on since the Dutch rebelled against Spanish Habsburg rule in 1568. By 1631 it had spilled into the Atlantic world - the Dutch West India Company had seized Pernambuco in 1630, taking Recife and Olinda, and was moving to consolidate control of the Brazilian sugar economy. Madrid responded by sending Antonio de Oquendo out of Lisbon on May 5, 1631, with roughly twenty men-of-war. His orders were twofold: reinforce Paraiba, Pernambuco, and Bahia with fresh troops, then escort a convoy of sugar merchantmen back to Portugal. To give the Dutch time to commit their fleet, Oquendo went first to Bahia and the Bay of All Saints. The feint worked.

## Admiral Pater's Calculation

Adrian Pater had 33 ships at Pernambuco. He took 16 to sea. His reasoning was arithmetic: he considered only 8 of Oquendo's ships battle-worthy, and a force of 16 Dutch warships would be enough. The calculation left 17 ships in port. It is the kind of decision that looks fine on paper and ruinous in retrospect. Pater sailed on September 11 and sighted the Iberian fleet the same evening, driven southeast by contrary winds toward the Abrolhos. His flagship was the 46-gun Prins Willem, one thousand tons. His vice-flagship was the 50-gun Geunieerde Provintien. He prepared for battle overnight. Oquendo prepared too. Each man had the other at last.

## Six Hours at Sea

Fighting began around mid-morning. Vice Admiral de Vallecilla's San Antonio opened fire on Thijssen's Geunieerde Provintien, which closed to board along with the Provincie Ultrecht. The lines tangled. The hulls crashed together. Smoke from the guns blinded the gunners themselves, who had to keep firing by feel. At around four in the afternoon a Spanish shot from Oquendo's flagship set fire to the Prins Willem. The blaze spread. Sailors broke from their stations to fight it. Oquendo, watching from across the water, ordered his musketeers to fire at anyone attempting to douse the flames. The Dutch flagship burned. Admiral Pater went into the water with a small number of survivors and drowned. At nearly the same moment the San Antonio broke up and sank by the stern, taking most of its crew. The Provincie Ultrecht sheered off in flames and later sank. Three ships and many hundreds of sailors - Spanish, Portuguese, Dutch - were gone.

## Strategic Victory

Tactically the battle was closer to a draw than a triumph. Ships were lost on both sides; the Dutch vice admiral Marten Thijssen inherited command and limped back to Recife on September 21 and 22, his fleet mauled but intact. What the Iberians gained was strategic. Oquendo landed his reinforcements at Barra Grande near Porto Calvo - though only about 700 of the 1,200 soldiers under the Neapolitan commander Giovanni Vincenzo de San Felice, Conde de Bagnuoli, actually reached Fort Arrail do Bom Jesus. The sugar convoy of twenty merchantmen continued to Lisbon. And the Dutch garrison at Pernambuco, shaken by the loss of its admiral and flagship, evacuated Olinda in November to concentrate its dwindling force around Recife. The Dutch held Brazil for another twenty-three years, but their grip loosened from here.

## What the Rocks Remember

The Abrolhos Islands are still there - volcanic outcrops at about 18 degrees south latitude, a marine national park now, a haunt of humpback whales rather than warships. The seabed around them holds whatever iron and oak did not rot away from the wrecks of the Provincie Ultrecht and the San Antonio. The name of the battle belongs to one of the strangest naval engagements of the seventeenth century - a fight between three empires on the open ocean, decided by a single shot that started a single fire, fought within sight of rocks whose name meant keep your eyes open. The sailors who drowned there were Dutch and Spanish and Portuguese. Many were pressed men, many were slaves on the Brazilian caravels, few had chosen the war that killed them. The abrolhos keep their eyes open still, and the water washes over everything.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 18.03 S, 38.67 W in the Atlantic Ocean about 70 km off the coast of Bahia state. The Abrolhos archipelago consists of five volcanic islands now protected as Brazil's first marine national park. Nearest airports are Teixeira de Freitas (SNTF) on the mainland. Visible landmarks from altitude include the coral reefs, the volcanic islands themselves, and the vast open Atlantic. Marine traffic remains active on the sugar and shipping lanes that still follow the old routes.
