# Battle of Abrolhos

On September 12, 1631, a fire on the open ocean off Brazil ended a Dutch admiral's life and bought Iberian sugar another passage home.

**Location:** -18.0333, -38.6667

**Categories:** Naval battles, Dutch-Portuguese War, Eighty Years' War, Brazilian history

## Introduction

The name was a warning. Abre olhos, eyes open - the Portuguese name for the half-submerged rocks and reefs that stick out of the sea some seventy kilometers off the coast of Bahia. On September 12, 1631, eyes were more than open. They were watching fire leap across the deck of a Dutch flagship as a Spanish admiral directed musketeers to shoot at anyone trying to put it out. Before the day was out, Admiral Adrian Pater had gone into the water and drowned. The Prins Willem was gone. A convoy of sugar merchantmen was on its way to Lisbon, and the Dutch West India Company had lost its admiral in Brazil.

## Sugar at Stake

The Eighty Years' War had been grinding on since the Dutch rebelled against Spanish Habsburg rule in 1568. By 1631 it had spilled into the Atlantic world - the Dutch West India Company had seized Pernambuco in 1630, taking Recife and Olinda, and was moving to consolidate control of the Brazilian sugar economy. Madrid responded by sending Antonio de Oquendo out of Lisbon on May 5, 1631, with roughly twenty men-of-war. His orders were twofold: reinforce Paraiba, Pernambuco, and Bahia with fresh troops, then escort a convoy of sugar merchantmen back to Portugal. He went first to Bahia and the Bay of All Saints, giving the Dutch time to ready their fleet.

## Admiral Pater's Calculation

Adrian Pater had 33 ships at Pernambuco, but he left about half of them in port. His reasoning was arithmetic: he considered only 8 of Oquendo's ships battle-worthy. He sailed from Recife at the end of August, and by the time he found the enemy, with two ships separated on the way, he had 16 warships. It is the kind of decision that looks fine on paper and ruinous in retrospect. On the evening of September 11 Pater sighted the Iberian fleet, which contrary winds and currents had driven southeast toward the Abrolhos. His flagship was the 46-gun Prins Willem, one thousand tons. His vice-flagship was the 50-gun Geunieerde Provintien. He prepared for battle overnight. Oquendo prepared too. Each man had the other at last.

## Six Hours at Sea

Fighting began around mid-morning. Vice Admiral de Vallecilla's San Antonio opened fire on Thijssen's Geunieerde Provintien, which closed to board along with the Provincie Ultrecht. The lines tangled. Pater's flagship steered straight for Oquendo's Santiago de Oliste; the Dutch held their broadsides until point-blank range, then fired and grappled. The smallest Portuguese galleon, Nossa Senhora dos Prazeres, was sunk under their guns. At around four in the afternoon a Spanish shot from Oquendo's flagship set fire to the Prins Willem. The blaze spread. Sailors broke from their stations to fight it. Oquendo ordered his musketeers to fire on the Dutch crew to hamper their firefighting. The Dutch flagship burned. Admiral Pater went into the water with a small number of survivors and drowned. At nearly the same moment the San Antonio broke up and sank by the stern, taking most of its crew. The Provincie Ultrecht sheered off in flames and later sank, and the Spanish Buenaventura was captured. Four ships and many hundreds of sailors - Spanish, Portuguese, Dutch - were gone.

## Strategic Victory

Tactically the battle was closer to a draw than a triumph. Ships were lost on both sides; the Dutch vice admiral Marten Thijssen inherited command and limped back to Recife on September 21 and 22, his fleet mauled but intact. What the Iberians gained was strategic. Oquendo landed his reinforcements at Barra Grande near Porto Calvo - though only about 700 of the 1,200 soldiers under the Neapolitan commander Giovanni Vincenzo de San Felice, Conde de Bagnuoli, actually reached Fort Arrail do Bom Jesus. The sugar convoy of twenty merchantmen continued to Lisbon. And the Dutch garrison at Pernambuco, shaken by the loss of its admiral and flagship, evacuated Olinda in November to concentrate its dwindling force around Recife. The setback was not fatal to the Dutch: by 1634 they held the coast from Rio Grande do Norte to Cabo de Santo Agostinho, and they kept Recife until 1654.

## What the Rocks Remember

The Abrolhos Islands are still there - volcanic outcrops at about 18 degrees south latitude, a marine national park now, a haunt of humpback whales rather than warships. Somewhere on the seabed of this stretch of ocean lies whatever iron and oak did not rot away from the wrecks of the Prins Willem, the Provincie Ultrecht and the San Antonio. The name of the battle belongs to one of the more unusual naval engagements of the seventeenth century - a fight between the Dutch Republic and the joined Spanish and Portuguese crowns on the open ocean, turned by a single shot that started a single fire, fought in waters named for rocks that meant keep your eyes open. The sailors who drowned there were Dutch and Spanish and Portuguese. The abrolhos keep their eyes open still, and the water washes over everything.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 18.03 S, 38.67 W in the Atlantic Ocean about 70 km off the coast of Bahia state. The Abrolhos archipelago consists of five volcanic islands now protected as Brazil's first marine national park. The nearest airport with scheduled flights is Teixeira de Freitas (SNTF / TXF), about 120 km west-northwest; Porto Seguro (SBPS / BPS) is about 185 km north-northwest. Visible landmarks from altitude include the coral reefs, the islands themselves, and the vast open Atlantic. The exact battle site is uncertain; the coordinates mark the Abrolhos, where the fleets were driven.
