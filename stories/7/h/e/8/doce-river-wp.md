# Doce River

The Sweet River has long fed steel mills and coffee country from the mountains of Minas to the Atlantic; in 2015, it carried something else entirely.

**Location:** -19.6558, -39.8148

**Categories:** Rivers of Espirito Santo, Rivers of Minas Gerais

## Introduction

The name means Sweet River in Portuguese, and for most of its 853 kilometers that description held true. The Doce forms where two smaller rivers, the Piranga and the Carmo, meet downstream of Ponte Nova, some 65 kilometers east of the historic city of Ouro Preto. From there it carves northeast through Ipatinga, bends wide around Governador Valadares, and turns southeast again past Conselheiro Pena on its run to the Atlantic at Regencia, in the municipality of Linhares in Espirito Santo. Along its course the river has always been a working waterway, feeding iron furnaces, washing coffee beans, providing drinking water to a basin of more than three million people. On November 5, 2015, it became something else: the artery of what is widely called the worst environmental disaster in Brazilian history.

## From the Mountains to the Sea

The headwaters emerge at roughly 1,200 meters in the foothills of the Mantiqueira and Espinhaco ranges, two of the great mountain systems of southeastern Brazil. Cool mountain streams gather into the Piranga and Carmo, which merge east of Ouro Preto and become the Doce. From that point, the river flows for 853 kilometers through some of the most economically productive land in Latin America. Its tributaries include the Piracicaba, Casca, Matipo, Caratinga-Cuiete, Manhuacu, Santo Antonio, and Suacui Grande within Minas Gerais, then the Pancas, Guandu, and Sao Jose as it crosses into Espirito Santo. Below the mouth of the Piracicaba it forms the eastern boundary of the Rio Doce State Park, one of Brazil's major protected areas, and part of its basin falls within the 3,562-hectare Augusto Ruschi Biological Reserve.

## The Economy of Water

The Doce basin is often said to host the largest steel-making complex in Latin America. Three of the five largest companies in Minas Gerais in the year 2000 - the steelmakers Belgo Mineira, Acesita (now Aperam) and Usiminas - have plants here. Vale, founded at Itabira in the basin in 1942 as Companhia Vale do Rio Doce, the Doce River Valley Company, took its name from the river. These conglomerates play a major role in Brazilian exports of iron ore, steel, and, through Cenibra, cellulose. Coffee grows in the surrounding hills on both the Minas and Espirito Santo sides; fruit pulp comes from Espirito Santo. By one estimate, fifteen percent of the entire Minas Gerais state GDP originates in the Doce basin, and Ipatinga alone produces 5.4 percent of that total. Ipatinga anchors the Vale do Aco Metropolitan Area, the Steel Valley, whose blast furnaces have lit the sky for decades.

## A Population in Retreat

An older IBGE yearbook put the Doce basin's population at about 3.1 million, 68.7 percent of them urban; the basin committee now counts more than 3.6 million people, about 70 percent urban. That concentration is not accidental. Rural areas and small towns have lost population steadily, in some cases up to 40 percent, in a rural exodus driven by industrialization. The major cities along or near the river are Timoteo, Coronel Fabriciano, Ipatinga, Governador Valadares, Colatina, and Linhares. Agriculture in the basin still produces dairy and beef cattle, pigs, coffee, sugar cane, fruit and vegetables, cocoa. Industry covers siderurgy, metallurgy, mechanical manufacturing, chemicals, food, alcohol, textiles, leather, paper, and pulp. Mining extracts iron, gold, bauxite, manganese, and precious stones. The variety suggests resilience; the depopulation suggests something else.

## November 5, 2015

The Fundao tailings dam sat above the small town of Bento Rodrigues near Mariana, Minas Gerais. It was owned by Samarco, a joint venture between the Brazilian mining giant Vale and the Anglo-Australian BHP Billiton, and it held back waste water from iron ore processing. On November 5, 2015, the dam collapsed. Iron-laden mud swept through Bento Rodrigues with no mass alert of any kind, killing 19 people and injuring about 50. The village, and Paracatu de Baixo further downstream, were destroyed. Tens of millions of cubic meters of mining waste flowed into the Doce River system, contaminating it with arsenic, manganese, lead and iron, and the current carried it downstream day after day through Minas Gerais and into Espirito Santo; Governador Valadares lost its water supply. Seventeen days after the collapse, on November 22, the brown mudflow reached the Atlantic Ocean near Linhares. In 2024 Brazil's Supreme Court ratified a R$170 billion reparation agreement, and in November 2025 London's High Court found BHP liable for the collapse; the legal, ecological, and human consequences continue to unfold a decade later.

## Three Climates, One River

Under the Koppen classification, the Doce basin holds three distinct climates. The tropical altitude climate with summer rains and cool summers prevails on the slopes of the Mantiqueira and Espinhaco ranges at the river's sources. A second variant of the tropical altitude climate, also with summer rains but hot summers, appears at the sources of the tributaries. The middle and lower sections of the Doce and its tributaries fall under a hot climate with summer rains. These gradients are subtle in the abstract but stark on the ground: cool upland forests give way to coffee country, then to the hot, drier valleys around Governador Valadares, and finally to the coastal plains where cacao grows.

## Flight Context

The Doce River runs roughly east from the Piranga-Carmo confluence near Ponte Nova (about 20.24 degrees S, 42.90 degrees W) to its Atlantic mouth at Regencia, Linhares (19.66 degrees S, 39.81 degrees W). Principal airports along its course include Belo Horizonte-Confins (SBCF), about 130 km west-northwest of the confluence, Governador Valadares (SBGV) in the middle basin, Ipatinga Usiminas (SBIP) in the Steel Valley, Linhares (SNLN), about 43 km northwest of the mouth, and Vitoria (SBVT), about 85 km southwest of the mouth. From altitude the river often reads reddish-brown, heavy with sediment from erosion and mining in its basin. Look for the industrial smokestacks of the Steel Valley cluster near Ipatinga.
