# Doce River

The Sweet River once carried iron and coffee from the mountains of Minas to the Atlantic; in 2015, it carried something else entirely.

**Location:** -19.6558, -39.8148

**Categories:** Rivers of Espirito Santo, Rivers of Minas Gerais

## Introduction

The name means Sweet River in Portuguese, and for most of its 853 kilometers that description held true. The Doce forms where two smaller rivers, the Piranga and the Carmo, meet near the historic city of Ouro Preto. From there it carves northeast through Ipatinga, bends wide around Governador Valadares, and turns southeast again past Conselheiro Pena on its run to the Atlantic at Linhares in Espirito Santo. Along its course the river has always been a working waterway, feeding iron furnaces, washing coffee beans, providing drinking water to cities with a combined population exceeding three million. On November 5, 2015, it became something else: the artery of the largest environmental disaster in Brazilian history.

## From the Mountains to the Sea

The headwaters emerge at roughly 1,200 meters in the foothills of the Mantiqueira and Espinhaco ranges, two of the great mountain systems of southeastern Brazil. Cool mountain streams gather into the Piranga and Carmo, which merge near Ouro Preto and become the Doce. From that point, the river flows for 853 kilometers through some of the most economically productive land in Latin America. Its tributaries include the Piracicaba, Casca, Matipo, Caratinga-Cuiete, Manhuacu, Santo Antonio, and Suacui Grande within Minas Gerais, then the Pancas, Guandu, and Sao Jose as it crosses into Espirito Santo. Along the way it forms the eastern boundary of the Rio Doce State Park, one of Brazil's major protected areas, and part of its basin falls within the 3,562-hectare Augusto Ruschi Biological Reserve.

## The Economy of Water

The Doce basin hosts the largest steel-making complex in Latin America. Three of the five largest companies in Minas Gerais in the year 2000, Companhia Siderurgica Belgo Mineira, Arcelor Mittal through Acesita, and Usiminas, are all located here. Companhia Vale do Rio Doce operates what the company has described as the largest open-pit mine in the world. These conglomerates dominate Brazilian exports of iron ore, steel, and cellulose through Cenibra. Coffee grows in the surrounding hills on both the Minas and Espirito Santo sides; fruit pulp comes from Espirito Santo. Fifteen percent of the entire Minas Gerais state GDP originates in the Doce basin, and Ipatinga alone produces 5.4 percent of that total. Ipatinga anchors the Vale do Aco Metropolitan Area, the Steel Valley, whose blast furnaces have lit the sky for decades.

## A Population in Retreat

According to the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics, about 3.1 million people live in the Doce basin, with 68.7 percent of them concentrated in urban areas. That concentration is not accidental. Rural areas and small towns have lost population steadily, in some cases up to 40 percent, as farming gave way to industry and younger generations moved to the city for work. The major cities along or near the river are Timoteo, Coronel Fabriciano, Ipatinga, Governador Valadares, Colatina, and Linhares. Agriculture in the basin still produces dairy and beef cattle, pigs, coffee, sugar cane, fruit and vegetables, cocoa. Industry covers siderurgy, metallurgy, mechanical manufacturing, chemicals, food, alcohol, textiles, leather, paper, and pulp. Mining extracts iron, gold, bauxite, manganese, and precious stones. The variety suggests resilience; the depopulation suggests something else.

## November 5, 2015

The Fundao tailings dam sat above the small town of Bento Rodrigues near Mariana, Minas Gerais. It was owned by Samarco, a joint venture between the Brazilian mining giant Vale and the Anglo-Australian BHP Billiton, and it held back waste water from iron ore processing. On November 5, 2015, the dam collapsed. Toxic iron-laden mud swept through Bento Rodrigues with almost no warning, killing at least 19 people and injuring more than 50. The village was effectively erased. About 40 million cubic meters of mining waste flowed into the Doce River system, where the current carried it downstream day after day through Minas Gerais and into Espirito Santo. Seventeen days after the collapse, the toxic brown mudflow reached the Atlantic Ocean near Linhares. It remains the largest environmental disaster in Brazilian history, and its legal, ecological, and human consequences continue to unfold a decade later.

## Three Climates, One River

Under the Koppen classification, the Doce basin holds three distinct climates. The tropical altitude climate with summer rains and cool summers prevails on the slopes of the Mantiqueira and Espinhaco ranges at the river's sources. A second variant of the tropical altitude climate, also with summer rains but hot summers, appears at the sources of the tributaries. The middle and lower sections of the Doce and its tributaries fall under a hot climate with summer rains. These gradients are subtle in the abstract but stark on the ground: pine-like forests on the cool upland slopes give way to coffee terraces, then to the hot, drier valleys around Governador Valadares, and finally to the coastal plains where cacao grows.

## Flight Context

The Doce River runs roughly east from its source near Ouro Preto (near 20.38 degrees S, 43.50 degrees W) to its Atlantic mouth at Linhares (19.66 degrees S, 39.81 degrees W). Principal airports along its course include Confins International (SBCF) near the headwaters, Governador Valadares (SBGV) in the middle basin, Ipatinga Usiminas (SBIP) in the Steel Valley, and Vitoria (SBVT) near the coast. From altitude the river is distinguishable by its persistent reddish tint, a lingering legacy of the 2015 disaster and of continuing mining sediment. Look for the industrial smokestacks of the Steel Valley cluster near Ipatinga.
