# Arantina

A town of 2,800 people in southern Minas Gerais that grew up around a railway junction, survived the railway's closure, and now holds its place in the hills between the Mantiqueira range and the Rio Grande.

**Location:** -21.9108, -44.2558

**Categories:** Municipalities in Minas Gerais, Atlantic Forest, Mantiqueira Mountains

## Introduction

Arantina came into existence because a railway stopped there. The Estrada de Ferro Oeste de Minas, the West of Minas Railroad, was meant to link the south of Goias with the port of Angra dos Reis in Rio de Janeiro state, and at one point in the hills of southern Minas Gerais it met another line heading east to Barra Mansa. Where the lines met, in the middle of the second half of the nineteenth century, a small settlement called Varzea do Paiol began to form around the Arantes railway station. A farmer named Juca (in IBGE's history, Joao) Pereira owned most of the land and kept a storehouse there for his corn. Ceramics manufacturing took root. The town grew. By 1963 it was its own municipality, emancipated from Bom Jardim de Minas. The rail link to Barra Mansa that made it possible is long defunct. The town of about 2,800 people remains, folded into a landscape where sixty percent of the ground is hill country.

## Rails in the Mantiqueira

The EFOM was a nineteenth-century engineering project of considerable ambition. Cutting a railway through southern Minas Gerais meant pushing cuttings and tunnels through the Mantiqueira foothills, bridging dozens of mountain streams, and contending with landslides during the rainy season. The line was meant to connect the interior of Brazil - the agricultural plateau of Goias - to the coast at Angra dos Reis, hauling coffee and cattle to a port where they could be shipped out. Where the main line ran north toward Rio Grande and the Barra Line peeled off toward Barra Mansa, the station at Arantes became a transfer point. The station gave its name to the district that became a district of Bom Jardim de Minas in 1943, and then to the municipality that was created from it twenty years later. The Barra line itself went defunct. Passengers drifted to buses, freight drifted to trucks. What remains in Arantina from the railway age is the grid of the town, the old station site, and the name.

## A Town That Fits in a Square Kilometer

The urban area covers just 0.37 square kilometers. Call it thirty-seven hectares - about the size of a decent vineyard or a suburban subdivision in a North American city. In that compact footprint live essentially all of Arantina's residents; only a fraction work and live in the rural stretches of the municipality. The rural land is mostly ranched or farmed. The urban core sits at 1,050 meters elevation, with the Serra do Caxambu reaching 1,379 meters on the municipal edge and the Capixingui stream dropping to 1,030 meters at the low point. Standing in the main square, you can look in any direction and see hills. They do not let up.

## Cold Winters and Slow Rains

Arantina's elevation gives it a climate different from what most people imagine Brazil feels like. The Koppen system categorizes it as Cwb - subtropical highland, the same classification as Mexico City or the Kenyan highlands. Winters are cool and dry, with July temperatures averaging 17.3 degrees Celsius and average lows of 9.8 degrees. Summers are mild and wet. January, the hottest month, averages 24.1 degrees. Annual rainfall is 1,413 millimeters, concentrated in the summer - December brings 260 millimeters, August barely 14. The dry winters and long dry spells in the rainy season mean brush fires are a recurring concern in the hills, especially in the rural zone, contributing to deforestation of what Atlantic Forest fragments still hold in the municipality. The air, some days in September, carries the faint smell of smoke from distant grass fires.

## What the Hills Still Carry

The vegetation is South American Atlantic Forest - mata atlantica - in its southern, cooler, highland variant. Where the hillsides have not been burned or cleared, the forest still holds stretches of canopy where tropical and temperate species meet, where Brazilian pines stand among ipe, Brazilian rosewood, angico and cedar, and the manaca-da-serra blooms purple and white. Much of this is gone from Arantina now. What remains is in the steeper folds and higher ridges, where cattle cannot easily graze and cassava cannot easily be planted. The Carnival of Arantina, held each year before Lent, is one of the town's signature annual events, drawing visitors from neighbouring towns to dance in the streets of a municipality most Brazilians have never heard of. The handicrafts, and the memory of the ceramics trade that sustained the town in its first decades, are another thread that has held.

## The Geography of Belonging

Administratively, Arantina has bounced between categories. For decades it belonged to the Microregion of Andrelandia, within the Mesoregion of South and Southwest Minas. Then, in 2017, the IBGE - Brazil's statistics institute - reorganized the country into Immediate and Intermediate Geographic Regions, and Arantina found itself reassigned to the orbit of Juiz de Fora, a much bigger city to the east. What does not change is what the old maps already knew: Arantina is on the northwestern shoulder of the Mantiqueira range, 323 kilometers south of Belo Horizonte, with Bom Jardim de Minas to the east, Andrelandia to the northwest, and Liberdade to the south. The hills, as always, make everything a little longer than it looks on the map.
