# Baependi

In the mountains of southern Minas Gerais, a town of nineteen thousand still wakes with the sun and sleeps with the stars.

**Location:** -21.9589, -44.8900

**Categories:** Municipalities in Minas Gerais, Historical towns, Estrada Real

## Introduction

Scientists came to Baependi to study sleep. What they found was a town that had quietly opted out of the modern night. In 2015 researchers reported that people in the countryside around this Minas Gerais town prefer to rise at 6:30 and sleep by 9:20, while those inside town push it only slightly later - 7:15 and 10:20. Artificial lighting exists here, of course. Electricity arrived long ago. But the rhythms of ancestral humans persist, which has made Baependi a living laboratory for questions our own cities stopped being able to answer.

## Gold Country, Backwards

The Paulistas came looking for gold in 1692. Antonio Delgado da Veiga, his son Joao, his uncle Miguel Garcia, and Captain Manoel Garcia Velho crossed the Serra da Mantiqueira from Taubate and found an open clearing the Tupi people called mbae-pindi - the open glade, though another reading makes Baependi 'water of the flattened thing'. Baependi grew from that crossing. It became a waypoint on the Estrada Real, Brazil's first real road, which ran from the mines of Minas Gerais to the port of Paraty where gold was loaded onto ships bound for Europe. For a while the town flourished on extracted wealth. Then the gold thinned out, the caravans slowed, and Baependi settled into the slower business of tobacco and cattle. Until the mid-1800s it was the tobacco center of the province. Then that faded too. The town kept going.

## Nha Chica's Church

Baependi has had a parish church since 1723, and its Holy Week processions have continued for more than two centuries. But the church that draws pilgrims is the one locals simply call Nha Chica - the Sanctuary of Our Lady of Conception, built and maintained by a devout Black woman whose name was Francisca de Paula de Jesus. She lived most of her life in a small house beside the church. Beatified by the Catholic Church, she remains a figure of devotion across southern Minas Gerais. The town also holds the parish church of Nossa Senhora do Montserrat from 1754, Nossa Senhora da Boa Morte from 1815, and the Rosario church from 1820 - a four-church ensemble that keeps Baependi's Holy Week processions vivid. Brass bands play, choirs sing, the passion is enacted again. The town remembers.

## 893 Meters Up

Baependi sits at 893 meters in the southern highlands, where the mountains push the air thin and the light sharp. The municipality stretches across 751 square kilometers of rivers, forests, and waterfalls, including nearly 40 percent of the Serra do Papagaio State Park. Quartzite comes out of the hills. Semi-precious stones are still worked by hand. And in workshops scattered across town, artisans make baskets and ornaments from bamboo, straw, corn husks, and coffee wood - crafts that travel to the markets of Sao Paulo, Rio, and Belo Horizonte while their makers stay put. The mountains that once brought bandeirantes hunting gold now keep the town partially enclosed from the currents that reshape cities elsewhere.

## A Laboratory for What We Lost

The sleep researchers were not romanticizing. They were working with a long-running family health study in the town, and compared its people with Londoners, whose rhythms had been thoroughly rewired by screens and streetlights. Baependi, with its rural surroundings and slower pace, offered something unusual: people who still slept when it got dark and woke when it got light. The difference between town and countryside - 45 minutes of later rising, an hour of later sleeping - was itself revealing. Electric light pulls humans into the night. Remove it, or reduce it, and we drift back toward the pattern our species evolved with. Whether the people of Baependi think of themselves as subjects of study or simply as people who go to bed on time is another question. The answer is probably the latter.

## The Town the Gold Left Behind

What remains in Baependi is what was there before the gold and what accumulated after. The mountains, the rivers, the 39.93 percent of a state park that falls within its borders. The parish churches with their Holy Week processions, their centuries-old routines. The craft workshops, the tobacco fields turned to pasture, the waterfalls that still draw visitors from the big cities down the mountain. The Paulistas kept a Tupi name for an open glade. They came for gold and mostly left when the gold did. Baependi stayed, and in staying became something more interesting than a boom town - a place that kept its own hours, kept its own devotions, and let the rest of Brazil rush past.
