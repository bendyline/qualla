# Fumaça

A Brazilian village named for the smoke of its own waterfall, hidden behind twenty kilometers of ruined road in the rainforest south of Rio.

**Location:** -22.2965, -44.3112

**Categories:** waterfalls, rainforest, rural brazil, adventure travel

## Introduction

The name translates as smoke, and that tells you almost everything. When Fumaça Waterfall drops through the rainforest canopy southwest of Rio de Janeiro, the spray rises in a pale column that looks like woodsmoke. The waterfall named the village, not the other way around. The village is barely more than a cluster of houses scattered along a stream, a few farmers, some cows that drift onto the last unpaved stretch of road as if the notion of traffic simply hasn't reached this part of Rio de Janeiro state.

## The Twenty Kilometers

Getting to Fumaça means committing. From the Via Dutra highway you exit at the Academia Militar das Agulhas Negras, the sprawling army base outside Resende, and turn onto a road that begins tamely enough. Twelve kilometers of tarmac run past farms and forest, and you start to think this is easier than advertised. Then the pavement ends. The next twenty kilometers twist through rainforest at a top speed of about 15 kilometers per hour unless you have serious ground clearance. There are no signs. There are no gas stations. There is nothing, really, except the railway line that at one point crosses above the waterfall itself, a thin iron thread slung across the gorge like a sentence finishing itself in midair. People who come here are advised to bring a second car. Breakdowns in this forest are not inconveniences. They are situations.

## The Fall Itself

The waterfall is the point, and the waterfall rewards the punishment of getting there. The river arrives at a ledge and simply leaves, dropping into a bowl of rock and forest where the water slows enough at the bottom to form a natural beach of pale sand. You can swim there. You can float out until the current gently reminds you that swimming back is on you. At the top of the falls, a narrow passage winds along the cliff to the lip of the drop, and here there are no railings, no fences, nothing between a tourist and the void. When the rocks are wet they become lethally slick. Flash floods follow rain that may have fallen the day before and miles upstream, arriving without warning. The view from the top, locals insist, is worth the risk. The locals are the ones who know where to stand.

## The Water You Can Drink

What distinguishes Fumaça from the many waterfalls of southeastern Brazil is the water itself. Natural mineral springs seep from the rock around the falls, and by local tradition you can drink from them directly, no filter, no treatment. Travel guides note the water is safe, with a caveat worth repeating: the mineral content is high enough that drinking too much of it will give you a mild stomach upset. Just over the mountains, southern Minas Gerais built a whole spa culture around springs like these. At Fumaça, you just cup your hands. The experience is old in a way that the bottled version is not.

## Life Off the Map

There is nowhere to sleep at Fumaça. You camp, or you drive the hour and a half back to Resende. There is nowhere to eat either, beyond a bar in the village that might be open and might not. The advice in every guide comes back to the same instructions: bring your own food, bring insect repellent, bring a map because the road signs never were. The village holds to its own rhythm of farming and quiet. Some visitors find this frustrating, a place that refuses to cater to them. Others find it exactly what they came looking for, which is Brazil without the gloss, a waterfall that still requires an expedition to reach, a place where the loudest sound on a weekday afternoon is the water itself and whatever forest birds choose to comment on it.

## What Neighbors Look Like

The nearest notable place is Penedo, a town founded by Finnish immigrants in the 1920s where you can still find rye bread and wooden churches in the middle of Brazilian rainforest. The Itatiaia National Park, Brazil's oldest, sprawls to the west with its own collection of waterfalls and peaks. Resende itself, gateway to all of this, is a working military town built around Brazil's equivalent of West Point. Fumaça sits in the overlap of these worlds: a small municipal nature park rather than a national one, not quite tourist economy, not quite reachable. The railway bridge above the falls reminds you that this gorge was once considered central enough to engineer through. Now it is a place people pass over, mostly literally.

## Flight Context

Located at 22.30°S, 44.31°W in the Serra da Mantiqueira foothills between the Paraíba valley and Itatiaia National Park. Nearest airport is Resende (SDRS), about 27 km southwest. Galeão International (SBGL) in Rio de Janeiro lies roughly 125 km east-southeast. The gorge that holds the waterfall is identifiable from cruising altitude by the railway trestle crossing above it. Surrounding terrain rises above 2,000 m in Itatiaia to the west.
