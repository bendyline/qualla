# Andrelandia

A Minas Gerais town of 12,000 in the Mantiqueira foothills that has been renamed six times and keeps returning to the name of Andre da Silveira, the farmer who asked for its first chapel in 1749.

**Location:** -21.7400, -44.3089

**Categories:** Municipalities in Minas Gerais, 1968 establishments in Brazil

## Introduction

It has been called Arraial do Turvo, Vila Bela do Turvo, Turvo, Porto do Turvo, and Turvo again. None of those names stuck. The one that did - Andrelandia - honors a farmer named Andre da Silveira, who in 1749 asked the Bishop of Mariana for permission to build a small chapel in a place called Turvo Pequeno, dedicated to Our Lady of Porto. The chapel was blessed in 1755. Houses sprang up around it. Two and a half centuries later, a town of about 12,000 people sits where that chapel stood, 300 kilometers south of Belo Horizonte, at a thousand meters elevation in the tangled hill country where the Serra da Mantiqueira breaks apart into ridges and stream valleys heading north toward the Rio Grande.

## Gold, Then Chapels

The Europeans arrived here chasing gold. By the mid-eighteenth century, the south of Minas Gerais was full of men who had come looking for fortune and stayed to farm. They received letters of sesmaria from the captaincy governor, locking down tracts of wasteland along the Turvo Grande River. The Congonhal region, on the right bank, was especially populous - its name from the abundance of a tree called congonha, whose leaves make a tea still drunk in rural Minas. Silveira's chapel anchored a small settlement that grew into the Arraial do Turvo. Religious life came fast: by 1827 the settlement was a parish, by 1864 a village, by 1868 a city. The first full wave of foreign settlers arrived later still. Lebanese immigrants came at the turn of the twentieth century. Italians - the D'Alessandro and Rivelli families - came too, along with Spanish arrivals named Laredo and Garrido and Portuguese Gaspars and Pereiras. Many had been drawn by the construction of the West of Minas Railroad, which reached Andrelandia in 1914.

## The Historian's Hometown

One resident has carried Andrelandia's name further than any railroad could. Jose Murilo de Carvalho, the Brazilian historian who spent his career untangling the politics of imperial and republican Brazil, was born here in 1939 and died in 2023. His books - on the construction of Brazilian citizenship, on the political elite of the Empire, on the evolution of Brazilian national identity - are standard reading in universities from Sao Paulo to Stanford. He became a member of the Brazilian Academy of Letters. For a town of 12,000 people in the Mantiqueira hills, producing one of the country's most important historians is not a small thing. It is the sort of fact a small town quietly treasures: the boy born here went on to write the books other Brazilians argue about.

## Festivals and Old Houses

What you can see now, walking the town, is baroque. Many of the old houses date from the town's early centuries and are listed as municipal heritage. Squares and churches preserve the style of the colonial clearing, when Minas Gerais was a frontier and every town needed a chapel to anchor it. The religious calendar fills most of the year: the Feast of Saint Sebastian, the Feast of Kings, Holy Week, the Feast of San Benedict, Corpus Christi, and in August the Feast of Our Lady of Porto - the patron saint whose chapel started everything. Rural tourism is a growing part of the economy, with old eighteenth-century fazendas opening their gates to visitors. The Congonheiras - a small group of descendants of early European settlers who lived near Bandeira Mountain - used to walk the streets selling produce from their small crops, and elders still remember them as the first street sellers in the city's markets.

## A Sea of Hills

The land does most of the talking in Andrelandia. About sixty percent of the municipality's thousand square kilometers is described in municipal records as a sea of hills and mountains; twenty percent is hilly; twenty percent is flat. The highest point is 1,535 meters at the Natureza range; the lowest is 934 meters along the Aiuruoca River. Town center sits at 1,000 meters, which gives it a climate that surprises visitors who think of Brazil as uniformly hot. Winters here are cold - frosts form in the lower valleys, morning breath fogs up - and summers, while warm and rainy, stay mild compared to the coastal lowlands. Rainfall exceeds 1,500 millimeters a year. December brings the most, and occasionally it falls as hail. On January 14, 2011, 183 millimeters fell in a single day, the heaviest burst on record.

## Where the Waters Go

Every stream in Andrelandia eventually heads north. The Rio Grande crosses the municipality on its way to the Parana basin, which runs all the way down to the Plata estuary between Argentina and Uruguay - which means rain falling on the hills above the town has its ultimate destination in the Atlantic, thousands of kilometers away, beyond Buenos Aires. The Capivari River is born in Andrelandia and flows out to the border with Santana do Garambeu. The Aiuruoca River, calmer and muddier, crosses on the other side, carrying the Turvo Grande and Turvo Pequeno rivers into the artificial lake of the Camargos Hydroelectric Plant. Nobody here grows old without knowing how the rivers lie on the land, because the rivers are why the town is where it is - why a farmer named his chapel for Our Lady of Porto, why the railroad came. The water runs north, and the town goes with it.

## Flight Context

Located at 21.74 S, 44.31 W in southern Minas Gerais, Brazil, 300 km south of Belo Horizonte and roughly 175 km northwest of Rio de Janeiro. Town center sits at 1,000 m elevation; surrounding terrain reaches 1,535 m at the Serra da Natureza. The Mantiqueira Mountains are immediately to the south; the Rio Grande flows just north of town. Nearest airports: Juiz de Fora (SBJF) to the east and Sao Lourenco (SNLO) to the southwest. Best viewed at 5,000-8,000 ft to appreciate the hill-country relief and the web of streams heading north toward the Rio Grande basin. Expect morning fog in the valleys year-round; winter dawns can see frost.
