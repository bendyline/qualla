# Baixo Guandu

The first city in Brazil to fluoridate its water, on the banks of a river whose name means sweet.

**Location:** -19.5189, -41.0158

**Categories:** Municipalities in Espirito Santo, Coffee towns, Rio Doce valley

## Introduction

In 1953 the small river town of Baixo Guandu became the first city in Brazil to add fluoride to its water supply. The target was childhood tooth decay. The method was public health science, set up by the federal public health service, FSESP. The result, eventually, was a model replicated across Brazil. It is a strange distinction for a municipality of about 31,000 in the Rio Doce valley - first at something most people do not think about - but it fits a place that has always moved ahead of expectation.

## The River and Its Name

Doce means sweet in Portuguese. The Rio Doce that runs through Baixo Guandu is not sweet in any literal sense - its basin of 83,500 square kilometers drains iron-stained hills and feeds 222 municipalities before emptying into the Atlantic. But the name stuck. Long before Portuguese travelers arrived, these banks belonged to the peoples the colonists called Botocudo - an outsiders' name, from botoque, the wooden discs some of them wore in their lips and ears; some called themselves Nac-nanuk, "sons of the soil." They were, according to the accounts of bandeirantes and nineteenth century travelers, the masters of the region between the Rio Doce and the Sao Mateus River - the last Indigenous peoples to hold ground here before colonial frontier wars, waged around 1790-1820 with deliberate brutality, drove them from the coastal districts.

## Italians with a Boat to Catch

Francisco Vieira de Carvalho Milagres wanted workers for his land. In 1866 he wrote to a friend named Guilherme Frederico announcing the departure from Genoa of forty Italian contract workers. On June 5 of that year, forty Italians left Rio de Janeiro for his lands on the Rio Doce. By 1894 Milagres was back in Europe recruiting again - the immigrants arrived aboard a vessel called Matteo Bruzzo, reached Espirito Santo in December, and made their way up the Rio Doce. Their descendants still live here. The colonization waves that followed - Italians again, Germans, French, Spanish - shaped the valley in ways that are still audible in family names. In 1910 Francisco da Cunha Ramaldes, a farmer from Minas Gerais, planted coffee in the mountains of Alto Mutum Preto. The plantation prospered. The coffee still does.

## Rails and Iron

The first train reached Baixo Guandu in 1907, and with it came the removal of the old-growth forests that had covered the valley. Wood went out by rail to the capital. The Vitoria-Minas Railway still runs through town - a 905-kilometer line carrying iron ore from the Iron Quadrangle of Minas Gerais to the Port of Tubarao on the Atlantic. In 2006 the line also carried 1.1 million passengers; its daily passenger train is one of Brazil's last long-distance services, a rare survival in a country that largely abandoned passenger rail. The trains pass daily, loaded with ore, and the sound of them is part of the town's weather. The rails were the reason the first major industries came. They remain the reason Baixo Guandu sits on a main artery of Brazilian commerce rather than at the end of a dirt road.

## The Dam and the Fluoride

In 1974 the Mascarenhas hydroelectric plant, still the second-largest in Espirito Santo, was inaugurated on the Rio Doce at Baixo Guandu - a reinforced concrete dam 45 meters tall and 539 meters wide, holding back 39.5 million cubic meters of water to power both Espirito Santo and neighboring Minas Gerais. Two decades earlier the town had made a different kind of engineering choice. In 1953 Baixo Guandu added fluoride to its municipal water to reduce cavities, especially in children. The decision was modest in scale and enormous in implication. Other cities followed - Marilia in 1956, Curitiba, the first state capital, in 1958 - and in 1974 a federal law made fluoridation compulsory wherever water was treated. Millions of Brazilian children with sounder teeth owe a small debt to a river-valley municipality that tried something new when trying something new was unusual.

## Coffee from the High Hills

What Baixo Guandu sells the world now is coffee from Alto Mutum Preto. The mountains behind the town climb toward 900 meters, and the altitude slows ripening, concentrates sugars, sharpens acidity. Many of these farms have passed down through the generations of immigrant families. The town itself, 186 kilometers by road from Vitoria and 499 from Belo Horizonte, remains small - the kind of place where the main church, the Matriz Church of Sao Pedro, anchors the central square and the river does most of the rest of the work. Trains pass. Coffee leaves. The water is fluoridated. The town keeps its quiet distinction.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 19.52 S, 41.02 W in western Espirito Santo state at about 77 meters elevation in the Rio Doce valley. The town sits on the border with Minas Gerais. The Baixo Guandu-Aimores airstrip (SNBG) lies about 3.5 km northwest; the nearest major airports are Vitoria (SBVT), about 110 km southeast, and Belo Horizonte-Confins (SBCF), about 310 km west. Visible landmarks include the Rio Doce winding through the valley, the Mascarenhas dam, the Vitoria-Minas Railway, and the coffee mountains of Alto Mutum Preto about 30 km to the north-northeast.
