# Baixo Guandu

The first city in Brazil to fluoridate its water, on the banks of a river whose name means sweet.

**Location:** -19.5189, -41.0158

**Categories:** Municipalities in Espirito Santo, Coffee towns, Rio Doce valley

## Introduction

In 1953, while most of Brazil was still boiling drinking water or praying it was clean, the small river town of Baixo Guandu became the first city in the country to add fluoride to its water supply. The target was childhood tooth decay. The method was public health science. The result, eventually, was a model replicated across Brazil. It is a strange distinction for a town of 31,000 in the Rio Doce valley - first at something most people do not think about - but it fits a place that has always moved ahead of expectation.

## The River and Its Name

Doce means sweet in Portuguese. The Rio Doce that runs through Baixo Guandu is not sweet in any literal sense - its basin of 83,500 square kilometers drains iron-stained hills and feeds 222 municipalities before emptying into the Atlantic. But the name stuck from the earliest Portuguese travelers, who found the water drinkable after the salt crossings of the coast. The Botocudo people had known these banks for centuries before those travelers arrived. The anthropologist's term comes from the wooden lip plugs some of them wore. They were, according to the accounts of bandeirantes and nineteenth century travelers, the masters of the region between the Rio Doce and the Sao Mateus River - the last indigenous peoples to hold ground here before settlers pushed in.

## Italians with a Boat to Catch

Francisco Vieira de Carvalho Milagres wanted workers for his land. In 1866 he wrote to a friend named Guilherme Frederico announcing the departure from Genoa of forty Italian contractors. Days later another forty Italians shipped from Rio de Janeiro on June 5. By 1894 Milagres was back in Europe recruiting again - the immigrants arrived aboard a vessel called Matteo Bruzzo, reached Espirito Santo in December, and made their way up the Rio Doce. Their descendants still live here. The colonization waves that followed - Germans, French, Spanish - shaped the valley in ways that are still audible in family names and visible in the farmhouses of Alto Mutum Preto, where Francisco da Cunha Ramaldes planted coffee in 1910 at elevations high enough to produce beans of striking quality. The plantation prospered. The coffee still does.

## Rails and Iron

The first train reached Baixo Guandu in 1907, and with it came the removal of the old-growth forests that had covered the valley. Wood went out by rail to the capital. The Vitoria-Minas Railway still runs through town - a 905-kilometer line carrying iron ore from the Iron Quadrangle of Minas Gerais to the Port of Tubarao on the Atlantic. In 2006 the line also carried 1.1 million passengers, a rare survival in a country that largely abandoned passenger rail. The trains pass daily, loaded with ore, and the sound of them is part of the town's weather. The rails were the reason the first major industries came. They remain the reason Baixo Guandu sits on a main artery of Brazilian commerce rather than at the end of a dirt road.

## The Dam and the Fluoride

In 1974 the state's largest hydroelectric plant at that time opened on the Rio Doce at Baixo Guandu - a reinforced concrete dam 45 meters tall and 539 meters wide, holding back 39.5 million cubic meters of water to power both Espirito Santo and neighboring Minas Gerais. Two decades earlier the town had made a different kind of engineering choice. In 1953 Baixo Guandu added fluoride to its municipal water to reduce cavities, especially in children. The decision was modest in scale and enormous in implication. Before long, cities across Brazil followed. The town rarely advertises this fact. But every Brazilian child with sound teeth owes a small debt to a river-valley municipality that tried something new when trying something new was unusual.

## Coffee from the High Hills

What Baixo Guandu sells the world now is coffee from Alto Mutum Preto. The mountains behind the town climb high enough that the beans grown there command premium prices - the altitude slows ripening, concentrates sugars, sharpens acidity. The Italian and German descendants who planted many of these farms have passed them down through four or five generations. The town itself, 186 kilometers from Vitoria and 499 from Belo Horizonte, remains small - the kind of place where the main cathedral, the Matriz Church of Sao Pedro, anchors the central square and the river does most of the rest of the work. Trains pass. Coffee leaves. The water is fluoridated. The town keeps its quiet distinction.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 19.52 S, 41.02 W in western Espirito Santo state at about 130 meters elevation in the Rio Doce valley. The town sits on the border with Minas Gerais. Nearest airports are Vitoria (SBVT) 186 km east and Confins (SBCF) 499 km west near Belo Horizonte. Visible landmarks include the Rio Doce winding through the valley, the Vitoria-Minas Railway, and the coffee mountains of Alto Mutum Preto to the south.
