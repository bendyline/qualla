# Campos Basin

A deepwater sedimentary basin off the Rio de Janeiro coast, Campos holds the oil history of Brazil - where offshore drilling first turned Brazil into a major oil producer.

**Location:** -22.4811, -34.8333

**Categories:** Campos Basin, Sedimentary basins of Brazil, Geology of Brazil, Oil fields of Brazil, Atlantic Ocean

## Introduction

In 1977, oil began to flow from a field called Enchova, in 124 meters of water off the Rio de Janeiro coast - the basin's first production, three years after its first discovery, Garoupa. The start was modest by global standards. It was not, for Brazil, modest at all. For decades the country had imported most of its oil, exposed to every shift in the world market. Now Petrobras had proof that something substantial lay beneath the continental shelf - proof that what began as seafloor spreading roughly 112 million years ago, when the ancient supercontinent Gondwana tore apart and the South Atlantic opened, had left behind reservoirs large enough to matter. The Campos Basin, a deepwater sedimentary basin running along the northern coast of Rio de Janeiro state, became the engine of Brazilian oil production for the next three decades. By 2003 it was pumping 1.21 million barrels a day, from 41 fields scattered 50 to 140 kilometers offshore in water depths from 80 to 2,400 meters. Petrobras developed nearly all of them, though it has since sold a number to other operators. All of them owe their existence to geology that was set in motion when dinosaurs still walked the Earth.

## How an Ocean Made an Oil Field

The story starts with a continent coming apart. Gondwana, the southern half of Pangea, began to rift in the Early Cretaceous, roughly 133 million years ago. The split moved northward from what would become Uruguay and Namibia. By 112 million years ago, in the Early Albian, seafloor spreading had reached the Campos region, and the Atlantic was born here as a narrow, restricted sea. Even before the sea arrived, rift lakes had laid down organic-rich shales - the source rocks that would eventually cook into oil under pressure. Above them, as the sea crept in, evaporite salts thickened in the restricted waters. Above the salts, turbidite sandstones settled as underwater avalanches carried sediment down the continental slope. The recipe is standard oil geology, but Campos happened to have every ingredient in abundant quantities. When Petrobras drilled, it found a stack of reservoirs - turbidites, fractured basalts, coquinas, limestones - each holding oil trapped in a different way.

## The Fields, Named for Fish

Brazilian geologists named the fields after sea creatures, and the naming has a poetry that surveys normally lack. Garoupa, the grouper - the first found. Enchova, the bluefish - the first to produce. Then Albacora the albacore, Marlim the marlin, Barracuda, Espadarte the swordfish, Roncador the croaker. Jubarte is the humpback whale. Cachalote is the sperm whale. The naming runs through Albian-Cenomanian marine carbonates and deepwater Late Cretaceous sandstones. Marlim, discovered in 1985 about 110 kilometers offshore in water 650 to 1,050 meters deep, was long the giant of the basin - a subsea complex of pipes and pumps and production vessels floating above the wells. By 2003 the basin had produced 3.9 billion barrels of oil cumulatively, with remaining reserves estimated at 8.5 billion. In February 2010, Petrobras announced a new 65-million-barrel find near Barracuda - proof that the geology still had surprises after three decades of drilling.

## The Sugarloaf Connection

When you stand in Rio de Janeiro and look at Sugarloaf Mountain rising from Guanabara Bay, you are looking at the same ancient rocks that underlie the Campos Basin. Sugarloaf is an inselberg - a hard-rock knob carved by erosion from the Aracuai-Ribeira mobile belts, a swath of ancient bedrock that runs along the Brazilian coast. Those rocks formed in the Neoproterozoic and Cambrian, roughly between one billion and 500 million years ago, during the Pan-African-Brasiliano orogeny when continents crashed together. They are granites and gneisses, the ruined roots of an old mountain range. Below the younger sediments of the Campos Basin, these same rocks form the basement. Above them, just before the rifting began, the Tristan da Cunha hotspot pumped flood basalts across vast tracts of what are now the Parana Basin inland and the Etendeka Traps in Namibia. Basalts similar to these have been found beneath the Santos Basin, and basalt flows about 120 million years old form the base of the Campos sequence. The rifting was violent. The volcanism was prolific. The oil that would follow needed all of it.

## A Basin in Transition

For many years, Campos was Brazilian oil - period. That began to change when the pre-salt fields of the Santos Basin to the south were discovered in the late 2000s, holding oil trapped beneath thick salt layers at depths that had been previously unreachable. The Santos finds are larger and newer: by 2025 the Santos Basin supplied nearly four-fifths of Brazil's oil and gas output, Campos about a fifth. Campos has moved into a different phase - the mature phase, where wells decline, fields are sold off, and new operators take over the shallow-water parts while Petrobras focuses resources on deeper frontiers. In 2020, Petrobras completed the sale of ten concessions to Trident Energy: Enchova, Enchova Oeste, Marimba, Pirauna, Bicudo, Bonito, Pampo, Trilha, Linguado, and Badejo. These are mostly the older, shallower fields along the northern Rio de Janeiro coast - the same fish-named wells that started the whole industry. What was once the frontier has become the back pages. But the oil is still there. Many of the platforms still pump. On clear nights, the glow of the gas flares marks the basin from far out at sea.

## Flight Context

The article's map point (22.48 S, 34.83 W) lies in the open Atlantic some 540 km east of the producing fields, which sit roughly 21-23.5 S, 39.5-41.5 W off the northern Rio de Janeiro coast, between the Cabo Frio High in the south and the Vitoria High off southern Espirito Santo. The fields span water depths from about 80 m on the shelf to about 2,400 m on the slope, in a basin of about 115,000 square km. Best viewed in daylight for the platforms and in darkness for the flare glow from 8,000 ft and above. Macae Airport (SBME / MEA) is the main onshore service base for offshore crews; Campos dos Goytacazes (SBCP / CAW) lies to its north-northeast. Rio-Galeao (SBGL / GIG) lies about 160 km southwest of Macae. Offshore airspace is heavily trafficked with helicopter routes to the platforms - check NOTAMs. Weather is often hazy over the warm Brazil Current.
