# Mossoró

The northeastern Brazilian city that freed its slaves five years before the rest of the country and beat back the most feared bandit of the sertão.

**Location:** -5.1878, -37.3439

**Categories:** brazilian city, history, rio grande do norte, oil, salt

## Introduction

On 30 September 1883, Mossoró declared every enslaved person within its limits to be free. The Lei Áurea - the "Golden Law" that would end slavery across Brazil - was still almost five years away. Mossoró did not wait. The date is still a municipal holiday, one of the "four libertarian acts" the city commemorates in theater and parade, and it has earned this city of 264,577 people its self-chosen title: *Terra da Liberdade*, the Land of Freedom.

## Abolition, Before the Rest of Brazil

The Mossoró abolitionist movement was not unique in northeastern Brazil - Ceará freed its enslaved population a year later, in 1884, and similar declarations happened in smaller towns - but the city made its decision with unusual deliberation. Local leaders, merchants, and clergy moved through 1883 raising funds to compensate enslavers and, more importantly, to argue in print and in public for emancipation. On the morning of 30 September, the names of the freed people were read aloud in a public ceremony. The city celebrates three of its four libertarian acts each September in the Freedom Festival: the Women's Revolt of 1875, when women fought a military conscription that would have taken their sons; the abolition of slavery in 1883; and the 1927 women's vote, when a small number of Mossoró women cast ballots in the first election in which Brazilian women could legally do so.

## The Day Lampião Did Not Win

On 13 June 1927, the *cangaceiro* leader Virgulino Ferreira da Silva - known across the northeast as Lampião - rode toward Mossoró with about fifty armed men. Lampião had spent years terrorizing the *sertão*, raiding towns, extorting ranchers, and humiliating the state police sent to catch him. He was feared the way later Americans would fear Bonnie and Clyde, and he was better armed. Mayor Rodolfo Fernandes had time to prepare. He organized the city's residents into a defensive militia, dug trenches, and positioned marksmen on rooftops around the São Vicente Chapel at the eastern edge of the city. When Lampião's gang attacked, the townspeople fired back in coordinated volleys. One of Lampião's lieutenants, Jararaca, was captured and killed. Several other cangaceiros died. Lampião himself was wounded and withdrew - the only defeat he is recorded to have suffered in his entire career as a bandit. The city still reenacts the battle each June, at the chapel where it happened, in a play called *Chuva de Bala no País de Mossoró*, or *Bullet Rain in the Land of Mossoró*.

## Salt, Oil, and Melons

Mossoró is the largest onshore oil producer in Brazil, with about 47,000 barrels a day pumped from more than 3,500 wells scattered across the surrounding backlands. It is also the country's largest salt producer, drawing on the extensive evaporation flats that stretch toward the coast at Areia Branca. In a different mode entirely, the city anchors the Mossoró/Baraúna/Assu agricultural hub, the largest melon-growing region in Brazil - the hub produced about 254,000 tons of melons in 2007, and more than 200,000 tons of those went for export to the United States, Japan, and Europe. The region has been recognized as a *fruit-fly-free zone* since 1990, which is what allows those exports to cross borders without quarantine. A city that runs on oil, salt, and fruit is an unusual combination, but it is what Mossoró is.

## What the City Remembers

Municipal holidays include 30 September for abolition and 13 December for Saint Lucy, the patron saint whose cathedral stands in the city center. The Santa Luzia Cathedral was built between 1772 and 1773, rebuilt and inaugurated in 1830, and elevated to parish church in 1842 before becoming the seat of the Diocese of Mossoró when Pope Pius XI created it on 28 July 1934. The Feast of Saint Lucy in December draws up to 100,000 pilgrims and concludes with a procession on the 13th. Mossoró Cidade Junina - the city's June festival - draws over a million visitors across the month, making it one of the largest *festa junina* celebrations in the northeast. The festival recounts the Lampião battle each year and fills the old railway station grounds with food stalls, quadrilha dance troupes, and live forró.

## A Modern City Still Writing Its Story

The city has struggled with violence in recent years. The 2016 Violence Atlas ranked Mossoró as the ninth most violent municipality in Brazil, with a homicide rate of 71.5 per 100,000 inhabitants. The Santo Antônio neighborhood has borne most of the violence. A federal penitentiary opened in 2009 has added complications of its own. Yet the city has also built two universities of national standing - the State University of Rio Grande do Norte and the Federal Rural University of the Semi-Arid Region, which grew out of an agricultural school founded in 1967 - and runs one of the oldest newspapers in Latin America, *O Mossoroense*, founded on 17 October 1872 and now published online. Mossoró is a city that made itself free before it had to, beat the most feared outlaw of its century, and keeps trying to be the kind of place that earns those histories.
