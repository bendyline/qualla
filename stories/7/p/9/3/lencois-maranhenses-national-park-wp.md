# Lençóis Maranhenses National Park

Once the rains stop, the lagoons between the dunes lose a metre of depth a month - a national park that spends half the year as a lake district and half as a desert that isn't one.

**Location:** -2.4858, -43.1283

**Categories:** national park, sand dunes, unesco world heritage, brazil

## Introduction

Once the rains stop, the lagoons drain upward. There is no outlet and no river to carry them away; the sun simply takes them, as much as a metre of depth a month, until the hollows between the dunes are dry white sand again and the fish that lived in them are gone. Then January comes, the rain returns, and roughly two-fifths of Lençóis Maranhenses National Park turns back into water. Nothing here is permanent except the arrangement that produces it: sand delivered by rivers, moved by wind, and sitting on a floor of rock that refuses to let the rain through.

## Delivered by River, Arranged by Wind

The sand is not local. The Parnaíba and the Preguiças carry it down to the Atlantic coast of Maranhão, where the surf works it up onto the beach and the prevailing wind takes over, pushing it as far as fifty kilometres inland. The result is a dune belt running along seventy kilometres of coastline and extending five to twenty-five kilometres back from the sea, with individual dunes reaching forty metres. From the air the pattern reads as long parallel ridges, which is where the name comes from — lençóis, bedsheets, laid out to dry. The whole field is in slow motion. Every dry season the wind edits it, and the map of the lagoons redraws itself accordingly.

## The Floor That Holds

Deserts, by the standard definition, receive under 250 millimetres of rain a year. This place gets about 1,200, roughly seventy percent of it between January and May, which disqualifies it from the category it so obviously resembles. What makes the lagoons possible is not the rain alone but what lies beneath the sand: a layer of impermeable rock that stops the water draining away. It collects in the interdune hollows instead, in lagoons up to a hundred metres long and three metres deep, covering something like forty-one percent of the park at their fullest. The water is warm — between 27.5 and 32 degrees Celsius — and distinctly acidic, with a pH between 4.9 and 6.2. It is also, being rainwater filtered through clean sand, startlingly clear.

## Living on a Timer

Surveys have recorded 133 plant species, 112 birds and at least 42 reptiles here, along with four threatened species: the scarlet ibis, the neotropical otter, the West Indian manatee and a small wild cat. The organism that best explains the place, though, is a fish. Hoplias malabaricus, the traíra, survives the dry season by burrowing into the mud at the bottom of a lagoon and waiting — months, sometimes, in the dark, until water returns above it. How fish come back to pools that demonstrably vanished is one of the first questions visitors ask, and the mud is most of the answer. Colonising a landscape that deletes itself every year takes a particular kind of patience.

## Decree 86,060

Federal protection arrived on 2 June 1981, under Decree 86,060, covering roughly 155,000 hectares — Brazilian figures put the area at 156,584 — across the municipalities of Barreirinhas, Primeira Cruz and Santo Amaro do Maranhão. About 90,000 hectares of that is open, unvegetated dune. The park is administered by the Chico Mendes Institute for Biodiversity Conservation, and the boundary was drawn around more than scenery: it takes in beach, restinga, mangrove at the river mouths, and the handful of small communities living inside the dune field. Queimada dos Britos and its smaller neighbour Baixa Grande sit in oases several hours' walk apart, on the trekking line between Atins and Santo Amaro. Motorised transport inside the park is banned; only registered residents may run quad bikes, so everyone else arrives on foot or on an animal. Baixa Grande was down to about thirteen families in 2017. The children go to school in Barreirinhas, and most of them stay there.

## The Year the List Caught Up

In July 2024, forty-three years after the decree, UNESCO inscribed Lençóis Maranhenses on the World Heritage list as a natural property, citing its exceptional beauty and the fact that the phenomenon it protects is found nowhere else on earth. The recognition ratified something the visitor numbers had already established. Older sources describe a park receiving perhaps 60,000 people a year; Brazilian figures for 2023 put arrivals above 400,000. Some of that traffic followed the cameras — the dunes stood in for the planet Vormir in Avengers: Infinity War and Avengers: Endgame, and long before that they were the setting for the Brazilian film The House of Sand. Whether a landscape that erases and rebuilds itself every year can absorb visitor numbers of that order is now the park's central management question, and the answer is not yet in.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 2.49°S, 43.13°W, on the Atlantic coast of Maranhão east of the Baía de São José. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-6,000 feet: low enough that individual lagoons stay distinct against the sand, high enough to read the parallel dune ridges that give the park its name. Marechal Cunha Machado International Airport (ICAO SBSL) at São Luís lies about 250 km west; Parnaíba (ICAO SBPB) is roughly 200 km east. Barreirinhas, on the Rio Preguiças at the park's eastern edge, is the usual ground gateway and a useful visual fix. The lagoons are fullest from roughly May through September; by November many have dried out entirely, and the wet season from January to April brings haze and convective activity.
