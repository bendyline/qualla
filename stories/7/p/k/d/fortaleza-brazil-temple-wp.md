# Fortaleza Brazil Temple

A white-granite Latter-day Saint temple overlooking the Atlantic in Fortaleza's Dunas neighborhood, whose 2019 dedication was the first in church history conducted entirely in Portuguese.

**Location:** -3.7478, -38.4608

**Categories:** 21st-century Latter Day Saint temples, Buildings and structures in Ceará, Buildings and structures in Fortaleza, Temples (LDS Church) completed in 2019, Temples (LDS Church) in Brazil, 2019 establishments in Brazil

## Introduction

The twin towers were already on the blueprints when the city of Fortaleza intervened. In early 2015, local authorities rejected the original design for not complying with building code, sending the architects back to their drawing boards with a decade of planning already invested. The single domed tower you see today, rising from a sloping site near the Atlantic in the Dunas neighborhood, is the compromise that emerged. It is clad in Branco Ceará granite quarried nearby, and carved into its exterior are floral motifs inspired by native Brazilian orchids. More than a decade passed between announcement and dedication, which is unusual even by the careful standards of Latter-day Saint temple construction.

## The Long Road to Dedication

Church president Thomas S. Monson announced the temple on October 3, 2009, during the faith's semiannual general conference. At that moment, the Latter-day Saint community in Brazil had been growing for decades, and Ceará alone could not sustain easy temple attendance; the nearest temple at the time was a long journey south. Groundbreaking took place on November 15, 2011, presided over by David A. Bednar of the church's Quorum of the Twelve Apostles. Then the design had to change. Then came the slow work of sourcing local granite, shaping the interiors, and cultivating the grounds across from FANOR University. When doors finally opened for a public tour in the spring of 2019, approximately sixty thousand visitors walked through.

## A Historic First

On June 2, 2019, Ulisses Soares dedicated the temple in three sessions. Soares, a Brazilian by birth and a member of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles, presided in Portuguese. It was the first time in the church's history that an entire temple dedication was conducted in a language other than English. The moment carried weight for Latter-day Saints in Brazil, a country where the faith has grown from a handful of early converts in the twentieth century to more than a million members across hundreds of congregations. The Fortaleza temple is the seventh built in Brazil and the first in Ceará, and the first converts in the region had waited decades for a temple of their own to visit.

## Granite, Orchids, and the Sea

The architects chose a neoclassical vocabulary softened with local materials. The white granite is native to Ceará, quarried from deposits that have supplied Brazilian buildings for generations. Floral detailing inspired by Brazilian orchids runs through the ornamental work, tying the structure to its tropical setting. The ten-acre site holds more than the temple itself: an arrival center, a meetinghouse for regular Sunday worship, housing for missionaries serving in the region, and a maintenance building complete the complex. The sloping ground gives the temple something unusual in Fortaleza's flat coastal city: a natural platform that raises the building above the surrounding neighborhood, letting it catch the Atlantic light from mid-morning through late afternoon.

## The Quiet Interior

Inside, the building is arranged for the specific rites that Latter-day Saint temples are built to host. There are two ordinance rooms, where ceremonies for members in good standing take place, and two sealing rooms, where marriages are performed in the theology of the faith. A baptistry supports proxy baptisms, a practice central to the church's understanding of family ties extending across generations. The temple is not open to the public after dedication; admission requires a recommend issued by local leaders to practicing members. A president and matron oversee the temple for three-year terms, handling both administrative work and training of the volunteer staff. Francisco J. da Silva and Maria E. da Silva took up those duties in 2024.

## A Building That Made the City Listen

The temple sits at a particular address in a particular neighborhood, but it also sits at an intersection of local planning and global religious organization. When Fortaleza's building code forced the redesign, the story could have become one of friction. Instead, it became one of accommodation: a global institution accepting the judgment of a city authority and producing a better-proportioned building as a result. The single dome now reads more naturally against the low-rise Atlantic skyline than the original twin towers would have. From the observation decks of passing flights into Pinto Martins Airport, the white granite catches sunlight like a small lighthouse inland from the dunes, legible even when Fortaleza's own coastline drops below the horizon.

## Flight Context

The Fortaleza Brazil Temple sits at 3.75 degrees south, 38.46 degrees west, in Fortaleza's Dunas neighborhood about seven kilometers east of Pinto Martins International Airport (SBFZ / FOR). Elevation is roughly 20 meters above sea level. Best viewing altitude is 3,000-4,000 feet above ground on an approach from the northeast; the white granite is most visible in mid-morning to late-afternoon light. Landmarks for orientation include the Atlantic coastline to the north, FANOR University across the street, and the Cocó Park green corridor to the southwest. Nearest major airport is SBFZ.
