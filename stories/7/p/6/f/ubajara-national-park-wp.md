# Ubajara National Park

Carved into the western edge of Ceará's Ibiapaba plateau, Ubajara is Brazil's smallest national park and the guardian of a patient work of limestone.

**Location:** -3.7663, -40.8980

**Categories:** 1959 establishments in Brazil, National parks of Brazil, Protected areas of Ceará, Caatinga

## Introduction

A centimetre of crystal takes three years to grow. That arithmetic - slow water, slow dissolution, slow rebuilding - made the stalactites and stalagmites of the Ubajara grotto, and it is still making them. The cave sits in the flank of the Serra da Ibiapaba, in the state of Ceará, about 320 kilometres inland from Fortaleza. The national park around it is the smallest of Brazil's thirty-five federally protected parks, covering only 6,271 hectares. But scale can be deceptive. Much of Brazil's biological drama happens in pockets like this, on the crumpled edges where one biome meets another.

## At the Edge of Two Worlds

Ubajara sits at the western rim of the Ceará peripheral depression, where the land steps up onto the Ibiapaba plateau in a long limestone escarpment. The park straddles three municipalities - Ubajara, Tianguá, and Frecheirinha - and presses against the vast 1.59-million-hectare Serra da Ibiapaba Environmental Protection Area to the west. The official biome designation is Caatinga, the semi-arid scrubland of the Brazilian Northeast, but at these altitudes the climate softens: average annual rainfall reaches 1,440 millimetres, and temperatures settle around a comfortable 22 Celsius. That mix produces pockets of humid forest layered on top of drier vegetation, a transitional zone that supports a roster of species you might not expect in Caatinga country.

## Nature's Patient Chemistry

The grotto is the park's signature feature, and the reason it exists as a protected area at all. Set into a hillside in the Ibiapaba Mountains, the cave has formed over tens of thousands of years as mildly acidic rainwater seeped through the limestone, dissolving stone in one place and redepositing it in another. The result is a gallery of stalactites, stalagmites, flowstones, and columns that grow slowly enough to feel almost frozen. At roughly three years per centimetre, a metre-tall formation took three centuries to reach full height. Visitors descend to the cave entrance by chairlift, following the same route the water has taken since long before anyone had a name for this mountain. Tours are supervised by Ibama, the national environmental body, and must be arranged in advance.

## Protected Residents

Ubajara is classified as an IUCN Category II national park - meaning its core purpose is preserving intact ecosystems while permitting research, education, and careful tourism. The species list the park shelters is weighted toward the rare and range-restricted. The oncilla, Leopardus tigrinus, is a small spotted wildcat classed as vulnerable. The cougar, Puma concolor, still patrols the more remote forest. The Maranhão red-handed howler monkey, Alouatta ululata, calls from the canopy at dawn, its rolling howl carrying for kilometres. The variable antshrike works the understory. And in damp leaf litter live two tiny frogs that occur essentially nowhere else - Adelophryne baturitensis and Adelophryne maranguapensis - a reminder that a park does not need to be vast to protect species that exist on the head of a pin.

## The Mountains Around the Park

Beyond the grotto itself, the Ibiapaba range offers its own set of landmarks. The Morro do Céu, the 'Hill of the Sky,' rises 820 metres above the surrounding plain and makes for one of the best viewpoints in the area. The Pedra de Itagurussu is the source of the Pirangi waterfall, one of several falls that tumble off the escarpment. Trails wind from the rim through forest remnants, where the original vegetation survives in patches alongside secondary growth and scattered exotics. The park was created by decree in April 1959, then expanded and adjusted in 1973 and again in 2002 - a small legal history for a small place, but one that reflects the ongoing work of deciding what the country decides to keep.

## Small Park, Long Memory

The Chico Mendes Institute for Biodiversity Conservation, named after the Acre rubber tapper and environmental activist murdered in 1988, administers Ubajara today. That pairing - a park formed by water and limestone over eons, managed by an agency named for a man who died twenty-nine years after it was founded - says something about how conservation works at small scales. The geology is almost indifferent to human time. The species in the park have been evolving here for longer than any written record. But the fact that the grotto is lit, mapped, patrolled, and open to visitors is a decision renewed every year. Brazil has larger parks. It has wilder parks. Ubajara stands out for being small enough to comprehend, and patient enough to keep teaching.

## Flight Context

Ubajara National Park sits at 3.77°S, 40.90°W in the Serra da Ibiapaba, western Ceará. The 820-metre escarpment is visible from altitude as a sharp linear feature against the Caatinga to the west. The nearest major airport is Fortaleza's Pinto Martins International (SBFZ / FOR), about 320 km east-northeast. The area is warm and generally clear; morning cloud occasionally wraps the plateau rim. Cruising altitude provides a good view of how the plateau steps down toward the coast - the geological setting that made the grotto possible in the first place.
