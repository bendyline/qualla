# Teatro Arthur Azevedo

By 1989 the only original part left of Brazil's second-oldest theatre was its front wall - so São Luís knocked down the rest and built 1817 again from the drawings.

**Location:** -2.5290, -44.3025

**Categories:** theatre, architecture, history, brazil

## Introduction

By 1989, almost nothing authentic remained of the theatre that had opened in São Luís in 1817. The building had slid from playhouse to cinema to near-ruin, and what survived of the original was essentially the façade. The state's response was drastic and, in preservation circles, still argued over: demolish the rest and build it again, working from the original plans and from historical research to recover details no living person had seen. The Teatro Arthur Azevedo you can walk into today is therefore both very old and quite new — a reconstruction of a house that first opened its doors when Brazil was still a kingdom joined to Portugal, in a city whose entire historic core would later be judged worthy of the World Heritage list.

## Two Merchants and a Kingdom

The project began in 1815 with two Portuguese merchants, Eleutério Lopes da Silva Varela and Estêvão Gonçalves Braga, who put up the money for a proper theatre. Construction started in 1816 and the house opened on 1 June 1817, seating 756. It was not São Luís's first theatre — by most counts it was the fourth — but it was the first that felt like a European one, with a neoclassical exterior and a scale and comfort that were genuinely novel in northern Brazil at the time. They called it the Teatro União, a nod to the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves, proclaimed the year the project began. The name was a piece of loyal politics with a short shelf life. Within five years Brazil had declared independence, and the union the theatre celebrated was gone.

## The Church Objected

The original design was bigger. As drawn, the building would have run all the way to the Largo do Carmo, and it was the Church that stopped it — objecting to what the project implied on religious grounds, and forcing the merchants to shrink their plan. A footprint is an unusual place to find a theological argument, but that is what the theatre's dimensions record. Reduced or not, the finished house dominated its street, and it worked: for most of the nineteenth century this was where São Luís went to be entertained, a city with money enough to hold opinions about the quality of its touring companies.

## Three Names in a Century

In 1852 the theatre was rechristened Teatro São Luiz, after the city itself. Its present name arrived in the 1920s, when it was dedicated to Artur de Azevedo, the playwright São Luís considered its own. Renaming buildings for writers is common enough; renaming a theatre for a man who spent his working life far to the south in Rio de Janeiro, and whose funniest work was a satire of the federal capital, has a certain local pride to it. Maranhão has always exported writers. This is one of the ways it keeps claiming them back.

## The Man on the Playbill

Artur Nabantino Gonçalves de Azevedo was born in São Luís on 7 July 1855 and died in Rio de Janeiro on 22 October 1908. He was a journalist, a chronicler, a Parnassian poet and a short-story writer, but above all he was the man who made the comedy of manners a Brazilian form — beginning with Amor por Anexins in 1872 and ending with O Dote in 1907. His masterpiece, A Capital Federal, premiered in 1897 and skewered the country's new republican capital with a farmer's family loose in its salons. He founded and held the 29th chair of the Brazilian Academy of Letters, choosing as its patron Martins Pena, the comic dramatist he saw himself following. His younger brother Aluísio Azevedo, author of O Cortiço and O Mulato, took the same city and wrote it as tragedy.

## Rebuilt, and Put Back to Work

The reconstructed theatre seats 750, plus six places reserved for officials, distributed across stalls, boxes, balconies and gallery — within a few seats of the 1817 capacity. It is a listed heritage property, and it sits inside the Historic Centre of São Luís, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in December 1997: some 220 hectares and roughly four thousand eighteenth- and nineteenth-century buildings, faced with the largest concentration of Portuguese azulejo tiles in Brazil, glazed surfaces that shed the equatorial rain and reflect the heat. The theatre is not a monument under glass. Through the Núcleo Arte-Educação programme, run jointly by the state culture and education secretariats, students from public schools use the building for courses in theatre, dance, choral singing and piano, and its calendar leans hard on Maranhão's own performers. A house rebuilt from drawings has to earn its authenticity somehow. It does it by staying open.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 2.53°S, 44.30°W, in the historic centre of São Luís on the northeastern end of São Luís Island. Marechal Cunha Machado International Airport (ICAO SBSL) lies roughly 10 km southeast. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500-3,000 feet: the historic centre appears as a dense grid of red-tiled roofs on the promontory between the Anil and Bacanga rivers, distinct from the modern high-rises along the Atlantic beaches to the northeast. The theatre itself is a single roofline within that grid — use the waterfront and the mouth of the Bacanga as the visual anchor. Best visibility July through December; the January-June wet season is hazy with frequent afternoon buildups.
