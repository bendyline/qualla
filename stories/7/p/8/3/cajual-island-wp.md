# Cajual Island

One of Brazil's most important fossil beds spends most of every day underwater, and the giant predator found there was named for the island's own quilombo.

**Location:** -2.4780, -44.4860

**Categories:** Landforms of Maranhão, Atlantic islands of Brazil, Paleontology, Fossil sites

## Introduction

For most of each day, one of the richest dinosaur bonebeds in Brazil is under two metres of seawater. The Laje do Coringa is a shelf of Cretaceous rock on the shore of Cajual Island, in the Baía de São Marcos off Maranhão, and the tides of that bay are large enough to swallow it and spit it back out twice daily. Paleontologists work it in windows of a few hours. Whatever the sea has loosened since the last low tide is what they get, and whatever they miss, the next tide takes.

## A Bonebed the Sea Is Grinding Up

Laje do Coringa is not a graveyard of articulated skeletons. It is a concentration of hundreds of isolated bones and teeth, tumbled and broken, exposed in the Alcântara Formation of the São Luís-Grajaú Basin. Wave action has done most of the damage and continues to do it. That makes the site both extraordinarily productive and extraordinarily frustrating: material erodes out constantly, and it erodes away just as constantly. The paleontologist Elaine Machado has called the key discovery here an accident, well-preserved specimens being rare in a place where the tide works on everything that surfaces. The rock dates to the Cenomanian stage of the Late Cretaceous, sometime between 100.5 and 93.9 million years ago.

## Oxalaia quilombensis

In 1999 the site gave up a pair of fused premaxillae - the front of an upper jaw - from an enormous predator. Alexander Kellner and colleagues formally described it in 2011 as Oxalaia quilombensis, from the holotype MN 6117-V, with a maxilla fragment, MN 6119-V, referred to the same animal. It was a spinosaurid: the long-snouted, crocodile-jawed lineage that includes Spinosaurus. Kellner's team estimated a skull around 1.35 metres long and a body of 12 to 14 metres weighing five to seven tonnes, which would make it the largest theropod known from Brazil. Some of its details are genuinely odd. The palatal surface of the premaxillae is sculptured, and it carried two replacement teeth per socket, which is unusual among theropods. The naming was deliberate and local. Oxalaia honours the orixá Oxalá; quilombensis honours the quilombos - communities founded by people who escaped slavery - including the one that still lives on the island where the fossil was found.

## Is It Its Own Animal?

The status of Oxalaia is not settled, and honesty requires saying so. In 2020 Smyth and colleagues argued it was nothing more than a junior synonym of Spinosaurus aegyptiacus, the distinguishing features attributable to variation between individuals rather than between species. Subsequent analyses between 2021 and 2023 pushed back, finding diagnostic differences in the shape of the snout and the position of the nostril sufficient to keep the genus standing. For now the name holds, but it holds on a pair of jaw bones from a bed the sea is actively destroying, which is a thin foundation for any taxonomic argument. More material would settle it. The tide is not cooperating.

## The Same Animals as Morocco

What surrounds Oxalaia in the deposit is as interesting as the animal itself. The Alcântara Formation has produced the giant coelacanth Mawsonia gigas, rays and sawfishes and a range of other fish, pterosaurs, diplodocoid and titanosaur sauropods, a large carcharodontosaur, noasaurids and dromaeosaurs - along with conifers and ferns from the vegetation. The environment was tropical and heavily forested, set within a broader arid-to-semi-arid landscape with strongly alternating rainfall. Nearly all of it matches the fauna of Morocco's Kem Kem beds of the same age. That correspondence is the point: 95 million years ago these were not two continents but one shoreline. South America and Africa were still parting, and the animals had not yet noticed. Cajual is a fragment of Gondwana that happens to sit in a Brazilian bay.

## The People on the Island

Cajual is inhabited. A small quilombola community lives there, part of a much larger pattern: the municipality of Alcântara, which contains the island, holds over two hundred quilombola settlements - the greatest concentration in Brazil. They took root as the colonial cotton and sugar economy collapsed and landowners walked away from the territory. Their situation since has been contested. The construction and expansion of the Alcântara Launch Center on the mainland displaced more than three hundred families from thirty-two communities in the 1980s, and the Inter-American Court of Human Rights subsequently ruled against Brazil, ordering compensation and the restoration of territory; in September 2024 President Lula signed decrees advancing quilombola land titling in the area. The dinosaur that made the island scientifically famous carries their name in its own. It is a rare case of a species epithet pointing at the living people of a place rather than past them.

## Flight Context

Cajual Island lies at 2.478°S, 44.486°W in the Baía de São Marcos, Maranhão, a low island surrounded by water and a clean waypoint for coastal navigation along Brazil's north coast. São Luís is roughly 30 km east across the bay; the colonial town of Alcântara is the nearest settlement on the mainland to the west. Nearest airport is Marechal Cunha Machado International at São Luís (SBSL / SLZ, 54 m), about 15 km from that city's centre. Recommended sightseeing altitude 2,000-4,000 ft AGL. Time any low pass to low water if the objective is the Laje do Coringa shelf - the fossil-bearing rock lies up to 2 m below the surface at high tide and is exposed only for a few hours a day. Note that the Alcântara Launch Center operates on the mainland immediately west of the island; check NOTAMs for launch-related airspace restrictions before transiting. Expect strong tidal currents and extensive mudflats and mangrove at low water throughout São Marcos Bay.
