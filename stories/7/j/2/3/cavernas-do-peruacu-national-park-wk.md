# Cavernas do Peruaçu National Park

A limestone canyon in northern Minas Gerais holding some 140 catalogued caves, more than 80 archaeological sites, and rock art painted over ten thousand years ago.

**Location:** -15.1225, -44.3243

**Categories:** National parks of Brazil, Protected areas of Minas Gerais, World Heritage Sites in Brazil, Caves of Brazil, Archaeological sites in Brazil

## Introduction

Somebody stood in this canyon more than ten thousand years ago with pigment on their hands and left a mark on the rock that is still there. Not a scratch — a composition, in colours chosen and repeated across walls, in cave after cave. Cavernas do Peruaçu National Park protects 56,448 hectares of dry limestone country in the north of Minas Gerais, and the geology alone would justify the fence. What makes the place hold you is the other thing: people were living in these caves, painting them, and burying their dead in them for most of the time humans have been in South America.

## How a River Ate a Mountain

Start absurdly early. Much of what is now Brazil lay under an inland sea, and when the water finally withdrew it left behind massifs of limestone — the compacted remains of everything that had lived and died in that sea. One of those massifs stood directly across the path of the Peruaçu River, a tributary running down toward the São Francisco. Limestone is soluble, and a river with nowhere else to go is patient. The Peruaçu dissolved its way through, then kept working the fractures on either side, until the block of rock became a honeycomb. Around 140 caves have been catalogued inside the park, and the river still crosses the park from northwest to southeast before delivering itself to the São Francisco. Speleologists keep finding grottoes nobody has documented.

## Painted Before Anything We Call History

The rock art here dates from the tenth millennium BCE, which is to say the painters were at work while the last ice age was still letting go of the planet. Figures cover the walls of Janelão and other caves in the system — bold, high-contrast, deliberately placed. More than eighty archaeological sites have been recorded across the canyon. It is worth sitting with the arithmetic for a second: the oldest paintings in the Peruaçu predate the earliest Egyptian dynasties by more than six thousand years, and were already ancient when the first stone went into Stonehenge. Nobody built a monument here. They used the rooms the river had already made, and they came back to them, generation after generation, for longer than any civilisation has managed to last.

## The Dead of Lapa do Boquete

One cave in particular has shaped how archaeologists read the canyon. Lapa do Boquete, in the municipality of Januária, served as a burial ground from roughly four thousand to one thousand years ago, and the funerary practice recorded there was anything but simple — multiple burials in the same ground, the deliberate handling and rearrangement of bones, stone structures, ceramics and worked stone left alongside. Walter A. Neves and Renato Kipnis are among the researchers who worked the site and published on its mortuary customs and the diets of the people buried there. Strip away the technical language and what remains is a community that carried its dead into a specific cave for three thousand years, and did something careful with them once they were inside.

## Between Two Dry Countries

Above ground the park straddles a border between biomes. Cerrado, the Brazilian savanna, meets caatinga, the thorn scrub of the northeast, and the transition produces seasonal deciduous and semi-deciduous forest that drops its leaves and waits. Average temperature sits around 18°C; annual rainfall stays under 1,200 millimetres and arrives mostly in summer. Maned wolves, cougars, jaguars and ocelots all range through the protected area. In the water there is something odder: Trichomycterus itacarambiensis, a small catfish adapted to life inside these caves and found nowhere else on Earth. It is a useful reminder that the karst is not merely scenery — it is a set of enclosed worlds, each one running its own long experiment.

## Xakriabá Ground, and a Line on a List

The park spans Januária, Itacarambi and São João das Missões, and the Xakriabá people have a documented presence in this landscape — a continuity with the canyon that no visitor route can replicate. ICMBio, Brazil's federal conservation agency, has administered the park since its creation on 21 September 1999, and access is deliberately rationed: six visitor routes reaching eight caves, guided, on a schedule. The restrictions exist because speleothems — the stalactites, stalagmites and flowstone that took millions of years to accumulate — break in a second and do not grow back on any human timescale. In 2025 UNESCO inscribed the park on the World Heritage List under criteria vii and viii, for natural beauty and for the record the rock keeps. The rock art, older than the listing by a hundred centuries, was doing that already.

## Flight Context

The park centres on 15.12°S, 44.32°W in northern Minas Gerais, spanning the municipalities of Januária, Itacarambi and São João das Missões. From the air the defining feature is the Peruaçu canyon itself — a pale limestone gash running northwest to southeast through dark deciduous scrub, ending where the river joins the broad brown thread of the São Francisco. Sightseeing altitudes of 3,000–6,000 ft AGL show the canyon walls and the dolines punched into the plateau above the cave system. Januária (SNJN) is the closest field, about 45 minutes by road from the park core via BR-135 out of Itacarambi; Montes Claros (SBMK) is the nearest larger airport, well to the south. The dry season, roughly May to September, gives long stretches of clear air and sharp shadow relief in the canyon; summer rain brings haze, fast convection and rising water inside the caves.
