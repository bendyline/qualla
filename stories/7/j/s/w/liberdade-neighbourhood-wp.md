# Liberdade

A cattle road renamed Freedom after the troops who won Bahia marched down it — and the Salvador neighbourhood that became the home address of Black Bahian cultural and political life.

**Location:** -12.9500, -38.5035

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in Salvador, Bahia, Afro-Brazilian culture, Carnival in Brazil, Education in Brazil

## Introduction

In February 1975 fewer than a hundred people came out of Rua do Curuzu in carnival costume, and the Salvador daily A Tarde met them on 12 February with a headline calling the group a racist bloc, a discordant note. The block was Ilê Aiyê, founded in Liberdade three months earlier by Antônio Carlos dos Santos — Vovô — and Apolônio de Jesus, who had wanted to call it Poder Negro until the Federal Police refused to register the name. Half a century later Ilê Aiyê counts some three thousand members, and the objection has aged badly. Liberdade is the neighbourhood that produced it: dense, self-contained, and closer to the centre of Black Bahian life than anywhere else in the city.

## The Road the Cattle Took

Before it was Liberdade it was the Estrada das Boiadas, the cattle road. Herds came down from the sertão of the interior, were traded at the Feira do Capuame — in what is now the municipality of Dias d'Ávila — and finished the walk to the port of Salvador along this ridge. Then, in 1823, the troops who had beaten the Portuguese and won Bahia for an independent Brazil marched into the city on the same road, and it was renamed the Estrada da Liberdade. Freedom. The word arrived sixty-five years before abolition, which is the kind of irony the neighbourhood has had to live with and, eventually, to reclaim: much of Liberdade's population settled here after 1888, when freed and formerly enslaved people needed somewhere in Salvador to build a life.

## A City Inside the City

Liberdade sits on the plateau that divides the lower city from the upper, on Salvador's western side, and almost everything it needs is within walking distance of itself. The port at Água de Meninos, the enormous Feira de São Joaquim market and the ferry terminal for Itaparica are all just downhill. Getting between the two levels has its own machine: the Plano Inclinado Liberdade-Calçada, an inclined railway opened on 13 March 1981, still moves around ten thousand people on weekdays between Liberdade and the Calçada neighbourhood below. By population, Liberdade is the second largest district in Salvador, exceeded only by Cajazeiras — a city's worth of people inside a metropolis, with its own commercial streets, its own radio stations and newspapers, and a social life that rarely needs to leave.

## Curuzu

Ilê Aiyê's address is Rua Direta do Curuzu, and its origins are religious as much as musical. Mãe Hilda, the ialorixá of the Ilê Axé Jitolu terreiro on the same street, was the block's spiritual mother for about three and a half decades, from its founding until her death in 2009, and the terreiro served as its meeting place and headquarters; her daughter Hildelice took over the house after her. The name Ilê Aiyê comes from Yoruba and is usually rendered as house or world of Black people. The block built a form — the bloco afro — that other groups then borrowed: carnival themes drawn from African kingdoms and diaspora history, songs that teach as much as they drive, and the Noite da Beleza Negra, at which the block crowns a Deusa do Ébano, an Ebony Goddess, in a country whose beauty standards had spent centuries saying otherwise. Ilê Aiyê also runs schooling and cultural programmes for children in the neighbourhood, and each 20 November, Black Consciousness Day, the march sets out from Curuzu.

## The School That Ran All Day

Brazil's most ambitious experiment in public education was built here. On 21 September 1950 the educator Anísio Teixeira opened the Centro Educacional Carneiro Ribeiro across Liberdade and the adjoining districts of Caixa d'Água, Pero Vaz and Pau Miúdo — the Escola Parque, a full-day school pairing ordinary classroom work with workshops in sewing, carpentry, leatherwork and art, some of it taught by artists of the standing of Mário Cravo and Carybé. It was named for Ernesto Carneiro Ribeiro, the Bahian physician and philologist. The model was copied and argued over for the rest of the century, feeding into the CIEP schools built in Rio de Janeiro in the 1980s. The buildings themselves fared less well, deteriorating for decades before state renovations around the turn of the 2000s.

## The Title and What It Misses

For years Liberdade was described as the neighbourhood with the largest Black population in Brazil, a claim repeated so often it became a form of identity. In 2012 the press reported IBGE census figures that handed the distinction to Pernambués, on the other side of Salvador. The correction is real and worth making, and it changes very little. Liberdade is densely populated and largely low income, and it has built most of what it has without waiting to be given it: the carnival block, the terreiros, the schools, the community radio stations and newspapers, the march every 20 November. Those are the institutions that shape how Bahia understands itself, and they were assembled here, uphill from the port, by people the city's investment maps have never favoured. The name on the road was a soldier's boast in 1823. What has been made of it since belongs to the people who live here.

## Flight Context

Located at 12.950 S, 38.504 W on the plateau dividing Salvador's Cidade Alta from the Cidade Baixa, about 2.4 km north of the Pelourinho and 20 km southwest of Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães International Airport (SBSV / SSA). From the air, look for the dense low-rise grid on the ridge west of the port, with the Baía de Todos os Santos and the Água de Meninos waterfront immediately downhill to the west and the cut of the Plano Inclinado Liberdade-Calçada dropping toward Calçada. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,500 ft AGL. Approaches from the north on the SBSV downwind pass almost directly overhead.
