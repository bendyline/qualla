# Battle of Itaparica

In January 1823 a Portuguese fleet of forty-two vessels attacked an island militia in the Bay of All Saints, and lost - a three-day defence that helped decide whether Brazilian independence would hold in Bahia.

**Location:** -12.9833, -38.6667

**Categories:** Battles of the Brazilian War of Independence, Battles involving Brazil, Battles involving Portugal, 1823 in Brazil, History of Bahia

## Introduction

Brazil declared independence on 7 September 1822. In Bahia, nobody informed the Portuguese garrison - or rather, they were informed and declined to leave. General Inácio Luís Madeira de Melo held Salvador with regular troops and a fleet in the bay, and for ten months the new empire's authority stopped at the water's edge. The revolutionary government had withdrawn upriver to Cachoeira, on the Paraguaçu, and the island of Itaparica sat directly between the two - a low green wedge of sand and coconut palm lying a short crossing off Salvador, controlling the approach to the rebel capital and the food supply moving across the Bay of All Saints. Whoever held Itaparica held the bay's logistics. On 7 January 1823, the Portuguese came to take it.

## Three Days of Guns

Forty-two vessels of different sizes took part in the attack, under João Félix Pereira de Campos, commander of the Portuguese squadron in Bahia. Against them stood the Itaparica Battalion - island militia, not line infantry - commanded by Antônio de Sousa Lima, alongside the naval officer João Francisco de Oliveira, known throughout the bay as João das Botas, and Captain Manoel Joaquim Tupinambá. The bombardment and landing attempts ran from 7 to 9 January. When it ended, the Portuguese had suffered roughly two hundred killed and wounded - young sailors and soldiers from a kingdom most of them would not see again, sent across an ocean to hold a colony that had already left. The Brazilian side counted three wounded.

## A Defence Built From the Ground Up

The disparity was not luck. Sousa Lima had organised a defensive system anchored on the seventeenth-century São Lourenço Fort, a Dutch-built stronghold from the earlier era when the Netherlands contested northeastern Brazil, and extended it outward with trenches dug at every plausible landing point. Positions went in at the Largo da Quitanda, at the Fonte da Bica spring, and at the Praia do Convento, then along the beaches of Amoreiras, Mocambo, Manguinhos and Porto dos Santos. Each held soldiers and small cannon. It was a defence designed by people who knew exactly which beaches were shallow enough to land on, because they had grown up swimming from them - the kind of local knowledge that does not appear on an admiral's chart.

## Pereira de Campos Explains Himself

Losing commanders write letters, and Pereira de Campos wrote his. In accounting for the defeat he pointed to two things: a garrison made up of an unexpected number of people, and what he called the island's way of defending. Read charitably, he had run into a problem the Portuguese command never solved in Bahia. This was not a conventional army that could be broken by a conventional blow. It was a population - militia, fishermen, boatmen, farmers - fighting on ground they knew, resupplied by a bay they controlled, with no single point of failure to strike. Forty-two ships could bombard the shoreline all day and still find every beach occupied.

## The Long Road to Dois de Julho

Itaparica did not end the war. Fighting in Bahia continued through the first half of 1823, with Itaparica firmly in Brazilian hands while Madeira de Melo's position in Salvador grew steadily more untenable. The Portuguese finally evacuated by sea, and on 2 July 1823 Bahian victory was proclaimed in Salvador - nearly ten months after the declaration at Ipiranga. That date, not 7 September, is what Bahia celebrates. Every 2 July, Salvador fills for the Dois de Julho processions, and the figures paraded through the streets are a caboclo and a cabocla, representing the ordinary Brazilians of mixed indigenous descent who did most of the actual fighting.

## What the Island Kept

Itaparica today is a place of ferries and weekend houses, its towns protected as a heritage ensemble by IPHAN for their colonial churches and manor houses. The São Lourenço Fort still stands. The Fonte da Bica still runs - a mineral spring that once gave the island a reputation as a health resort, and that in January 1823 was a fortified position on somebody's defensive line. Local memory runs deep here: the island was the site of a Jesuit mission village founded in 1561 to evangelise the Tupinambá, and of a smallpox epidemic two years later that killed the indigenous population in numbers the missionaries recorded as the first cases in Brazil. The battle of 1823 is one layer among many, but it is the one the island chose to remember as its own.

## Flight Context

The engagement was fought around 12.98°S, 38.67°W, along the eastern and northern shores of Itaparica Island in the Bay of All Saints. The island is unmistakable from the air: a long, low, densely wooded landmass running north to south, separated from the Salvador peninsula by a broad channel crossed by ferries running to Bom Despacho. The town of Itaparica sits at the northern tip, where the São Lourenço Fort and the historic centre face Salvador across the water - the same sightline the Portuguese squadron used. A pass at 2,500 to 4,000 feet from the northeast reproduces the attackers' approach and shows the beaches Sousa Lima fortified. Nearest airport is Salvador Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães (SBSV), about 20 nautical miles northeast. Conditions are typically good VFR outside the April-to-July wet season, with morning offering the calmest air over the bay.
