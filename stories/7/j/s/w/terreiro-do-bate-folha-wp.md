# Terreiro do Bate Folha

A Candomblé Angola house founded in Salvador in 1916, whose congregation has kept fifteen and a half hectares of Atlantic Forest standing inside the city — and kept its faith through decades when holding a ceremony required buying a police licence.

**Location:** -12.9362, -38.4632

**Categories:** Candomblé temples, National heritage sites of Bahia, Afro-Brazilian heritage, Religious buildings and structures in Salvador, Bahia

## Introduction

Fifteen and a half hectares of Atlantic Forest still stand inside Salvador, at Travessa de São Jorge 65 in the district of Mata Escura. They are there because a religious community has held that ground since 1916. The Terreiro do Bate Folha — Mansu Banduquenqué in the liturgical language of its nation, and Sociedade Beneficente Santa Bárbara do Bate Folha on its paperwork — is described as holding the largest surviving urban stand of Atlantic Forest in the city. The trees are not a garden around a temple. They are part of what the federal heritage listing protects, and part of what the house is.

## Bernardino

Manoel Bernardino da Paixão was born in Salvador in 1881. He was initiated into Congo traditions by Manoel Encoce, a Muxicongo priest — a man of the Kongo — who had himself been initiated in Africa, and he received the dijina Ampumandezu. After Encoce died, Bernardino went to live in the house of his close friend Maria Genoveva do Bonfim, Tuhenda diá Nzambi, known throughout Bahia as Maria Neném and regarded as the mother of Angola in the state. Her own spiritual father was Roberto Barros Reis, an enslaved Angolan man who went by the surname of the family that held him. On 13 June 1910 Bernardino fulfilled an obligation on the same day that Manoel Ciríaco de Jesus, born 8 August 1892, was initiated; the two were bound together from then on, and Ciríaco would go on to lead the house now known as Tumba Junsara.

## The House at Mata Escura

By the time Bernardino founded his own house in 1916, he was already well known. He established it in Mata Escura do Retiro, on land ringed by trees that were old then and are older now, and the ground was presented to Bamburucema, the house's second muquixi; the first had been Lemba. Thirteen years later, on 4 December 1929, he brought out his first barco — the group of initiates who pass through the rites together — and its rianga, the first son of the house, was João Correia de Mello, also of Lemba. The name Bate Folha translates roughly as strike the leaf. In a tradition where leaves carry medicine, initiation and the presence of the deities, a forest is not a backdrop.

## Nzambi's Vocabulary

Candomblé is not one religion but several, organised into nations that arrived from different parts of Africa and kept their own languages. Ketu houses use Yoruba and call their deities orixás; Jeje houses use Ewe and honour voduns. Bate Folha belongs to the Bantu tradition built by Kongo and Mbundu people, in which the supreme being is Nzambi and the deities are the inquices — nkisi, from a Kongo word meaning receptacle. Its liturgical vocabulary comes from Kikongo and Kimbundu. Priests hold the title tata, priestesses mameto. Bernardino's own lineage explains the house's particular blend: through Manoel Encoce it is Congo, and through Maria Neném it holds Angola. From the street those distinctions are invisible. Inside the house they are the difference between one inheritance and another.

## Under Licence

The Brazilian penal code of 1890 criminalised curandeirismo, feitiçaria and false medicine, and the courts used those articles against African-derived worship. The 1891 constitution guaranteed freedom of religion; it did not stop the prosecutions. In Bahia the arrangement that emerged was more humiliating than a straight ban: terreiros were permitted to hold ceremonies, but had to pay the police for a licence covering that specific date. Worship was free in the constitution and rentable from the vice squad in practice. Bate Folha's whole first half-century ran through that period — founded in 1916, first barco in 1929, growing steadily while the state treated its members as a policing problem rather than a congregation. The people who kept the calendar of obligations under those conditions were taking a real risk, repeatedly, for decades.

## Listed, 2003

On 10 October 2003 IPHAN, the federal heritage institute, listed the Terreiro do Bate Folha. The precedent had been set in the 1980s, when the Casa Branca terreiro became the first site of Black religious practice in Brazil granted federal protection — an argument that had to be won, since the monument lists had until then been reserved largely for churches and colonial mansions. The listing at Bate Folha covers the grounds, forest included, which is why the trees at Travessa de São Jorge outlasted the neighbourhoods that grew up around them. The house is presided over today by Tata Muguanxi, Cícero Rodrigues Franco Lima. Its congregation has been keeping this ground for more than a century, through criminalisation, licensing and finally recognition — and the recognition, when it came, protected what they had been protecting all along.

## Flight Context

Located at 12.936 S, 38.463 W in Mata Escura, in Salvador's inland eastern districts, roughly 5.7 km northeast of the Pelourinho and 16 km southwest of Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães International Airport (SBSV / SSA). The terreiro is easy to identify from the air precisely because of what surrounds it: a dark, closed block of mature Atlantic Forest — about 15.5 hectares — set inside continuous low-rise urban fabric, one of the few unbroken green masses left in the interior of the city. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,500 ft AGL. Traffic inbound to SBSV from the south passes nearby. Year-round VFR with heavy afternoon convection in the April-July wet season, when the forest canopy holds visible moisture after rain.
