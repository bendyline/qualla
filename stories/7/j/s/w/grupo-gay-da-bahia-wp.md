# Grupo Gay da Bahia

Founded in Salvador in 1980, Brazil's oldest surviving LGBTQ organisation began counting the country's murdered gay and trans people in 1982, because no government agency was doing it.

**Location:** -12.9733, -38.5078

**Categories:** LGBTQ organisations in Brazil, Human rights organisations based in Brazil, Organisations based in Salvador, Bahia, LGBTQ history in Brazil

## Introduction

In 1982, a small activist group in Salvador published a bulletin with an unremarkable title - Pesquisa sobre Homossexuais Assassinados no Brasil, Research on Homosexuals Murdered in Brazil - and an unusual contents page. It was a list of names. People who had been killed, gathered from newspaper clippings and letters, because there was no other place the information existed. Brazil's government did not count these deaths. It still, for many years afterward, did not. So the Grupo Gay da Bahia counted them, and has gone on counting them for four decades, publishing an annual report that has become the country's most-cited figure on anti-LGBTQ violence precisely because the official alternative is a blank page.

## Pelourinho, 1980

The group was founded in 1980 and remains the oldest Brazilian association of its kind still in operation - no small thing in a country then only beginning to emerge from sixteen years of military rule. Its headquarters sit in Pelourinho, Salvador's colonial heart, a district whose name comes from the whipping post where enslaved people were publicly punished. Among the founders, the anthropologist Luiz Mott is the one whose name travelled. Born in São Paulo in 1946, trained in social sciences at the University of São Paulo, with a master's from the Sorbonne and a doctorate from Unicamp, he became a professor in the anthropology department at the Federal University of Bahia and holds a permanent position as the group's honorary president. His scholarship and his activism ran on the same track: he wrote about same-sex desire among the Bororo, Guató, Trumai, Tupinambá, Wai Wai and Xavante, and about intimate partner violence among LGBTQ people in Salvador.

## Unmaking a Diagnosis

By its first anniversary in 1981 the group had a record to publish. It had campaigned to end the classification of homosexuality as a deviation and a mental disorder. It had marked International Gay Pride Day with lectures, graffiti and leaflets. It had organised caravans to cities across the Northeast, carrying the argument to places where nobody was making it. That first campaign was the consequential one. Brazilian medicine still carried paragraph 302.0 of the INAMPS health code, the administrative line that made homosexuality a diagnosable condition, and the group helped build the political support that removed it. Whatever else the organisation did afterward, it had participated in the deletion of a sentence that had licensed a great deal of harm.

## The Work Nobody Was Paid For

Recognition followed. In 1988 the group was appointed to the National AIDS Commission of Brazil's Ministry of Health, at a moment when governments elsewhere were still deciding whether the epidemic warranted a response. Since 1995 it has been a member of the International Gay and Lesbian Human Rights Commission, which the same year gave Mott its Felipa de Souza Award. In 1991 the group created the Troféu Triângulo Rosa, the Pink Triangle Award, considered the first LGBTQIA+ award in Brazil - a deliberate reclaiming of the badge the Nazis used to mark gay prisoners. In 2013 the organisation received Brazil's Order of Cultural Merit.

## How the Count Is Made, and Contested

The annual report is compiled the only way an unfunded NGO can compile it: from press accounts and direct correspondence, case by case, in the absence of government statistics. That method has limits, and critics have pressed on them. In 2015 the journalist Dario Di Martino published a case-by-case review of the 2013 report. In 2017 a civil servant, Daniel Reynaldo, began a blog examining cases the group had classified as LGBTphobic killings, and the geneticist Eli Vieira later led a similar review of the 2016 data. All three accept that killings motivated by homophobia genuinely occur in Brazil. Their argument is that a majority of the cases counted as hate crimes cannot be honestly linked to the victim's sexuality - that a gay man murdered in a robbery has been murdered, but not necessarily for being gay. The group's figures are widely reported in Brazilian media, and are not official statistics.

## The People in the Column

The dispute over methodology is worth stating plainly, and so is what sits underneath it. Whatever the correct proportion, the entries are not data points. Each one is a person who was killed, whose family learned of it, whose name reached the group by way of a newspaper column or a letter from someone who knew them. When the clipping began in 1982, no institution in Brazil was assembling that information, which meant that in any official sense the deaths left no trace. Counting them was an assertion that they should. Four decades on, the most durable thing the Grupo Gay da Bahia built may not be the number at the top of each year's report but the practice of keeping the list at all - the insistence that these lives be written down somewhere, by someone, even if it had to be by volunteers working out of Pelourinho with no budget for the task.

## Flight Context

The organisation's headquarters are in Pelourinho at 12.97°S, 38.51°W, in the Cidade Alta of Salvador above the Bay of All Saints. From the air, Pelourinho is the compact grid of pastel colonial rooftops on the escarpment north of the Lacerda Elevator, with the Bay of All Saints on one side and the dense modern city inland - the twin towers of São Francisco and the Cathedral Basilica are the clearest markers. A pass at 2,000 to 3,000 feet along the bay shoreline shows the full drop between the Cidade Alta and the port below. Nearest airport is Salvador Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães (SBSV), about 14 nautical miles northeast. Salvador offers good visibility for most of the year, with the wettest stretch from April to July and reliable sea-breeze cumulus building through the afternoon.
