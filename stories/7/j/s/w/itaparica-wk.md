# Itaparica

The largest of the ninety-one islands in the Bay of All Saints, where sugar, whaling and Brazil's first steam engine met a three-day battle that helped decide the war for independence.

**Location:** -12.9972, -38.6678

**Categories:** Islands of Bahia, Bay of All Saints, Brazilian War of Independence, Beaches of Brazil

## Introduction

João Ubaldo Ribeiro was born on Itaparica in January 1941 and carried off it two months later, and he spent much of his writing life bringing the island back. Viva o Povo Brasileiro, the 1984 novel he later self-translated as An Invincible Memory, runs its version of Brazilian history through these sugar lands and beaches, told from below - the enslaved, the poor, the stubbornly unimpressed. The island can carry that weight. It is the largest of the ninety-one islands in the Bay of All Saints, lying across the entrance like a breakwater, an hour by ferry from Salvador and a long way from it in most other respects.

## The Big Island in a Big Bay

The Bay of All Saints covers 1,223 square kilometres, the largest bay in Brazil, and Itaparica sits at its mouth roughly ten kilometres from Salvador. The island runs more than 36 kilometres from end to end but barely rises above the water; its highest point is about fifteen metres, so from a boat it reads as a long green line broken by church towers and palms. Forty kilometres of beach edge it. Two municipalities share the ground - Vera Cruz holds 87 percent of the land, the town of Itaparica the remaining 13 - a division that dates from 1962, when the old single municipality was carved up. Getting there means choosing a boat. The small passenger ferry leaves from near the Mercado Modelo in Salvador; the car ferry runs from a terminal a couple of kilometres north across to Bom Despacho. Once ashore, vans work the island routes, and flagging one down from the correct side of the road remains the cheapest way to travel.

## Sparkling Stone

Nobody agrees on the name. The Tupi scholar Eduardo de Almeida Navarro reads Itaparica as itápirika, flint - stone that throws sparks. Teodoro Sampaio argued instead for 'stone enclosure.' Frederico Edelweiss suspected Portuguese settlers bent whatever they heard toward Caparica, a place they already knew outside Lisbon. What is certain is the date the Europeans arrived: Amerigo Vespucci entered the bay on All Saints' Day, 1 November 1501, which is how the water got its name. Jesuits founded a settlement at Baiacu on the island in 1560. Itaparica was never allowed to become a backwater after that. It sat too close to the colonial capital, too plainly in view from the Cidade Alta, and too squarely across the shipping channel to be ignored.

## Sugar, Whales and the First Steam Engine

The Recôncavo around the bay grew cane, and Itaparica grew it too, on estates worked by enslaved Africans and their descendants. Whaling ran alongside the sugar and continued into the middle of the nineteenth century, the bay's whales taken and rendered within sight of the plantations. Then, in 1815, the Ingá-Açu estate installed the first steam engine in Brazil - an industrial revolution arriving on an island most maps treated as scenery. The machine gets remembered. The people who cut the cane that made the estate rich enough to buy it usually do not, and Ribeiro's novels are in part an argument about exactly that omission.

## The Island That Held

The war for Brazilian independence was decided in Bahia later than anywhere else, and Itaparica ended up on the winning side of it. Portuguese forces came for the island on 7 January 1823 with a fleet of 42 vessels and were beaten off in three days by the Itaparica Battalion under Antônio de Sousa Lima, fighting from trenches dug across every landing beach. What the island got out of the victory was a permanent adjective. An imperial decree of 25 October 1831 named the seat Denodada Vila de Itaparica — the dauntless town — and the phrase is still in official use, which makes for an odd sort of memorial: a municipality going about its business under a compliment paid to its great-grandparents. Bahia's war ended on 2 July 1823, and the island keeps that date with the rest of the state.

## Weekend Boats

Something over twenty thousand people live in the municipality of Itaparica, more again across the line in Vera Cruz, and the numbers swell every time Salvador decides it needs a weekend away. The town's original draw was medicinal — mineral water rising from a spring in the middle of it, taken by visitors who came for their health — and the modern one is plainer: forty kilometres of beach, warm shallow water, and a boat back to the city whenever you have had enough. The parish church of the Santíssimo Sacramento, finished in 1794, still anchors the centre. What Ribeiro understood, and put in the novel, is that none of this is scenery. Sugar, whales, war, church bells and ferry timetables all happened to the same families, on the same forty kilometres of sand, and mostly to people whose names never made it into the official account.

## Flight Context

Located at 12.997 S, 38.668 W, across the entrance to the Bay of All Saints roughly 39 km southwest of Salvador's Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães International Airport (SBSV / SSA). The island is unmistakable from the air: a low green landmass more than 36 kilometres long with a maximum elevation near 15 metres, separated from the Salvador peninsula by the ferry channel. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-4,000 ft AGL, which keeps both the island's western beaches and the Salvador skyline across the water in frame. Ferry wakes between Bom Despacho and the city mark the crossing. Tropical maritime climate with good VFR most of the year; heavy convective showers are common on autumn and winter afternoons, and haze off the bay can soften the horizon in the late dry season.
