# Ladeira da Preguiça

Salvador's Slope of Laziness was named by the people who watched enslaved porters haul sixty-kilo sacks up it — a joke that outlived them and became a stereotype about all of Bahia.

**Location:** -12.9771, -38.5161

**Categories:** Streets in Brazil, Historic Centre of Salvador, Afro-Brazilian heritage, Tourist attractions in Salvador, Bahia

## Introduction

“Sobe, preguiça!” — climb, laziness — came down from the windows of the good houses while men bent double under sacks weighing as much as sixty kilos worked their way up the hill. That is one account of how a street in Salvador got its name. The Ladeira da Preguiça still climbs from the harbour toward the upper city through the old Dois de Julho neighbourhood, and the joke embedded in it has outlasted everyone who made it. The Slope of Laziness was named for the exhaustion of people who had no choice about the climb, by people who watched them from the shade.

## Three Ways Up

Salvador is built on two levels, and the gap between them is the city's central fact. Ships, warehouses and money sat at the water's edge; churches, government and the merchant houses sat on the escarpment above. Everything that landed had to go up. The ladeiras — steep ramped streets — were the machinery that made that possible, and the Preguiça was one of the first three built, probably in the seventeenth century, opened shortly after the Ladeira da Misericórdia and the Ladeira da Conceição. Crates, barrels, sugar, imported goods for the shops of the Cidade Alta: all of it moved on the backs of enslaved people, or in ox-carts that enslaved men pushed from behind when the grade beat the animals.

## Whose Laziness

Two explanations of the name survive, and the difference between them is the whole story. In the first, the elite families whose houses lined the route entertained themselves by shouting encouragement at the porters below. In the second — the version repeated by Salvador's own tourism authority — it was the enslaved workers who named it, complaining that the climb gave them preguiça. One version has the powerful mocking the powerless; the other quietly transfers the laziness to the people doing the lifting. That second reading has had a long career. Scholars have written about how Bahian tourism advertising built an “imagined community” around the stereotype of the easygoing, unhurried baiano, and the Ladeira da Preguiça is exhibit one: a name that turns forced labour into a personality trait.

## The Coast It Named

You can measure how much traffic once moved through here by what took the slope's name. The shore below, in what is now the Dois de Julho neighbourhood, was called the Litoral da Preguiça — the Coast of Laziness. Streets rarely name coastlines. This one did, because for two centuries the Preguiça was among the few ways goods and people crossed between the lower and upper cities, and everyone knew where it was.

## The Elevator Took the Traffic

In 1873 the Elevador Lacerda opened, carrying passengers straight up between the Cidade Baixa and the Cidade Alta, and the old slopes lost their reason to exist. Later roads finished the job, taking the freight onto easier gradients. The Preguiça emptied out. For most of the twentieth century it was a corridor of shuttered and collapsing houses — colonial façades with sky behind the windows — close enough to the historic centre to be visible, poor enough to be skipped.

## Gil's Song, and What Came Back

Gilberto Gil wrote “Ladeira da Preguiça” in 1971, during the three-year London exile that ended with his return to Brazil the following year. The song has been recorded by many Brazilian singers since, Elis Regina among them, and it did for the slope what the ladeira had once done for the beach: it carried the name outward, to people who would never climb it. Something else has happened more recently. After decades of abandonment, residents and artists have pulled the street back into use, and the ladeira as a whole is now treated as a cultural centre — a working address for music and performance rather than a ruin with a famous name. The climb is still hard. Nobody living on it now is carrying sixty kilos up for someone else's amusement.

## Flight Context

Located at 12.977 S, 38.516 W, in the Dois de Julho district on the escarpment edge of central Salvador, about 1.7 km southwest of the Pelourinho and 22 km southwest of Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães International Airport (SBSV / SSA). From the air the landmark is the escarpment itself: the abrupt step between the port strip of the Cidade Baixa and the ridge holding the historic centre, with the Elevador Lacerda's shaft standing at the same edge a short distance northeast. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500-2,500 ft AGL for the terraced street pattern climbing the slope. Tropical climate, reliably VFR, with afternoon convection off All Saints' Bay in the wetter months and glare off the water on late westward approaches.
