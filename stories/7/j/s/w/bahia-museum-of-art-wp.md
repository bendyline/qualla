# Bahia Museum of Art

Bahia's first museum, founded in 1918, now occupies a 1920s palace assembled partly from salvaged pieces of older buildings - a doorway from 1674, a staircase rail lifted from a ruined convent.

**Location:** -12.9936, -38.5259

**Categories:** Art museums and galleries in Brazil, Museums in Salvador, Bahia, 1918 establishments in Brazil, Decorative arts

## Introduction

The stone doorway is older than the building it stands in. Carved in 1674, it was rescued from the Solar João de Aguiar Matos and set into the Palácio da Vitória, a neocolonial pile of reinforced concrete raised between 1924 and 1927. The staircase railing came from somewhere else entirely - the Convento de Santo Antônio do Paraguaçu, upriver in the Recôncavo. Portuguese azulejos from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries cover the walls. Before the Museu de Arte da Bahia displays a single painting, the house itself is already a collage of salvaged Bahia, which is a fitting way to enter a collection assembled the same way: piece by piece, over more than a century, from whatever the state could preserve.

## Law 1,255

The museum was created by law on 23 July 1918, under Governor Antônio Muniz Sodré de Aragão, as the Museu do Estado da Bahia. It was the first museological institution in the state and among the first ten in Brazil - a genuinely early date for a country that had only ended slavery thirty years before and become a republic in 1889. Francisco Borges de Barros, its principal founder, ran it from 1918 to 1930. The institution that José Antônio do Prado Valladares inherited in 1939 was still finding its shape, and over twenty years he consolidated it as a museum of the visual arts and began cataloguing the holdings systematically. Nearly every subsequent director left a mark, but Valladares decided what the museum would be about.

## Three Addresses

A museum with no purpose-built home spends its life moving. The collection was installed in the Solar Pacífico Ferreira in 1931 and opened to the public there, staying until 1946. It then shifted to the Palacete Góis Calmon, which the state had acquired in 1943 and which came with the decorative arts collection of the former governor whose name it carried - a bequest that still forms part of the holdings. That address held until 1982. On 5 November of that year, under the directorship of the sculptor and curator Emanoel Araújo, the museum reopened in the Palácio da Vitória on Avenida Sete de Setembro, a building that had begun life as headquarters for the state's education and health administration. It has stayed there since.

## The Bahian School

Bahia produced painters, and the museum is where you go to see them. José Joaquim da Rocha is represented by twelve double-sided panels showing scenes of the Passion on one face and angels on the other - work made for a church ceiling, now readable from both sides in a way its original setting never allowed. José Teófilo de Jesus contributes an allegorical series personifying the four continents, that eighteenth-century convention in which the world is reduced to four women with attributes. Manuel Lopes Rodrigues brought back the polish of academic Paris. Presciliano Silva painted the interiors of churches and convents, which means the museum holds pictures of the rooms some of its own architectural fragments came from. Around them sit European canvases from the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries - Italian, French, Spanish, Flemish, Dutch and English.

## Peacocks, Roosters and Jacarandá

Roughly five thousand works and something over six thousand objects fill the galleries, and the decorative arts are where Bahia's trading past becomes tangible. The porcelain is largely Chinese export ware of the Qing dynasty, including the two named services known as the Peacock Service and the Rooster Service; European pieces from Sèvres and from Jacob Petit fill out the cabinets. The furniture runs baroque in the eighteenth century and neoclassical in the nineteenth, worked in native hardwoods - jacarandá dark as coffee, vinhático warm and gold. The silver is mostly Bahian-made: liturgical vessels, neoclassical salvers, ornamental work of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Among the religious sculpture are colonial wooden images from Bahia and Portugal, and ivory figures from Goa and Ceylon (now Sri Lanka), carried down the same sea routes that brought the porcelain.

## Doors Opened Twice

A library of some twelve thousand volumes supports the collection, and the exhibition programme keeps the building from becoming a mausoleum - in 2017 the museum mounted a show of work by Judite Pimentel. Recognition arrived in 2019, when the Brazilian Regional Museology Council awarded it the Diploma and Medal for Museological Merit. Then came 2020, and the year that closed museums everywhere. The Museu de Arte da Bahia joined Google Arts and Culture, with an adapted Street View walkthrough of its interiors, so that a collection which had spent a century being physically moved from house to house could finally be visited without going anywhere at all.

## Flight Context

The museum stands at 12.99°S, 38.53°W on Avenida Sete de Setembro in the Vitória district of Salvador, on the high ground of the Cidade Alta above the Bay of All Saints. From the air the district reads as a green, low-rise ridge of walled gardens and older mansions between the dense towers of Campo Grande and the Barra headland with its lighthouse - the museum itself is a modest cream neocolonial block, not a landmark you spot unaided, so navigate by the shoreline instead. The Bay of All Saints, the Lacerda Elevator and the Farol da Barra are the reliable visual anchors. A pass at 2,000 to 3,000 feet along the bay side gives the best view of the escarpment the city is built on. Nearest airport is Salvador Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães (SBSV), about 13 nautical miles northeast. Salvador enjoys good visibility most of the year; the wettest months are April through July, and afternoon sea-breeze cumulus is common.
