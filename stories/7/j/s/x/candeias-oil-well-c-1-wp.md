# Candeias Oil Well (C-1)

The Bahian well that turned Brazilian oil from a rumour into an industry, and gave Getúlio Vargas the stage for the campaign that created Petrobras.

**Location:** -12.7050, -38.5665

**Categories:** Bahia, Industrial heritage, Petroleum, Brazilian history, Petrobras

## Introduction

The drilling rig had already made history once, and once had not been enough. In January 1939 it stood at Lobato, a district on the northern edge of Salvador, and brought up the first oil ever found in Brazil. The country celebrated, and then the celebration cooled, because Lobato's oil could not be worked at a profit. It was a discovery, not a business. Two years later the same machine was hauled roughly twenty-five kilometres north into the scrub of the Recôncavo, outside the town of Candeias, and set turning again. On 13 December 1941 it found oil a second time. This time the oil paid.

## Sixty Years of Clay

Brazilians had been hunting for oil since the empire. Dom Pedro II granted an Englishman, Thomas Denny Sargent, the right to extract oil and other minerals in the south of Bahia, and nothing came of it. In 1892 Eugênio Camargo put a drill into Bahian ground and pulled up clayey sand. By 1907 the Mineralogical Service of Brazil had joined the effort and widened the areas under analysis, which for a long while only meant widening the territory in which nothing was found. The search outlasted the empire and it outlasted the First Republic. Generations of prospectors spent their working lives on the proposition that a country that size had to have oil under it somewhere, and died without seeing it proved.

## The Well Called C-1

Work at Candeias began on 2 April 1941, though the bit did not actually turn until 30 June. The well carried an unglamorous name — Candeias 1, C-1 — and it was completed on 31 December, two and a half weeks after the discovery it is remembered for. What separated C-1 from Lobato was arithmetic as much as geology. Lobato had proved that Brazilian oil existed; Candeias proved that Brazilian oil could be pumped and sold. C-1 became the first commercial oil well drilled on Brazilian soil and the heart of the country's first oil field. The distinction sounds like bookkeeping, and retellings blur it constantly, but it is the whole point. Nineteen thirty-nine gave Brazil hope. Nineteen forty-one gave it an industry.

## The Oil Is Ours

A field needs somewhere to send its crude. Bahia's oil workers' union, Sindipetro, recorded how the Candeias discovery and the development that followed pushed the country toward building a refinery of its own, and in 1950 the Landulpho Alves refinery rose at São Francisco do Conde, the next municipality over. On 23 June 1952, President Getúlio Vargas came to Candeias in person and used the visit to argue for a state monopoly over Brazilian petroleum and for a national oil company to run it. The campaign behind him had a slogan that every Brazilian still recognises: o petróleo é nosso, the oil is ours. Petrobras was created on 3 October 1953, inheriting a country that produced roughly 2,700 barrels a day.

## What the Monument Marks

The Instituto do Patrimônio Artístico e Cultural da Bahia listed the well as protected heritage in 2011, on the reasoning that you cannot explain Petrobras without it. Beside the wellhead, Petrobras built a monument dedicated not to executives or geologists but to the first oil workers — the men who ran that secondhand rig through the second half of 1941 with no assurance that this hole would end any differently from the sixty years of holes before it. Their refinery outlived the monopoly the well helped create: Petrobras sold Landulpho Alves in 2021, and it operates today under the name Mataripe. The wellhead itself is a modest thing to stand in front of. Very little about Brazil's twentieth century makes sense without it.

## Flight Context

Candeias sits at 12.70°S, 38.57°W, on the northern shore of the Baía de Todos os Santos in the Recôncavo Baiano. From the air the landmark is not the well but the industrial geography it produced: refinery stacks and tank farms strung between Candeias and São Francisco do Conde, with the broad grey-green bay to the south and Salvador's skyline on the far shore. Salvador's Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães International Airport (SBSV) lies about 35 km southeast. A pass at 2,500–4,000 ft AGL shows the bay, the mangrove fringe and the petrochemical belt in one frame. Coastal haze and afternoon convection are common in the Recôncavo's wet months, roughly April through July; mornings give the cleanest visibility.
