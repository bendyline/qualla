# Feira de Santana

A city of more than 600,000 that began as a place where cattle drovers stopped to trade, and has never stopped being a crossroads.

**Location:** -12.2669, -38.9669

**Categories:** Bahia, Cities, Travel guides, Brazilian history, Festivals

## Introduction

A chapel, a cattle road, and somewhere to stop — that is the entire founding recipe. The owners of the Fazenda Santana dos Olhos d'Água raised a chapel in honour of Nossa Senhora, Sant'Ana and São Domingos on land the estrada das boiadas already crossed, the road the drovers used to move herds toward the coast. Drovers stopped. Travellers stopped. A little fair formed around the stopping, and the fair gave the place its name: Feira de Santana, Saint Anne's Fair. Two centuries on, 616,272 people live inside that fair.

## Where the Cattle Road Paused

The settlement became a vila on 13 November 1832 — some sources date it to 9 May 1833 instead — and in 1873 a provincial law elevated it to city status under a name that left nothing to interpretation: Cidade Comercial de Feira de Sant'Anna, the Commercial City of Saint Anne's Fair. Commerce was written into the charter because commerce was the only reason the place existed. The cattle fair drew ranchers off the sertão and buyers up from the coast, and the town grew in the space between them. Ruy Barbosa gave it the nickname it still carries when he called it the Princesa do Sertão in 1919 — Princess of the Backlands, a title that flatters the interior rather than the coast, which was rather the point.

## Two Statues, Two Centuries

Feira de Santana has monumentalised its own logistics. One statue honours the vaqueiro, the leather-clad cowboy of the northeastern backlands who drove the herds that made the fair. Another honours the truck driver. Between those two figures lies everything the city has been: an animal-powered market town that became a highway junction without ever changing its function. Today it is the second-most populous municipality in Bahia and the largest city in the interior of Brazil's north and northeast, home to the State University of Feira de Santana, a theatre scene around the Amélio Amorim Cultural Center, and a bohemian quarter along Rua São Domingos that locals will tell you is where the city's nightlife was born.

## Still a Junction

The BR-116, BR-101 and BR-324 meet in this neighbourhood, with the BR-324 running duplicated down to Salvador, and a ring road threads the traffic around the city rather than through it. The bus station handles services to Salvador, Vitória da Conquista, Itabuna, Aracaju, Recife, Brasília, Belém, Fortaleza and across the border to Asunción. That last destination explains Feiraguay, a vast market of imported goods — Chinese, Korean, Japanese, all of it carried up from Paraguay by shuttle traders — reckoned one of the three largest of its kind in Brazil. Gov. João Durval Carneiro Airport (SDIY) sits on the edge of town but runs no scheduled flights; for a plane ticket you go to Salvador.

## Two Weeks After Easter

The Micareta de Feira started in April 1937 and became one of Brazil's first out-of-season carnivals. It still lands two weeks after Easter, and it now bills itself as the largest such festival anywhere. September belongs to ExpoFeira, the agricultural exhibition at the Parque João Martins da Silva, where the cattle fair's descendants still parade their animals. In June the celebration moves out to the rural districts, where São João de Maria Quitéria and São Pedro de Humildes keep the winter festival cycle alive in the countryside that fed the city in the first place.

## The Soldier from Licurizeiro

One of those districts carries the name of a woman born there on 27 July 1792. Maria Quitéria grew up at Licurizeiro in the parish of São José das Itapororocas, now part of Feira de Santana. In October 1822, with Bahia still held by Portuguese forces and the war for Brazilian independence underway, she enlisted disguised as a man and served in the Parakeet Battalion until June 1823. She was promoted to cadet that July and to lieutenant in August, and Emperor Pedro I decorated her with the Imperial Order of the Southern Cross. She is recognised as the first woman to serve in a military unit in Brazil, and she is sometimes called the Brazilian Joan of Arc — a comparison she did not live to enjoy. She died in Salvador on 21 August 1853, poor and largely forgotten, and was rediscovered by her country decades later.

## Flight Context

Feira de Santana lies at 12.27°S, 38.97°W, roughly 100 km northwest of Salvador on the transition between the coastal zone and the dry interior. From altitude the city reads as a hub in the literal sense: a dense urban mass with highways radiating in five directions across pale, scrubby terrain, the green thinning noticeably as you look west. Gov. João Durval Carneiro Airport (SDIY) serves general aviation on the city's edge; the nearest commercial field is Salvador's Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães International (SBSV), about 100 km southeast. Overfly at 4,000–6,000 ft AGL to hold the whole road network in view. Visibility is generally good; dust haze can build in the dry months from roughly October to February.
