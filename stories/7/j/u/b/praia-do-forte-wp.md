# Praia do Forte

A Bahian beach named for a fortified house that took seventy-three years to build, anchored an estate the size of a tenth of Brazil, and now stands in ruins above a sea-turtle sanctuary.

**Location:** -12.5775, -38.0053

**Categories:** Bahia, Beaches, Colonial architecture, Wildlife conservation, Historic ruins

## Introduction

The beach is named for a fort, the fort is a ruin, and the ruin took seventy-three years to build. Work on the Casa da Torre began in 1551 and did not finish until 1624 — a fortified tower house on the coast north of Salvador, raised as an advanced lookout for a colonial capital barely two years old and as the seat of a family estate that would eventually be measured not in hectares but in fractions of Brazil.

## One Tenth of a Country

Garcia de Sousa d'Ávila, who lived from 1528 to 1609, was set to the task by Tomé de Sousa, Brazil's first governor-general. What began as a coastal watchpost became the headquarters of something without real parallel in the colony. Over generations the family's holdings stretched from Bahia to Maranhão, covering something on the order of 800,000 square kilometres — roughly a tenth of modern Brazil. The wealth came from sugar, from cattle driven deep into the sertão, and from the enslavement of Indigenous people whose labour made that territory profitable and whose names the estate's records mostly did not keep. It was from this house that herds first pushed inland, and out of that push came the vaqueiro, the leather-armoured cowboy who remains the emblematic figure of the Brazilian northeast.

## Seventy-Three Years, Then Ruin

The building went up in the fortified style of a much older Europe, thick-walled and defensive, with a hexagonal tower that today shelters a chapel dedicated to Nossa Senhora da Conceição. Its decline was legal before it was physical. Brazil abolished the morgado — the entailed estate that had kept the d'Ávila holdings intact across generations — in 1835, and the fortunes of the family drained away in the decades after independence. The house was progressively abandoned to the salt air. In 1938 the national heritage institute IPHAN listed what remained, and the ruins now stand open to visitors, maintained by the privately run Fundação Garcia d'Ávila. Roofless, windowless, still enormous, the walls give a better sense of colonial ambition than any restored building could.

## Thirty Thousand Hectares

For most of its later history Praia do Forte was simply a fishing village under the ruin, in the municipality of Mata de São João, some eighty kilometres up the coast from Salvador. That changed in 1970, when Klaus Peters and his associates bought thirty thousand hectares here. A pousada and hotel followed in the 1980s, and the village turned toward tourism, which is now essentially the whole economy. Mata de São João named a municipal park after Peters in 2013. The result is frankly a resort — tourist-oriented without any disguise, and priced accordingly — but it remains one of the most agreeable stretches of coast in the northeast, and it is a considerably gentler place to spend an afternoon than central Salvador.

## Tartaruga Marinha

Projeto TAMAR was founded in January 1980 by a group that included Admiral Ibsen de Gusmão Câmara, Maria Thereza Jorge Pádua and Guy Marcovaldi, several of them oceanography students who had spent the 1970s camping on remote nesting beaches. The name is a contraction of tartaruga marinha, sea turtle, shortened to fit on the small metal tags clipped to flippers to track migration — a conservation programme named after the size of its own record-keeping. Praia do Forte became its national headquarters, and the visitor station sits on the sand beside the church and the lighthouse. The project now runs twenty-two bases along more than 1,100 kilometres of Brazilian coast, and in its first thirty-five years it returned more than twenty-five million hatchlings to the sea.

## Rain Without a Season

This is a tropical rainforest climate in the Köppen sense: no month here is genuinely dry. September is the least wet, April through June the wettest, and temperatures stay nearly flat across the year, which means the difference between seasons is measured in how often the sky opens rather than how hot it gets. At low tide the reef flats fill with tide pools clear enough to snorkel without a mask. A couple of kilometres inland the Sapiranga reserve protects Atlantic forest, mangrove, dunes and lagoons full of birds. Offshore, the Instituto Baleia Jubarte keeps a research base — humpbacks breed along this coast, and it is not unusual to see one surface beyond the breakers while standing on the beach.

## Flight Context

Praia do Forte lies at 12.58°S, 38.01°W on Bahia's northern coast, about 50 km northeast of Salvador's Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães International Airport (SBSV). The visual cues from the air are unmistakable: a straight run of white sand backed by dense green, the pale stone rectangle of the Casa da Torre ruin standing on rising ground a short distance inland, and the reef line drawing a broken white seam offshore at low tide. The BA-099 coastal highway — the Linha Verde — parallels the beach. Fly the coast at 1,500–3,000 ft AGL for the ruin and the reef flats together. Morning offers the best light and calmest air; convective build-ups are routine on wet-season afternoons from April to June.
