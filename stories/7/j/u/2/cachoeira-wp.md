# Cachoeira

The sugar, cotton, and tobacco town on the Paraguaçu that Brazil declared a national monument - and where Ana Nery, the mother of Brazilian nursing, was born.

**Location:** -12.6178, -38.9558

**Categories:** Municipalities in Bahia, National heritage sites of Bahia, Populated places established in 1837

## Introduction

The town's name means waterfall, though there is no single cataract to look for. Cachoeira sits inland on the Paraguaçu River in the Recôncavo Baiano, the humid valley of sugarcane and tobacco that was Brazil's most productive colonial hinterland, and almost every street in its historic center is under federal protection. IPHAN - Brazil's National Institute of Historic and Artistic Heritage - has designated the whole historic center a national monument. Colonial churches, terraced sobrado houses, sugar mills at the edge of town, and more than a dozen candomblé terreiros under state or federal protection are packed into a footprint you can walk across in an afternoon. It exports sugar, cotton, and tobacco the way it has for three hundred years.

## A Town Made of Sobrados

The urban fabric of Cachoeira is a catalog of Portuguese-Brazilian colonial architecture. Walk Rua Ana Nery and the numbered houses read like a ledger: Sobrado 1, Sobrado 2, Sobrado 25, each a two- or three-story merchant residence with a shop at ground level and family quarters above. The Sobrado of João R. Adorno, the Estrela Manor House (Solar Estrela), and the houses around Praça da Aclamação have all been individually listed. The Casa de Câmara e Cadeia - the combined town hall and jail that every colonial Brazilian municipality was required to build - still presides over the old center. The Praça Dr. Aristides Mílton Fountain marks the meeting point of streets that have held their grid since the 18th century. IPHAN's decision to protect the entire historic ensemble, rather than individual monuments, is what has kept the colonial streetscape intact where so many other Recôncavo towns have lost theirs to concrete and rebar.

## The Birthplace of Brazilian Nursing

One doorway on Rua Ana Nery carries an unusually quiet weight. It is the birthplace of Ana Justina Ferreira Nery, born here in 1814 and remembered across Brazil as a mãe dos brasileiros - mother of the Brazilians - for her service as a nurse during the Paraguayan War from 1865 to 1870. Nery had already lost a husband to the war and seen her sons enlist when, at the age of 51, she wrote to the president of the province asking to join the army not as a soldier but as a caregiver for the wounded. She served in field hospitals in the far interior for five years, often under fire, founding the improvised wards that later became formal military hospitals. When she returned to Brazil, the government decorated her and paid for her education expenses; she is now considered the symbolic founder of modern Brazilian nursing, and the federal nursing school in Rio de Janeiro bears her name. The Casa Natal de Ana Nery is a modest Cachoeira townhouse. It is also one of the most consequential addresses in the country.

## Candomblé Terreiros Under Federal Seal

What sets Cachoeira apart among Brazilian colonial towns is what lies at the edges of its old center: the terreiros. Candomblé - the Afro-Brazilian religion carried across the Middle Passage and woven into Bahian life - has terreiros here that trace their lineages back two centuries or more. The Terreiro Zogbodo Male Bogun Seja Unde, known as the Roça do Ventura, and the Terreiro Loba Nekun Filho have been formally listed as protected cultural heritage by IPHAN, an acknowledgment that these houses are as much part of Brazil's history as the churches across town. The list goes on: Terreiro Aganjú Didê da Nação Nagô-Tedô, Terreiro Dendezeiros, Terreiro Egbé Éran Ope Olúwa. Cachoeira's Catholic churches and its candomblé terreiros do not just coexist on the same map; they share congregants, calendars, and often family trees - a duality that defines Bahian religious life and that this town has preserved more completely than almost any other.

## Sugar, Tobacco, and the Water at the Edge of Town

The economy that built Cachoeira still leaves its traces a short walk from the center. The Engenho da Ponta, Engenho Campina, Engenho Embiara, and Engenho Vitória are colonial sugar mills whose stone buildings and chapels survive in various states of repair; the Engenho Vitória in particular is individually listed. Cotton and tobacco moved through the same town. Beyond the urban core, the Cachoeira municipality includes 56 percent of the 10,074-hectare Baía do Iguape Marine Extractive Reserve, created in 2000 to protect the mangrove-lined estuary where the Paraguaçu empties into the Bay of All Saints - a working landscape where artisanal fishing communities gather crab and shrimp by old methods. The town has also given Brazil voices: the singers Mateus Aleluia of Os Tincoãs and Edson Gomes grew up in this county, as did footballers Giva and Dionatan Machado. IPHAN's protections keep the sobrados standing. The people keep the town alive.

## Flight Context

Cachoeira lies at 12.62°S, 38.96°W on the inland reach of the Paraguaçu River, about 60 nautical miles west-northwest of Salvador. A pass at 3,500 to 5,500 feet shows the red-tile cluster of Cachoeira and São Félix bridged across the river, with the Recôncavo's sugarcane flats spreading south and east toward the Baía do Iguape. Salvador (SBSV - Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães International) is the nearest major airport. Expect tropical humidity with afternoon cumulus; morning departures offer the best visibility along the river valley, and the rainy season from April to July can bring low ceilings to the Recôncavo lowlands.
