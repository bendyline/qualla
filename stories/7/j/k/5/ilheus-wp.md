# Ilhéus

Once Brazil's cocoa capital, where barons killed each other for plantations and Jorge Amado turned the bloodshed into world literature. Today a tourism center facing decisions about its own coast.

**Location:** -14.7889, -39.0489

**Categories:** Populated coastal places in Bahia, Populated places established in 1534, Former Portuguese colonies, Cities in Brazil

## Introduction

In his novels, Jorge Amado called them the land wars. Cocoa barons, shotguns, burned fields, men found in the morning with their throats opened. This was not metaphor. In the early twentieth century, the hills behind Ilhéus were the richest soil in Brazil for Theobroma cacao, and the barons who controlled them hired gunmen to settle property disputes the courts were too slow to decide. The violence is gone. The city, 211 kilometers south of Salvador, has remade itself around beaches, literature, and a port that wants to grow into something bigger than the coast can comfortably hold.

## 1534, and Everything After

The Portuguese founded Vila de São Jorge dos Ilhéus in 1534, one of the original captaincies carved out along the Bahian coast. The settlement struggled for centuries against attacks by the Aimoré people and the simple tropical fact that Atlantic rainforest grows faster than men can clear it. What finally put Ilhéus on the map was cacao. From the late 1800s into the 1980s, the region was one of the world's largest exporters of cocoa beans, shipping to chocolatiers across the United States and Europe. Then came the blight.

## Witch's Broom

Crinipellis perniciosa is the scientific name. In Portuguese, farmers call it vassoura de bruxa. The fungus arrived in Bahian cacao groves in 1989 and moved through the plantations with sickening efficiency. Production in the state collapsed. Farms went bankrupt. Workers left for the cities. Researchers have fought the fungus for more than three decades now, breeding resistant strains and experimenting with biocontrols, but Bahia has never returned to its former share of the global market. The economic gap left behind was filled, imperfectly, by tourism and by the city's reinvention as a literary destination.

## The Amado Effect

Jorge Amado wrote over twenty-five novels. They were translated into forty-nine languages. Bestseller lists in fifty-five countries carried his name. Gabriela, Clove and Cinnamon opens in 1925 Ilhéus and follows a cocoa-town affair that becomes a meditation on race, class, and desire. Dona Flor and Her Two Husbands became a 1976 film and then an American remake. His plots return again and again to the poor Black and mixed-race communities of Bahia, to the women who held families together, and to the cocoa barons who did not. Ilhéus built its contemporary identity on his books. The airport carries his name. His house is a museum.

## Between Protection and Port

The municipality covers a section of the 23,262-hectare Una Wildlife Refuge, fifteen percent of the 9,275-hectare Serra do Conduru State Park, and part of the 157,745-hectare Lagoa Encantada e Rio Almada Environmental Protection Area. These are remnants of the Atlantic rainforest, the biome that once stretched unbroken from Rio Grande do Norte to Rio Grande do Sul and now survives in fragments. The federal government has approved a new deep-water port near Ilhéus over environmental objections. Supporters argue the economy needs it. Opponents point to the threats already documented: pollution from inadequate sanitation, illegal fishing, deforestation, wildlife trafficking. The city is, in that sense, a microcosm of a larger Brazilian question.

## What the Coast Looks Like

From above, the city pinches between green hills and the Atlantic. The downtown sits a kilometer from the water, the colonial blocks giving way to newer apartments as the land rises. Jorge Amado Airport pokes a single runway out past the southern suburbs. LATAM, GOL, and Azul run daily flights to São Paulo, Belo Horizonte, and Salvador. BR-101 comes in from Itabuna. In the late afternoon the sea breeze softens the heat, and the long swell from the South Atlantic sends a slow white line of surf across the beaches south of the port. People walk out with chairs and beer. The same thing has happened here, in some form, every day since 1534.
