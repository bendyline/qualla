# Pau Brasil National Park

Nineteen thousand hectares of lowland Atlantic Forest inland from Porto Seguro, protecting the endangered tree that gave Brazil its name and Renaissance Europe its best red dye.

**Location:** -16.4975, -39.2644

**Categories:** National parks of Brazil, Protected areas of Bahia, Protected areas of the Atlantic Forest, 1999 establishments in Brazil, Protected areas established in 1999

## Introduction

Cut into the heartwood and it is orange-red, close to the colour of a live coal. Boil it, fix the extract onto cloth, and Renaissance Europe would pay nearly anything for the result. The compound is brazilin; the tree is Paubrasilia echinata; and the country is named after it. Terra do Brasil meant the land of brazilwood, which makes Brazil one of the very few nations on earth to take its name from an export commodity. The Portuguese crown turned the harvest into a monopoly within a few years of landfall in 1500, and by the eighteenth century the cutting had worked through most of the standing stock. Pau Brasil National Park protects one of the fragments that survived, a short drive inland from the beaches where the trade began.

## The Wood That Named a Country

Brazilwood grows to about fifteen metres, dense and slow, with a heartwood that oxidises from orange to deep red. Before synthetic dyes, that was worth more than almost anything else the new colony could put on a ship. Cutting began within a generation of Cabral's arrival and did not let up for two hundred years; the species is now listed as endangered and has been nearly wiped out across most of its original range. It is Brazil's national tree, honoured by a country that very nearly finished it. The demand did not stop, either. Bow makers still consider brazilwood superior to every other material for stringed-instrument bows, so a tree cut down for the colour of its blood is now cut down for the way it flexes. Botanists moved it out of the genus Caesalpinia in 2016, giving it a name that finally matches the country's: Paubrasilia.

## Nineteen Thousand Hectares

The park was created by decree on 20 April 1999 and covers 19,027 hectares, all of it inside the municipality of Porto Seguro. It lies between two rivers, the Frades and the Buranhém, on a coastal plain flat enough to look uninteresting from a road and cut through by deep gullies where the streams run. Most of those streams rise inside the park itself, which is the quiet argument for its existence: protect the forest and you protect the water that leaves it. A second decree in 2010 revised the boundaries. Since 2002 the park has formed part of the Central Atlantic Forest Ecological Corridor, an effort to link the surviving forest blocks of southern Bahia into something continuous. ICMBio administers it as an IUCN category II national park.

## Seventy-One Endemics

Rain falls at an average of 1,389 millimetres a year and the temperature sits around 23 degrees, which produces dense lowland rainforest and an extraordinary concentration of species found nowhere else. Seventy-one plant species here are endemic, among them Arapatiella psilophylla, Couepia belemii, Byrsonima alvimii and Manilkara multifida - names that mean nothing to most visitors and everything to a botanist, because losing this fragment would mean losing them outright. Harpy eagles hunt here and have bred in the park. More than two hundred bird species have been recorded, including the red-billed curassow, the black-headed berryeater, the ochre-marked parakeet and the Atlantic black-breasted woodpecker. Jaguar and cougar still range through it. So does the Hercules beetle, and in the forest streams the small fish Mimagoniates sylvicola and Rachoviscus graciliceps.

## Trilha das Antas

For most of its life the park was closed to casual visitors, a research and protection unit rather than a destination. That changed after 2018 legislation opened it to tourism, and it now receives visitors year-round, from 8.30 in the morning until four in the afternoon, by advance booking. The Trilha das Antas, the tapir trail, runs flat through the forest and is the easy introduction. The Trilha Ibirapitanga takes you up onto a suspended walkway among the canopy. The Mirante da Sede gives you the view out over the treetops, and longer trails at moderate difficulty end at waterfalls you are allowed to swim in. The booking requirement is not bureaucracy for its own sake; it is how a fragment this small absorbs attention without being worn out by it.

## Second Growth

Not all of it is old forest. A good share of the park is regenerating ground, land cleared and worked before protection arrived and now filling back in, which is the ordinary condition of the Atlantic Forest almost everywhere it survives. Roughly 88 percent of the biome's original extent has been cleared, so the difference between old growth and recovering ground matters less here than the simple fact of cover. Walk the Antas trail and you are moving through a forest that is partly a survivor and partly a repair, with brazilwood scattered through it rather than dominant - a national tree living as a remnant inside the park that carries its name.

## Flight Context

Pau Brasil National Park lies at 16.50°S, 39.26°W, roughly 20 kilometres inland and west of Porto Seguro, between the Rio dos Frades to the north and the Buranhém to the south. The terrain is a low coastal plain, so from 3,000-5,000 feet AGL the park reads as a dark, sharply bounded block of closed canopy set against a much lighter patchwork of pasture, eucalyptus plantation and cropland - one of the clearest illustrations anywhere of how little Atlantic Forest is left. Porto Seguro Airport (ICAO SBPS, IATA BPS) is the nearest field, on the coast to the east. Monte Pascoal's rounded summit stands about 45 kilometres to the south-southwest and makes a reliable visual fix. Expect steady easterly trades, average annual rainfall near 1,389 millimetres, and low cloud with heavy showers through the November-to-March wet season.
