# Chapada Diamantina National Park

A 152,000-hectare refuge for jaguars, endemic hummingbirds and orchids atop eroded Precambrian sandstone in central Bahia.

**Location:** -12.8803, -41.3722

**Categories:** National parks of Brazil, Protected areas of Bahia, 1985 establishments in Brazil, Caatinga

## Introduction

Decree 91.655, signed on 17 September 1985, set aside 152,142 hectares of eroded sandstone in central Bahia and declared it off limits to the diamond prospectors who had worked these mountains for more than a century. What they left behind, the decree now protects: a plateau of cliffs and caves where the Sincorá Range lifts itself above the Caatinga scrub, and where rain falling on the ridgeline finds its way either east to the Paraguacu River and the Atlantic or west to the Sao Francisco and eventually the interior. Chapada Diamantina National Park is a watershed in the geological sense and an ecological island in almost every other.

## A Divide Five Hundred Million Years in the Making

The rocks here are old enough to trouble the imagination. The Chapada Diamantina Group - sandstones, pelites, diamond-bearing conglomerates - predates the breakup of the ancient supercontinent of Pangaea. Erosion began in the Precambrian and never stopped. What remains is a plateau cut by canyons, its highest peak the 2,033-meter Pico do Barbado, the roof of the state of Bahia. Both gold and diamonds turned up here, and geology explains why. The hard caprock resists weathering, but the softer Paraguacu Group beneath folds and fractures under the weight, and the folds let water in. Water finds the softer rock, the softer rock gives way, and the park's defining canyons and caves are the result. The Sincorá Range also forces moist Atlantic air upward, wringing rainfall from it - which is why an island of greener, cooler country sits in the middle of Brazil's semi-arid sertão.

## A Biome Most Brazilians Have Never Heard Of

The national park straddles five municipalities - Palmeiras, Mucuge, Lencois, Ibicoara and Andarai - and preserves a slice of Caatinga, the only biome unique to Brazil. Above 500 meters the xerophytic thornscrub gives way to rocky fields and highland meadows; along the watercourses, strands of Atlantic Forest vegetation hang on. The botany turns up species that exist nowhere else: orchids with scientific names as ornate as the flowers themselves - Adamantinia miltonioides, Cattleya elongata, Cleistes metallina - each evolved in isolation on a specific ridge or cliff face. The hooded visorbearer, a small iridescent hummingbird whose scientific name (Augastes lumachellus) means roughly "shining glory," is endemic to this range. Spot one working a patch of flowers, and you are watching a bird that lives only on the mountains below your feet.

## The Big Animals Few Will See

The cats are here, but they do not come out for visitors. Jaguars (Panthera onca) and cougars (Puma concolor) hunt through the valleys; the smaller oncilla (Leopardus tigrinus), spotted and secretive, works the forest edges. The giant armadillo, nocturnal and now globally vulnerable, snuffles through leaf litter that tourists rarely cross at night. The giant anteater, which once ranged across most of South America, survives here in numbers that have collapsed almost everywhere else. Birdwatchers track the Chaco eagle, the ochre-marked parakeet, and two species named for the state itself: the Bahia tyrannulet and the Bahia spinetail. Even the titi monkey named for Barbara Brown, Callicebus barbarabrownae, clings on here - a species so threatened that its Bahia range is largely what keeps it from extinction.

## A Park That Does Its Job Quietly

Administered by the Chico Mendes Institute for Biodiversity Conservation, the park is categorized IUCN II - national park - which means it is meant to preserve intact ecosystems while allowing scientific research, environmental education, and a careful flow of eco-tourism. The Institute carries the name of the rubber tapper and activist murdered in 1988 for defending the Amazon forest, a reminder that conservation in Brazil has often come at a personal cost. This is also a landscape whose protection arrived late. Diamonds were pulled from these streams well into the twentieth century, and the mines left their marks on the rivers and the people downstream. What is protected now, just within living memory, is not quite wilderness and not quite not. It is a place where you can hear where the prospectors dug, and where the forest has begun - slowly, stubbornly - to return.

## Flight Context

Located at 12.88 degrees south, 41.37 degrees west in central Bahia. The plateau averages around 1,000 meters (3,280 ft) above sea level, with the highest point Pico do Barbado at 2,033 meters (6,670 ft MSL). Best viewed from 8,000 to 12,000 feet; the mesa structures throw deep shadows at low sun angles. Nearest airport: Horácio de Mattos/Chapada Diamantina Airport at Lençóis (SBLE/LEC). Salvador (SBSV/SSA) lies about 400 km east. Afternoon thunderstorms are common in the wet season (Nov-Mar); morning VFR preferred.
