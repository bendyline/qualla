# Cachoeira da Fumaça

A 340-metre waterfall in the Chapada Diamantina where the wind takes the river apart in mid-air, so that on most days the water never reaches the ground.

**Location:** -12.6006, -41.4551

**Categories:** Waterfalls of Brazil, Landforms of Bahia, Chapada Diamantina, Protected areas of Bahia

## Introduction

The river reaches the edge without ceremony, slides over a lip of quartzite, and then, instead of falling, it disappears. Three hundred and forty metres of open air wait below, and the water simply never arrives. An updraft coming off the valley wall catches the stream partway down, shreds it, and pushes it back as vapour. Lie on your stomach at the rim, push your head over the drop, and you can watch your own river drift upward past your face. Bahians call it Cachoeira da Fumaça, the Smoke Falls, and for most of the year that is a description rather than a metaphor.

## A Waterfall That Goes Up

The trick is a matter of proportion. The stream feeding the falls is modest, the drop enormous, and the gap between the two gives the wind more than enough time and distance to dismantle a column of water into mist. The rim sits at roughly 1,280 metres, high on the escarpment of the Chapada Diamantina, and the air moving up the cliff face rarely rests. In the wet months the flow thickens and something recognisable as a waterfall makes it partway down. In the dry season the whole thing can vanish, leaving a wet stain on the rock and a smell of damp stone. For decades Cachoeira da Fumaça was called Brazil's tallest waterfall, a title since contested by a taller drop measured in the state of Amazonas. What has never been in dispute is that it is the strangest.

## Six Kilometres, or Three Days

There are two ways to meet it, and they produce completely different memories. From above, the walk is about six kilometres from the ecological base in the Vale do Capão, a steady climb onto the plateau that ends with no warning at all: scrub, rock, and then nothing under your boots. From below, the approach is a trek of roughly three days out of Lençóis, the old diamond town that serves as the region's front door, walking up through valleys toward the base of the cliff to look at the fall the way it was meant to be seen. Neither route is a stroll. The plateau above the falls is featureless enough that hikers do get lost on it, and guides are the local norm rather than an upsell.

## The Pilot's Name

People in the valleys below had always known the waterfall was there. What changed in 1960 was who else found out. A bush pilot named George Glass spotted the drop from the air and told the wider world, and the falls picked up a second name it has never quite shed: Cachoeira do Glass, Glass Falls. It is a small, familiar pattern. A landmark that had been part of daily geography for generations acquires the surname of the outsider who reported it, and both names go on living side by side. Ask in Vale do Capão and you will hear Fumaça. Read an older map and you may well find Glass.

## Diamond Country

The falls sit inside the Chapada Diamantina, the Diamond Highlands, a landscape that was ransacked before it was protected. Lençóis was founded in 1844 after alluvial diamonds turned up in its streams, and the mining that followed was brutal work done largely by enslaved people and, later, by labourers who lived badly for a share of whatever they could wash out of the gravel. The stones thinned, the rush ended, and the region went quiet for most of a century. In 1985 the Chapada Diamantina National Park was created over some 1,500 square kilometres of table mountains, canyons, caves and waterfalls, Fumaça among them. The prospectors have been replaced by hikers, which is a better fate than most goldfield landscapes get.

## The Bollywood Jump

One more constituency knows this cliff intimately, and it is not Brazilian. In the 2006 Hindi film Dhoom 2, Aishwarya Rai's character Sunehri is dared to leap from the edge with no safety line to prove herself to Hrithik Roshan's Aryan. She jumps. He follows her over, bungee cord in hand. The scene put the Smoke Falls in front of an audience that will likely never hike the Vale do Capão trail, and it means a certain share of the visitors standing at the rim today are not there for the geology at all. They are there because they watched somebody jump.

## Flight Context

Cachoeira da Fumaça lies at 12.60°S, 41.46°W, in the municipality of Palmeiras on the western escarpment of the Chapada Diamantina, central Bahia. The rim is around 1,280 metres above sea level, so a sightseeing pass at 6,000-8,000 feet AGL puts the plateau, the drop and the Vale do Capão in one frame. The nearest field is Coronel Horácio de Mattos Airport at Lençóis (ICAO SBLE, IATA LEC), about 20 kilometres from that town at 506 metres elevation with a 2,082-metre asphalt runway; the regional gateway is Salvador (ICAO SBSV), roughly 400 kilometres east. Navigate visually by the flat-topped massifs of the Chapada, which stand out sharply against the surrounding scrub. Morning air is usually smooth; the same updraft that turns the waterfall to smoke makes for reliable afternoon turbulence along the escarpment, and the wet season from November to March brings low cloud that can bury the plateau entirely.
