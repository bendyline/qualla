# Oeiras, Piauí

Piauí's first capital lost the seat of government to Teresina in 1852 and stopped growing at a capital's pace — which is precisely why its colonial center is still standing.

**Location:** -7.0250, -42.1308

**Categories:** cities, history, colonial-architecture, brazil, piaui, heritage

## Introduction

Losing is what saved it. For well over a century Oeiras was the political center of Piauí, the place where a captaincy carved out of Maranhão learned to govern itself. Then in 1852 the government packed up and left for Teresina, a brand-new city laid out on the Parnaíba where the river traffic was. Oeiras kept its churches, its stone bridge and its narrow streets, and simply stopped expanding at the speed capitals expand. A century and a half later, that stalling reads as preservation. Federal law declared Oeiras a national monument in 1989, and in 2012 IPHAN, Brazil's national heritage institute, listed the whole *Conjunto Histórico e Paisagístico de Oeiras* — the historic and landscape ensemble, meaning not only the buildings but the hills and watercourses they sit among.

## Vila da Mocha

It started with mud and straw. Around 1697, Jesuits under Padre Miguel Carvalho put up a small chapel beside a stream called the Mocha, in cattle country deep in the Brazilian interior. The settlement that gathered around it took the stream's name: Vila da Mocha. In 1712 King João V granted it the status of *vila*, a formal town. Six years later the Crown split the Captaincy of Piauí off from Maranhão and named Vila da Mocha the new captaincy's capital — a decision made in Lisbon about a place that almost nobody in Lisbon had seen, on the strength of a chapel, a stream and a lot of cattle.

## The Governor Who Took Forty-One Years

A royal decree is one thing; a functioning government is another. The autonomy granted to Piauí in 1718 translated into very little on the ground for four decades. The first governor, João Pereira Caldas, did not arrive until 1759. Two years after that the settlement was raised to the rank of city and renamed Oeiras, honoring the Count of Oeiras — Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, remembered by his later title as the Marquis of Pombal, the minister who rebuilt Lisbon after the 1755 earthquake and who, in 1759, expelled the Jesuits from Portugal and its empire. So the town that began as a Jesuit chapel now carried the name of the man who threw the Jesuits out.

## January 1823

Piauí's break with Portugal was declared here. On 24 January 1823, Oeiras proclaimed the province's adhesion to Brazilian independence, in a movement led by Manuel de Sousa Martins, later Barão da Parnaíba. This was not a ceremony held after the outcome was settled. Independence in the North of Brazil was contested rather than conceded, and Portuguese loyalist forces were still active in the region when the declaration was made. A small inland city with no port and no garrison of consequence had picked a side.

## What Is Still Standing

The mother church, the Igreja Matriz de Nossa Senhora da Vitória, anchors the historic center, with the Igreja de Nossa Senhora do Rosário nearby. Crossing the water is the Ponte Grande, described as the first stone bridge built in Piauí — infrastructure from an era when the state's road network essentially was this town and the tracks radiating from it. What makes the ensemble worth the federal listing is not any single monument but the fact that the relationship between the buildings and the ground survived. Colonial capitals that kept their function tend to have their eighteenth-century fabric buried under nineteenth- and twentieth-century ambition. Oeiras never had the ambition, so it kept the fabric.

## Holy Week and Heat

The year in Oeiras turns on Semana Santa. Holy Week fills the streets with processions — the Procissão do Bom Jesus dos Passos, the Descida da Cruz — and draws pilgrims from across Piauí, giving the town for a few days something close to the crowds it once held as a seat of government. Two patronal feasts punctuate the rest of the calendar: Nossa Senhora da Vitória on 15 August, and Nossa Senhora da Conceição on 8 December. In between it is very hot. On 25 September 2023 the town recorded 42.4°C, one of the higher readings taken anywhere in Brazil that year. The 2022 census counted 38,161 residents, which makes Oeiras a small city that used to run a state.

## Flight Context

Oeiras sits at 7.03 degrees south, 42.13 degrees west, in the interior of Piauí in northeastern Brazil, at low elevation in dry *sertão* country. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,500–4,000 feet AGL to pick out the colonial center: the mother church's towers are the most legible feature from the air, set among low tiled roofs on a compact street grid, with the stream valley cutting past. Nearest airports are Teresina (SBTE) about 230 km north-northwest, Petrolina (SBPL) about 310 km south-southeast, Juazeiro do Norte (SBJU) about 315 km east-southeast, and Fortaleza (SBFZ) considerably further northeast. Skies are usually clear; the rainy season runs roughly December to April, and daytime heat above 40°C is common, so expect thermals and reduced density altitude performance in the afternoon.
