# Nova Olinda, Ceará

A Cariri town of under 16,000 people where a museum, a library, a theatre and a cultural foundation are run by the local children — beneath a sandstone escarpment that UNESCO counts as a geopark.

**Location:** -7.0919, -39.6808

**Categories:** cities, culture, museums, geology, brazil, ceara, unesco

## Introduction

A colonial house in a town of fewer than sixteen thousand people, in the interior of Ceará, contains a museum of the region's Indigenous past, a library, a theatre and a restaurant. The unusual part is who runs it. The Fundação Casa Grande – Memorial do Homem Kariri opened in December 1992, founded by the archaeologist Rosiane Limaverde and Alemberg Quindins in a house dating from the era when the Cariri economy ran on leather. Its declared business is not entertaining children but training them: the educational and cultural formation of children and young people as protagonists in cultural management, in the foundation's own phrase. Over three decades that has meant successive cohorts of Nova Olinda kids learning a collection, an archive and a stage from the inside — and then handing them to the cohort behind.

## Memorial do Homem Kariri

The second half of the foundation's name points at the Kariri, the Indigenous people this whole region is named for and whose material traces are still coming out of the ground around the town. Alongside the archaeological holdings the foundation keeps an archive of its own making — DVDs, comics and other material produced in-house — so the collection documents both an ancient culture and the ongoing effort of a small town to hold onto it. Recognition followed. Nova Olinda's Casa Grande became the first *Pontão de Cultura* in Ceará, designated during Gilberto Gil's tenure as Brazil's Minister of Culture, and in 2009 IPHAN gave it the title of *Casa do Patrimônio*. Alemberg Quindins received the Ordem do Mérito Cultural in 2004, along with honorary doctorates from universities in the region.

## The Stone Bridge

On the descent from the Chapada do Araripe, at the Sítio Olho d'Água de Santa Bárbara, a natural sandstone arch spans a stream that only carries water after rain. It is called the Ponte de Pedra, and it is one of nine designated geosites of the Araripe UNESCO Global Geopark — 3,796 square kilometers across six Cariri municipalities, Nova Olinda among them. UNESCO first recognized the area in 2006; it became a UNESCO Global Geopark in 2015. The geopark's international reputation rests on Cretaceous fossils from the Araripe Basin, above all pterosaurs, preserved in fine laminated rock laid down when the splitting of Pangaea opened the basin. Standing on the arch, you are on the seam between two Northeasts: the forested top of the chapada behind you, where people still gather pequi, and the open sertão dropping away in front.

## Older Hands on the Same Route

The arch was a crossing long before it was a viewpoint. Rock engravings and paintings survive near it, and the surrounding ground yields ceramic sherds and worked stone left by the Kariri and the peoples who preceded them on this escarpment. Later the same gap in the rock served vaqueiros moving cattle up and down between the chapada and the plain. Few places let you read geological time and human time in one view; this is one of them, which is a large part of why it was made a geosite rather than simply a scenic overlook.

## Leather and a Theatre

The town's other museum is the Museu do Ciclo do Couro — the Museum of the Leather Cycle — built around the work of the saddler Espedito Seleiro, whose vividly dyed leather goods are known across the Northeast and whose craft is the visible survivor of the economy that built the house Casa Grande now occupies. A short walk away is the Teatro Violeta Arraes, opened on 19 November 2002 and named for Violeta Arraes, its architecture drawing on the sugarcane mills of the Cariri. It was built expressly so that the children of the community would have somewhere to encounter performance — the same logic, and largely the same generation, as the foundation up the street.

## A Green Edge on the Semi-Arid

Nova Olinda covers 284.4 square kilometers at roughly 445 meters of elevation, some 560 kilometers from Fortaleza, with about 15,800 residents by IBGE's 2021 estimate. Average temperatures run 24 to 26°C and the rain arrives between January and May. The vegetation lists for the municipality include thorny deciduous forest — proper caatinga — and evergreen rainforest in the same breath, which is not a combination the Brazilian Northeast usually offers; the Chapada do Araripe pulls enough moisture out of the air to support both within a few kilometers. Brazil's Ministry of Tourism counts Nova Olinda among 65 national tourism-inducing destinations, one of only four in Ceará. That is conspicuous company for a place that was a district of Santana do Cariri, and before that a settlement called Tapera, until it became a municipality in its own right in 1957.

## Flight Context

Nova Olinda lies at 7.09 degrees south, 39.68 degrees west, at about 445 meters elevation on the northern flank of the Chapada do Araripe in southern Ceará. Recommended viewing altitude is 3,000–5,000 feet AGL: the defining feature is the escarpment of the chapada itself, a long flat-topped sandstone plateau running east–west, with the town sitting on the lower ground below its rim and the Ponte de Pedra arch on the descent. Nearest airport is Juazeiro do Norte / Orlando Bezerra de Menezes (SBJU), roughly 45 km east, which serves the whole Cariri conurbation of Crato, Juazeiro do Norte and Barbalha — a bright cluster of lights along the same escarpment at night. Petrolina (SBPL) is about 270 km south-southwest and Fortaleza (SBFZ) about 390 km northeast. The rainy season runs January to May with afternoon convection; the dry months bring haze and good long-range visibility along the plateau edge.
