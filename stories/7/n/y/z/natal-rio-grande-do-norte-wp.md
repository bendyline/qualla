# Natal

A capital named Christmas that cannot agree on who founded it, wrapped around a river mouth, a colonial fort and 1,172 hectares of dunes inside the city limits.

**Location:** -5.7950, -35.2089

**Categories:** city, history, coastal, culture, brazil, rio-grande-do-norte

## Introduction

Natal is Portuguese for Christmas, and the city earned the name the plain way: on 25 December 1599 a mass was said on the rise where the Parish Church of Our Lady of the Presentation now stands, and that mass is the founding. Who ordered it is another matter. The documents that would have settled the question were destroyed during the Dutch occupation three decades later, so the city's historians have split ever since between Jerônimo de Albuquerque and Manuel de Mascarenhas Homem. Natal knows its birthday to the day and cannot name its own founder.

## The River Does the Dividing

Everything here is organised by the Potengi. The river cuts the North Zone off from the rest of the city, empties into the Atlantic at a sandbar guarded since 1598 by the star-shaped Forte dos Reis Magos. Through the cotton boom of the later nineteenth century, warehouses and quays crept along its banks where the Port of Natal stands today; the Ribeira Railway Station opened on the same flank on 28 September 1881 and is still the oldest in Rio Grande do Norte. For most of Natal's life the only way across was upriver at Igapó. That changed on 21 November 2007, when the Newton Navarro Bridge — named for the natalense artist Newton Navarro Bilro — arced over the bar between Santos Reis and Redinha.

## New Amsterdam

A Dutch fleet of fourteen ships landed at Ponta Negra in 1631 intending to take the Captaincy of Rio Grande, and failed. The real occupation began in December 1633, when Dutch forces seized the fort at the bar, renamed it Castelo Ceulen, and renamed the town New Amsterdam after their own capital. Twenty-one years of Dutch rule followed before the Portuguese expelled them in 1654; the Netherlands conceded the northeast formally in 1661 under the Treaty of The Hague. What came after the reconquest was not peace. As the Portuguese pushed inland, Indigenous peoples across the captaincy were persecuted and decimated, and the stability historians date to the close of the seventeenth century was purchased at that price.

## Seven Hundred People

Growth was glacial for three hundred years. When Natal celebrated Brazilian independence on 22 January 1823 — the news from São Paulo had taken four months to matter this far north — the city held roughly seven hundred inhabitants. The telegraph arrived in 1878, and in 1888 Matriz Square became André de Albuquerque Square, the zero milestone from which every distance in the city is still measured. Then the pace broke. Cidade Nova was laid out in 1901, becoming today's Tirol and Petrópolis, and the Carlos Gomes Theatre opened in 1904. By the 1920 census the population had reached 30,696, nearly double the 16,596 counted in 1900.

## Dunes Inside the City Limits

Natal answers to two nicknames, the City of the Sun and the City of the Dunes, and both are earned. Lying about six degrees south of the equator, it collects more than three thousand hours of sunshine a year; February averages a high of 30.7 °C, and July, the coolest month, still reaches 28.4 °C. Rain falls heavily from March to July, around 1,690 millimetres a year. The dunes are the stranger asset. The Natal Dunes State Park covers 1,172 hectares of protected sand and coastal forest — the second-largest urban park in Brazil — and between that park and the ocean runs the Via Costeira, opened in 1985. The road remade the southern beaches and turned Ponta Negra from a fishing village into the city's most valuable address.

## A Truck, a Band, and a Million People

Carnatal is not carnival. It runs in early December, months out of season, and that is the point: the first edition in 1991 took over three blocks of the city centre, and the festival is now listed in the Guinness Book as the largest off-season carnival in the world. About a million people turn out across its four days. The mechanics are simple and enormous. Traffic engineers close roughly three kilometres of street into a ring — since 2012 the circuit runs around the Arena das Dunas — and a trio elétrico, a truck carrying a full band on its roof, crawls the loop at walking pace while the crowd follows it singing.

## 2014, and the Rain

The Arena das Dunas opened on 22 January 2014 where the old Machadão stadium had stood, and hosted four group-stage matches of that year's World Cup. The tournament did not go to plan. Between 13 and 16 June the city declared a state of public calamity as heavy rain brought flooding and landslides, and in the hillside neighbourhood of Mãe Luíza a slope failed on a scale that ran from Guanabara Street down to Areia Preta Beach, destroying homes. Families lost everything they had while the stadium a few kilometres away filled with visiting supporters. The city's answer came the following December: the Mãe Luíza Stairway, officially the Portal do Sol Klebson Nascimento, an engineered route down the slope that had collapsed under its neighbours.

## Natalenses

The 2022 census counted 751,300 people in Natal and about 1.9 million across Greater Natal, packed into 167 square kilometres — the second-smallest Brazilian state capital by area, and one of the densest, because the dunes, the park and the river left it nowhere else to grow. They call themselves natalenses, and the city has sent out more than its size suggests: the folklorist Luís da Câmara Cascudo, and Café Filho, still the only Rio Grande do Norte native ever to hold the presidency.

## Flight Context

Position 5°47'42"S, 35°12'32"W, on the Atlantic at the mouth of the Potengi River — near the easternmost corner of South America. Best appreciated from 3,000–5,000 feet on a coastal track: the pale wedge of the Natal Dunes State Park is the clearest landmark, with the Via Costeira running dead straight between it and the surf, Ponta Negra's high-rise wall at the southern end, and the Newton Navarro Bridge spanning the river bar to the north. Greater Natal International Airport (SBSG) is 30 km west-northwest at São Gonçalo do Amarante and sits essentially at sea level; Natal Air Force Base (SBNT) lies south at Parnamirim. Trade winds are steady and onshore year-round, so expect low-level turbulence over the dunes. Visibility is generally excellent; the March–July wet season brings heavy convective rain, and the September–January dry stretch offers the cleanest air.
