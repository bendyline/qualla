# Morro do Careca

Natal's great bare dune was the city's favourite ride until 1997, when it was closed to save it — a landmark you can only photograph.

**Location:** -5.8856, -35.1634

**Categories:** Landforms of Rio Grande do Norte, Dunes of Brazil, Natal, Rio Grande do Norte

## Introduction

The word was skibunda — roughly, bum-skiing. You climbed the huge bare dune at the southern end of Ponta Negra beach, sat down, and let gravity and loose sand handle the rest. For decades that was the thing to do in Natal: bathers and tourists queuing up the slope, then coming down it fast. The ride was also, it turned out, part of the reason the hill kept getting shorter.

## A Hill That Would Not Hold Its Height

Sand is not a building material; it is a suspension held in place by roots. On Morro do Careca the roots belong to restinga, the scrubby coastal vegetation that grips the flanks of the dune and keeps them from running. Strip the restinga with thousands of feet, and the slides begin. By the 1990s the sand was slipping badly enough that the dune's own height was falling, and in 1997 the city closed it. All activity on the dune has been suspended ever since. The point of the closure was never the view or the noise — it was that Natal was watching its symbol shrink.

## How Tall Is It, Exactly

Even the measurement refuses to settle. Brazilian sources put the dune at about 107 metres; English-language ones say 120. The gap is not carelessness so much as an honest problem of definition: where does a dune begin? The base is drawn by a vegetation line that itself moves, and the summit has been losing sand for years. The name translates as Bald Hill, and the bare face is unmistakable — a broad pale wedge of sand set against the green of everything around it, high enough to catch the first light and hold it while the beach below is still in shade.

## A City Built in Sand

Morro do Careca is not an anomaly; it is the southern exclamation point of a sand coast. Rio Grande do Norte's shoreline north of Natal runs almost entirely under dunes, a formation that belongs to the semi-arid climate pressing down from the interior, and the state has fenced off a slice of it as the Natal Dunes State Park. Further north still, at Genipabu, the dunes are big enough to ride across on dromedaries. What makes Careca different is position and profile: it stands alone at the end of a city beach, framed by hotels, close enough that people spent decades treating a landform as playground equipment.

## The Postcard the City Chose

Morro do Careca is the main symbol of Natal and, by extension, of Rio Grande do Norte. It appears on tourist postcards, in state campaigns, and among the Seven Wonders of Rio Grande do Norte. It anchors the far southern end of Ponta Negra, the capital's most famous beach, in a neighbourhood named for the dark stones scattered along its shore. Ponta Negra has grown up around the view: after the Via Costeira opened the southern coast to development from the 1980s onward, hotels, bars and restaurants filled in behind the sand. The neighbourhood counted about 24,700 residents in 2010 and roughly 35,150 by 2022.

## Nearly Thirty Years of Looking

A conservation measure taken in 1997 has now lasted most of a generation, which is long enough that visitors arriving today have no idea it was ever anything but a view. Nobody climbs it. Nobody slides down it. The city gave up its favourite playground in order to keep its favourite image of itself, and the trade has held. From the beach at dusk the dune reads as a single clean shape against the sky, the light sliding off the sand from gold to rose, the restinga darkening at its feet. It is the rare landmark improved by being left alone.

## Flight Context

5.886°S, 35.163°W, on the Atlantic shore at the southern limit of Ponta Negra beach in Natal. A coastal pass at 1,000–2,500 ft AGL gives the classic profile: the bare sand wedge falling straight into the surf, with the hotel strip of Ponta Negra running north from its base and the Via Costeira beyond. Natal Air Force Base (SBNT) lies about 10 km southwest at Parnamirim; the region's civil airport is Governador Aluízio Alves International (SBSG) at São Gonçalo do Amarante, northwest of the city. Steady southeasterly trade winds and generally excellent visibility; morning light puts the dune's east face in full sun.
