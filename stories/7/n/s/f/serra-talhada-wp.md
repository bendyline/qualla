# Serra Talhada

A working city of ninety thousand in the Pernambuco sertão that happens to be the birthplace of Lampião — and has spent nearly a century deciding what to do about it.

**Location:** -7.9858, -38.2958

**Categories:** cities, brazil, pernambuco, history, sertao, culture

## Introduction

Virgulino Ferreira da Silva was born on 7 July 1897 at a farm called Passagem das Pedras, in a town that no longer answers to the name it had that day. It was Vila Bela then — Beautiful Village — a cattle settlement on the Pajeú river in the Pernambuco backlands. Forty-two years later, on 15 June 1939, the state governor Agamenon Magalhães signed the name away and replaced it with the one on the map today: Serra Talhada, cut ridge, after the escarpment above town that looks as though something sheared it to a plumb line. Virgulino had been dead for not quite eleven months. By then almost nobody called him Virgulino. They called him Lampião, and they still do.

## The Chapel and the Plumb Line

The town started, as sertão towns tend to, around a chapel. It was raised somewhere between 1789 and 1790, dedicated to Nossa Senhora da Penha, and built by enslaved workers at the direction of Filadelfa Nunes de Magalhães — a detail worth holding onto, because the labour that founded most of these settlements is usually the part that goes unrecorded. The name Vila Bela attached to a cattle ranch belonging to the Portuguese settler Agostinho Nunes de Magalhães. Provincial Law 280 made it a vila on 6 May 1851, with Manoel Pereira da Silva presiding over the first council; state law raised it to a city in 1909. The 1939 rename stuck completely. Ask anyone in Pernambuco about Vila Bela today and you will mostly get a blank look — unless they know the cangaço, in which case they will know exactly which town you mean.

## Passagem das Pedras

What happened to the Ferreira family is documented, and it is not complicated. They were farmers locked into a feud with neighbours, the kind of dispute that the sertão's weak law and strong clans turned lethal with some regularity. On 18 May 1921, police killed Virgulino's father, José Ferreira. Virgulino went into the cangaço and did not come out. He rode first with the bandit leader Sebastião Pereira, took over what was left of the band in 1922, and for sixteen years led up to a hundred armed men across seven northeastern states — extortion, kidnapping, raids on towns and estates, running battles with state police forces that could not catch him. The nickname is said to come from his rifle work: he could fire a lever-action so fast that at night it looked like he was holding a lamp.

## Angicos

It ended on 28 July 1938 at the Angicos farm in Poço Redondo, Sergipe, where a paramilitary police column under João Bezerra caught the band sleeping. Lampião died there, along with Maria Bonita — who had joined him in 1930 and whose partnership with him gave the story the outlaw-couple gloss it has carried ever since — and nine others. Around forty cangaceiros escaped. The eleven dead were beheaded, and the heads went to Salvador to be examined by physicians looking for the criminal skull that the racial science of the period assured them was there. Then the heads were put on public display at Piranhas. They stayed on exhibit until 1969 — thirty-one years — before finally being buried at the Quintas cemetery. Lampião and Maria Bonita had a daughter, Expedita, born in 1932 and raised by his brother João. She grew up with a name that meant something in every town in the Northeast.

## The Sound of Dragged Sandals

The cangaço left Serra Talhada something besides notoriety. Xaxado is a dance from this sertão, worked up in the second decade of the twentieth century and spread across the Northeast by Lampião and his band, who danced it to mark their victories. Men line up single file or in a ring, step sideways with the right foot and drag the left after it, and the rifle butts come down on the ground to keep time. The name is simply the noise: xa-xa-xa, the sound of sandals scraping dirt. It is recognised today as Brazilian cultural heritage, and Serra Talhada calls itself the capital of it, with a Festival de Xaxado and a Museu do Cangaço in town. A dance invented by armed men on the run has become a thing that schoolchildren learn.

## The Rest of the Century

None of which is what Serra Talhada actually does for a living. Around 92,000 people live here across 2,980 square kilometres of caatinga at an altitude of 429 metres, 415 kilometres from Recife, in a semi-arid climate that averages 25 degrees and 686 millimetres of rain concentrated into January through April. The Pajeú runs through it. The municipality was named a priority area when the Caatinga Ecological Corridor was established in 2006. It is the second most important city in the Pernambuco sertão after Petrolina, a hub for health care, education and commerce for a wide stretch of dry country. On Sunday afternoons the argument that matters is not about the cangaço but about the derby between Ferroviário and Serrano at the Pereirão, a stadium that holds five thousand people in a city of ninety thousand — which is, in its way, the more honest measure of the place.

## Flight Context

Serra Talhada sits at 7.99 degrees south, 38.30 degrees west, at 429 metres elevation in the Pajeú valley of the Pernambuco sertão, about 415 km inland from Recife. Recommended viewing altitude is 3,000-5,000 feet AGL to read the terrain: grey-green caatinga scrub, bare granite inselbergs, and the sheared ridge that gave the city its name standing over the town. The Pajeú river is a broad sand channel, dry or nearly dry for much of the year, and is the clearest linear feature. BR-232 runs east-west through the city and PE-365 branches north toward Triunfo. Serra Talhada Airport lies just outside town; nearest larger fields are Petrolina (SBPL) about 260 km southwest and Recife/Guararapes (SBRF) to the east. Visibility is excellent through the long dry season, with strong thermals and dust haze from August to December; the January-April rains bring brief, heavy convective cells.
