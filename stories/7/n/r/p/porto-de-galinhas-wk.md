# Porto de Galinhas

Brazil's most-voted beach carries a cheerful name that came out of the illegal slave trade: word that new chickens had arrived at the port.

**Location:** -8.5042, -35.0011

**Categories:** beaches, coastal, brazil, pernambuco, history, slavery

## Introduction

The chickens were not chickens. For years this beach in the municipality of Ipojuca was called Porto Rico — rich port — for the brazilwood shipped out of it. The name it carries now came later, and it came in code. Brazil outlawed the Atlantic slave trade on 7 November 1831, and then did almost nothing to enforce the law, a habit Brazilians named at the time: para inglês ver, for the English to see. Trafficking continued on a huge scale for another nineteen years. On this stretch of Pernambuco coast, Africans were landed hidden beneath crates of guinea fowl — galinhas-d'angola — and the arrival of a shipment was passed along the shore in a sentence that gave nothing away to an inspector and everything away to a buyer. Tem galinha nova no porto. There are new chickens in the port.

## New Chickens in the Port

It is worth saying plainly who the phrase referred to. These were people — men, women and children taken from West and Central Africa, packed into ships that were already illegal under their own country's law, and unloaded onto sand at night under a layer of poultry crates. They had names nobody here wrote down. The traffic did not slow after 1831; Britain's Aberdeen Act of 1845 let the Royal Navy seize Brazilian slave ships, and the effect was a panic buying spree, with the largest arrivals of the entire illegal period falling in 1847, 1848 and 1849. Brazil finally shut the trade with the Eusébio de Queirós Law on 4 September 1850, and abolished slavery itself only in 1888, the last country in the Western hemisphere to do so. Both the municipality and the standard reference works tell the etymology this way, without hedging. The town has kept the name. It is a beach resort's cheerful, slightly comic label, and it is also a receipt.

## Two Hours of Aquarium

What draws the crowds is a geological accident. A line of sandstone and coral reef runs parallel to the shore a short way out, and when the tide drops far enough the reef surfaces and the water trapped behind it becomes a chain of clear pools — the piscinas naturais, said to be the closest natural pools to the coast anywhere in Brazil. They are only there for about two hours a day, at very low tide, and the schedule shifts with the moon, so the whole town organises around a tide table. Jangadas, the flat sailing rafts of the northeastern coast, ferry visitors out from the beach in relays. Snorkellers who have swum Hawaii or the Riviera Maya sometimes come back unimpressed by the fish count. Nobody comes back unimpressed by standing chest-deep in warm water a kilometre from a bar.

## Seahorses at the Pontal

Walk south along the sand and Porto quiets down into Maracaípe, which has surf where Porto has none, and kitesurfers working the wind off Pontal do Maracaípe. The Pontal is also where the mangroves start, and where a protected breeding area for seahorses sits in the estuary — the Recanto do Cavalo Marinho, reachable by jangada, and dependent on visitors keeping their hands to themselves. In midsummer, on full-moon nights, sea turtles come ashore on these beaches to nest. Locals plan around it the same way they plan around the tide.

## What a Weekend Looks Like

Porto de Galinhas is roughly sixty kilometres south of Recife as the crow flies and about eighty by road, which makes it the default weekend for Recife and everyone in its metropolitan area. Buses from the Cais de Santa Rita and from the airport run from before five in the morning until half past ten at night. On Saturday the pracinha — the little square where the two main streets meet the beach — fills with bands, and the pousadas closest to it stop being restful. Buggies wait at the top of Rua da Esperança to run people down the coast. Carts on the sand sell piña colada served in the pineapple. Readers of the Brazilian magazine Viagem e Turismo have voted this the best beach in the country year after year, citing the pools, the trails, the hotels and the short drive from an international airport, and the voting has become part of the brand.

## The Name People Keep

The town has never traded the name in for something blander, and there is a reasonable case that it should not. The three words on the sign are the only part of this history that a visitor arriving purely for the snorkelling is guaranteed to encounter. Ipojuca was carved out as a district on 12 November 1895, seven years after abolition, and the coast has been rebuilt several times over since — pousadas into resorts, fishing beach into brand, and the industrial port complex of Suape rising a short way north. The name is older than any of it, and it is still doing exactly what it was built to do: carrying information about what arrived here, to anyone who knows how to hear it.

## Flight Context

Porto de Galinhas lies at 8.50 degrees south, 35.00 degrees west, on the coast of Ipojuca municipality about 60 km south-southwest of Recife. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,000-4,000 feet AGL. The defining feature from the air is the offshore reef: a pale, broken bar running parallel to the shoreline a few hundred metres out, spectacular at low tide when the trapped pools show as turquoise patches against darker water. The Maracaípe estuary and its mangroves mark the southern end of the built-up strip; the industrial cranes and breakwaters of the Port of Suape are unmistakable roughly 20 km north and make the best navigation fix on this coast. PE-09 and PE-60 run inland. Nearest airport is Recife/Guararapes (SBRF), about 50 km north, whose approach paths cross this coastline. Expect good visibility, afternoon onshore southeasterlies, and the wettest, cloudiest conditions from April through July.
