# Recife

The reef that gave Recife its name is not coral but hardened ancient beach - and everything about the city, from its fifty bridges to its sharks to its carnival, follows from the water it was built in.

**Location:** -8.0539, -34.8808

**Categories:** brazil, cities, pernambuco, coastal, music-and-carnival

## Introduction

Everyone assumes it is coral. It is not. The long ridge running parallel to Recife's shoreline, breaking the Atlantic into white water a few hundred metres out, is a consolidated ancient beach - sand pressed into rock, now as firm and hard as stone. It made a harbour where the coast offered none, and sailors named it before anyone bothered naming the town. The royal Charter Act of 12 March 1537 records the settlement's arrecife dos navios, the reef of the ships. At that point Recife was a scatter of fishing shacks, inns and warehouses on the delta between the Capibaribe and the Beberibe rivers. The reef gave it a name and a reason to exist, and it has been arguing with the sea ever since.

## Three Islands, Fifty Bridges

The city proper is built on three islands - Recife, Santo Antônio and Boa Vista - with the Beberibe and the Capibaribe running between them and the Jiquiá, Tejipió, Jordão and Pina threading the rest. More than fifty bridges stitch the pieces together, which is why the place gets called the Venice of Brazil, and why a recifense crosses water several times before lunch without noticing. The whole city sits around ten metres above sea level. Water decided where the port went and which town would win the argument: Olinda, up the coast, was older and grander, but Recife had the anchorage. In the Mascate War of 1710-11 the merchants of Recife fought the planters of Olinda over precedence. The harbour won. Recife had also spent the years 1630 to 1654 as Mauritsstad, capital of Dutch Brazil, before the Portuguese took it back after the two Battles of Guararapes - a chapter with its own literature, and its own scars.

## The Rooster of the Small Hours

Carnival here starts before the sun. On Carnival Saturday the Galo da Madrugada, a bloco founded in 1978 by Enéias Freire, fills the streets of São José; Guinness lists it as the largest carnival parade in the world by number of participants, and in 2013 that number passed 2.5 million. The music is frevo, whose name is traced to frever, a local variant of ferver, to boil. UNESCO inscribed it in 2012 as Frevo, performing arts of the Carnival of Recife. The dancers, passistas, took their steps from capoeira and carry small bright umbrellas they spin and throw. Alongside it comes maracatu nação, the baque virado, whose drum groups descend from the investiture ceremonies of the Reis do Congo - enslaved men granted authority over their own communities by the colonial administration. On the Pátio do Terço, the Night of the Silent Drums honours the enslaved who died in the city's prisons. It is the quietest thing that happens all week.

## Crabs with Brains

Recife's other great export was invented in 1991 by people fed up with a city they thought had stalled. Fred 04 and DJ Renato L wrote a manifesto called Caranguejos com Cérebro - crabs with brains - proposing the mangrove as the model for Recife: muddy, unglamorous, apparently stagnant, and in fact one of the most productive systems on earth. Manguebeat ran maracatu drum patterns into rock, rap and electronics. Chico Science and Nação Zumbi made it famous. Chico Science, born Francisco de Assis França in 1966, lost control of his car on 2 February 1997 and died; he was thirty. The movement outlived him, and so did its image - a satellite dish planted in the mud.

## Boa Viagem

South of the centre, Boa Viagem runs for kilometres of pale sand behind a wall of apartment towers; with 100,388 residents in 2008 it was the city's most populous neighbourhood. It is also why Recife turns up in shark literature. Before the 1990s attacks here were essentially unknown. The city began keeping official records on 28 June 1992, and between 1992 and 2021 there were 64 attacks along a 20-kilometre stretch of coast, 26 of them fatal, the last death on 10 July 2021. Surfing has been banned on the urban beaches since 1995. The mechanism appears to be local geography: a sandbank runs out roughly a thousand metres offshore, and the channel between it and the beach, five to eight metres deep, concentrates rays and other prey and draws bull sharks in close. Warning signs stand along the sand, and the state's shark hazard council tells bathers to avoid dawn and dusk, cloudy water, high tide, and anything deeper than the waist.

## The Working City

The 2022 census counted 1,488,920 people in Recife itself; its metropolitan area is the fifth most populous in Brazil and the largest in the Northeast. Most cargo now leaves from Suape, down the coast at Ipojuca, which moves over 8.4 million tonnes a year and hosts a Petrobras refinery among more than ninety companies, while the Port of Recife sits in the middle of the old city. The metro covers 39.5 kilometres and 29 stations plus nine light-rail stops, though the average commute by public transport runs to 96 minutes a day. Football arrived in 1902, when English and Dutch sailors played a game on the beach; Sport, Santa Cruz and Náutico have been arguing about it since. And every 16 July the city stops for Nossa Senhora do Carmo, its patroness since 1909. Behind all of it, still, is that ridge of hardened sand offshore - the accident that turned a fishing camp into a port.

## Flight Context

Recife sits at 8.05 degrees south, 34.88 degrees west, on the Atlantic coast of northeastern Brazil at about 10 metres elevation. The defining visual feature from the air is the reef line running parallel to the shore, a straight pale bar of surf a few hundred metres out, with the three-island core of the city - Recife, Santo Antônio and Boa Vista - immediately inland where the Capibaribe and Beberibe meet the sea. Boa Viagem's tower wall runs south along the beach; the Suape industrial port is further south at Ipojuca. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000 to 5,000 feet to resolve the bridges and the island geometry, 8,000 to 10,000 feet for the whole metropolitan sprawl and the reef's full length. Recife/Guararapes-Gilberto Freyre International (SBRF) lies on the southern side of the city behind Boa Viagem; João Pessoa (SBJP) is the nearest alternate to the north and Maceió (SBMO) to the south. The climate is tropical monsoon: June is the wettest month at around 390 mm and brings low ceilings and heavy showers, while October and November are the driest and clearest, with November averaging about 39 mm.
