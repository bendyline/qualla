# Capela Dourada

Recife's Golden Chapel, built by wealthy Franciscan lay brothers from 1696, is a room where almost no surface was left unglided - and where an anonymous devotee once scratched the faces off the villains in two of its paintings.

**Location:** -8.0624, -34.8774

**Categories:** brazil, pernambuco, recife, baroque, religious-architecture, colonial-history

## Introduction

Somebody got angry at a painting here, and the damage is still visible. High on the wall of the Capela Dourada, near the benches, hang two large panels depicting the arrest and death of Franciscan friars. The friars are intact. The faces of their tormentors have been scratched out - gouged away at a date nobody recorded, by a devotee who apparently could not sit through mass looking at them. It is the most human thing in a room otherwise designed to overwhelm. The Capela Dourada is a small chapel in central Recife where carved wood covers nearly every square centimetre of wall, altar and ceiling, and nearly all of that wood is covered in gold.

## Before the Gold, a Fort

The chapel belongs to the Franciscan complex of Santo Antonio, and the ground it stands on had a rough century first. Franciscan friars settled here on 28 October 1606, on what was then called the Ilha dos Navios, on land donated by a sugar planter named Marcos Andre; by 1613 they had an oratory and a hospital attached to it. Then the Dutch West India Company took Pernambuco. Through the occupation the convent was not a convent at all - it was walled, bastioned and garrisoned as Forte Ernestus, and for a period the Anglican church used part of it to serve Company officers. The friars got their buildings back after the Dutch withdrew in 1654. The chapel's cornerstone would be laid forty-two years later.

## The Merchants' Chapel

It was not the friars who built it. The Venerable Third Order of Saint Francis of the Wounds - the lay brotherhood attached to the convent - counted a great many of Recife's prosperous merchants among its members, and they wanted a chapel for the Order's novices. Hence its other name, Capela dos Novicos. Captain General Caetano de Melo e Castro laid the cornerstone on 13 May 1696, with the Portuguese captain Antonio Fernandes de Matos serving as master builder. The chapel opened to the public on 15 September 1697, at a mass presided over by the commissary visitor Friar Jeronimo da Ressurreicao, though it was nowhere near finished; work continued until 1724. This was a period of real prosperity in Pernambuco, and the room is an unembarrassed statement of what sugar and trade could buy.

## Where the Name Comes From

Gold leaf over carved wood, applied until the wood stops reading as wood. The walls, the altars, the ceiling - the ornament fills practically every available space, and the effect in a room this size is less like decoration than like being inside a reliquary. The ceiling is divided into coffers holding oil-painted panels. The main chapel has a central niche for a large crucifix, with side niches for Saints Cosmas and Damian. Along the side walls run panels of azulejos, the blue-and-white tilework that Portuguese builders carried everywhere they went, interspersed with smaller altars and dozens of painted panels showing saints and personifications of Faith, Hope, Charity and Constance.

## Named Hands and Unnamed Ones

Some of the makers are known. Luis Machado, working in the seventeenth century, produced the ceiling with its outer arches, the crossing and the main chapel, along with auxiliary furniture. Antonio Martins Santiago carved the main chapel in 1698; Manuel de Jesus Pinto gilded it in 1799. The painters are another matter. The historian Fernando Pio, a former minister of the Third Order, went through the brotherhood's own archive looking for their names and found nothing - not one attribution, despite patient searching. What the Order's book of receipts and expenses does record is when: the paintings were executed between 1699 and 1700, and the ceiling panels between 1701 and 1702. Dozens of pictures, precisely dated, by nobody.

## The Small Things

Among the statues, the ones people stop for are Saint Elizabeth of Portugal, the Cristo atado a coluna - Christ bound to the column - and the Senhor dos Passos, the Lord of the Steps, a life-size image inlaid with rubies. In the sacristy stands furniture carved from rosewood in 1762, together with a marble table and a washbasin imported from Estremoz, the Portuguese town whose quarries supplied stone across the empire. Between 1776 and 1777 the chapel's ceiling underwent serious repair, carefully enough that the coffered panels came through it undamaged, including the two near the benches with the scratched-out faces.

## Still There, Still Working

The chapel sits inside the Santo Antonio district in the heart of Recife, part of a complex that also holds the Church of the Third Order of Saint Francis and a Franciscan museum of religious art. Three centuries of coastal humidity, a Dutch occupation, and a city that grew from a reef-side port into a metropolis of a million and a half have not moved it. What it offers is a very particular kind of time travel: a room built by merchants who wanted their faith to look expensive, decorated by artists whose names their own brotherhood failed to write down, and defaced in one small corner by someone who took the story on the walls entirely too personally.

## Flight Context

The Capela Dourada stands at 8.062 degrees south, 34.877 degrees west, in the Santo Antonio district on one of the river islands at the heart of Recife, between arms of the Capibaribe. Recife/Guararapes-Gilberto Freyre International (SBRF) lies about eight kilometres south, with approaches over Boa Viagem beach. The chapel itself is invisible from the air - it is a small building buried in a dense colonial street grid - so navigate to the island: from 2,000 to 4,000 feet you can pick out the braided channels of the Capibaribe splitting the old city into islands, the long sandstone reef offshore that gave Recife its name, and the low-rise colonial core standing in obvious contrast to the high-rise wall of Boa Viagem to the south. Humid and often hazy; clearest mornings fall in the September-to-February dry season.
