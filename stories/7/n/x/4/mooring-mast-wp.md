# Zeppelin Mooring Mast, Recife

A nineteen-metre steel stub in a Recife park is the last zeppelin mooring tower left standing anywhere, and for eight years it was the point where transatlantic air travel first touched the Americas.

**Location:** -8.0784, -34.9263

**Categories:** aviation-history, airships, engineering, brazil, preserved-landmarks

## Introduction

It took three hundred soldiers to catch it. On 22 May 1930 the Graf Zeppelin came down over Campo do Jiquiá, on the flat western edge of Recife, and a landing party three hundred strong took hold of its ropes and walked the airship out of the sky by hand. Scheduled service followed the next year, and for most of the next decade Recife was where a crossing from Europe first touched the Americas. What marked the spot was not the soaring tower of the airship posters but a squat steel mast of sixteen and a half metres. It is still there, in a neighbourhood park, behind a fence - and every other one like it in the world has been scrapped.

## Why an Airship Needs a Post

Airships almost never came to grief in the air. They came to grief within a few metres of the ground, in the minutes when a structure the length of an ocean liner was trying to sit still in moving air. Sheds could not solve it - you had to get the ship into the shed first, crosswind, and many were damaged doing exactly that. The answer, worked out over a century ago and refined from Leonardo Torres Quevedo's designs, was to stop fighting the wind and let the ship ride it: attach the nose to a mast fitted with a rotating tip, and the whole hull weathervanes downwind like a wind sock. Engineers in France, Britain, the United States and Germany then spent a decade arguing about height. Tall masts looked magnificent - the spire of the Empire State Building was built as one - but mooring a dirigible for any length of time to a high mast in the middle of a city turned out to be unworkable, and New York's spire became a broadcast antenna instead. The low or 'stub' mast won.

## Two Hundred Men on the Ropes

The procedure was less aviation than seamanship. Before descending, the ship raised the ground crew by radio or flag to learn the wind and the surface temperature. Someone lit a smoky fire so the wind direction could be read from the air. The Graf then made a long approach, sinking at about a hundred feet a minute, and dropped her lines as she crossed the landing flag. Yaw lines from the nose went out to thirty men on each side. Twenty more a side hauled on spider lines - short ropes radiating from a block like legs. When the hull touched down, fifty men held the rails of the control car and twenty held the after car, with thirty in reserve: two hundred men to park one aircraft. It was not safe work. In a famous piece of film shot in 1932, three members of a US Navy party failed to let go of the handling lines as the airship Akron rose; two of them fell to their deaths, and the third clung on until the crew could haul him aboard.

## Recife on the Timetable

The Germans had not used mooring masts at all until the Graf Zeppelin entered service in 1928, and they never used high ones. What they built at Jiquiá was a stub mast on an open field, and between 1930 and 1938 it made Recife one of the first cities in the Americas with a direct, non-stop connection to Europe. Recife was the first American landfall after the Atlantic crossing; passengers going on to Rio de Janeiro carried on by air after the Graf had been serviced. Tickets were sold in Brazil by the Sindicato Condor. In all, the Graf Zeppelin made sixty-four round trips to Brazil on what was the first regular intercontinental commercial air passenger service in history. A purpose-built hangar and tower for Rio were completed at the end of December 1936, and in April of that year the composer Heitor Villa-Lobos boarded the Hindenburg for Europe.

## Bad Days at Jiquiá

Recife was not an easy stop. Tropical weather soaks into an airship's outer cover and puts weight on it fast, and in April 1935 the Graf Zeppelin was forced down there heavy, damaging her rudder and tearing the envelope. That November the crew held off landing for three days because of civil unrest in the city, then gave up and put down at Maceió instead. The tower itself had to grow to keep up. The 1930 original stood 16.5 metres and weighed over three tonnes; in 1936 it was replaced by a version three metres taller, at 19.5 metres, with heavier steel to take the strain of the LZ-129 Hindenburg, an airship of more than two hundred tonnes.

## The Last One Standing

The Hindenburg burned at Lakehurst in May 1937 and the passenger airship simply stopped being a thing that existed. Operations at Jiquiá were wound up in 1938. The field became a park, the tower became a curiosity, and for decades nobody was quite sure what to do with it: there was a restoration in the 1980s, and another in 2013, when the artist and conservator Jobson Figueiredo returned the structure to its original condition along with the Second World War powder magazines that share the Parque Jiquiá grounds. Its survival is genuinely improbable. Masts like it went up in many countries through the 1920s and 1930s, and every other one was cut up for scrap or quietly rusted away. The stub of painted steel in a Recife neighbourhood park is the only zeppelin mooring tower left anywhere in the world in its original form.

## Flight Context

The Torre do Zeppelin stands at 8.0784 degrees south, 34.9263 degrees west, in Parque Jiquiá on the western side of Recife, roughly four kilometres inland from the Capibaribe's mouth. It is small - about nineteen metres of open steel lattice - so it will not be picked out from cruise altitude; the feature to look for is the flat green rectangle of the old Campo do Jiquiá landing ground amid dense low-rise housing. Recife/Guararapes-Gilberto Freyre International (SBRF) lies about four nautical miles to the south-southeast, and the park sits under the northern approach and departure paths. Best viewing is a slow orbit at 1,500 to 2,500 feet in the morning, before the sea-breeze cumulus builds; the wettest months, March through July, bring low cloud and heavy showers off the Atlantic.
