# Olinda

The colonial capital that lost its port, its trade and eventually its status to Recife next door - and kept, in exchange, one of the best-preserved eighteenth-century townscapes in the Americas.

**Location:** -8.0089, -34.8550

**Categories:** unesco-world-heritage, colonial-architecture, brazil, carnival, historic-towns

## Introduction

Olinda lost, and that is exactly why it is still here. For three centuries this hill was the seat of the captaincy of Pernambuco - the richest sugar territory on earth - while the swampy reef-sheltered harbour a few kilometres south was merely where the ships tied up. Then the Dutch burned the hill, the harbour town grew into Recife, and in 1827 Recife took the capital as well. Recife got the commerce, the government and the modern buildings; Olinda kept the hill, and the hill kept its eighteenth century. In 1982 UNESCO put the whole historic centre on the World Heritage list.

## Ó, Linda

The hills above this stretch of coast were held by Caeté and Tupinambá communities, frequently at war with one another, and the Portuguese - who were not the first Europeans here, French traders having got there before them - built their stronghold on a former Caeté village on the high ground. The settlement was founded in 1535 by Duarte Coelho Pereira and raised to a town on 12 March 1537. Legend gives the name to an exclamation Coelho supposedly made when he saw the view: Ó, linda - oh, beautiful. What paid for the churches was less lyrical. Sugarcane made Olinda a colonial power and the labour was performed by enslaved Africans, shipped in for a system that outlived the Portuguese, the Dutch, and the Portuguese again; slavery was not abolished in Brazil until the Lei Áurea of 1888.

## Burned, Then Left Alone

The Dutch took Olinda in February 1630, held it briefly, and put it to the torch in 1631 as they pulled back to fortify Recife and the islands. The Church of São João is among the handful of buildings that came through it. When the Portuguese returned in 1654 they rebuilt, but the centre of gravity had already shifted downhill and out to sea: Recife had the port and the money, and it kept them. Most of what stands in Olinda today therefore dates from the eighteenth century, put up by a town that was slowly ceasing to matter commercially. Recife formally became capital of Pernambuco in 1827. Olinda is now a municipality of roughly 350,000 people, on 41.7 square kilometres, effectively a district of the Recife metropolitan area - and its cathedral remains the primary seat of the Archdiocese of Olinda and Recife.

## The Hill of Churches

Walking Olinda is a matter of climbing. The streets run up in short, steep, disorienting cobbled stretches between walls washed in ochre, blue and white, and every few blocks another baroque façade appears at an angle you did not expect. At the summit is the Alto da Sé and the cathedral of São Salvador do Mundo, with a belvedere in its yard from which the Atlantic lies to the east and the towers of Recife to the west. The Mosteiro de São Bento, built in 1582, houses extraordinary woodcarving in its chapel and once held Brazil's first law school; Sunday mass there is still sung in Gregorian chant. The Convento São Francisco of 1585 contains the Capela de São Roque and the Igreja de Nossa Senhora das Neves. The Igreja da Misericórdia, from 1540, is lined with azulejos and gilt carving, and Nossa Senhora do Amparo dates from 1613.

## Studios, Serenades and an Inquisition Jail

The town has been a working artists' quarter for decades rather than a museum piece. Rua do Amparo is the spine of it: studios, small hotels, restaurants and galleries in eighteenth-century houses. The Mercado da Ribeira, itself an eighteenth-century building, is now art galleries and shops. The Museu do Mamulengo keeps the puppets of Pernambuco's traditional glove-puppet theatre. The Museu de Arte Contemporânea holds Portinari, Di Cavalcanti, Cícero Dias and Francisco Brennand in a building of 1756 that was the only ecclesiastical prison in Brazil during the Inquisition - a fact worth sitting with while you look at the paintings. On Friday evenings, serenade groups still work their way through the streets singing old songs, and forró and maracatu fill the clubs and squares year-round.

## Six Days in February

And then there is carnival, which is the reason many Brazilians know the name at all. Olinda's is nothing like Rio's: no bleachers, no roped-off corridors, no tickets, no spectators as such. Hundreds of small musical groups - some of them a single performer - play frevo and maracatu through the historic centre, and blocos parade with the giant dolls, the bonecos gigantes, held up above the crowd on people's shoulders. It runs continuously from Friday evening to Wednesday morning, and it opens at the stroke of midnight on the Saturday when O Homem da Meia-Noite comes out. If you are staying in the centre during those days, understand what you are choosing: the noise does not stop.

## Flight Context

Olinda sits at 8.009 degrees south, 34.855 degrees west, six to seven kilometres north of central Recife on the Atlantic coast, with an average elevation of only about 16 metres - but concentrated into hills steep enough to make the historic centre unmistakable from the air. Look for a tight cluster of terracotta roofs and white church towers on high ground, ringed by flat modern city, with surf breaking a few hundred metres to the east. Recife/Guararapes-Gilberto Freyre International (SBRF) lies about nine nautical miles to the south; a taxi from central Recife takes twenty minutes. The Alto da Sé belvedere gives the reciprocal view - Recife's tower blocks on the southern horizon. The climate is tropical monsoon, humid all year; clearest air is September to February, with the heaviest rain from March to July.
