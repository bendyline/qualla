# O Homem da Meia-Noite

A giant puppet that opens Olinda's carnival at the stroke of midnight, invented in 1931 by six men who had been blackballed from another club's board and decided to get out in front of it.

**Location:** -8.0106, -34.8528

**Categories:** carnival, folk-traditions, brazil, intangible-heritage, olinda

## Introduction

It began as a grudge. In early 1931 a handful of men in Olinda were refused seats on the board of the carnival club Cariri Olindense, and rather than sulk they founded a rival - with one idea that settled the argument permanently. Cariri had always been the first group to parade. So the new club would parade at midnight, in the gap between carnival Saturday and Sunday, before anyone else could. They built a giant to do it. Ninety-odd years later, O Homem da Meia-Noite - the Midnight Man - still steps out into the historic centre as the clock turns, riding above the crowd on the shoulders of whoever is carrying him, and Olinda's carnival still does not begin until he does.

## Founded on a Snub

The club was formally founded on 2 February 1931 by Benedito Bernardino da Silva, Luciano Anacleto de Queiroz, Sebastião da Silva, Cosme José dos Santos, Heliodoro Pereira da Silva and a shoemaker named Manoel Joaquim dos Santos, better known as Neco Monstro. The dispute that produced them was small and domestic - a board that would not have them - and the response was disproportionate in the best way. Choosing the midnight hour was not just theatre. It was a jurisdictional claim on the calendar, and it worked: the Midnight Man has been the opening act of Olinda's carnival ever since, the boneco gigante whose appearance in the historic centre tells thousands of people in the street that the thing has started.

## The Handing Over of the Key

Grudges in carnival towns tend to end in ritual rather than reconciliation, but this one managed both. Homem da Meia-Noite and Cariri Olindense made peace, and the peace acquired a ceremony: immediately after the Midnight Man's parade, his club hands the key of the city over to Cariri. The group that stole the opening slot ninety years ago now formally passes the celebration on to the group it stole it from, every single year, in front of the crowd. It is a small piece of choreography that says something true about how Olinda's carnival works - hundreds of independent groups, no central authority, order maintained entirely by agreements that everybody has decided to keep.

## A Clock and a Doll

The first version was almost nothing. Through 1931 and 1932 the club paraded with two objects: a standard bearing a clock with its hands at midnight, and the doll himself. Everything else - the music, the retinue, the accumulated business of a full carnival block - came later. Frevo is the rhythm the block is built around, that fast, brass-driven Pernambucan music written for people who are going to be walking and jumping for hours, though samba and marchinhas get played too. The years have not all been easy ones. Between 1950 and 1953 the Midnight Man stayed home, because the club could not afford to bring him out.

## The Family

A giant needs relatives. In 1967 the craftsman Julião das Máscaras built A Mulher do Dia - the Woman of the Day - taking his inspiration from the Mona Lisa and giving her long dark hair, a gold-toothed smile, and clothes of yellow and blue in reference to Yemọjá and Oshun, orixás of the Afro-Brazilian religious traditions that run deep through Pernambuco. She parades only on Sunday, which means that she and the Midnight Man cross paths just once, at the gathering when all the giant dolls come out together. In February 1990 the two were 'officially' married in a ceremony. Their first child, O Menino da Tarde, had actually arrived sixteen years earlier - Julião made him in 1974, and dressed him, with a straight face, as a grown man. In 1977 Sílvio Botelho added A Menina da Tarde, a vain girl who turns up in new clothes every carnival.

## Living Heritage

In 2006 the state of Pernambuco recognised O Homem da Meia-Noite as intangible cultural heritage, which is the modern bureaucratic way of saying what everyone in Olinda already knew. The thing being protected is not the puppet, exactly. It is the arrangement: an unroped, unticketed street carnival that runs around the clock from Friday evening to Wednesday morning, hundreds of small groups playing frevo and maracatu, giant figures moving above the crowd on people's shoulders, and one exact moment at midnight when a very tall man appears in a cobbled street and everybody yells.

## Flight Context

The Midnight Man's parade route runs through Olinda's historic centre at 8.0106 degrees south, 34.8528 degrees west, on the hill immediately north of Recife. From the air the landmark is the hill itself: a compact cluster of red pantile roofs and white church towers rising to the Alto da Sé, set apart from the flat urban sprawl on every side, with the Atlantic a few hundred metres east. Recife/Guararapes-Gilberto Freyre International (SBRF) lies about eight nautical miles south. Carnival falls in February or March, in the middle of the northeast's wet season, so expect low broken cloud and afternoon showers; a slow pass at 2,000 feet in the early morning gives the cleanest view of the historic centre before the sea breeze builds.
