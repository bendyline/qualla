# Palácio de Friburgo

The most monumental building in seventeenth-century Brazil had a lighthouse in one tower and an observatory in the other - and was pulled down so completely that it now survives only as engravings, a floor plan, and a computer model.

**Location:** -8.0609, -34.8773

**Categories:** brazil, pernambuco, history, lost-buildings, science

## Introduction

Go to the Praça da República in Recife and you will find a statue of John Maurice of Nassau-Siegen standing in front of the Palácio do Campo das Princesas, with the Santa Isabel theatre and the Palace of Justice nearby. The statue marks a place rather than commemorating a man who belongs to it. Under that square once stood the largest and most elaborate building in Brazil: the Palácio de Friburgo, Vrijburg to the Dutch, the Palácio das Torres - Palace of the Towers - to everyone else. It was finished in early 1642 and demolished before the eighteenth century was out. Not one wall is visible. Almost everything known about it comes from pictures.

## Two Towers Facing the Sea

John Maurice bought land on the island of Antônio Vaz in 1639 - the ground is now the Santo Antônio neighbourhood - and construction began the following year. The palace faced east, towards the old port and the Atlantic, with a staircase leading down into a walled rectangular courtyard rounded at the front; the back looked west, across the mouth of the Capibaribe. Two square towers of five storeys rose at either end, joined by a covered walkway, and from a distance the silhouette read less like a residence than a church. A large moat and mounted cannon protected the site, with Fort Ernesto in the vicinity, on ground now occupied by the Palace of Justice and the Franciscan convent of Santo Antônio. When it went up it was the most monumental structure in Brazil, and nothing built in the colony for a long time afterwards came close.

## A Light and a Sky

Those towers were not ornament. On a coast that offers sailors few fixed marks, they were navigational aids in their own right, and one of the pair was fitted out as a lighthouse visible six leagues away - far enough that a ship's watch would pick it up before it picked up the land. The other tower held an observatory. The sources describe it as the first founded in the Southern Hemisphere; the wider superlative, first in the Americas, attaches in those same sources to the lighthouse and to the garden below rather than to the telescope. Either way the arrangement is peculiar and worth sitting with: a governor's residence that doubled as a navigation beacon and an astronomical station, in a colony barely a decade old, on the edge of a continent Europe had scarcely finished drawing.

## Two Thousand Coconut Palms

Around the building John Maurice laid out a zoo-botanical garden - the first botanical garden in the Americas, by the account that has come down. Two thousand coconut palms went into the ground along with assorted fruit trees. The enclosures held parrots, macaws, swans, guinea fowl, peacocks, armadillos, tapirs, rabbits, monkeys and rodents. This was not a rich man's menagerie for its own sake. The count had brought working scientists and painters across the Atlantic with him: the naturalist Georg Marcgrave, the landscape painter Frans Post, the portraitist Albert Eckhout. The garden was a collection in the same sense their notebooks and canvases were - an attempt to hold the tropics still long enough to describe them.

## Demolished by Order

The palace outlived Dutch rule by more than a century, but not comfortably. It was damaged during the Pernambuco Insurrection, the long revolt that ended the Dutch presence, and by the 1770s it was a ruin standing in the middle of a growing Portuguese city. Between 1774 and 1787 the provincial governor José César de Meneses had it taken down. The Erário Régio, the royal treasury building, rose on the site and reused the old foundations along one of its sides. That is as close as the Palace of the Towers comes to still existing: load-bearing stone underneath someone else's building, on a square where nothing above ground remembers it.

## Rebuilt in Pixels

What survives is graphic. Frans Post produced an engraved view of the palace and its grounds; Georg Marcgrave drew a floor plan published in 1647; the back of the building turns up incidentally in a period engraving of Boa Vista, seen from the mainland where Avenida Conde da Boa Vista now runs. Between 2013 and 2015 Itaú Cultural in São Paulo commissioned a virtual model of the palace and its surroundings, assembled from that material and incorporating paintings by Post and Eckhout - the most complete reconstruction anyone has attempted. So you can walk through the Palácio de Friburgo. You simply cannot do it in Recife.

## Flight Context

The site lies at 8.061 degrees south, 34.877 degrees west, on what was the island of Antônio Vaz and is now the Santo Antônio district of central Recife, roughly 5 metres above sea level. From the air the marker is the Praça da República: a green rectangle of mature trees between the Palácio do Campo das Princesas, the Teatro Santa Isabel and the Palace of Justice, immediately west of the Capibaribe's final bend and about a kilometre inland from the reef line. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500 to 3,000 feet to make out the square and the surrounding island geometry the palace was built to command; the original east-facing orientation still reads from overhead, with the old port and the Atlantic ahead and the river mouth behind. Recife/Guararapes-Gilberto Freyre International (SBRF) is on the southern edge of the city, a short approach over Boa Viagem beach; João Pessoa (SBJP) lies north along the coast. Visibility is best from September through December; June brings the heaviest rain and lowest ceilings.
