# Dutch Brazil

For twenty-four years a Dutch republic governed the richest sugar coast on earth from a city called Mauritsstad, importing painters and naturalists along with the guns, and then lost the whole thing in a planters' war.

**Location:** -8.0637, -34.8743

**Categories:** colonial-history, dutch-empire, brazil, sugar-economy, religious-tolerance

## Introduction

The first coins ever struck in Brazil were minted by a government that was already losing. Cut off from Amsterdam and unable to pay its troops, the Dutch West India Company's administration in Recife hammered out emergency gold pieces in 1645 and 1646, then silver again in 1654, in its final months. Those coins carry the earliest appearance of the word "Brasil" on money anywhere. They were issued by a state that no longer exists. New Holland lasted twenty-four years, ruled at its peak nearly half the European-settled area of Brazil, and left behind ideas about tolerance and knowledge that outlasted the flag by centuries.

## Sugar Was the Whole Point

Nobody crossed the Atlantic for the scenery. Pernambuco in the 1620s was the largest and richest sugar-producing region in the world, and the Dutch Republic, at war with Habsburg Spain, wanted it. A first attempt went badly: Admiral Jacob Willekens took Salvador in May 1624 with twenty-six ships and 3,300 men, and a Spanish-Portuguese armada of fifty-two ships and 12,500 men took it back the following April. Piracy on a spectacular scale made a second try possible - Piet Heyn's capture of a Spanish silver convoy in Matanzas Bay in 1628 filled the company's treasury. In 1629 the target was set, and sixty-five ships under Hendrick Corneliszoon Loncq sailed for Pernambuco. Olinda fell on 16 February 1630; Recife and the island of António Vaz followed by 3 March. The sugar was made by enslaved Africans, and controlling the trade in people mattered as much as controlling the cane - the Dutch went on to seize Elmina Castle on the Gold Coast and, for a time, Luanda in Angola.

## The Count Who Collected a Country

In 1637 the company handed its Brazilian conquests to Johan Maurits van Nassau-Siegen, great-nephew of William the Silent, who landed at Recife that January. He governed for seven years and treated the colony as a subject worth documenting. He brought the painters Frans Post and Albert Eckhout, whose canvases of Brazilian landscapes, still lifes and portraits of Africans, Tupi and mixed-race Brazilians are among the earliest European images of the tropical Americas made from life. He brought the naturalists Georg Marcgrave and Willem Piso, whose fieldwork produced the Historia Naturalis Brasiliae of 1648 - the first organised European compendium of American natural knowledge, and a standard reference for well over a century. On António Vaz he laid out a new town, Mauritsstad, with streets, bridges and an astronomical observatory and meteorological station - the first of either built by Europeans anywhere in the Americas. He also created municipal and rural councils seating both Dutch and Portuguese members - less a democratic gesture than an admission of arithmetic.

## A Colony That Let You Pray

The arithmetic was this: the Dutch were a ruling minority in a Portuguese-speaking country. Company men mostly went home when their contracts ended, and a plantation required capital few Dutch emigrants had. So the colony sold something Iberian Brazil could not - freedom of worship and security of property. By 1635 Portuguese settlers were choosing Dutch-held land over Portuguese-held land for exactly that reason. Under Maurits, Jews who had been forced into Christian baptism in Portugal and Spain were permitted to return openly to Judaism; an estimated 700 Jews lived in Dutch Brazil, roughly 4.7 percent of the population. Calvinists worshipped freely. So, remarkably for the decade of the Thirty Years' War, did the Catholic majority, and the Franciscan, Carmelite and Benedictine orders kept their monasteries and went on preaching. The limits deserve precision. Tolerance in New Holland was a recruitment policy as much as a principle, and it ran alongside an economy built on the forced labour of people granted no liberty at all.

## Who Actually Fought

The war for Brazil is usually told as Dutch against Portuguese, which leaves out most of the people who did the fighting. Indigenous nations were drawn in on both sides; most allied with the Dutch, but not all. A Potiguara leader baptised as Dom Antonio Filipe Camarao fought for the Portuguese and was made a knight of the Order of Christ for it. Both sides put enslaved Africans into the field, sometimes on a promise of freedom. Henrique Dias, who commanded a Black regiment for the Portuguese crown, was granted noble status but never received the knighthood he had been promised. When the Dutch were expelled, Portuguese forces hunted down the Indigenous communities that had backed them in reprisals, and Portuguese who had stayed under Dutch rule spent years in court against those who had fled and now wanted their sugar mills and cane fields back.

## Twenty-Four Years, Then Nothing

The company recalled Maurits in 1644 to cut military spending. Within a year the planters of Pernambuco - who had never accepted Dutch rule and resented the interest rates Dutch lenders charged them to rebuild - rose in revolt, and in August 1645 beat a Dutch force outside Recife. By 1646 the company held four toeholds on the coast. The Portuguese recapture of Angola severed the supply of enslaved labour the colony's economy depended on, and two defeats at Guararapes, the second in February 1649, broke Dutch land power for good. The garrison in Recife made its last stand and signed the capitulation on 26 January 1654. By the Treaty of The Hague of 6 August 1661, the Netherlands formally ceded New Holland to Portugal in return for an indemnity paid over sixteen years. The sugar boom never returned; the disruption pushed planters toward the Caribbean, and Brazil's near-monopoly ended with it.

## Flight Context

Dutch Brazil's capital sat at roughly 8.06 degrees south, 34.87 degrees west, on the low islands and reefs where the Capibaribe and Beberibe rivers meet the Atlantic - today the Recife Antigo district of Recife. Recife/Guararapes-Gilberto Freyre International (SBRF) lies about seven kilometres south of the old town, with approaches over Boa Viagem beach. From 3,000 to 5,000 feet the geography that decided the war is obvious: a long sandstone reef running parallel to the shore, a natural harbour behind it, and a chain of islands that a small European garrison could fortify and a large Portuguese army could not easily storm. Olinda's hill stands out four nautical miles to the north. The coast is hazy and humid year-round; the clearest air is usually early morning between September and February, outside the heavy autumn rains.
