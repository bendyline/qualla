# Second Battle of Guararapes

On 19 February 1649 a smaller Luso-Brazilian force of Portuguese settlers, Potiguara warriors and Black soldiers came out of the swamps behind the Dutch line and broke the Dutch West India Company's hold on Pernambuco.

**Location:** -8.1122, -35.0156

**Categories:** brazil, pernambuco, military-history, colonial-history, dutch-empire, battlefields

## Introduction

The Dutch were watching the wrong road. Colonel Van den Brinck marched out of Recife on 17 February 1649 with a force built to fight the way European armies fought - in ranks, on open ground, along the good coastal roads a serious enemy would obviously use - and he set his line to cover exactly those roads. Two days later the enemy arrived from the west instead, out of the wetlands and down through the Guararapes hills, having walked in on minor trails nobody on the Dutch side had thought to defend. What followed took several hours and effectively ended Dutch rule in Pernambuco.

## Why There Was a Second One

The first had gone badly too. The Portuguese planters of Pernambuco had risen against the Dutch West India Company, and the Dutch, freed from their war with Spain by the peace concluded in 1647, sent an expeditionary force to settle the matter: forty-one ships, six thousand men. It reached Recife - Mauritsstad, as the Company called it - late, and immediately ran into trouble. In April 1648, on the same ground south of the city, the Portuguese routed it at the First Battle of Guararapes. Ten months later Van den Brinck led roughly three and a half thousand troops back to the same hills, against about twenty-six hundred defenders.

## Who Actually Stood There

The force waiting in the hills is the reason this battle is remembered the way it is in Brazil. Overall command sat with Francisco Barreto de Meneses. Under him, Joao Fernandes Vieira - a landowner born in Funchal, Madeira - and Andre Vidal de Negreiros each led an infantry terco. Filipe Camarao commanded Potiguara warriors, fighting on ground his people had known for far longer than any European had. Henrique Dias, whose parents' free or enslaved status historians still dispute, held the title governador da gente preta and led a regiment of Black soldiers, many of them formerly enslaved men from plantations the war had swept through. The Dutch army opposite was larger and better equipped and mostly did not want to be there: its ranks were filled with mercenaries recruited across Europe, principally from the German lands, who had no stake in Pernambuco at all. They fought in thick, brightly coloured European clothing and heavy armour that slowed them in the heat, and contemporary accounts describe them as pale and sickly.

## Nineteen February

The Luso-Brazilian force worked the terrain rather than the line. Musket volleys came out of the trees, thinning the Dutch ranks from cover, and then the attackers closed with blades. Coming from the west, they took the Dutch position in the flank - the direction the defensive line had not been drawn to face. Van den Brinck fell mortally wounded. After several hours the Dutch broke and retreated north toward Recife, abandoning their artillery on the field. Casualty figures vary between accounts, but every version records the same lopsided result: Dutch losses running past a thousand killed, wounded and captured, against Portuguese losses in the dozens. Both sides had been fighting on this coast long enough to understand what the numbers meant.

## An Enemy's Report

The most useful description of what happened comes from the losing side. Michiel van Goch, writing a few days afterwards, tried to explain the defeat to his superiors and produced something closer to an admission. The enemy's men, he wrote, were naturally agile and surefooted, able to advance or retreat speedily - Brazilians, Tapuyas, Negroes, Mamelucos, all natives of this country, along with Portuguese and Italians whose constitution let them adapt readily to the terrain, so that they could range the woods, cross the swamps and climb or descend the hills with remarkable speed. His own men, he went on, fought ranged in serried ranks after the manner of the fatherland, and were sluggish and flabby, unsuited to this kind of country. It is a rare thing: a defeated officer describing his opponents without contempt, and reserving it for his own side.

## What It Ended

The Dutch did not leave immediately. The Company held Recife until 1654, and a treaty formally settling matters was not signed until 1661. But the two defeats at Guararapes, together with the Portuguese recapture of Angola that cut off the enslaved labour the Brazilian colony's sugar economy depended on, changed the arithmetic in Amsterdam. Opinion there concluded that Dutch Brazil no longer had a future worth fighting for, which effectively sealed the colony's fate. Guararapes convinced the Dutch, in one contemporary judgement, that the Portuguese were formidable opponents - something which they had hitherto refused to concede.

## A Nation's Founding Myth, With Footnotes

Filipe Camarao and Henrique Dias both received honours from the Portuguese crown, and the memory of a force combining white settlers, Indigenous warriors and Black soldiers became central to how Brazil tells the story. Since 1994 the Brazilian Army has officially commemorated Guararapes as the moment its seeds were planted, marking the date of the first battle, 19 April 1648, as Army Day; a 2012 federal law inscribed the commanders in the Book of Heroes of the Nation. Historians add the necessary footnote - there was no Brazilian nation and no Brazilian army in 1649, and no modern unit has institutional continuity with the men in those hills. The battlefield is now the Parque Historico Nacional dos Guararapes. Gillis Peeters painted the fight in Antwerp in 1650; two centuries later Victor Meirelles painted it again, as Brazil went looking for its own beginning and decided it had happened here.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lies at 8.11 degrees south, 35.02 degrees west, in what is now Jaboatao dos Guararapes in metropolitan Recife, preserved as the Parque Historico Nacional dos Guararapes. Recife/Guararapes-Gilberto Freyre International (SBRF) is directly adjacent - the airport carries the battle's name, and its runway sits barely two kilometres from the ground the Dutch tried to hold. Fly it at 1,500 to 3,000 feet on a downwind for Recife and the tactical problem is obvious from the air: a low chain of wooded hills standing between the coastal strip and the wetlands inland, with Recife's high-rise wall of Boa Viagem to the north and northeast. The Dutch line faced the coast; the attack came from the green, boggy ground on the far side. Visibility on this coast is best early on dry-season mornings between September and February.
