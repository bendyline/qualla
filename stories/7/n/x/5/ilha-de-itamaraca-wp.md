# Ilha de Itamaracá

Sixty-six square kilometres of Atlantic forest and reef-sheltered beach off Pernambuco, holding the first sugar mill in Portuguese America, one of the earliest Jewish congregations in the hemisphere, and the dance that fishermen's wives invented waiting on the sand.

**Location:** -7.7478, -34.8258

**Categories:** Atlantic islands of Brazil, Populated coastal places in Pernambuco, Former Portuguese colonies, Municipalities in Pernambuco, Beaches of Brazil

## Introduction

Until 2008 the Queen of Ciranda worked as a school lunch lady. Maria Madalena Correia do Nascimento was born on Ilha de Itamaracá on 12 January 1944, took the stage name Lia de Itamaracá, recorded an album called A Rainha da Ciranda in 1977 and was paid nothing for it. Recognition came late and from outside: a producer named Beto Hees put her on the bill at the Abril Pro Rock festival in 1998, and her first CD followed four years after that. The island she carries in her name is a small one — sixty-six square kilometres of forest, mangrove and beach off the coast of Pernambuco — and it has been producing outsized history for five centuries.

## The Stone That Rattles

Itamaracá comes from Tupi: itá, stone, and mbara'ká, the rattle. Stone that rattles, or stone that sings, depending on who is translating. Two-thirds of the island remains under protected Atlantic forest, the Mata Atlântica that once ran unbroken down most of the Brazilian coast and now survives in fragments. Settlement clings to a narrow strip behind twelve kilometres of white sand on the eastern shore. A reef three kilometres offshore takes the force out of the Atlantic, so the water inside it stays shallow, flat and warm, between 26 and 31 degrees most of the year. Since 1995, municipal rules have capped buildings at two storeys and barred industry outright. There are no towers on this island, which on this coast is worth noticing.

## The First Mill in Portuguese America

A sugar mill was established on Itamaracá in 1516, the first in Portuguese America, and the settlement around it is counted as the oldest continuously inhabited Portuguese place in the hemisphere. A chapel dedicated to Nossa Senhora da Conceição was already functioning here by 1526. In 1534 King John III made the island the seat of a hereditary captaincy; the Captaincy of Itamaracá took its name from this scrap of land while covering most of what is now the state of Paraíba. Vila Velha was its capital, and by 1630 it held more than a hundred buildings, two churches and a Santa Casa de Misericórdia. Sugar and coconut carried the island for a century and a half, worked by enslaved people whose names the mill ledgers rarely troubled to record. When gold pulled Brazil's centre of gravity south in the late seventeenth century, Itamaracá was left behind.

## A Book Printed in Hanau

The island is credited with one of the first Jewish communities in the Americas, and almost nothing physical survives to prove it. Almost. In the Yosef Goldman Collection there is a copy of Sefer Shefa Tal, a kabbalistic work printed in Hanau, Germany, in 1612. On its pages someone wrote a statement of ownership: Rabbi Jacob Lagarto, of a congregation in Tamarica, Brazil. Lagarto had come to Brazil as a young man and wrote a volume of aphorisms of which no copy is known to exist. One inscription, in one book, is the entire surviving material record of a whole congregation.

## The Circle on the Sand

Ciranda was invented here, by the wives of fishermen waiting on the beach for the boats to come back. The dance is a wide ring of people holding hands, moving to a slow repeating rhythm that can absorb newcomers indefinitely; you join by taking two hands, and that is the whole entry requirement. It became one of Pernambuco's signature forms, and Lia de Itamaracá became its most recognisable voice — honoured with a touring exhibition of her costumes and history in March 2023 and a national music award in Brasília that June. Forte Orange, built by the Dutch in 1631 and rebuilt by the Portuguese in 1654, gets most of the postcards. The ciranda is what the island actually exported.

## The Leeward Side

Two things about Itamaracá rarely appear in the tourist material. The island holds a state prison complex, sited on the sheltered western side well away from the beaches — prisons were put on islands here, as elsewhere, on the assumption that most people could not swim. A long-running programme to relocate the inmates to a new facility on the mainland near Itaquitinga has been under way for years. The second is more cheerful: a federal centre for aquatic mammals operates on the island, caring for Antillean manatees, Trichechus manatus, a species that has all but vanished from this coast. Around 24,540 people live here, on fishing, tourism and small commerce. The bridge and causeway from Itapissuma is the only way in.

## Flight Context

Ilha de Itamaracá sits at 7.75 degrees south, 34.83 degrees west, immediately off the Pernambuco coast north of Recife, separated from the mainland by the narrow Santa Cruz channel and linked to Itapissuma by a bridge and causeway. Maximum elevation is about 30 metres. The island is an unmistakable coastal waypoint: 66 square kilometres of dark forest canopy with a bright 12-kilometre beach along the eastern edge and a reef line breaking about 3 kilometres offshore, the low star-shaped outline of Forte Orange at the northern tip. Best viewed from 2,000 to 4,000 feet AGL. Recife/Guararapes–Gilberto Freyre International (SBRF/REC) lies roughly 45 kilometres south; João Pessoa–Presidente Castro Pinto (SBJP/JPA) about 80 kilometres north. Expect scattered cumulus and showers from May to July, and a persistent southeasterly trade wind year round.
