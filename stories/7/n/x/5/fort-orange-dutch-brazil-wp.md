# Fort Orange (Dutch Brazil)

A Dutch earthwork held by a Polish captain, rebuilt in stone by the Portuguese who took it, left to rot for two centuries, and kept standing in the 1980s by a woodcarver selling his own work at the gate.

**Location:** -7.8106, -34.8394

**Categories:** brazil, pernambuco, forts, history, archaeology

## Introduction

The officer who held Fort Orange for the Dutch was Polish. Krzysztof Arciszewski - written into the Dutch records as Crestofle d'Artischau Arciszewski - commanded a detachment of 366 men on a low island off the southeastern tip of Itamaracá, a position that controlled the southern channel of Santa Cruz. Dutch forces under Steyn Callenfels had begun throwing up a field fortification there in May 1631 and named it for the House of Orange-Nassau, then ruling the Netherlands. The small island the fort was built on has since vanished under the sea. The fort has not.

## 1632

The Portuguese came for it in 1632, under the Conde de Bagnoli, and were beaten. They withdrew in enough disorder to leave their artillery behind - four brass pieces they had hauled up from the Arraial Velho do Bom Jesus. That single defence set up everything after it. With the channel held, the Dutch took the whole island of Itamaracá in 1633 from the forces of Salvador Pinheiro, and the fortification that had been a temporary earthwork was repaired and enlarged into something they intended to keep.

## Renamed by the Winners

In June 1646 the counter-offensive reached the island: André Vidal de Negreiros and João Fernandes Vieira, with Sergeant-Major Antônio Dias Cardoso given the job of attacking and razing the Dutch works. Eighteen artillery pieces were taken. Whether the fort itself fell is not clear from the records, but it was badly damaged and rebuilding began in 1649. After the Dutch capitulation at Recife in 1654 the position passed to Portuguese troops under Colonel Francisco de Figueroa, and Portuguese military engineers rebuilt it properly - stone masonry and lime, laid out as a regular quadrangular polygon with pentagonal bastions at the corners in the Vauban system, with stone bartizans, an armorial gate, barracks and casemates tucked under the ramparts and wrapped around the embankment. Officially it became the Fort of Santa Cruz de Itamaracá. Almost nobody has ever called it that.

## Two Centuries of Not Quite Falling Down

An inspection in 1696 found the garrison consisting of a sergeant-major, a captain, a lieutenant, a sergeant, a constable and two companies of the Terços of Recife, holding twenty-five guns of calibres from twenty down to twelve. Repairs came again in 1777. By 1800 the place had been abandoned and was in ruins. Restoration was ordered in 1817, the year the forces of Padre Tenório occupied it during the Pernambucan Revolt, and when Augusto Fausto de Souza surveyed Brazil's fortifications in 1885 he counted twenty-three pieces here and described the whole thing as a ruin. Three and a half centuries of coastal weather do not treat stone gently, and for most of that period nobody had a use for it.

## The Woodcarver

National heritage protection arrived in 1938, with consolidation work in 1966 and 1973 as Itamaracá began marketing itself as a beach resort. The Army began its own repairs in the early 1980s, and the municipality took over administration in 1984. But what actually kept the monument in one piece through that decade was, in large part, one man. José Amaro de Souza Filho was a craftsman who had served a prison sentence, and he took on the fort's care and its storage, funding the work out of the sale of his own carvings on site - an arrangement that ran until 1992. In 1991 he founded the Fort Orange Foundation, which administered the place until 1998. It is an unusual line in the history of a fortification: colonels and governors for three hundred years, and then a man with chisels.

## Digging

Archaeology came in stages. In 1971 the archaeology laboratory of the Federal University of Pernambuco prospected part of the site, identified the kitchen, the chapel, the rooms and the casemates, and recovered personal objects, weapons and ammunition of assorted calibres. In 1998 the property went from the city to the Ministry of Culture and on to Fade/UFPE, the university's development foundation, which from 2000 coordinated a full research project funded by the MOWIC Foundation, the Dutch Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Brazil's Ministry of Culture through IPHAN, and the Pernambuco state government - the Netherlands helping to pay for the excavation of a fort it had lost. Two campaigns followed, January to March 2002 and October 2002 to June 2003, along with new sea defences and a museum built to hold the finds. The fort opens daily from eight until five and draws something like seventy thousand visitors a year, which makes a sandbar redoubt from 1631 one of the busiest attractions in Pernambuco.

## Flight Context

Fort Orange sits at 7.811 degrees south, 34.839 degrees west, at the southeastern tip of Itamaracá Island on the north coast of Pernambuco, essentially at sea level. From the air it is a clean geometric shape against sand and water: a square stone work with four pointed corner bastions, right on the shoreline, with the Santa Cruz channel separating Itamaracá from the mainland behind it and open Atlantic ahead. The channel is the key navigation feature and explains the whole siting. Recommended viewing altitude 1,000 to 2,500 feet to resolve the bastion geometry and the reef and sandbank pattern offshore; 5,000 feet takes in the full island and the mangrove channel. Recife/Guararapes-Gilberto Freyre International (SBRF) lies south down the coast and João Pessoa (SBJP) north; the fort is roughly 40 to 50 kilometres from Recife depending on the route, reached by BR-101 North and the PE-01 and PE-35 state roads. Coastal haze is common in the morning; September to December gives the clearest air, June the heaviest rain.
