# Toritama

Roughly one in seven pairs of jeans made in Brazil comes from a town of 41,000 people that fits inside thirty square kilometres of dry agreste.

**Location:** -8.0067, -36.0567

**Categories:** towns, industry, textiles, economy, brazil, working-life

## Introduction

About fifteen percent of the jeans produced in Brazil come from a single town in the Pernambuco agreste — a municipality so small it barely covers thirty square kilometres and holds around forty thousand residents. Toritama has no port, no irrigation water and a river that runs only part of the year. What it has is sewing. This place rebuilt its entire economy twice in half a century, and the second rebuild worked so completely that buyers now drive here from across the country to load their cars with denim and drive home again.

## Stone Region

The name is Tupi: tori, stone, and tama, region, in reference to rock formations some thirty metres high on the right bank of the Capibaribe, standing out of the landscape like a tower. In the middle of the nineteenth century this was a cattle farm on the river's left bank and not much besides. Toritama became a municipality on 29 December 1953, carved out of the districts of Vertentes and Taquaritinga do Norte; Joaquim Aurélio Correia de Araújo and Antônio Manoel da Silva administered it provisionally, and José Jota de Araújo became the first elected mayor three and a half years later. The Capibaribe here is seasonal. It never carried enough water to irrigate anything, and that one geographic fact set the terms for everything that came after.

## From Shoes to Seams

If you cannot farm, you make things. Through the 1970s Toritama became a serious centre of footwear manufacture, turning out leather shoes for the Brazilian market, and for a while that was the answer. It did not last. Larger producers elsewhere in the country undercut the town, the shoe factories went bankrupt in quick succession, and a place that had built its living on leather lost it inside a few years. What happened next is the part people remember. The workforce did not leave; it changed product. Denim took hold, and it took hold fast enough that within a generation Toritama's name meant jeans to buyers a thousand kilometres away — attracted, as the record dryly puts it, by quality and low price.

## Parque das Feiras

The trade concentrates in one place. Parque das Feiras, on the BR-104, opened in September 2001 with 875 stalls of three square metres each spread across nine hectares, plus restaurants and parking for two thousand vehicles. The stalls filled. The market was enlarged by another 1,518, taking the total past two thousand, and the parking lot turned out to be the important number. Outside the market square, seven of every ten vehicles on Toritama's streets carry licence plates from somewhere else in Brazil. That statistic explains the town's function better than any production figure does: it is a wholesale destination wearing the clothes of a small country town, and for part of every week the population it serves is not its own.

## The Pace

A town of forty thousand producing a seventh of a nation's jeans is doing something arithmetic alone cannot flatter. Toritama is genuinely proud of what it made out of a dry riverbed and a collapsed shoe trade, and that pride is earned — nobody handed this industry down from a capital city. But garment production is piecework, and piecework sets its own terms: income arrives unevenly, hours follow orders rather than clocks, and the risk sits with whoever owns the machine. Marcelo Gomes's documentary Estou me Guardando Para Quando o Carnaval Chegar, which premiered in the Panorama section at the Berlin Film Festival in 2019 and won Brazil's Grande Otelo for best documentary feature the following year, spent its running time among workers in one of the country's largest denim hubs. It neither celebrates them as entrepreneurs nor pities them as victims, which is roughly the right register for the whole subject.

## Small on the Map

Toritama sits at 349 metres, 153 kilometres west of Recife, and by area it is either the smallest municipality in Pernambuco or very close to it — published figures range from about 26 to about 31 square kilometres, small enough that crossing the boundary is a matter of a few minutes' driving. The 2022 census counted 41,137 residents. By the 2006 accounting, services made up 82 percent of the local economy against 17 percent for industry and well under one percent for agriculture, which is what happens when the factory floor is scattered and the shopfront is enormous. The town has not spread outward. It has intensified, and that is a harder kind of growth to see from the road.

## Flight Context

Toritama lies at 8.0067°S, 36.0567°W at 349 metres, about 32 kilometres north of Caruaru and 153 kilometres west of Recife. From the air the seasonal bed of the Capibaribe and the thirty-metre granite towers on its right bank are the natural landmarks; the man-made one is Parque das Feiras, a nine-hectare block of market stalls beside the BR-104 with a two-thousand-space parking lot that fills and empties on a weekly rhythm. The built-up area is compact and dense against otherwise open agreste, which makes the town easy to pick out at 2,500–4,000 ft AGL. Nearest airports are Caruaru (SNRU / CAU, elevation 576 m, 1,800 m asphalt runway 13/31) roughly 32 km south, and Recife Guararapes International (SBRF) on the coast.
