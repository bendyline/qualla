# Gravatá

Eight degrees south of the equator, a Pernambuco hill town serves fondue — and Recife drives up the escarpment for the cold.

**Location:** -8.2008, -35.5650

**Categories:** towns, resorts, highlands, outdoors, brazil, climate

## Introduction

Gravatá's restaurants are famous for fondue. That is an odd thing to be famous for at eight degrees south of the equator, an hour and a half from the beaches of Recife, in a state otherwise associated with sun and salt. The explanation is 447 metres of altitude. Up here the annual average temperature sits around 22°C and the coldest nights fall to roughly 15 — nothing a European would call cold, and everything a coastal Pernambucano would. Gravatá sells temperature. Its entire resort economy rests on the plain fact of being cooler than home.

## The Highway Changed Everything

The town was emancipated on 15 March 1893, in the first wave of municipal reorganisation after Brazil became a republic, and for the next half-century it was an unremarkable agreste settlement — a parish church from 1810, a city hall from 1909, a jail from 1911, and chalets along Avenida Joaquim Didier for whoever had money. Then the BR-232 opened in 1950. Recife was suddenly eighty-four kilometres away rather than a serious journey, and the calculation shifted for everyone in the capital who wanted a weekend somewhere else. Second homes went up on the slopes. Hotels and pousadas followed the second homes. Around 86,500 people now live in the municipality's 513 square kilometres, and a good share of the houses on its edges belong to people who are not among them.

## Scrub That Pierces

The name is Tupi. Karawatã means, roughly, the scrub that pierces — a reference to the bromeliads that once grew thick across these hills, low and spiny-leaved, relatives of the caroá whose fibre the interior spun and of the pineapple the region still grows. It is a plant name for a plant place. Gravatá farms what the altitude permits: cut flowers, strawberries, pineapples, with the hill village of Russinhas given over almost entirely to pineapple. Rainfall averages only about 563 millimetres a year, which is not a great deal. What the town sells was never rain. It was elevation, and the fifteen degrees of difference that elevation buys on a July night.

## Stairs of Happiness

The Alto do Cruzeiro lookout is reached by a staircase locals call the Escadaria da Felicidade, and at the top stands a Christ the Redeemer — smaller than Rio's, watching over a far smaller city, and no less the town's fixed point for it. Below, the old railway station has become the Estação do Artesão, a crafts centre; the 1911 jail is now the Memorial da Cidade. Gravatá has a habit of handing its institutional buildings over to culture, which is a modest and faintly comic form of civic confidence. The Matriz de Sant'Anna dates from 1810. Sixteen kilometres out, in the district of Uruçu-Mirim, the Caranguejo sugar plantation survives from the era when the coast's economy still reached this far inland.

## Rock and Tunnel

The serras around town — das Ruças, das Cãibras, da Estrela, do Maroto — do the actual work of tourism here. Fourteen railway tunnels run through the Serra das Ruças, relics of the British-built line that once climbed the escarpment, and the nineteenth-century Cascavel rail bridge still stands and now serves rappellers. Climbers work Pedra Branca, a seventy-five-metre face, and Pedra do Tao, where orchids grow in the cracks; the Pernambuco Orchid Centre at Recanto Baraúna collects what the hills produce. There are waterfalls at Cachoeira da Palmeira and natural pools at the Balneário de Dona Nadir. Trails run out from half a dozen farms. For a town whose reputation rests on cool evenings and melted cheese, Gravatá turns out to be surprisingly vertical.

## May and June

Two festivals anchor the year. In May, pilgrims walk from the Matriz de Sant'Anna out to the Riacho do Mel chapel, where local tradition holds that Frei Damião celebrated his first mass in Brazil — the Italian Capuchin friar who became one of the most venerated figures in the northeast. Late June brings São João, and Gravatá does what every agreste town does in June: forró until late, bonfires, corn in every conceivable form, and a population swollen with visitors. Caruaru, fifty-four kilometres further west along the same highway, throws the far bigger party. Gravatá's compensating advantage is that when the music finally stops, the night up here is cold enough to want a blanket.

## Flight Context

Gravatá sits at 8.2008°S, 35.565°W at 447 metres, roughly 84 kilometres west of Recife on the BR-232 and about 46 kilometres east of Caruaru. It marks the point where the highway has finished climbing the escarpment out of the Zona da Mata, so the terrain transition below is the clearest cue — coastal green giving way to the paler agreste. Visual landmarks include the Christ statue on the Alto do Cruzeiro above the town and the ridgelines of Serra das Ruças, Serra da Estrela and Serra do Maroto, with the seventy-five-metre face of Pedra Branca standing out in raking light. Recife Guararapes International (SBRF) is the nearest major field; Caruaru (SNRU / CAU, elevation 576 m) lies west. Expect ridge turbulence on windy days and viewing best at 3,000–5,000 ft AGL.
