# Siege of Fort St. Jean

A grueling 45-day siege in the fall of 1775 that opened the gates to Montreal but cost the Continental Army precious weeks -- time that would prove fatal to the American invasion of Canada.

**Location:** 45.2989, -73.2517

**Categories:** military-history, american-revolution, historic-site, siege, colonial-history

## Introduction

Richard Montgomery described his soldiers as "half-drowned rats crawling through a swamp." For seven weeks in the fall of 1775, that was precisely what they were -- Continental Army troops knee-deep in waterlogged trenches, laying siege to a British fort on the Richelieu River while disease, desertion, and dwindling supplies threatened to end the American invasion of Canada before it reached Montreal. The siege of Fort St. Jean lasted from September 17 to November 3, 1775, and while the Americans ultimately took the fort, the time it consumed may have cost them the entire campaign.

## The Cork in the Bottle

Fort Saint-Jean guarded the entry to the province of Quebec on the Richelieu River, at the northern end of Lake Champlain. Any army marching north from the American colonies had to pass this way, and the British knew it. After Benedict Arnold and Ethan Allen captured Fort Ticonderoga and raided Fort St. Jean in May 1775, Governor Guy Carleton concentrated his defenses here. By September, Major Charles Preston commanded roughly 750 defenders: regulars from the 7th and 26th Regiments of Foot, Royal Artillery, ninety locally raised militia, and twenty of Colonel Allan Maclean's Royal Highland Emigrants. The fort itself consisted of two earthen redoubts on the river's west bank, surrounded by ditches lined with chevaux de frise, with cleared fields of fire on all sides. An armed schooner, the Royal Savage, patrolled the river. Preston's orders were simple: hold the fort and buy Carleton time to organize the defense of Montreal.

## False Starts in the Swamp

The Continental Army's first attempts to approach Fort St. Jean were disasters of inexperience. On September 6, General Philip Schuyler led 1,500 men in bateaux toward the fort, only to have them ambushed by a hundred Indigenous fighters led by Tice and Lorimier. The untested militia troops panicked and retreated to their boats. The Quebec Gazette gleefully reported that sixty Indigenous warriors had driven off 1,500 Americans. On September 10, Montgomery led a thousand men back for another try. In the darkness and swampy terrain, his own troops became separated, encountered each other, and panicked -- each group mistaking the other for the enemy. They retreated again. When word came that the Royal Savage was approaching, the entire force fled upriver to Ile-aux-Noix in such disorder that the command staff was nearly left behind. Only after reinforcements brought his strength to over 2,000 men -- including Seth Warner's Green Mountain Boys and New Hampshire troops under Timothy Bedel -- did Montgomery feel confident enough to attempt a proper siege.

## Rats in the Trenches

The formal siege began on September 17, when Montgomery's army landed south of the fort and sent John Brown with a detachment to cut the road to Montreal. Conditions were miserable from the start. The ground around the fort was marshy, and trenches filled with water as fast as soldiers could dig them. Food and ammunition ran dangerously low. The fort's defenders, well supplied with munitions, used their advantage in firepower to harass the siege works relentlessly. Major Preston noted in his journal that deserters revealed the positions of American batteries, allowing the defenders to "distress them as much as we can with shells." The turning point came when large guns finally arrived from Ticonderoga. By October 13, an American battery was complete, and the next day it opened fire. Within twenty-four hours, the Royal Savage lay in ruins. Meanwhile, Ethan Allen, impatient as ever, had tried to capture Montreal on his own with a small force -- and was captured at the Battle of Longue-Pointe on September 25, an embarrassment that accomplished nothing.

## Chambly Falls, and Hope With It

The break the Americans needed came not at St. Jean but at nearby Fort Chambly. James Livingston, a local grain merchant who had raised nearly 300 Canadian militia sympathetic to the American cause, proposed taking the lightly defended post. On October 18, after just two days of bombardment, Major Joseph Stopford surrendered Fort Chambly and its garrison of 82 men. Crucially, Stopford failed to destroy his supplies before capitulating. The Americans captured six tons of gunpowder, 6,500 musket cartridges, 125 muskets, 80 barrels of flour, and 272 barrels of food -- a windfall that revitalized the siege. At St. Jean, the news of Chambly's fall was devastating. Some militia defenders wanted to surrender immediately, but Preston refused. He was still counting on General Carleton to break the siege from Montreal. That hope died on October 30, when Carleton's relief force of roughly 1,000 men attempted to cross the St. Lawrence at Longueuil and was driven back by Seth Warner's artillery -- the very guns captured at Chambly.

## The General Who Never Learned

On November 3, 1775, Preston's troops marched out of Fort St. Jean and surrendered their weapons, the regulars in full dress uniform. He gave up 536 officers and soldiers, 79 Canadian volunteers, and eight English volunteers. The way to Montreal was open, and American troops entered the city without opposition on November 13. Richard Montgomery was promoted to Major General on December 9 for his capture of St. Jean and Montreal, but the news never reached him. On December 31, 1775, Montgomery was killed leading the assault on Quebec City -- the battle that the seven weeks spent at Fort St. Jean had made almost impossible to win. The delay had given Carleton time to reach Quebec and prepare its defenses, and the American invasion of Canada collapsed that winter. The fort's stubborn defense had served its purpose after all.

## Flight Context

Located at 45.30N, 73.25W in Saint-Jean-sur-Richelieu, Quebec, on the west bank of the Richelieu River approximately 40 km south of Montreal. The fort site is now occupied by the Royal Military College Saint-Jean campus. The Richelieu River runs north-south from Lake Champlain to the St. Lawrence -- the historic invasion corridor visible from altitude. Nearby airports include Montreal-Saint-Hubert (CYHU) approximately 15 km northwest. Fort Chambly, a related historic site, is visible roughly 20 km downstream.
