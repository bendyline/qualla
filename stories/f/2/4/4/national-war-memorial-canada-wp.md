# National War Memorial (Canada)

A granite arch in the heart of Ottawa where 22 bronze figures march forever toward duty, honoring every Canadian who has served in every conflict past and future.

**Location:** 45.4240, -75.6955

**Categories:** war-memorial, national-monument, military-history, sculpture, historical-site

## Introduction

The sculptor died before his masterpiece was finished. Vernon March, a young artist from Farnborough, England, won the 1926 competition to design Canada's National War Memorial with a vision he called The Response -- 22 bronze figures surging through a granite arch, answering the call of duty. He worked on the project for four years before his death in 1930, leaving his six brothers and sister to complete what would become the most significant war memorial in Canadian history. Today, the monument stands at the center of Confederation Square in Ottawa, a permanent procession frozen in bronze and stone that has expanded its meaning with each generation's sacrifice.

## A Family's Unfinished Work

When the Canadian government launched a design competition in 1925, the brief was clear: the monument should evoke the spirit of heroism, self-sacrifice, and all that is noble in those who served in the Great War. One hundred and twenty-seven entries arrived from seven countries. From among seven finalists, Vernon March's design won on January 18, 1926, beating 66 Canadian proposals and dozens more from France, Belgium, Italy, and the United States. His concept was distinctive -- not a solitary soldier or an abstract obelisk, but a crowd of figures in motion, pressing through an arch from war toward peace. After March's death, his siblings adjusted and expanded the design, adding three figures to the original 19 to fill gaps created when the arch was widened. The bronze sculptures were displayed in Hyde Park, London, where onlookers noticed the artillery carriage could not actually fit through the archway, prompting the family to enlarge both the arch and its plinth.

## Twenty-Two Faces of Service

The figures are deliberately one-third larger than life, positioned high on a pedestal to avoid foreshortening when viewed from the street. A Lewis gunner leads on the left, a kilted infantryman with a Vickers machine gun on the right. Behind them march a pilot, an air mechanic, a sailor, two mounted figures emerging side by side through the arch, infantry riflemen, two nurses, a stretcher bearer, an engineer, and a forestry worker, among others. At the rear, soldiers haul a QF 18-pounder gun. Vernon March rendered every figure without markers of region, ethnicity, or language, creating a deliberate unity. Their postures are strained and animated, their expressions conveying pride, longing, defiance, and firm resolve. Above them all, two allegorical figures crown the arch: one bearing a torch, evoking the Roman goddess Demeter, and one winged with a laurel, depicting Nike. Their closeness represents the inseparability of peace and freedom.

## A Monument That Keeps Growing

Originally dedicated on May 21, 1939, by King George VI before an estimated 100,000 people, the memorial was built to honor Canadians killed in the First World War. But the timing was bitterly prophetic -- within months, Canada was at war again. For decades, the Royal Canadian Legion debated whether the monument's faithful World War I uniforms made it unsuitable for broader commemoration. The public, however, had already decided. Through its prominent location in downtown Ottawa and its annual use for Remembrance Day services, the memorial gradually became the symbol of all Canadian war dead. The Crown formally agreed, rededicating the monument in 1982 to include the Second World War and Korean War. In 2014, it was expanded again to encompass the Second Boer War and the War in Afghanistan, along with all Canadians who served in the past, serve today, and will serve in the future.

## The Unknown Soldier Comes Home

In 2000, the remains of an unidentified Canadian soldier, exhumed from a cemetery near Vimy Ridge in France, were interred in a new Tomb of the Unknown Soldier directly in front of the memorial. Governor General Adrienne Clarkson dedicated the tomb on May 28 of that year. The addition transformed the memorial from a monument to an active place of mourning. Visitors leave mementos, photographs, and coins at the base; after each Remembrance Day ceremony, the Canadian War Museum collects these items for review and possible preservation, while any money is donated to the Perley and Rideau Veterans' Health Centre. Visiting foreign dignitaries regularly lay wreaths here, from President John F. Kennedy in 1961 to Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev in 1990 to French President Francois Hollande in 2014.

## Standing Guard

On the morning of October 22, 2014, Corporal Nathan Cirillo was standing ceremonial guard at the memorial when he was fatally shot by a gunman who then stormed the Centre Block of Parliament. The tragedy shocked the nation but deepened the memorial's significance. Sentries had been posted at the site since 2006, initially prompted by an incident of public disrespect during Canada Day celebrations. They stand watch from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. between April 9 and November 10 each year. Every November 11, between 25,000 and 45,000 people gather around the memorial for the nationally televised Remembrance Day ceremony, attended by the governor general, the prime minister, veterans, and the Silver Cross Mother. Before the ceremony begins, workers repair and level stones, wax the bronze figures, and apply protective coating to the Tomb's lettering -- careful maintenance of a monument that, like the 503 tonnes of rose-grey Canadian granite it is made from, was built to endure.

## Flight Context

Located at 45.424N, 75.695W in Confederation Square, downtown Ottawa. The memorial sits at ground level between Parliament Hill (northwest) and the Rideau Canal (northeast). Best viewed from low altitude where the granite arch and bronze figures are visible. Nearby airports include Ottawa/Macdonald-Cartier International (CYOW, 10 km south) and Gatineau-Ottawa Executive (CYND, 8 km north).
