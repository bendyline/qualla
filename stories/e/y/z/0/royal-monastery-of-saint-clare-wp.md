# Royal Monastery of Saint Clare

For more than six centuries, cloistered nuns have lived and prayed inside the walls of a Muslim pleasure palace in Murcia, where a rediscovered painting of a woman playing the flute survives among the finest Islamic art in the city.

**Location:** 37.9881, -1.1303

**Categories:** Monasteries and convents, Islamic art, Al-Andalus history, Museums, Medieval Spain, Region of Murcia

## Introduction

Somewhere in the plaster of this building, hidden for centuries, a woman kept playing her flute. She belonged to a painted fragment from a Muslim pleasure palace, an image of music and leisure that a later, stricter dynasty found offensive and covered over. The paint waited in the dark while the palace became a ruin, then a convent, then a monastery of silent nuns. When archaeologists finally uncovered her, she was still lifting the instrument to her lips. In Murcia they call the fragment The Flautist, and it is one of the most delicate things to survive from al-Andalus.

## The Woman with the Flute

The story begins with pleasure, not prayer. The earliest structure here was the Dar As-Sugra, a recreational palace built outside Murcia's walls in the Arrixaca suburb, fed by water from an irrigation canal that ran along its southern edge. Ibn Mardanis, the ruler known as the Wolf King, expanded it during his reign from 1147 to 1172, filling it with the ornament of a confident court. When the Almohads conquered Murcia in 1172, their religious orthodoxy had no patience for such worldly images. The palace fell into disuse and its paintings were deliberately concealed. That act of censorship is why The Flautist, a muqarnas fragment showing a woman playing a wind instrument, survived at all, sealed away rather than scraped clean.

## A Smaller Paradise

Between 1228 and 1238, under the rule of Ibn Hud, the site was reborn as the Al-Qasr al-Sagir, a smaller recreational palace whose structure is remarkably preserved within the monastery today, among its most valuable archaeological treasures. The ruling family also held the grander Alcazar Mayor near the Segura River, but that changed after 1243. Under the Treaty of Alcaraz, Murcia became a protectorate of Castile, and a Christian garrison took the main fortress. The Al-Qasr al-Sagir became the last true home of the Muslim Murcian royal family, a modest paradise of courtyards and stucco where a fading dynasty kept house while the world shifted around it.

## A King's Gift

When Murcia was permanently absorbed into Castile after the Mudejar revolt, the palace passed to the crown. Sections had already gone to the Franciscans when, in 1365, Peter I of Castile, remembered by turns as the Cruel and the Just, donated his royal chambers to Abbess Berenguela de Espin and the Order of the Poor Clares. They have held it ever since. In 1367, Bishop Nicolas de Aguilar granted the nuns permission to turn the semi-ruined palace into a convent, and the old Muslim courtyard became the beating center of monastic life. Patronage from the Catholic Monarchs late in the 15th century paid for a Gothic cloister and church; a Baroque church by Melchor de Luzon followed in the 1600s, later dressed in Rococo.

## Two Faiths, One Courtyard

The building now holds the Museum of Santa Clara, and its two halves tell the whole improbable story. One section displays the Islamic art and archaeology unearthed here: stucco, muqarnas, and flowing calligraphic epigraphy, the physical vocabulary of a lost court. The other, called Times of Silence, gathers the monastery's religious art, including sculptures by the great Murcian carver Francisco Salzillo and paintings by Senen Vila. The name fits a place built for contemplation, where the two collections face each other across the centuries. Part of the complex now serves as a museum, while the western wing houses the Las Claras cultural center; the rest remains a working convent. The nuns remain. Behind their walls, an Almohad-era palace and a Catholic convent share the same arches, the same courtyard, the same patch of ground, proof that Murcia never really erased its past so much as prayed on top of it.

## Flight Context

The monastery stands in central Murcia at about 37.988 N, 1.130 W, just north of the Segura River and a short walk from the cathedral, whose Baroque tower is the region's tallest landmark and the easiest fix from the air. Best appreciated from 2,000 to 4,000 feet above ground, where the tight medieval street pattern of the old town reads clearly. Nearest airport is Region de Murcia International (LEMI), roughly 25 km southwest; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) sits about 65 km northeast. Murcia's inland setting brings dependable sunshine and clear air for most of the year.
