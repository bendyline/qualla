# Al-Qasr al-Sagir

A Muslim pleasure palace in Murcia that housed a rebel king, then Christian monarchs, and for over six centuries has been home to cloistered nuns.

**Location:** 37.9881, -1.1304

**Categories:** Islamic architecture, Al-Andalus, Palaces, Monasteries and convents, Medieval Spain, Region of Murcia

## Introduction

A woman with a wind instrument at her lips has survived eight centuries inside a fragment of painted plaster. Called The Flautist, she once decorated a muqarnas ceiling in a Murcian pleasure palace, and she is a small miracle, because the rulers who came after her makers found such images too worldly and had them hidden away. Her survival is the whole story of Al-Qasr al-Sagir in miniature: a Muslim palace that outlived its dynasty, changed faiths, and never once stopped being lived in. Today it stands behind convent walls, where Poor Clare nuns have kept house for more than 650 years.

## The Wolf King's Murcia

The oldest palace on the site was called Dar As-Sugra, built before 1145 as a recreational retreat outside the walls of the Arab medina, fed by the waters of the Aljufia irrigation canal that still runs along the convent's southern side. This late Almoravid building was renovated, or rebuilt, by Ibn Mardanis, the ruler the Christians nicknamed the Wolf King, who governed an independent Murcia between 1147 and 1172. He enlarged it and made it more luxurious. When the Almohads conquered the city in 1172, the palace fell into disuse, and their stricter religious sensibility is why paintings like The Flautist were concealed rather than displayed. Ibn Mardanis had held out against the Almohads longer than most, and his Murcia was for a time a rare island of independence in a peninsula being redrawn by conquest.

## A Palace Between Two Crowns

In the early thirteenth century, under the ruler Ibn Hud, the older palace was rebuilt again as Al-Qasr al-Sagir, the Lesser Alcazar, a smaller retreat than its predecessor but still grand. It was the junior partner to the Alcazar Mayor, the main royal residence down by the Segura River. That hierarchy inverted after conquest. Following Ibn Hud's assassination in 1238 and the Treaty of Alcaraz in 1243, Murcia became a protectorate of Castile with its Muslim population still governing itself. A Christian garrison took over the Alcazar Mayor, and so the Lesser Alcazar became the primary seat of the Murcian royal family. When the Mudejar revolt of 1264 was suppressed and the city was absorbed into Castile in 1266, the palace passed to Christian hands and hosted Alfonso X of Castile, his queen Violant of Aragon, and her father James I.

## From Throne Room to Cloister

The estate around the palace, with its baths, gardens, and orchards, was carved up among royal beneficiaries. Then in 1365, Peter I of Castile gave his royal chambers to Abbess Berenguela de Espin and the Poor Clares, an order of cloistered nuns, who have held the site ever since. Two years later, with a bishop's permission, the sisters converted the semi-ruined palace into a convent. The old Muslim courtyard, once the setting for a king's leisure, became the center of monastic life, ringed with arcades as new buildings were added around it. The transformation is complete and yet incomplete: prayer where there was once music, but the same walls, the same courtyard, the same water in the canal. Even the estate's other structures, its baths, orchards, and lesser buildings, were parceled out and long remembered in property names like the Real de la Reina, the queen's estate.

## The Most Islamic Corner of the City

Because the convent grew around the palace rather than sweeping it away, it preserves the structure of Al-Qasr al-Sagir better than anywhere else in Murcia. Its stuccoed arches and the surviving decoration count among the most important works of Islamic art in the city, a rare thread of al-Andalus running unbroken through a Christian religious house. The Flautist, playing on in her scrap of plaster, is a reminder that this ground has changed hands and changed gods, but has been continuously inhabited for the better part of nine hundred years.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 37.99 N, 1.13 W in central Murcia, at the Royal Monastery of Santa Clara near the Gran Via, a short distance north of the cathedral and the River Segura. The site is enclosed and best seen from the street, but the dense historic center along the Segura, marked by the cathedral tower, identifies the district from the air. Fly at 1,500 to 3,000 feet in clear conditions. Nearest airports: Region de Murcia at Corvera (LEMI), Murcia-San Javier (LELC), and Alicante-Elche (LEAL).
