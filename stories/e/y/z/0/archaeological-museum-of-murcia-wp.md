# Archaeological Museum of Murcia

A museum born by royal decree in 1864 that gathers the Iberian, Roman, and Islamic pasts of the Murcia region under one roof.

**Location:** 37.9897, -1.1306

**Categories:** Museums, Archaeology, Iberian history, Roman history, Cultural heritage, Region of Murcia

## Introduction

A stone lion carved by Iberian hands more than two thousand years ago keeps a silent post in the heart of Murcia. Known as the Lion of Coy, after the place in the region where it was found, it dates to the second half of the first millennium BC, a survivor from the culture that occupied this land before Rome. It is the kind of object the Archaeological Museum of Murcia exists to hold: a fragment of a people, pulled from the local earth and kept where the public can meet its gaze.

## The Lion of Coy

The Iberians who lived across southeastern Spain left behind a striking tradition of stone sculpture, and their lions are among its most memorable images, guardians rendered in weathered limestone. The Lion of Coy belongs to that world, made by a society Rome would later absorb but never entirely erase. To stand before it is to look back past the Romans, past the Phoenician traders who worked this coast, to the people who farmed and fought over the Segura valley in the centuries before written history reached them here. Set near the museum's heart, the lion serves as a kind of threshold guardian, the first culture in a sequence the galleries trace forward through Rome and into the Islamic centuries.

## A Museum Born by Royal Order

The institution has roots in a specific moment: a royal order of 6 July 1864 that created the Museum of Antiquities of Murcia. It began not as an independent body but as a section attached to a Museum of Painting and Sculpture that had been established earlier the same year. From the start, then, the region's ancient artifacts were treated as a collection worth gathering and displaying, part of a nineteenth-century impulse across Spain to inventory and protect the nation's heritage rather than let it scatter into private hands or foreign markets. The order placed Murcia within a wider movement to found provincial museums, institutions meant to keep a region's antiquities within reach of the people who lived among their ruins.

## Three Homes in a Century

The collection has not sat still. In 1910 it moved into the Cerdan building, and in the 1950s it settled into its current home, a building known as the Casa de la Cultura, the House of Culture. Ownership and governance shifted as well. In 1984, the Spanish State handed management of the museum to the regional administration of Murcia while keeping ownership for itself, part of the broad devolution of cultural responsibilities to Spain's autonomous communities. Each move reflected an institution outgrowing its walls, its holdings swelling as excavation across the region turned up more of the past than any single room could show. The museum's own history, in other words, mirrors the region's modern story of taking charge of its own heritage.

## What Portman Left Behind

The Roman centuries are here too, and some of the finest pieces come from the coast at Portman, in the neighboring municipality of La Union. From a Roman villa there, called the Huerta del Paturro, came an Ionic capital, its carved scrolls a signature of classical architecture, and the head of a satyr, one of the wine god's wild companions rendered in stone. Together the lion of the Iberians and the satyr of the Romans sketch the arc the museum keeps: a landscape settled, conquered, and resettled, each culture leaving something in the ground for a later age to find and set on a pedestal. A villa was a working country estate as much as a home, and the survival of carved ornament from it hints at the comfort and cultivation of provincial Roman life on this coast, far from the capital yet fully part of the empire.
