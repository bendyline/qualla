# San Juan de Dios museum

Under an oval Rococo church near Murcia Cathedral, the heart and entrails of Alfonso X the Wise once rested for two and a half centuries, above the underground mihrab of the mosque where his church was born.

**Location:** 37.9832, -1.1281

**Categories:** Churches and cathedrals, Museums, Rococo architecture, Al-Andalus history, Medieval Spain, Region of Murcia

## Introduction

Alfonso X of Castile won many cities, but he asked to leave his heart in only a few. In his will, the king they called the Wise stated plainly that he wished to be buried in Murcia, the city he had taken as a young prince. If that proved impossible, he wrote, at least his entrails should be removed and sent there. In the end both his heart and his viscera were carried to this spot and interred, a monarch dividing his own body to keep a promise to a place. They rested beneath what is now the San Juan de Dios museum for two and a half centuries, until Emperor Charles V ordered them moved to Murcia Cathedral in 1525.

## A King's Heart

Alfonso's bond with Murcia was old and deliberate. A royal charter from 1277 records that he had already ordered a church built inside the alcazar, the former Muslim fortress, and as early as 1272 priests from the city's parishes were said to celebrate Mass here every Saturday. The building sits over sacred ground twice. Tradition holds that Murcia's first Christian church rose directly atop the palace mosque, whose remains still lie underground. When Alfonso chose this place for his heart, he was not picking a random chapel but the very hinge where Islamic Murcia had become Castilian, the seat of the power he had claimed. Alfonso was no ordinary conqueror. Remembered as the Wise, he presided over an outpouring of law, science, and poetry, gathering scholars of three faiths to translate and compile. That such a king wanted a piece of himself left in Murcia says how much the city meant to him, one prize among many that he refused to let go of even in death.

## The Oval Church

The church you see today is far younger than the king who haunts it. Built between 1764 and 1781 by Martin Solera, it is a masterpiece of Rococo, and unusual to its core. Its plan is an ellipse rather than a cross, designed as a grand shrine for the Eucharist, with eight pillars carrying the roof and six chapels arranged in a radiating ring. The facade lifts two square towers dressed in Baroque and Rococo detail. Solera looked to Rome for inspiration, and the debt is visible: the design echoes Bernini's oval church of San Andrea al Quirinale, a flourish of Italian drama transplanted to a Murcian square.

## Templars and the Sick

Between the medieval fortress and the Rococo church, the site spent centuries in the business of mercy. The Knights Templar took over the church and installed a hospital on part of the grounds. When their order was dissolved in 1314, the Order of Calatrava assumed control of the alcazar, the church, and the hospital, and the work of healing carried on. This remained Murcia's most important hospital until the middle of the twentieth century, an astonishing span of continuity. The fortress itself faded from military use, and early in the 15th century Henry III of Castile ordered a new fortification built nearer the Segura River bridge, letting the old stronghold settle into its gentler second life.

## The Mosque Underground

Descend into the museum's lower level and the deepest layer emerges. Here the excavated remains of the Muslim alcazar are on display, including a mihrab, the prayer niche that once pointed the faithful toward Mecca, and tombs dating to the 12th century. Upstairs, the desacralized church now serves as part of the Museum of Fine Arts of Murcia, its radiating chapels hung with religious sculpture from the 15th to the 19th centuries, including work by Francisco Salzillo, and the collection of Juan Gonzalez Moreno, donated by the artist in 1996. Mosque, fortress, hospital, and gallery occupy a single vertical stack of Murcian history, a king's heart once resting somewhere in the middle of it all before its journey to the cathedral down the street.

## Flight Context

The museum stands in Murcia's historic center at about 37.983 N, 1.128 W, beside a traditional plaza just steps from Murcia Cathedral, whose towering Baroque bell tower is the dominant landmark from the air. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 4,000 feet above ground frames the compact old town well. Nearest airport is Region de Murcia International (LEMI), roughly 25 km southwest; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies about 65 km northeast. The interior plain offers long clear-weather visibility for much of the year.
