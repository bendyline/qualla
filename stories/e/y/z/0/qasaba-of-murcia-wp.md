# Qasaba of Murcia

Beneath the streets of central Murcia lie the walls of a vanished Islamic citadel that ruled the city, then was conquered, converted, and rebuilt into a church, a Templar hospital, and finally a museum.

**Location:** 37.9832, -1.1280

**Categories:** Al-Andalus history, Castles and citadels, Archaeology, Medieval Spain, Region of Murcia

## Introduction

For generations, historians were certain a fortress had once stood at the heart of Islamic Murcia, though they had only words to go on. The old chronicles described a great walled citadel near the site of today's cathedral, a seat of power for the rulers of al-Andalus. No one could point to the stone. Then the excavators went down beneath the modern streets and found it: foundations, walls, the buried bones of a lost stronghold. The Qasaba of Murcia had been there all along, waiting under the city it once commanded.

## A Citadel Beneath the City

A qasaba is a citadel, the fortified nerve center from which a Muslim ruler governed. Murcia's was no mere garrison. Within its walls stood the qasr, the official palace and residence of the monarch or governor, alongside a small oratory for prayer, baths where the court cleansed itself, and a rawda, a royal pantheon where the dead were laid to rest. Around these clustered the other buildings that a functioning court required. Traditional historiography long suspected such a complex existed here, and archaeology eventually confirmed it. This was the military and political engine of the Emirate of Murcia, a compact city-within-a-city where the business of ruling was carried out behind thick walls. To live inside the qasaba was to live at the summit of Murcian society, sealed off from the town that fed and served it. The baths steamed, the oratory called the court to prayer, and the rawda held the honored dead in ground that the living rulers walked above each day. Power here was not an abstraction. It had rooms, water, and walls, and it announced itself in stone to everyone who looked up at the citadel.

## The Day the Prince Walked In

In 1243, the Emirate of Murcia signed the Treaty of Alcaraz and became a protectorate of the Crown of Castile. The bargain preserved the city while surrendering its independence. On the first of May that year, Prince Alfonso, the future Alfonso X, entered Murcia and took possession of the Alcazar Mayor, the great fortress. The Muslim royal family, still nominally kings of Murcia, gathered what remained of their sovereignty and relocated to the Alcazar Menor in the Arrixaca suburb, along with a growing share of the city's Muslim population. It was a quiet, humiliating shift of address, a dynasty stepping down from its own citadel while keeping the empty courtesy of its title.

## The Right of Conquest

The uneasy protectorate did not hold. When the Mudejar revolt of 1264 to 1266 swept through the region, James I of Aragon intervened to suppress it, and the terms changed for good. Aragon's king granted the complex to Alfonso Garcia de Villamayor, the chief adelantado of Murcia, and the fiction of shared rule collapsed into the blunt fact of the right of conquest. Sometime after 1243, a church had already risen on the site, planting Christian worship inside the walls that had once sheltered a mosque and an emir. The transfer of power was total, and it was written into the very stones of the old citadel.

## What Power Leaves Behind

The ground never lost its habit of authority. The church endured, and later the Knights Templar folded part of the site into a hospital. When the Templars were dissolved, others took up the work, and healing continued where soldiers had once mustered. Today the surviving fragments belong to the San Juan de Dios museum, whose underground galleries display the excavated remains of the qasaba itself. Stand there and the layers press close: an emir's palace, a conqueror's church, a crusading order's infirmary, a modern museum, all stacked on the same patch of Murcian earth. Few places wear their history so literally, one identity poured over the last.

## Flight Context

The Qasaba site lies in Murcia's historic center at roughly 37.983 N, 1.128 W, a stone's throw from the Segura River and the soaring tower of Murcia Cathedral, which is the clearest visual landmark from the air. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 4,000 feet above ground gives the best read of the dense old city grid. Nearest airport is Region de Murcia International (LEMI), about 25 km southwest; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies roughly 65 km to the northeast. The interior plain enjoys frequent clear skies and long visibility.
