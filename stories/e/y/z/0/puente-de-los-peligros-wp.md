# Puente de los Peligros

Murcia's oldest bridge, completed in 1742 after decades of floods, delays, and a king's demand for money, and the first to survive the River Segura's rages.

**Location:** 37.9828, -1.1316

**Categories:** Bridges, Architecture, 18th century, Historic landmarks, Region of Murcia, Spanish history

## Introduction

For centuries the River Segura would rise without warning and take Murcia's bridge away. The one that finally held is named, fittingly, for danger. The Puente de los Peligros, the bridge of the hazards, is the oldest bridge in the city, and it earned its stubborn permanence the hard way, over a quarter-century of floods, funding crises, and false starts. Its name comes from a wooden statue of Nuestra Senora de los Peligros, Our Lady of Hazards, tucked into a niche at the bridge's southern end, watching over a river that had swept its predecessors downstream.

## The Flood of 1701

The story begins with a disaster. On 26 September 1701, an extreme flood destroyed the sixteenth-century bridge that connected Murcia's center with the far bank of the Segura, cutting the city off from the starting point of the royal road to Cartagena. Rebuilding a stone bridge across a river prone to violent, unpredictable rises was no small task, and Murcia would spend the next four decades learning just how hard it was. This was the crossing everyone needed and no bridge had yet managed to keep. The Segura is a modest river for much of the year, but its floods were legendary, capable of rising in hours and tearing away whatever stood in their path. Building across it was less an engineering problem than a wager against the water.

## Stops and Starts

Work was slow to begin and slower to finish. Only in 1717, after a new mayor took office, was the order to start finally given, and an engineer drew up the materials needed. That spring, timber for the cofferdam and scaffolding was floated down from the upper Segura basin, a journey that took roughly six months. The first stone was laid on 10 September 1718, but almost at once the work stopped: King Philip V demanded 14,000 doubloons from the city to help pay for his lavish new Royal Palace at La Granja de San Ildefonso, and the bridge lost its funding. Progress crawled through changes of leadership until the architect Jaime Bort took charge, widening the approaches on both banks, even knocking through part of the old city wall to do it. The lead engineer, Toribio Martinez de la Vega, had already spent seven years alone raising the central pillar and the abutments, the slow, unglamorous core of the work on which everything else depended.

## The Bridge That Held

Stalled once more, construction resumed in 1739 and this time did not stop until the bridge was complete in 1742. On 12 September that year, the statue of Our Lady of Hazards was set in place, giving the crossing its enduring name. Later a neoclassical niche was built to shelter her, and figures of the archangels Michael and Raphael were added on the bridge's cutwaters. The nineteenth century brought two rounds of widening, in 1850 and 1867, adding metal structures for sidewalks and setting the layout you cross today.

## Enduring the Segura

The whole point of the bridge was to outlast the river, and it did. Where earlier crossings had been carried off, the Puente de los Peligros became the first permanent bridge in Murcia's history to withstand the Segura's periodic floods, including the catastrophic flood of Santa Teresa in 1879 that devastated much of the region. It has done its work for nearly three centuries now, an unglamorous stone survivor still carrying traffic across the water it was built to defy, watched over the whole time by the lady in her niche. For a city whose prosperity depended on crossing its river, the bridge was more than infrastructure. It was proof, after two centuries of loss, that Murcia could finally build something the Segura could not take away.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 37.98 N, 1.13 W, spanning the River Segura in central Murcia beside the historic core. The low arched stone bridge is a genuine landmark from the air, best picked out at lower altitudes, with the ornate cathedral bell tower rising just to the north and the green huerta framing the city. Fly at 1,500 to 3,000 feet in clear conditions for a clean look at the arches and the river. Nearest airports: Region de Murcia at Corvera (LEMI), Murcia-San Javier (LELC), and Alicante-Elche (LEAL).
