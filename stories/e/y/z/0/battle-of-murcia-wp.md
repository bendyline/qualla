# Battle of Murcia (1706)

When an Anglo-Dutch army marched on Murcia in 1706, a bishop defended the city by flooding its own fields.

**Location:** 37.9833, -1.1333

**Categories:** Military history, War of the Spanish Succession, 18th century, Battles, Region of Murcia, Spanish history

## Introduction

When the enemy came for Murcia in the late summer of 1706, its defender was not a general but a bishop, and his weapon was water. As 6,000 British and Dutch infantry advanced across the plain, Luis de Belluga ordered the irrigation canals that fed Murcia's famous gardens to be opened, turning the flat, fertile huerta into a bog that mired the attackers before they could reach the walls. It was an act of improvised genius in a war being fought over a throne, and it earned its own name, the Battle of the Huerto de las Bombas.

## A Throne With No Heir

The fighting had its origins in a royal deathbed. Charles II of Spain died in 1700 without a child to succeed him, and in his will he named Philip of Anjou, a Bourbon and grandson of Louis XIV of France, as his heir. The prospect of France and Spain drawing close alarmed the other great powers, which rallied around a rival candidate, Archduke Charles of Austria, forming a Grand Alliance. From that quarrel came the War of the Spanish Succession, a continental struggle that turned Spain itself into a battlefield between those loyal to the Bourbon king and those who backed the Austrian claimant. Philip took the throne and entered Barcelona, swearing to uphold the Catalan constitutions, but the settlement satisfied no one for long, and the alliance against him hardened into open war.

## The War Reaches the South

The conflict rolled into southeastern Spain after the English captured Gibraltar in 1704. The Archduke sailed from Lisbon into the Mediterranean and, stopping at Altea, was proclaimed king, igniting a revolt of the maulets in the Valencian lands under Juan Bautista Basset. The pro-Austrian movement spread down the coast to Cartagena, where power changed hands and the Archduke was declared sovereign. In the Kingdom of Murcia, though, the Bourbon cause held firm, and its resistance was organized by Bishop Belluga, a churchman who found himself directing the defense of a region. For places caught in the middle, the choice of king was also a choice of loyalties, neighbor against neighbor and town against town, with Murcia holding for Philip while Cartagena raised the Archduke's banner.

## The Bishop's Flood

On 4 September 1706, the Anglo-Dutch column moved to occupy Murcia. It was a serious force: six thousand infantry, artillery pieces, and a team of engineers hauling a portable wooden bridge to carry the troops over obstacles. Belluga's answer was the land itself. He had the huerta's irrigation channels flooded, so that the ground the attackers needed to cross dissolved into mud and standing water. The two sides traded artillery fire across the sodden fields, but the assault could not develop. Unable to close on the city, the pro-Austrian forces withdrew, beaten as much by the flooded plain as by the guns. That a cleric should be found opening sluices and trading cannon fire says much about a war that reached into every corner of Spanish life, drawing bishops, farmers, and townspeople alike into a dynastic quarrel begun in the courts of Europe.

## The Bourbon Tide

The reprieve became a turning of the tide. Reinforced by the arrival of the Duke of Berwick, a natural son of the deposed English king James II now serving the Bourbon cause, the Bourbon forces went over to the offensive and pushed south into the Kingdom of Valencia. By the end of 1706 they had reclaimed Orihuela, Elche, and Cartagena, rolling back the pro-Austrian gains along this stretch of coast. Murcia's brief moment of danger had passed, and the huerta whose canals had saved it went back to doing what it did best: growing food rather than drowning armies. The bishop's improvised defense had bought the time that turned the whole southern campaign.
