# Monastery of la Murta

A monastery that once owned paintings now hanging in the Prado stands half-swallowed by its own valley, a romantic ruin that drew poets long after the monks were gone.

**Location:** 39.1289, -0.3611

**Categories:** Monasteries, History, Art, Ruins, Religion, Valencian Community

## Introduction

The paintings are in museums now. Works attributed to El Greco, Durer, and Sebastiano del Piombo, gathered over centuries by the monks of La Murta, hang today in the Prado and in Valencia. The monastery that owned them sits in a wooded valley outside Alzira, half-swallowed by the very nature that surrounds it, its walls draped in green and its glory long spent. That slow surrender to the forest is exactly what made it famous a second time. Of all the Hieronymite monasteries of Valencia, La Murta in ruin inspired the most poets.

## Hermits Fleeing Africa

The story begins, by tradition, in the year 568, in the reign of the Visigothic king Liuvigild. Hermits who had fled North Africa are said to have founded a monastery in this valley, then called Miralles, their leader dying and being buried there. The Arab conquest of 711 swept it away and scattered the hermits. For centuries the valley kept only the memory. The documented monastery is younger, born in the 14th century when Arnau de Serra, a knight of Alzira and lord of the lands of La Murta, with the authorization of King Peter IV of Aragon, gave the ground to a band of hermits on the condition that they found a religious community. Living under the Hieronymite rule, they received the papal bull to establish the monastery in 1376.

## Raised Under Cotalba's Wing

The new house grew under the protection of the great Monastery of Sant Jeroni de Cotalba, whose prior sent a group of monks to Alzira in 1401. Construction began that February, the buildings rising around a cloister, and in 1410 Saint Vincent Ferrer came to visit. The church itself took shape toward the middle of the 15th century, its initial work attributed to Jaime Gallent, a master builder of Valencia who had worked on the city's Portal de Quart and its Royal Palace. From the start, illustrious families attached their names and their fortunes to the place in exchange for the right to be buried within it, and the monastery's splendor grew with their gifts.

## The Vich Family's Gilded Age

No family shaped La Murta more than the Vich. Cardinal Guillermo Raimundo de Vich, ambassador in Rome, sought to give the monastery a new church, and his kinsman Jeronimo Vich, ambassador in Italy for Ferdinand the Catholic and Emperor Charles, paid for the work. In 1586 King Philip II himself came, accompanied by the future Philip III and the Princess Isabella Clara Eugenia, and opened a new bridge across the ravine that was christened with his name. The library and much of the monastery's peak splendor came from Juan Vich Manrique de Lara, archbishop of Tarragona. His descendant Diego Vich, the last of the line and the monastery's most devoted protector, saw the new church completed in 1623 and its altarpiece painted and gilded by Pedro de Orrente in 1634.

## Masters on the Walls

For a rural monastery, La Murta held an astonishing collection. Its treasures gathered over the centuries included works attributed to El Greco, Juan de Juanes, Albrecht Durer, Jacopo Bassano, the Flemish landscapist Paul Bril, Francisco Ribalta, Pedro de Orrente, and Jose de Ribera, along with pieces by Sebastiano del Piombo now preserved in the Prado. An alabaster altarpiece was brought from Italy by the traveling Vich ambassadors. This was not merely a place of prayer but a serious repository of European art, one of the most important historical and artistic monuments of the Valencian Community, though its later obscurity would keep that reputation quiet for a very long time.

## A Ruin That Drew the Poets

The 19th century broke it. The Ecclesiastical Confiscations of Mendizabal closed the monastery in 1835, and the eleven monks who remained walked away. In 1838 it fell into private hands, and then came the long unraveling: abandonment, looting, and finally total ruin as the surrounding forest crept in and engulfed the buildings. Yet that image, a monastery dissolving back into wild nature, seized the Romantic imagination, and writers came to the valley to mourn and marvel at it. In 1989 the city of Alzira acquired the site, and from 1995 both the convent and the La Murta Valley around it have been under slow restoration, an attempt to hold on to a beauty that ruin itself created.

## Flight Context

The monastery lies at 39.1289 N, 0.3611 W in the wooded La Murta Valley in the hills east of Alzira, in the Ribera Alta of the Valencian Community. From the air it appears as a cluster of ruined and partly restored stone structures set deep in a green valley ringed by low ranges, more integrated into the forest than most monuments, so its walls can blend with the tree cover. Best viewed at 1,500 to 3,000 feet, and a lower, slower pass helps distinguish the buildings from the vegetation that engulfed them. Valencia (LEVC) is the nearest major airport, about 45 km north; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies to the south. Winter and early spring, when the canopy thins, offer the clearest look at the ruins.
