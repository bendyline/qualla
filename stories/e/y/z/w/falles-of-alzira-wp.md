# Falles of Alzira

Seventy satirical monuments rise in the streets of Alzira each March, sculpted over months by professional artists, and by the last night every one is deliberately set on fire.

**Location:** 39.1500, -0.4350

**Categories:** Festivals, Culture, Traditions, Valencian Community, Spain

## Introduction

For a year, artists build them. On one night, the town burns them all. In Alzira, seventy towering monuments of wood and papier mache go up in the streets each March, some of them thirty meters tall, mocking politicians and current events in cartoon form. Then, on the night of the 19th, at the moment called the Crema, the whole town torches its own artwork in the very spots where it stands. Only Valencia's Falles are bigger. Alzira's are older than a century and, by the sheer count of participants, among the most fervent in the entire Valencian Community.

## Monuments Meant for the Flames

A falla is a monument built to be destroyed. Sculptors, painters, and craftsmen labor for months on constructions of combustible material, each one depicting a scene, held up on a wooden framework and populated by ninots, the cartoon-like figures that are the heart of the whole art. The scenes are satirical almost by nature, skewering the year's news and public figures, with signs in Valencian spelling out the joke for anyone who misses it. In recent years lightweight styrofoam has crept in alongside the traditional papier mache, brighter and easier to raise by crane. But the ending never changes. Everything that was made to be admired is made, ultimately, to burn.

## A Week Run Like Clockwork

The festivity opens in early March with La Crida, the call to begin, and then the Falles week follows a schedule enforced to the minute. On the 16th comes the Planta, the raising of the monuments, which must stand complete before the judges arrive or face disqualification. The 17th brings the awards ceremony at the town hall. On the 18th, fallers carry flowers in the Ofrenda to Our Lady of Lluch, patron saint of Alzira, in a procession that parades through the evening and deep into the night. The 19th, the feast of Saint Joseph, is the climax: an afternoon of commissions dancing the pasodoble through the streets, judged and prized, and then, after dark, the fire.

## Flowers, Firecrackers, and Thunder

Falles is not a quiet festival, and it does not want to be. On the mornings of the 17th, 18th, and 19th, each commission gathers at eight o'clock at its casal, its clubhouse, for the desperta, marching through the neighborhood throwing firecrackers to wake everyone whether they like it or not. Breakfast follows, then hours of wandering from one casal to the next. At two in the afternoon the crowds pack the Plaza del Reino for the mascleta, the famous daytime pyrotechnic display prized less for how it looks than for the rhythmic percussion you feel in your chest. It is noise as celebration, a town choosing, for a few days, to be as loud as it possibly can.

## The Rules of Belonging

For all its chaos of fire and smoke, Falles is governed by careful tradition. The festivity is regulated by the Junta Local Fallera, and its first centenary was marked in 1989. Monuments compete within sections grouped by budget, no more than ten to a section, to keep the contests fair. Dress is prescribed in detail: fallers in black jackets and sashes, falleres in formal Valencian farmers' dress with their hair coiled into chignons, ties and bows forbidden, every ornament required to be traditional. Presiding over it all are the Fallera Major and the Fallera Major Infantil, the elected queen and her junior counterpart, the girl no older than fourteen. They alone wear the sash in the colors of the Spanish flag, the mark of the ones who lead the town through its week of controlled fire.

## A Town That Rebuilds Itself

There is something almost defiant in the whole enterprise. A town spends months and real money conjuring seventy monuments, raises them for everyone to admire and mock, and then, on a single March night, reduces each one to ash and starts planning the next. The satire is forgotten, the artistry is gone, and only the ritual endures, older than a century now and showing no sign of tiring. To burn what you have built is to insist that the making mattered more than the keeping, that a thing can be worth doing even when nothing of it survives the week. Come spring, Alzira makes that argument again in fire, and the ash settles, and the workshops quietly begin once more.

## Flight Context

Alzira sits at 39.15 N, 0.435 W in the fertile Ribera Alta plain of the Valencian Community, on the Xuquer (Jucar) river about 40 km south of Valencia. From the air the town is a compact urban core wrapped by the river and surrounded by a patchwork of orange groves; during Falles week in March the streets themselves become the venue, with monuments raised at intersections and plazas. Best viewed at 2,000 to 3,500 feet. Valencia (LEVC) is the nearest major airport, roughly 40 km north; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies farther south. Note that the festival's fireworks and night-time Crema fires are a March ground-level spectacle; clear spring days give the best overhead view of the town and its river bends.
