# Battle of Lauron

The young Pompey stood with his entire army and was forced to watch, unable to move, while a rebel general had his soldiers slaughtered before his eyes.

**Location:** 39.1522, -0.4425

**Categories:** History, Ancient Rome, Battles, Archaeology, Valencian Community

## Introduction

Pompey had thirty thousand veterans and nowhere to send them. In 76 BC, on a plain in eastern Hispania, the rising star of Rome found himself pinned in place while his own soldiers were cut down in full view of his lines. Every instinct told him to charge to their rescue. Every calculation told him it would destroy his army. So he stood there and watched them die. The rebel general Quintus Sertorius had built the trap precisely, and the humiliation of Lauron would follow Pompey for years.

## The Rebel Rome Could Not Beat

Sertorius was a Roman fighting Rome. A loyal man of the Marian faction, he had been driven out of Hispania in 81 BC when the dictator Sulla's forces took the Iberian provinces, and he washed up in North Africa, where he seized the city of Tingis. When the Lusitanians, a fierce Iberian people, invited him back to lead them, he sailed across and landed at the little fishing town of Baelo near the Pillars of Hercules, the strait we now call Gibraltar. He won at the Baetis river, and then he kept winning. His guerrilla tactics wore the proconsul Metellus Pius down to exhaustion. Alarmed, the Senate sent Pompey with an even larger army to finish the job. Instead, Sertorius chose the ground for their first real meeting, and he chose the city of Lauron, which sat squarely between the two Roman armies and had made the mistake of siding against him.

## The Trap Baited With Grass

Sertorius reached Lauron first and laid siege. Pompey, confident in his veterans, camped so close he expected to force a quick battle. Sertorius answered not with a charge but with patience. He sent his cavalry and light troops to harass Pompey's foragers, but only those working the nearby fields, leaving the distant tracts untouched. Worn down by the raids, the Pompeians shifted their foraging far out to the remote ground, exactly as Sertorius had intended. Under cover of night he sent twenty cohorts and two thousand cavalry, under Octavius Gracinus and Tarquitius Priscus, to lie in wait. The bait had done its work. The men gathering food were about to be surrounded.

## A General Forced to Watch

When Pompey learned his foragers were in trouble, he sent his legate Decimus Laelius with a full legion to cover their retreat. It marched straight into the ambush. Sertorian cavalry drew the legion forward, infantry met it head-on, and then the cavalry that had circled behind struck it in the rear. Caught front and back, the legion broke. By the time Pompey led out his whole army, Sertorius had drawn up his remaining troops facing him, and Pompey saw the impossible arithmetic at once. Advance, and his trapped men would be annihilated while he fought uphill against fresh lines. Retreat to save them, and Sertorius would take his flank. He stayed put. Ten thousand of his soldiers died where he could see them, men he could not reach, and then Sertorius burned Lauron to the ground.

## Spectacular, But Not the End

Only the arrival of Metellus and his army kept Sertorius from destroying Pompey entirely. Unwilling to be caught between two Roman forces, the rebel withdrew, and so the victory, for all its brilliance, settled nothing in the strategic sense. Yet it delivered a message no city could miss. The towns of the Iberian east coast had largely sided with Rome, and by burning Lauron while a Roman general watched helplessly from across the field, Sertorius showed every wavering community exactly what Roman protection was worth. Pompey remained a power in Hispania, and the war ground on for years across the peninsula. In the end Sertorius fell not to a battlefield rival but to betrayal, murdered at a banquet by a conspiracy of his own officers. The men who could not defeat him in the field killed him at the table. Frontinus and Plutarch both recorded Lauron in detail, preserving the day a future master of Rome learned how it feels to command everything and control nothing.

## Flight Context

The site associated with ancient Lauron lies near 39.1522 N, 0.4425 W in the interior of the Valencian Community, west of the Ribera lowlands and the modern town of Alzira, in country of low hills, river valleys, and cultivated plain that would have offered the open ground and foraging fields the ancient accounts describe. No battlefield markers survive, so navigate by the broader landscape: the flat huerta farmland threaded by irrigation channels between coastal ranges. Best surveyed at 2,500 to 4,000 feet in clear light. Valencia (LEVC) is the nearest major field, roughly 40 km north; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies to the south. Inland summer heat can raise haze by midday, so morning gives the cleanest view of the terrain.
