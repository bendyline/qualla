# Siege of Alzira

When artisan guilds rose against their king, Alzira and neighboring Xativa became the last two towns to keep the rebel banner flying, holding out through a failed royal siege into December 1522.

**Location:** 39.1500, -0.4333

**Categories:** History, Sieges, Revolt of the Brotherhoods, Valencian Community, Spain

## Introduction

By the winter of 1522, the great artisan revolt that had swept the Kingdom of Valencia was down to two towns. Everywhere else the rebels had been crushed or had surrendered, but Alzira, cradled within a loop of the Xuquer river, and its neighbor Xativa still flew the banner of the Brotherhoods. A royal army of more than eight thousand men marched to end their defiance. It laid siege, and then, after a single month, it failed and marched away. The last strongholds of the uprising would not fall until the year was almost out.

## When the Guilds Rose

The Revolt of the Brotherhoods, the Germanies, began in 1519 as a rebellion of artisan guilds against the government of King Charles I, the same monarch the world would know as Emperor Charles V. Plague had struck Valencia that year, killing some nobles and sending others fleeing to the countryside, and into that vacuum the guildsmen and urban poor pressed their grievances: crushing taxes, feudal lords, and a distant king who ruled from abroad. Under the cloth-dresser Joan Llorenc, the rebels formed a governing Council of Thirteen and built a movement that was anti-feudal and autonomist in spirit, cousin to the comuneros then rising in Castile. For a time, the artisans of Valencia governed themselves.

## A Revolt With Two Faces

The uprising carried real hope for the working people who joined it, but it also carried a cruelty that history should not soften. Alongside their fight against the crown and the nobles, the agermanats turned on the kingdom's Muslim peasantry, the mudejars, attacking communities that had farmed these lands for generations and forcing conversions at the point of a sword. The same men demanding justice from their king denied it to their neighbors. It is a reminder that a popular revolt can be both a genuine cry against oppression and a source of new suffering, and that the mudejars caught in its path were people with homes, faith, and families of their own.

## A Fire Across Two Kingdoms

The Germanies were among the boldest popular uprisings of their age, an anti-feudal and autonomist movement that drew inspiration from the self-governing republics of Italy and briefly dared to imagine a Valencia run by its guildsmen rather than its lords. They did not rise alone. At almost the same moment, the Comuneros of Castile were making war on the very same young king, so that Charles faced revolt at both ends of his Spanish inheritance. Both movements were ultimately beaten, and the crown set about restoring the old order town by town. In the Kingdom of Valencia that meant reconquering the rebel cities one after another, grinding the uprising down until only a pair of holdouts on the Xuquer river remained to be subdued.

## Down to the Last Two Towns

The rebellion's military back was broken at the Battle of Oriola in 1521. The Brotherhood army collapsed, and the royal forces reoccupied the Kingdom of Valencia town by town. Soon only Xativa and Alzira remained under rebel control, two holdouts on the map of a defeated cause. The task of subduing them fell to the viceroy Diego Hurtado de Mendoza, first Count of Melito. With Valencia itself subdued behind him, he brought his army against Alzira, but the town's river was a wall in its own right, and simply crossing it to reach the walls took his men three full days.

## The Siege That Broke, and the Banner That Fell

Once across, the royal army settled in to starve Alzira out. The siege held for a month before it cracked, not because the defenders sallied out, but because three thousand agermanats marched from Xativa to relieve their besieged allies. Rather than be caught between the town and the relief force, the royal army abandoned the siege. It was a small, stubborn victory for a cause already lost everywhere else. The reprieve could not last. On the 9th of December, 1522, both Alzira and Xativa, the final strongholds of the Brotherhoods in the Valencian Country, at last capitulated, and the great revolt of the guilds came quietly to its end.

## Flight Context

Alzira sits at 39.15 N, 0.4333 W on the Xuquer (Jucar) river in the Ribera Alta of the Valencian Community, about 40 km south of Valencia. From the air the historic core is defined by the river that loops around it, the same water barrier that once cost a besieging army three days to cross, now ringed by the orange groves of the Ribera plain. Best viewed at 2,000 to 3,500 feet in clear weather, when the river bends and the old town street pattern read clearly. Valencia (LEVC) is the nearest major airport, roughly 40 km north; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies farther south. Inland haze can build on hot afternoons, so morning offers the sharpest view of the river and town.
