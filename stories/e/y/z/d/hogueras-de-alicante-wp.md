# Hogueras de Alicante

Every June, Alicante spends a year building towering satirical monuments in wood and cork, then burns them all in a single ecstatic night of fire.

**Location:** 38.3453, -0.4831

**Categories:** Festivals, Alicante, Fire festivals, Valencian Community, Spanish culture, Midsummer

## Introduction

Just before midnight on the 24th of June, the shortest night of the year, the people of Alicante set fire to art they spent a year making. Towering sculptures of wood, cork, and papier-mache - some four or five storeys tall, many of them merciless parodies of politicians - go up in flames along dozens of streets at once. This is la crema, the burning, and it is the whole point. For six days the city has danced, eaten, and detonated fireworks around these monuments; now it watches them collapse into ash and cheers. The Bonfires of Saint John are Alicante's official festival, declared a Fiesta of International Tourist Interest in 1983 and a Bien de Interes Cultural in 2014, and nothing else the city does all year comes close.

## Fire Older Than the Saint

Long before there was a saint attached to it, there was the solstice. Midsummer bonfires burn across much of Europe on the eve of Saint John, and their roots reach back to far older celebrations of the year's turning. People once lit them to keep evil spirits at bay in the season when the sun began its long retreat southward; in later folklore, witches were thought to be abroad on the same night. Along the Spanish Mediterranean - in Catalonia, Galicia, and the Valencian Community - the tradition took hold with particular warmth. Families gathered on beaches to build fires from old furniture, shared the flat sweet pastry called coca de Sant Joan, and watched children leap over the flames. In Alicante, that simple midsummer fire would grow into something far more elaborate.

## One Man's Idea, 1928

Before 1928, Alicante burned its bonfires the way everyone else did - a heap of discarded furniture set alight on the night of the 24th. Then Jose Maria Py, who felt his city needed a great fiesta of its own, proposed something bolder: marry the midsummer fire to the Valencian tradition of the fallas, the satirical monuments that Valencia burns each March. The idea caught. From that year on, Alicante's bonfires were no longer piles of junk but designed constructions, planted in the streets and judged as art. The city insists the result is not a copy of the Valencian fallas. Its first monuments were built not by faller craftsmen but by local painters and sculptors working in an Art Deco and avant-garde spirit, and that independent streak has run through the festival ever since.

## Satire You Can Set Alight

The monuments are called fogueres - bonfires - and they are built to be destroyed. Framed in wood and cardboard, sculpted now in cork and polyurethane, each one crowds together comic figures called ninots. A ninot might caricature a footballer or a film star, but more often it skewers a politician, and the whole tableau becomes a running commentary on the year's absurdities, local and global. Neighbourhood commissions, whose members are called foguerers, raise the money all year through lotteries, raffles, and shop donations. The competition is fierce and expensive; the top Special Category alone requires monuments costing more than sixty thousand euros. The district of Benalua has won more often than any other, eighteen times across the festival's history. When the judging is done, the single winning ninot is plucked out and spared; everything else burns.

## Six Days of Noise and Ceremony

The festival is loud by design. Each morning at eight, the desperta wakes the neighbourhoods with a barrage of noise; each afternoon at two, the mascleta answers with a rhythmic string of firecrackers built to be felt in the chest as much as heard. Between them run the parades - bands, prize-givings, and the tender Flower Offering to the Virgin of the Remedy, in which women wearing the white round mantilla of the Novia Alicantina carry bunches of flowers through the streets. Presiding over everything is the Bellea del Foc, the Beauty of the Fire, chosen months in advance to reign as queen of the whole city's festival. By night the districts fill their racos and barracas, the open street parties and the members-only ones, where people eat, drink, and dance until dawn.

## La Crema

It ends the way it began, with fire. In the small hours of the 25th, a cascade of fireworks called la palmera unfurls over the city, and then, one by one, the monuments are lit. The same neighbours who built them stand and watch a year's work fall into embers, and for many the moment is unexpectedly moving - a small, deliberate lesson in impermanence, staged with brass bands and firefighters standing ready. By morning the streets are swept clean. The following night, on Postiguet Beach beneath the castle, a fireworks competition sends the last light of the season out over the Mediterranean, and Alicante begins, quietly, to plan next year's fire.

## Flight Context

Alicante sits on the Costa Blanca at 38.345 degrees N, 0.483 degrees W, its old town wrapped around the base of Mount Benacantil and the Castle of Santa Barbara. During the June festival, monuments stand in dozens of streets across the city, and the final night's fires and the fireworks over Postiguet Beach are visible far out to sea. Alicante-Elche Airport (LEAL) lies about 10 km southwest; Valencia (LEVC) is roughly 150 km north up the coast. Summer skies here are typically clear, with the best visibility in the calm evenings when the bonfires are lit.
