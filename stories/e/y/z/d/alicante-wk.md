# Alicante

A Mediterranean port crowned by a castle on a white crag, where a Carthaginian general's fortress became a Roman town, a medieval trading city, and the capital of the Costa Blanca.

**Location:** 38.3453, -0.4831

**Categories:** Cities, Mediterranean Ports, Costa Blanca, Historic Sites, Beaches, Valencian Community

## Introduction

Long before Hannibal marched his elephants toward the Alps, his father chose this white headland for a fortress. The Carthaginian general Hamilcar Barca established a fortified settlement here and called it Akra Leuka, Greek for 'White Point,' and the pale limestone crag that gave it the name still rises straight from the seafront today. Two millennia of rulers have layered themselves onto that rock. What sits at its foot now is Alicante, a city of some 366,000 people, capital of its province, and the sun-warmed gateway to the beaches of the Costa Blanca.

## The White Point

Alicante keeps changing its name and its masters while staying stubbornly itself. After the Carthaginians came the Romans, who took the settlement and renamed it Lucentum. The bright headland that Hamilcar prized is Mount Benacantil, and the Valencian name Alacant still echoes those ancient syllables. From the sea the mountain reads as a natural citadel, an obvious place to plant a flag, which is precisely why every power that ruled this coast, from Carthage to Rome to the emirs of al-Andalus, wanted the height for itself. The city grew in the mountain's shadow, always looking up at the same commanding rock.

## The Castle on the Face

Crowning Benacantil today is the Castell de la Santa Bàrbara, one of Spain's largest medieval fortresses, its ramparts stacked across the summit like layers of the mountain's own stone. Look at the slope below the walls and the rock plays a trick. A natural profile of ledges and shadows resolves into a human face gazing skyward, the feature locals have long called the Cara del Moro, the Moor's Face. From the battlements the whole city unrolls: the grid of the old town, the arc of the harbor, the Mediterranean fading into haze. It is the single best vantage point on the Costa Blanca, earned by a short, hot climb or an elevator bored through the rock.

## A Port That Traded in Sunlight

Alicante has always made its living from the sea in front of it. In medieval times the city became a major Mediterranean trading station, shipping out the produce of a generous hinterland: rice, wine, olive oil, oranges, and wool. Those exports read like a portrait of the land itself, everything the warm coast and irrigated plains could grow. The port still defines the city's eastern edge, and the deep archaeological memory of the place is gathered in the MARQ, the provincial archaeological museum, where the Iberian, Roman, and Islamic layers of this ground are laid out in sequence.

## The Old Town and the Table

Between Mount Benacantil and the waterfront lies the Casco Antiguo, a triangular tangle of narrow streets easy to get pleasantly lost in. Here stand the town hall, the cathedral, an old convent, and clusters of small museums, and after dark the same lanes become the heart of the city's nightlife. Alicante eats late and eats well. Rice and seafood rule the table, with paella in the front line and local specialties beside it: arroz alicantino, the city's own take on the dish, and arroz con costra, rice baked under a crust of egg and sausage. The bargain everyone learns fast is the menú del día, a full midday meal for a handful of euros.

## Fire and Sand

Each June the city gives itself over to Las Hogueras de San Juan, its midsummer festival, when elaborate sculpted monuments rise on street corners across town, only to be set ablaze on the shortest night of the year in a controlled roar of flame. When the embers cool, the beaches take over. The Playa del Postiguet lies right in the heart of the city at the mountain's foot, while a tram ride north reaches the Playa de San Juan, seven kilometers of sand counted among the finest in Spain. Farther out, the dune-backed Playa de los Arenales trades crowds for quiet. Fire and sea, in the same warm week.

## Flight Context

Alicante sits at 38.35°N, 0.48°W on the Mediterranean coast. The unmistakable landmark is Mount Benacantil, an isolated pale crag rising about 166 meters straight from the shoreline and crowned by the sprawling Santa Bárbara Castle, with the old town, harbor, and palm-lined seafront at its base and the long curve of San Juan beach stretching north. From 3,000-6,000 feet the castle-topped rock is visible for miles. Alicante-Elche Airport (LEAL) lies roughly 10 km southwest of the center, a busy field serving the whole Costa Blanca; Región de Murcia (LEMI) is farther south. Clear, dry Levantine air offers excellent visibility, with the sharpest light in early morning before coastal haze builds.
