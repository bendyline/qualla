# A Coast Named for Visitors

The Costa Blanca's name was coined in the 1950s to promote tourism, but the roughly 200-kilometre shore of Alicante province, from the slopes of Montgó to the palm grove of Elche, carries layers left by Iberians, Greeks, Romans and al-Andalus.

**Location:** 38.3540, -0.4080

**Categories:** Coastlines, Beaches, Resort Towns, Province of Alicante, Valencian Community

## Introduction

Long before anyone called it the Costa Blanca, this shore had been settled by Iberians, colonised by Phoenicians and Greeks, ruled from Rome and folded into al-Andalus. The name is the newcomer. It was coined in the 1950s as a way to promote tourism, and it stuck: the "White Coast" now labels about 200 kilometres of Mediterranean shoreline in Alicante province, running from Dénia in the north to Pilar de la Horadada in the south, and its tourism industry is especially popular with British and German visitors. The spot marked here lies near the water just east of Alicante, the provincial capital and largest city, close to the middle of that long arc.

## In the Shadow of Montgó

The northern stretch is also known as the Marina Alta. Seaside towns and resorts such as Dénia, Xàbia (Jávea in Spanish) and Moraira sit at its far north, and between Dénia and Xàbia rises Montgó, a 753-metre (2,470-foot) mountain whose nearness to the sea gives it a spectacular visual impact. Inland lies Guadalest, an especially scenic mountain village. The landscape is a mix of flat, fertile valleys, hidden coves and grand mountain ranges that give the whole coast its backdrop. The rock draws a different kind of visitor too. With its limestone crags and Mediterranean weather, the Costa Blanca is a popular climbing destination, so the same sun that pulls people onto the beaches pulls others up the rock.

## Where the Crowds Come

Moving south, the resorts grow larger. Altea, Calp (Calpe in Spanish), Benidorm, Alicante and Torrevieja attract millions of tourists a year, and Benidorm and Alicante are the coast's major tourist centres. Benidorm is known above all for its British residents and visitors. Alicante, the provincial capital, anchors the middle of the coast; its international airport and Valencia's, further north, are the two main gateways, and a light-rail line, the Alicante Tram, runs from the city north up the shore. Beyond Alicante, Elche holds one of the world's largest palm groves, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. Toward the southern end, Torrevieja is famous mainly for its beaches and for a high proportion of expatriate residents. From a traveller's point of view the main features are simple: white sandy beaches and pleasant resorts, with the odd golf course. The A-7 motorway follows the coast south from Barcelona through Valencia toward Murcia, tying the whole strip together.

## Older Than the Name

The Iberians are among the earliest peoples of what is now Alicante province to be known by name, though people had lived in the territory since the Middle Palaeolithic, roughly 100,000 years ago. La Serreta, near Alcoy, is where a lead plaque in the Iberian language, written in Greek-derived letters, was found in 1921; it is considered probably the first and longest text of its kind, and the Iberian language itself is still only partly understood. In the same era Phoenicians founded a colony at Guardamar and Greeks settled the coast north of Alicante city, and both interacted with the Iberians; the Lady of Elche is a famous archaeological find from that time. After a brief Carthaginian period the Romans took over, and cities thrived along the Via Augusta, the road that connected this corner of Iberia to the rest of the empire. One of them, Ilici Augusta, now Elche, reached the rank of colonia. Two centuries of Visigothic rule followed before Islamic armies captured the region and made it part of al-Andalus.

## Kingdoms and Borders

From the 13th century, kings including Ferdinand III and Alfonso X of Castile and James I and James II of Aragon slowly reconquered the cities of the area. The Treaty of Almizra at first split what is now Alicante province between the Crown of Castile and the Crown of Aragon. Later the whole territory came under the Kingdom of Valencia, one of the domains of the Crown of Aragon. Today many of the coast's towns are known by both a Valencian and a Spanish name: Alacant and Alicante, Elx and Elche, Torrevella and Torrevieja, La Vila Joiosa and Villajoyosa. The marketing name arrived some seven centuries after the reconquest, laid over a coast whose old villages, scattered along the shore, still hold plenty of everyday Spanish life and traditional festivities.

## Flight Context

The story's point sits at about 38.35°N, 0.41°W, near the shoreline just east of Alicante. From altitude the Costa Blanca reads as a long arc: northeast toward Benidorm and the mountainous Marina Alta around Montgó and Dénia, southwest toward Santa Pola and Torrevieja. The A-7 motorway runs roughly parallel to the coast, in places some kilometres inland, and makes a useful line feature. Alicante-Elche Miguel Hernández Airport (LEAL) lies about 8 nautical miles west-southwest; Valencia Airport (LEVC) is about 68 nautical miles north. Around 3,000-5,000 feet gives a good view of the shoreline, coves and beaches; summer haze can soften long views out over the sea.
