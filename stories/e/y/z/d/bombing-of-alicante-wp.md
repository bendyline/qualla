# Bombing of Alicante

On a market morning in 1938, Italian bombers allied with Franco struck the heart of Republican Alicante, killing hundreds of civilians in one of the Spanish Civil War's deadliest air raids.

**Location:** 38.3500, -0.4833

**Categories:** Spanish Civil War, History of Alicante, Aerial Bombardment, 20th Century History, Valencian Community

## Introduction

It was a market morning. People had come out to buy food, threading through the stalls of the central market and the streets around it, when the bombers reached Alicante on 25 May 1938. There were no soldiers to hit here, no barracks, no guns, only shoppers and vendors going about an ordinary Wednesday. Within minutes several hundred of them were dead. The raid on the Mercado Central would be remembered as one of the deadliest aerial attacks of the entire Spanish Civil War, and it fell entirely on people who had no way to fight back.

## A Market Morning

The dead were counted the way such things are counted after a massacre, imperfectly and in grief. Estimates range from 275 to 393 killed: around a hundred men, fifty-six women, ten children, and more than a hundred bodies so badly maimed that no one could identify them. Close to a thousand more were wounded, and the historian Paul Preston put the toll at several hundred civilians. Most were caught in the open, doing the morning shopping at the stalls behind the market hall, because the sirens never sounded. The Italian planes had come in low from over the land rather than from the sea, slipping past a warning system that, in an age before radar, had no way to see them coming. So there was no dash for shelter, no warning at all. Behind each figure in the count was a person who had walked to the market that morning with a basket and a list, a life measured out in the small errands of a city at war but still, somehow, going on.

## Bombs From Mallorca

The aircraft came from the island of Mallorca, flown by the Aviazione Legionaria, an expeditionary corps of the Italian Royal Air Force fighting on Franco's side. Between seven and nine Italian SM.79 and SM.81 bombers reached the city and released some ninety bombs, many of them onto the central market and the surrounding streets. The market held no military value whatsoever. Survivors described the planes coming down low over the wreckage, guns firing at people in the open. This was not an attack that missed its target and struck civilians by accident. The civilians were the target.

## A Policy of Terror

The raid was one act in a deliberate strategy. After the Aragon Offensive, Franco set out to strangle Republican maritime commerce and break Republican morale, and to that end he authorized the Aviazione Legionaria and the German Legion Condor to carry out indiscriminate bombing of Republican cities. Valencia, Barcelona, Alicante, Granollers, and other towns were struck in turn. The Spanish Civil War became one of the first conflicts in which air power was turned systematically against urban populations, a grim rehearsal for the far larger campaigns of the world war that followed. Alicante's market was chosen precisely because destroying it would terrorize the living.

## The Reckoning Abroad

News of the slaughter traveled. The bombings of Alicante and Granollers, together with attacks on British merchant shipping in Republican ports, provoked protests in London, where the killing of civilians and the endangering of British sailors could not be ignored. Diplomacy moved slowly, though, and the war ground on for another ten months before the Republic fell in the spring of 1939. Alicante would become one of the last cities to surrender, its harbor crowded with refugees hoping for passage that, for many, never came. The market raid was neither the first blow the city absorbed nor the last, but it was the one that cut deepest into ordinary life.

## What the Clock Remembers

The building that absorbed the bombs still stands, and still sells fish and fruit. Alicante's Mercado Central is a Valencian modernist landmark completed in 1922, all iron, brick, and colored glass, and inside it hangs a clock stopped at 11:18, the minute the blast reached it. It has never been restarted. Set into the pavement of the plaza, a 2013 memorial marks the raid without a list of names, most of which were never known: nine aluminum plates for the nine bombers that came that day, marked with ninety dots for the bombs they dropped, and threaded through with some three hundred fiber-optic points that glow red for ten minutes every day at noon, one light for each life the raid is thought to have taken. The plaza behind the market, where the most victims fell, now carries the date itself: Plaza 25 de Mayo. Composers from the city have written for them, among them a symphonic poem and an elegy for solo cello. Every May the square fills again, this time with people who come to remember. It is the kind of history a city keeps, not to reopen the wound, but to insist that those hundreds be remembered as people, not as a number.

## Flight Context

The site of the 1938 raid centers on Alicante's Mercado Central, at roughly 38.35°N, 0.48°W, in the heart of the city just inland from the harbor and below Mount Benacantil with its Santa Bárbara Castle. The modernist covered market building remains a recognizable landmark among the dense downtown streets. From 3,000-5,000 feet the castle-topped crag orients the whole city; the market lies in the built-up center between it and the port. Alicante-Elche Airport (LEAL) is about 10 km southwest; Región de Murcia (LEMI) lies farther south; Valencia (LEVC) is roughly 130 km up the coast to the northeast. Clear Mediterranean air gives good visibility over the compact urban core, best in morning light.
