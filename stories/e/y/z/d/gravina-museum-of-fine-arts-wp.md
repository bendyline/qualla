# Gravina Museum of Fine Arts

Housed in an 18th-century Alicante palace, the MUBAG gathers four centuries of a single province's art, from Baroque sculpture to 19th-century history painting.

**Location:** 38.3456, -0.4797

**Categories:** Art Museums, Museums, Historic Buildings, Fine Arts, Alicante, Valencian Community

## Introduction

Most fine-arts museums gather the world's masterpieces under one roof. This one does something quieter and rarer: it gathers a single province's four-century attempt to picture itself. The Gravina Museum of Fine Arts, known locally as the MUBAG, is devoted almost entirely to the painting and sculpture of Alicante, the art made here and about here, from the 16th century to the early decades of the 20th. It is less a treasury of borrowed glories than a portrait of a place, painted by the people who lived in it.

## A Palace Sixty Years in the Making

The setting is itself a work of art. The museum occupies the Palacio del Conde de Lumiares, a stately four-floor mansion that took an unhurried six decades to build, rising between 1748 and 1808 and later declared a historical monument. That span meant the building crossed styles and generations before it was finished, a common fate for great houses of the age. After careful restoration the palace opened as the MUBAG in 2001, giving Alicante's own art a home worthy of it. Walking its floors, a visitor moves through the architecture and the paintings at once, the container and the contents both speaking of the same centuries.

## The Count of Lumiares

The name and the walls both carry history. The museum stands on Calle Gravina, and it is from that street that it takes its everyday name. The palace once belonged to the Count of Lumiares, Antonio Valcárcel Pío de Saboya, born in Alicante in 1748, the very year from which the mansion's long construction is dated. He grew up to become, by common reckoning, the first Valencian archaeologist, a nobleman who spent his life unearthing and cataloguing the Roman past of his region. He wrote a study of Lucentum, the Roman town whose name Alicante still echoes, listing its inscriptions, statues, coins, and lamps. There is a fitting symmetry in it. A house tied to a man who devoted himself to preserving his corner of Spain now serves to preserve how that corner learned to picture itself.

## A Province Paints Itself

The collection draws on the funds of the provincial council and numbers close to five hundred works. What binds them is not a single school or century but a place. Together they trace how Alicante and its region were seen and depicted across some four hundred years, by local hands and regional talents rather than distant celebrities. There is a particular intimacy to a museum built this way. Instead of asking what the great capitals produced, it asks what this corner of the Mediterranean made of itself, and it answers with canvases and carvings that most of the wider art world has never had cause to notice.

## Names on the Walls

That said, real mastery hangs here. The collection includes a sculpture by Francisco Salzillo, the supreme sculptor of the Murcian Baroque, whose polychrome figures set the standard for religious carving in southeastern Spain. There is a portrait of Ferdinand VII by Vicente López Portaña, the Valencian painter who served as court portraitist and ranked among the finest of his day. And the galleries gather the leading regional painters of the 19th century, Antonio Gisbert, Joaquín Agrasot, Lorenzo Casanova, and Fernando Cabrera Cantó, artists whose reputations were made across Spain even as their roots stayed local. Gisbert became one of the country's foremost history painters, while Casanova's Alicante academy trained a whole generation of local talent, the younger Cabrera Cantó among his pupils. The thread runs on into the 20th century with Alicante's own Emilio Varela, whose light-soaked views of the city and its coast close out the collection's long arc. Seeing their work gathered where it was made, rather than scattered through the great national museums, restores something these paintings can lose in a Madrid gallery: their sense of belonging to a specific stretch of coast.

## The Nineteenth-Century Mirror

Those 19th-century works are more than pretty surfaces; they are a record of what the era cared about. The collection reflects the major currents of the century in turn: historicism, with its dramatic scenes drawn from the national past; the loving depiction of local customs and everyday life; portraiture, fixing the faces of a rising provincial society; and landscape, the light and land of the Alicante coast rendered for its own sake. Read together, these paintings form a kind of mirror held up to a region finding its confidence. To stand before them is to see not just how people looked, but how they wished to be remembered.

## Flight Context

The Gravina Museum of Fine Arts stands at 38.35°N, 0.48°W in Alicante's historic center, near the cathedral and the old town, just inland from the harbor and below Mount Benacantil's Santa Bárbara Castle. The 18th-century palace is a modest landmark from the air, best identified on low, clear passes among the dense downtown streets, with the castle-topped crag as the obvious orienting feature to the east. Alicante-Elche Airport (LEAL) lies about 10 km southwest; Región de Murcia (LEMI) is farther south; Valencia (LEVC) is roughly 130 km up the coast to the northeast. Bright Mediterranean daylight favors midday and afternoon, when the sun reaches into the narrow surrounding lanes.
