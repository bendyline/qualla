# Conquest of madina Laqant

The slow 13th-century fall of Muslim Alicante, where the last emir of Valencia made a final stand by the sea, buying time to evacuate his people before Christian rule emptied the medina.

**Location:** 38.3453, -0.4831

**Categories:** Reconquista, Al-Andalus, Medieval History, History of Alicante, Valencian Community

## Introduction

In 1240 a defeated king offered to trade an entire island for the safety of his people. Zayyan ibn Mardanish, the last emir of Valencia, met James I of Aragon at Bairén and proposed exchanging distant Menorca for the small fortified town of Laqant on the coast, a refuge where the Muslims of his crumbling realm might still live. James refused, unwilling to break the treaties that bound him to Castile. But the offer bought Zayyan something almost as valuable as land: time. And time, at Laqant, would mean the difference between escape and captivity.

## The Last Emir's Stand

Alicante, known then as madina Laqant, had belonged to the Muslim taifa world of eastern al-Andalus for centuries. When Valencia fell to Christian forces in 1238, its last emir did not simply vanish into exile. Zayyan installed himself in 1241 as rais, or local governor, of Laqant, and set about holding what little of the old Valencian emirate remained free. In 1243 the neighboring emir of Murcia accepted vassalage under Castile through the Treaty of Alcaraz. Zayyan refused the same bargain. He rejected Castilian sovereignty outright, declared Laqant independent, and threw in his lot with the wider Mudéjar revolt led by the rebel al-Azraq across the southern towns.

## A Door to the Sea

Laqant's defiance was possible because of geography. In the 13th century it held the only Muslim-controlled seaport along the entire coast between the Ebro delta and Almería, a natural harbor at the beach of Baver with active trade to Ténès on the North African shore. Pressed on land from the north by the armies of Aragon and from the south by Christian-allied towns, the medina had one road left open, and it led out to sea. That door became a lifeline. Andalusi families, the elite of Alacant, Valencia, and Murcia among them, took ship for Ifriqiya rather than live under feudal rule. Around 1247 Zayyan himself was the last to leave, sailing to Kairouan, where the Almohad caliph al-Mustansir received him with honors and made him governor of that great Tunisian city.

## The Castle That Would Not Fall

With Zayyan gone, the defense of the fortress passed to a wazir named al-Azraq, who went on to lead the first Mudéjar revolt against the Christian occupiers between 1247 and 1258. The stronghold on the crag of Benacantil was a genuinely hard nut. The town below clung to a coastal cliff, and no attacker could enter the medina without first taking the height, so the occupation dragged out gradually across two decades, from 1247 to 1266. Historians still cannot fix the exact day the castle fell, but they agree the defenders resisted fiercely. One thing is certain: the name Castle of Santa Bárbara came much later. As late as 1640 a chronicler still called it the Castle of the Face, after the Cara del Moro, the Moor's Face traced in the rock of the mountainside.

## A City Emptied

Christian rule arrived to find a largely emptied city, and it emptied it further. The Muslim families who remained were expelled from the medina, thereafter called the Vila Vella, the Old Town. The Andalusi population was scattered across the countryside, made to abandon their homes and surrender the communal lands that had sustained them, which passed to new Christian owners. They resettled in the valleys of the Alacant plain and the Vinalopó, without defense or protection. The human loss was also an economic one. With the exile of artisans, merchants, seasoned sailors, and the irrigation experts who had kept the huerta green, the prosperity Laqant once drew from its port simply drained away, and recovery would wait until the guilds of the 14th century.

## A Bride's Dowry

For all the blood spent taking it, Laqant changed hands through marriage as much as war. James I had granted the city as part of the dowry of his daughter Violant of Aragon when she wed Alfonso X of Castile, and the frontier between the two crowns was fixed by the Treaty of Almizra of 1244. So the town first came under Castilian rule and was folded into the Kingdom of Murcia, only to be recovered for the Kingdom of Valencia in 1296 by James II of Aragon. Later chroniclers invented tidy December foundation dates to dignify the conquest, but historians now dismiss them. The truth was messier and slower, a city argued over by kings and defended, to the last, by the people it belonged to.

## Flight Context

The events centered on medina Laqant unfolded around 38.35°N, 0.48°W, at the site of modern Alicante. The commanding feature then and now is Mount Benacantil, the coastal crag whose fortress, later renamed the Santa Bárbara Castle, could not be bypassed by any attacker. From 3,000-6,000 feet the castle-topped rock rising straight from the seafront is visible for miles, with the old town, the harbor, and the coastline that once carried Andalusi refugees toward North Africa spread below. Alicante-Elche Airport (LEAL) lies about 10 km southwest; Región de Murcia (LEMI) lies farther south toward the old Castilian frontier. Clear, dry Levantine air gives long visibility, best in the morning.
