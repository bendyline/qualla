# Siege of Alicante

During the War of the Spanish Succession, an Allied garrison held Alicante's castle until besiegers tunnelled a giant mine into the mountain beneath them and blew it open, killing their commander.

**Location:** 38.3500, -0.4833

**Categories:** Military history, War of the Spanish Succession, History of Alicante, Castles, Sieges, 18th century

## Introduction

Somewhere in the rock beneath the castle, through the winter of 1708 and into 1709, men were digging in the dark. They were tunnelling into the flank of Mount Benacantil, the great rock that carries Alicante's Castle of Santa Barbara, and they meant to fill the tunnel with gunpowder and blow the fortress apart from below. Inside the castle, an Allied garrison under Major-General John Richards - an Irish officer serving in the Portuguese army - knew the mine was coming and could do nothing to stop it. The Siege of Alicante, one small front in a war fought across half of Europe, would be remembered for the terrible moment that tunnel went off.

## A Fortress Worth Delaying For

The War of the Spanish Succession pitted two claimants against each other for the throne of Spain: the Bourbon Philip V, backed by France, and the Habsburg archduke Charles, backed by a Grand Alliance of Britain and its partners. Alicante had changed hands with the fortunes of that war. The Allies took it in 1706, in the wake of their landing at Barcelona the year before. Then Philip's forces won a crushing victory at the Battle of Almansa in 1707 and went back on the offensive, clawing back town after town. By December 1708 they had come for Alicante, and the Allied garrison holding the castle had one real task: to hold on, and make the enemy pay in time.

## The Mine Beneath the Rock

Unable to storm the castle outright, the French and Spanish besiegers went underground. Engineers drove a mine into the body of Mount Benacantil, beneath the fortress walls, and over weeks of patient, hidden labour packed the chamber with gunpowder. For the defenders it was a slow torment - the knowledge that the very mountain under their feet was being turned into a bomb, with no way to reach the diggers. In February 1709 the mine was fired. The blast tore away wall and rock, hurling tons of stone into the air, and it killed John Richards along with many of his troops. It remains the single most notorious act of the whole siege: a fortress attacked not from the front but from within the mountain itself.

## Terms of Honour

Astonishingly, the explosion did not end the defence. The surviving garrison held out for weeks more, their commander dead and their walls breached, until they had done what they came to do - delaying the enemy through the long months of the siege. Only then did they agree to terms. The capitulation, settled in the spring of 1709, let the defenders march out with honour and be evacuated to British ships waiting offshore. Alicante passed back into Bourbon hands, one more piece restored to Philip V, while the men who had held its castle sailed away. The mountain kept the scar, and the castle of Santa Barbara still crowns the rock that the miners once tried to blow out from under it.

## A Small Front in a Wide War

Alicante was a minor episode in an enormous conflict. The War of the Spanish Succession set much of Europe fighting over who should inherit the crown of Spain, with campaigns stretching from Flanders to Italy to the Iberian coast, and the fate of a single Mediterranean port turned on the same rivalry that moved armies a thousand miles away. That is why the garrison's stubbornness mattered out of all proportion to its numbers: every week the defenders tied down Bourbon soldiers beneath their walls was a week those soldiers were not fighting somewhere else. In the end the wider war went Philip V's way. He kept the throne, and the Bourbon dynasty he founded would shape Spain for generations to come - a grand result to which the blood spilled on this one rock made its small, half-forgotten contribution.

## Flight Context

The siege centred on the Castle of Santa Barbara, atop Mount Benacantil at 38.35 degrees N, 0.483 degrees W, directly above the old town and harbour of Alicante. The castle and its 166-metre rock are a commanding landmark, visible far out to sea and from the surrounding plain - the same height the besiegers tunnelled into three centuries ago. Alicante-Elche Airport (LEAL) lies about 10 km southwest; Valencia (LEVC) is well to the north. The coast enjoys generally clear Mediterranean visibility year round.
