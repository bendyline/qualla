# Concatedral de San Nicolás, Alicante

Alicante's co-cathedral, a severe Herrerian shell crowned by a 45-meter blue dome and hiding one of Spanish Baroque's loveliest chapels inside.

**Location:** 38.3454, -0.4825

**Categories:** Churches and Cathedrals, Baroque Architecture, Religious Sites, Historic Buildings, Alicante, Valencian Community

## Introduction

Its architect had learned his craft from the man who built El Escorial, and the cathedral shows it in every plain, unornamented wall. Agustín Bernardino was a pupil of Juan de Herrera, the architect who gave Philip II's great monastery-palace its famous severity, and when Bernardino designed the Co-Cathedral of St. Nicholas of Bari in Alicante, he carried that discipline south. From the street the building is deliberately sober, all restraint and clean stone. The surprise waits inside, and above.

## A Pupil of El Escorial

Bernardino drew up the plans between 1610 and 1615, and the church rose over the following decades, built between 1613 and 1662. The Herrerian training is written across the exterior, which the sources themselves call quite sober. Even the two main facades speak in the measured grammar of classical architecture rather than ornament: the principal front on the east side follows the plain, sturdy Doric order, while the facade on the south side adopts the slightly more slender Ionic. There are no theatrics here, no writhing stone. It is the architecture of an age that equated dignity with restraint, and of a designer schooled by its master.

## The Blue Dome

Rise above the rooftops and the cathedral finally announces itself. A blue dome lifts forty-five meters over the crossing, a cool disc of color against the warm tones of the old town, and it is by far the building's most visible signature. Bernardino did not shape it alone; for the dome he turned to a local man, the Alicante-born architect Miguel del Real, who lined its interior with Roman coffers that pull the eye upward. Beneath it the plan is a Latin cross, though the transepts are kept short, so the interior reads more as a single powerful axis than a sprawling one. Six interconnecting side chapels flank the nave, and an ambulatory sweeps around the apse behind the high altar, itself a Baroque work dedicated to Saint Nicholas that warms the far end of the nave. The geometry is confident and legible, a clear idea executed without fuss.

## Baroque Behind the Grey

For all that outward austerity, the cathedral hides a jewel. Tucked beneath the dome, the Chapel of Holy Communion is built as a small temple on a Greek cross plan, compact and perfectly proportioned, and it is counted among the most beautiful examples of the Spanish Baroque anywhere. Raised in the 18th century, it is dressed in marble and stucco of vivid, shifting color, a little theater of the illusionism that Spanish craftsmen loved in that age. The contrast with the plain shell is the whole point. A visitor passes through sober grey classicism and then steps into this burst of splendor, the two moods arranged like movements in music. These buildings were designed to be experienced in sequence, and here the sequence is the drama.

## Mosque, Church, Cathedral

The story here runs deeper than the 17th-century stone. Tradition holds that Alicante changed hands on 6 December 1244, the feast day of Saint Nicholas of Bari, and that the young Infante Alfonso, the future Alfonso X the Wise, ordered a church raised to the saint on whose day the city had fallen to him. Historians now regard that tidy December date as a later invention, promoted to justify the church's own claim to co-cathedral status rather than a record of the actual, more gradual conquest of the city. That first church stood on the ground of a mosque, and when it was rebuilt in grand style centuries later one older element survived the change: the cloister, first raised in the 15th century in the Valencian Gothic style, a quiet medieval remnant now attached to the 17th-century church. The building's highest honor came much later. On 9 March 1959, Pope John XXIII elevated the church of St. Nicholas to the rank of cathedral, and today it serves as co-cathedral of the Diocese of Orihuela-Alicante, sharing that dignity with Orihuela's own cathedral. Spain recognized its worth in 1974, declaring the church a historic-artistic monument. The patron reaches far beyond the city. Saint Nicholas of Bari was a 4th-century bishop remembered across the Christian world for anonymous acts of charity, and his name has ridden with this church from mosque to parish to cathedral, keeping pace with the city around it.

## Flight Context

The Concatedral de San Nicolás stands at 38.35°N, 0.48°W in Alicante's old town, just inland from the harbor and below Mount Benacantil's Santa Bárbara Castle. Its defining feature from the air is the blue dome rising 45 meters over the crossing, a distinct cool-toned disc among the terracotta roofs of the historic center. From 2,000-4,000 feet the dome is the easiest way to pick the cathedral out of the dense street grid, with the castle-topped crag looming just to the east. Alicante-Elche Airport (LEAL) lies about 10 km southwest; Región de Murcia (LEMI) is farther south; Valencia (LEVC) sits roughly 130 km up the coast to the northeast. Clear, bright Mediterranean light makes midday and afternoon ideal for spotting the dome's color.
