# Siege of Xàtiva (1707)

After Philip V's army burned Xàtiva in 1707 and deported its people, the town took its revenge in the only way left: it still hangs the king's portrait upside down.

**Location:** 38.9903, -0.5211

**Categories:** War of the Spanish Succession, Xativa, Military History, Kingdom of Valencia, Sieges, 18th Century

## Introduction

In a small museum in Xàtiva, a portrait of a Spanish king hangs upside down on purpose, and has for three centuries. The monarch is Philip V, the first Bourbon to rule Spain, and the town's grudge against him is very specific. In the summer of 1707 his soldiers besieged Xàtiva, massacred its defenders, deported its people, and burned the city to the ground. Inverting his image is the town's long, quiet answer, a refusal to let the man who erased Xàtiva be shown the right way up in the place he tried to destroy.

## After Almansa

The siege belonged to the War of the Spanish Succession, the continental struggle over who would inherit the Spanish throne: the Bourbon Philip V or the Habsburg Archduke Charles. Much of the Crown of Aragon, Valencia included, had thrown its support behind the Habsburg cause. That gamble collapsed on 25 April 1707 at the Battle of Almansa, where a Franco-Castilian army broke the Allied forces and threw the whole Kingdom of Valencia open to Philip's reprisal. Xàtiva, a rebel town that had backed the losing side, now lay directly in the path of a victorious and unforgiving army.

## The Siege

Between 8 May and 6 June 1707 the town was invested by an overwhelming force. Somewhere between 9,000 and 11,000 Castilian and French troops, fighting for Philip of Bourbon under the command of Claude Francois Bidal d'Asfeld and Jose Antonio de Chaves Osorio, closed around Xàtiva. Facing them stood roughly 2,000 defenders, an Aragonese and British garrison led by Miguel Purroi and Josep Marco. The arithmetic left little doubt about the outcome. For nearly a month the smaller force held its walls beneath the town's hilltop castle, but numbers, artillery, and the momentum of Almansa were all against them, and in early June the defense broke.

## Exemplary Punishment

What followed was not the ordinary business of a captured town but a punishment meant to be seen. The defenders were massacred, put to death as what the victors called an example to any other town tempted to resist. The bulk of the surviving population was uprooted and deported far from home, families marched away from the streets their ancestors had lived in for generations. Then the city itself was set alight and burned. For the people of Xàtiva this was not an abstract change of dynasty; it was the deliberate unmaking of a community, carried out to teach a lesson in obedience written in fire and exile.

## San Felipe

Having destroyed the town, the crown renamed what was left. Xàtiva became San Felipe, styled a new royal colony bearing the victorious king's own name, as if the old rebellious city could be overwritten along with its buildings. The imposed name clung to the place for well over a century before the town, in time, reclaimed its true one. But the erasure had been the point. To burn a city, scatter its inhabitants, and then stamp the conqueror's name over the ashes was to try to end a place's memory of itself, and Xàtiva refused to let that stand.

## The Grilled People

The memory survived instead as identity. To this day the people of Xàtiva are nicknamed socarrats, the grilled or scorched ones, a grim badge worn in reference to the burning of 1707. It is a strange kind of civic pride, a town naming itself after the worst day in its history and refusing to let anyone forget it. And in the museum of L'Almodi hangs their steadiest act of defiance: the portrait of Philip V, deliberately inverted, the king who ordered the flames left forever hanging on his head. Generations of townspeople have kept it that way, a gesture repeated so long it has become a tradition in its own right. It is one of the more eloquent grudges in Spain, a whole town's refusal to forgive, kept not in stone or ceremony but in the simple, pointed act of turning a picture upside down and leaving it there for three hundred years.

## Flight Context

Xàtiva lies at 38.990 N, 0.521 W on the Valencian plain about 55 km south of Valencia city. The town is impossible to miss from the air: the long double-castle of the Castell de Xàtiva runs the length of the ridge that rises directly above the old quarter, the very high ground the 1707 defenders held. Recommended viewing altitude 3,500 to 6,000 ft AGL to take in the castle ridge and the town spread below it. Valencia (LEVC) is the nearest major field to the north; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies farther south. The fertile huerta around the town is generally clear, with best light and least heat haze over the ridge in the morning.
