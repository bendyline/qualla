# Xàtiva

A Valencian city that has kept its pre-Roman name for millennia, introduced paper to Europe, cradled the Borgias, and still hangs a king's portrait upside down in revenge.

**Location:** 38.9887, -0.5192

**Categories:** Cities & Towns, History, The Borgias, Castles & Fortifications, Food & Drink, Valencia

## Introduction

In a museum in Xàtiva, a portrait of a Spanish king hangs upside down. It is Philip V, and the insult is deliberate. In 1707, after the citizens sided with his rival in the War of the Spanish Succession, Philip had the town burned, its people banished, and its very name erased — Xàtiva was to be called San Felipe. The city was eventually rebuilt, but it never forgave. In 1940 a proud Valencian curator turned the king's portrait head over heels, and there it still hangs, a grudge preserved in oil and gilt frame.

## A Name Older Than Rome

Xàtiva has been lived in without interruption since Iberian times, and it is one of the few Spanish cities to have carried its pre-Roman name across the centuries. To the Iberians it was Saiti; the sound survives, worn smooth, in the modern Xàtiva. The Romans prized it for its textiles. Waves of rulers came and went — Iberian, Roman, Visigothic, Muslim, Christian — each leaving a layer in the streets. Walk the old centre today and the past is not behind glass but underfoot: narrow lanes, tiled fountains, stone thresholds smoothed by generations of feet. Few towns wear their antiquity so casually.

## Where Paper Came to Europe

Xàtiva holds a quieter claim to fame, one that reshaped the world more than any battle. It was here, under Muslim rule, that paper manufacturing took hold in Europe. The Moors established a papermaking centre in the town, and from this Valencian workshop the technology that would eventually carry books, ledgers, and letters across the continent spread outward. Long before the printing press, the raw material of literate Europe was being pressed and dried in Xàtiva. It is a strange thing to consider in such an unassuming place: the sheets that recorded a civilization began, in part, here.

## The Castle on the Ridge

Above the rooftops, a fortress sprawls along the crest of the ridge, following the stone spine of the hill for what seems an impossible distance. This is the Castell de Xàtiva, the reason the town exists where it does — a stronghold guarding the inland route between the coast and the Meseta. Its walls climb and dip with the terrain, towers punctuating the skyline, the whole complex commanding the plain below. From the battlements the view runs out over orchards and red-tiled roofs to the mountains beyond. It is the kind of dramatic, ridge-top silhouette that fixes a place in memory.

## The Borgias' Cradle

Xàtiva was the hometown of the Borja family — the Borgias, as Italy came to know and fear them. Here, in 1431, Rodrigo Borgia was born, the man who would become Pope Alexander VI, father of Cesare and Lucrezia, and one of the most scandal-shadowed figures of the Renaissance papacy. Before the poison plots and the Vatican intrigues, before the family name became shorthand for ruthless ambition, the Borjas were simply a Valencian clan from this hillside town. The city that gave the world a byword for corruption also gave it, arguably, the age's most consequential dynasty.

## Rice, Rosemary, and Seawater

Xàtiva eats well, and it eats distinctively. The town is known for its rice dishes, above all arrós al forn — rice baked in a clay pot with blood sausage and vegetables, dense and savory. For something sweeter there is arnadí, a pumpkin pie studded with almonds and pine nuts and warmed with cinnamon. To drink, two local craft beers capture the town's stubborn originality: La Socarrada, seasoned with fresh rosemary and rosemary honey, and Er Boquerón, brewed with actual Mediterranean seawater. Neither tastes like anywhere else. That, in the end, is the whole point of Xàtiva.
