# Collegiate Basilica of Xàtiva

The grand collegiate church of a modest Valencian town that gave the world two Renaissance popes — the cradle and treasury of the House of Borgia.

**Location:** 38.9881, -0.5182

**Categories:** Route of the Borgias, Roman Catholic churches in the Valencian Community, Renaissance architecture in Spain, Herrerian architecture, House of Borgia, History of Xàtiva

## Introduction

Xàtiva climbs a hillside in the Valencian interior, watched over by the long double castle strung along the ridge above it. Near the town's heart stands the church locals simply call La Seu — the See. It is a strikingly grand building for so modest a place, and there is a reason. This is the cradle of the Borgias, the Valencian family that gave the world not one but two Renaissance popes. Their name would ring through Rome as a byword for ambition and scandal, but it began here, on these streets, and the great collegiate basilica is where the hometown still keeps their memory.

## La Seu

Work on the present basilica began in 1596, in the severe, geometric manner then sweeping Spain — the Herrerian style, named for Juan de Herrera, architect of Philip II's austere palace-monastery at El Escorial. This is Renaissance building stripped of ornament: clean lines, restraint, gravity. A church like this is not raised quickly. Its imposing main facade, crowned with bronze statues of the town's two popes, was not completed until the first third of the twentieth century — more than three hundred years after the first stone was laid. La Seu presides over the square that bears its name, at the very center of the old town, its bulk rising above the tiled roofs like a promise the town spent centuries keeping. For a hill town in the Valencian interior, such ambition needed a justification, and Xàtiva had one that no rival could match.

## The First Borgia Pope

To understand why a town this size built so ambitious a church, you must follow the Borja family — Borgia, in its Italian spelling. Alfons de Borja, born near Xàtiva in 1378, was a canon lawyer and diplomat who climbed the Church's ranks until, in 1455, the cardinals elected him pope as Calixtus III. He was already elderly, and his reign lasted only three years, but it left a mark. He made recovering Constantinople from the Ottomans his first cause, and when Christian forces repelled the Turks at Belgrade in 1456 he commemorated the victory with a new feast day. He also reopened the trial of Joan of Arc and declared her innocent, a quarter-century after she was burned at the stake. And, in the fashion of his age, he lavished favors on his nephew — planting the family's foot firmly in Italy.

## The Second, and the Notorious One

That nephew was Rodrigo Borgia, born in Xàtiva on the first day of 1431. His uncle made him a cardinal, and Rodrigo climbed higher than any Borja before him: in 1492 he was elected pope as Alexander VI. Two popes, uncle and nephew, from the same Valencian streets — few places on earth can claim as much. But Alexander VI also gave the name its darker fame. As the worldly father of Cesare and Lucrezia Borgia, he became the great villain of Renaissance memory, a figure of poison, politics, and dynastic ambition retold endlessly in the centuries since. In Xàtiva, though, he is simply a local boy who wore the papal crown.

## What the Basilica Keeps

The family never forgot where it came from, and neither has La Seu. Several members of the House of Borgia are buried within its walls, and the collegiate museum guards relics of their rise: an altarpiece dedicated to Cardinal Alfons de Borja and a silver chalice inscribed with the name of Pope Calixtus III, alongside other Borgia works. To walk through it is to trace the improbable arc of a provincial family that reached the summit of Christendom, then sent fragments of that grandeur home to the church of its childhood. Today the basilica anchors the Route of the Borgias, a trail winding through the family's Valencian heartland — and proud or notorious, the Borgias remain inseparable from this hillside town. Their portraits and relics turned an ordinary parish into a shrine to worldly success, reminding every visitor that the road from a provincial pew to the throne of Saint Peter had once, improbably, begun right here.

## Flight Context

The Collegiate Basilica sits in Xàtiva's old town at roughly 38.988°N, 0.518°W, some 55 km south of the city of Valencia. From the air, the surest landmark is the great Castle of Xàtiva — a long, twin-crowned fortress stretched along the crest of the Serra Vernissa directly above the town, with the basilica and its dome anchoring the tiled streets below. Best viewed from 3,000–5,000 ft in clear light. Nearest airport is Valencia (LEVC) to the north; Alicante–Elche (LEAL) lies to the south. Inland skies here are often bright and dry, with good visibility across the surrounding orchard plains.
