# Cova Foradà

A cave near Oliva that yielded the most complete Neanderthal skeleton ever found in Iberia, and evidence of a Neanderthal using a toothpick to ease an aching, diseased jaw.

**Location:** 38.8831, -0.0907

**Categories:** Archaeology, Prehistoric sites, Neanderthal sites, Caves, Paleoanthropology, Valencian Community

## Introduction

One of the people who sheltered in this cave was in real pain. His gums were inflamed, his teeth badly worn, and the sockets had pulled away from the bone. So he did what a person in that kind of discomfort does: he found a small pointed tool and worked at the sore spots, leaving two faint grooves on the surviving teeth that survived him by tens of thousands of years. He was a Neanderthal, and his jaw is one of the oldest hints that our ancient cousins tried to treat their own aches.

## A Neanderthal with a Toothache

The evidence is a nearly complete upper jaw, a maxilla, recovered along with other fragments between 2000 and 2003. It belonged to an adult who died somewhere between 35 and 45 years old, and it tells a small, vivid story of chronic suffering. The bone shows periodontal disease and heavy wear, with a striking gap, around eight millimeters at the canine, opening between teeth and jaw. That would have hurt constantly. The two grooves polished into the sides of two teeth are read by researchers as the marks of a toothpick, used again and again against the tender gums. As one study put it, the habit of picking the teeth with a tool may count as early evidence of treatment to ease sore gums. It is a profoundly relatable image: someone in pain, reaching for whatever relief was at hand.

## The Most Complete of All

Then, in 2010, on the very spot where the jaw had emerged, excavators uncovered something rarer still: an almost complete Neanderthal skeleton, its bones still articulated along the spine. It is the most complete Neanderthal skeleton ever found on the Iberian peninsula. That completeness is precious. Most of what we know about Neanderthals comes from scattered teeth and splinters of bone; a body preserved in its own arrangement lets researchers see how a whole individual was built, moved, and aged. What is usually a puzzle of fragments here became, for once, nearly a portrait.

## Between Mountain and Lagoon

The setting explains why anyone lived here at all. Cova Forada is a karst cave about five kilometers southeast of the town of Oliva and only three and a half kilometers from the Mediterranean, tucked into a low rise among the hills known as the Muntanyetes de Oliva, where the Serra de Mustalla finally gives way to the coastal floodplain. It was, by the standards of the Stone Age, a fine address. Mountains rose on one side, wetlands and lagoons spread on the other, and between them lay a rich, varied larder of game, birds, and plants. People kept coming back for it. The cave was occupied from the Middle Paleolithic through the Mesolithic and on into the Bronze Age, a span of use measured not in centuries but in tens of thousands of years.

## Reading the Bones

Systematic excavation began in 1977, led by the archaeologist Jose Aparicio Perez for the provincial government of Valencia, and the cave has been giving up its secrets ever since. The remains are not only of adults. Among the fragments recovered was a molar from a very young child, roughly two and a half years old, found alongside the older individual. That small detail resists any temptation to treat these as mere specimens. A middle-aged man who nursed a painful jaw, a toddler who did not survive, a place returned to across immense stretches of time: Cova Forada holds not a catalog of bones but the traces of real lives, lived and ended between the mountain and the sea.

## Flight Context

Cova Forada lies at 38.883 degrees north, 0.091 degrees west, in the low Muntanyetes de Oliva hills about 5 km southeast of Oliva and only 3.5 km inland from the Costa Blanca coast. From 1,500 to 3,500 feet the coastal floodplain, the line of the beach, and the hills at the foot of the Serra de Mustalla provide clear reference points where the mountains meet the sea. The nearest major airport is Valencia (LEVC), roughly 70 km to the north; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies farther south and Murcia (LEMI) beyond it. Coastal visibility is usually good, with occasional afternoon haze over the shoreline in summer.
