# Siege of Elche

In 1332 the frontier town of Elche became one flashpoint in the Battle of the Strait, the decades-long struggle among Christian kingdoms, Granada, and Morocco for the edge of al-Andalus.

**Location:** 38.2669, -0.6983

**Categories:** Sieges of the Reconquista, Medieval History, Battle of the Strait, Conflicts in 1332, Valencian Community

## Introduction

In 1306 the council of Guardamar wrote to James II of Aragon with an uncomfortable admission. Whenever raiders planned a strike on their lands, the townsfolk said, they assumed Guardamar itself would be the target, because its castle and wall were the weakest along the frontier. It was the letter of a community that knew exactly how exposed it was. A generation later, in the campaigns that swept the southern Valencian border in the early 1330s, that fear proved entirely justified, and the siege of Elche of 1332 became one episode in a war for the very edge of Christian and Muslim Iberia.

## A Border Barely Drawn

The frontier the raiders tested was startlingly young. Elche had belonged to the Crown of Aragon for barely a generation. Between 1296 and 1300, James II of Aragon pushed south into the Kingdom of Murcia, long a dependency of Castile, and the Treaty of Torrellas in 1304 divided the spoils: Orihuela, Elche, Elda, and Alicante passed to the Kingdom of Valencia, while the greater part of Murcia reverted to Castile. The Treaty of Elche the following year adjusted the line once more, sending the port of Cartagena back to Castilian hands. So when a Granadan army came north in the 1330s, it struck a border only recently drawn and never fully settled, defended by towns like Guardamar that already understood how thin their walls really were.

## Crusade and Truce

The wider conflict was the Battle of the Strait, the long contest for control of the waters between Iberia and Africa. In 1329 the forces of Granada recovered Algeciras, and early the next year Pope John XXII granted three years of church tithes to the kings of Aragon, Castile, and Portugal, on the condition that each campaign against the Emirate of Granada in person. Alfonso XI of Castile pressed the western front while Catalan and Valencian raiders struck the Murcian border. Then, on 19 February 1331, a truce ended Alfonso's campaign. Relieved of pressure in the west, Granada was suddenly free to concentrate its full strength somewhere else.

## The Army Turns East

That strength was considerable. The emirate massed roughly twenty-five hundred horsemen and twelve thousand on foot under the command of Abu-l-Nuaym Ridwan ibn Abd Allah, drew reinforcements from Morocco, and pointed the whole force at the Crown of Aragon's southern frontier. From their base at Algeciras the Granadans took Gibraltar and pressed north. In October 1331 Ridwan's army fell on Guardamar, the very town that had named itself the weak point, and carried off fifteen hundred captives. Another fifteen hundred Muslims from the Elda valley left with the army as well, some compelled and some by choice, to resettle within the Emirate of Granada. Behind the tally of numbers were three thousand people uprooted in a single season, families pulled from homes they would likely never see again.

## The Frontier Answers

Elche stood squarely in the path of this campaign, and in 1332 it too came under siege. Frontier lords could not let the blow go unanswered. In the Orihuela district, the royal procurator's spokesman Jofré Gilabert de Cruïlles, the Montesa commander Dalmau de Cruïlles, the powerful nobleman Don Juan Manuel, and the bishop of Murcia agreed to strike back together. Don Juan Manuel is remembered today as much for his pen as his sword. Grandson of Ferdinand III and nephew of Alfonso X the Wise, Lord of Villena, he was one of medieval Castile's finest prose writers; within a few years of these campaigns he would finish El Conde Lucanor, a collection of fifty moral tales still counted among the masterpieces of early Spanish literature. Yet on this raw border he was simply a war leader. At the end of August 1333 the combined force set out from Lorca into Muslim territory, another raid in a rhythm of raid and counter-raid that would grind on for years.

## A War Without a Clean End

No neat victory closed the immediate account. After withdrawing from the Valencian frontier, the Granadans, reinforced by the Marinid dynasty of Morocco, turned back and besieged Gibraltar again, taking it once more. The Battle of the Strait was like that: fortresses changing hands, columns of captives marched south and north, treaties signed and broken across a border that was less a line than a wide, dangerous zone. Resolution came only years later, and far to the west. In 1340 a combined Castilian and Portuguese army shattered the Marinids and Granada at the Río Salado near Tarifa, the last time a North African army would invade the peninsula in force, and in 1344, after a twenty-one-month siege, Alfonso XI took Algeciras with Aragonese and Genoese fleets at his back. Control of the strait passed for good into Christian hands. But for the people of Elche, Guardamar, and the Elda valley in the 1330s, none of that was yet imaginable. The grand strategy of popes and kings reached them as something far more immediate: the sight of an army on the horizon, and the question of whether the walls would hold.

## Flight Context

Elche (Elx) lies at 38.27°N, 0.70°W on the southern Valencian frontier, about 20 km southwest of Alicante. From 3,000-5,000 feet the town's most striking feature is not medieval fortification but the Palmeral, the vast historic palm grove that surrounds the old center in a sea of dark green fronds unlike anything else on the coast. Alicante-Elche Airport (LEAL) sits about 10 km east of the town, making low approaches routine; Región de Murcia (LEMI) lies to the southwest, toward Lorca and the old Murcian frontier the campaigns crossed. Dry, clear inland air gives excellent visibility, sharpest in the morning.
