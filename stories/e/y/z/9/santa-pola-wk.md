# Santa Pola

A Costa Blanca fishing town where a 16th-century anti-pirate fortress, a Roman harbor, flamingo-filled salt pans, and a six-million-year-old fossil reef meet the working Mediterranean.

**Location:** 38.1897, -0.5556

**Categories:** Coastal Towns, Costa Blanca, Fishing Ports, Historic Fortifications, Nature Reserves, Valencian Community

## Introduction

The fortress sits square in the middle of town, at sea level, its thick walls enclosing a courtyard where a garrison once lived with their wives and children. Santa Pola raised it in 1557 not for a king's vanity but out of fear. Barbary corsairs prowled this stretch of the Spanish Mediterranean, landing to loot villages and carry the survivors into slavery, and along a coast with few defenses, a stone box mounted with cannon was the only reliable answer. Today the same walls shelter a market and a museum, and the sea beyond them carries ferries instead of raiders.

## Stone Against the Corsairs

Bernardino de Cárdenas, Viceroy of Valencia, ordered the castle built, and Italian military engineers gave it the clean geometry of the Renaissance: four straight walls, corner bastions, a central parade ground. The garrison was small and permanent, roughly three dozen souls in all. A mayor, his second lieutenant, thirty soldiers, and a chaplain lived inside with their families, watching the horizon for sails. For the fishing communities scattered along this coast, the fortress was less a monument than a promise. When the alarm sounded, there was somewhere to run. The building survives largely intact, one of the best-preserved coastal fortresses in the region, and its rooms now hold the Museo del Mar, a museum of maritime archaeology, fishing, and fortification. The courtyard hosts concerts and exhibitions rather than musters, but the walls still remember what they were for.

## The Older Harbor

Long before the corsairs, this was a Roman anchorage. In the first century AD, the harbor of Portus Illicitanus rose here to serve the inland city of Illici, today's Elche, giving that thriving town its window on the sea. Ships loaded and unloaded alongside Cartagena's traffic in a busy Mediterranean trade, and the port grew into something like a small city of its own, complete with warehouses, a necropolis, and a factory for salting fish. That last detail is telling. Two thousand years ago and today, the wealth of Santa Pola has come from the same two sources: what the sea gives up, and the salt that preserves it.

## A Coast of Salt and Birds

Just inland, the sea is coaxed into shallow pans and left to evaporate under the fierce Levantine sun, leaving crystallized salt behind. These salinas are no mere industrial curiosity. The salt flats form a natural park of some 2,470 hectares and a protected zone for birds, and the same brackish water that yields salt also draws flocks of waders. Ducks and gulls work the margins, and greater flamingos stand in the pink-tinged shallows, filtering the water for the tiny organisms that tint both the brine and the birds themselves. The 16th-century Torre del Tamarit still stands among the lagoons, a watchtower that once guarded not the sea but the salt, signaling inland to the towers of the Elche countryside. Seen from a low pass overhead, the pans read as a checkerboard of rose, rust, and white, geometry laid across a marsh.

## The Cape and the Ancient Reef

South of the harbor the land lifts into the Sierra de Santa Pola, a low limestone cape that ends in cliffs above the water. It hides something remarkable. The rock itself is a fossil coral reef, built roughly six million years ago in the Messinian age, when the Mediterranean shrank toward crisis and its warm shallows filled with living coral. Geologists count it among the most important geosites in the western Mediterranean, a rare atoll-type reef now stranded on dry land, its ancient Porites colonies and algal mats still legible in the stone. Atop the cape, around 140 meters above the sea, a lighthouse marks the headland where an old watchtower, the Atalayola, once scanned for corsairs. From the viewpoint beside it, the whole coast opens out: the town, the pans, and the flat blue run of water toward the island offshore.

## The Working Sea

Santa Pola remains, above all, a fishing town, and the honesty of that shows on every plate. The port still lands its catch, and finding excellent seafood here takes no effort at all, though the kitchens keep looser hours once the summer crowds thin. The beaches carry the blue flag, the European Foundation for Environmental Education's mark of clean water and good stewardship, so the town balances its trawlers against its sunbathers with practiced ease. From the harbor, ferries slip out every hour toward Tabarca, the little island riding the horizon to the southeast, carrying day-trippers across water the Romans, the corsairs, and the salt-makers all knew well.

## Flight Context

Santa Pola sits at 38.19°N, 0.56°W on the Costa Blanca, about 20 km south of Alicante. From 3,000-5,000 feet the square Renaissance fortress anchors the town center, but two landmarks stand out: the salinas to the west and southwest, a geometric mosaic of rose, rust, and white evaporation pans that no natural feature mimics, and the cliffed Sierra de Santa Pola to the south, capped by its lighthouse around 140 m above the sea. Offshore to the southeast, the low island of Tabarca marks the horizon. Alicante-Elche Airport (LEAL) lies roughly 15 km northwest, a 15-minute drive; Región de Murcia Airport (LEMI) sits to the southwest. Clear, dry Levantine air gives long visibility, best in morning light before afternoon sea haze builds.
