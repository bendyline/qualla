# Ciudad de la Luz

A vast film studio built on the Alicante coast to lure Hollywood with Spanish sunshine, switched off by a European ruling on illegal subsidies, then powered back to life a decade later.

**Location:** 38.3089, -0.5293

**Categories:** Film, Culture, Modern History, Architecture, Spain

## Introduction

The light in the name was never a metaphor. Alicante enjoys some of the most generous sunshine in Europe, hundreds of clear days a year, and light is the one raw material every filmmaker needs and none can manufacture. So when the regional government of Valencia set out in the 2000s to build a film studio that could pull international productions to Spain, they built it here on the coast, and they called it Ciudad de la Luz, the City of Light. For seven years the cameras rolled. Then, in a courtroom sense, someone found the switch and turned the whole city off.

## A City Built for Light

Ciudad de la Luz opened in 2005 as one of the largest and most modern film complexes in Europe, a purpose-built town of soundstages, workshops, and backlots spread across the flat coastal land south of Alicante. Its centerpiece was a set of enormous sound stages and, crucially, a giant outdoor water tank, the kind of facility that lets a production drown a street or stage a shipwreck without ever leaving dry Spain. The pitch was simple and seductive: world-class stages, a deep well of local crew and technicians, and a climate that hands directors reliable sunlight almost year-round. Between opening and its abrupt end, roughly sixty films were made within its walls.

## The Films That Came

The talent that passed through was real and the work often ambitious. Francis Ford Coppola shot his family drama Tetro here. The Alicante stages hosted Manolete, the story of Spain's legendary bullfighter, with Adrien Brody and Penélope Cruz, and the sprawling comedy Asterix at the Olympic Games, with Gérard Depardieu. Most memorably, the studio's great water tank became the sea itself for The Impossible, Juan Antonio Bayona's harrowing film about a family caught in the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami. The wall of water that tears a family apart on screen was engineered here, on the calm Alicante shore, proof that the City of Light could conjure catastrophe as convincingly as any studio in the world.

## The Bill from Brussels

Ambition on this scale needs money, and the money came from the public purse. That proved to be the fatal flaw. In May 2012, the European Commission ruled that the subsidies the studio had received from the regional government amounted to illegal state aid under European competition law, and it ordered Ciudad de la Luz to repay 265 million euros. A studio built to compete could not survive being told its competitive edge was against the rules. In October 2012 the complex was shut down and prepared for sale. Projects in the pipeline simply evaporated, among them a historical epic called King Conqueror, planned with Tim Roth in the cast, which economic troubles killed before a frame was shot.

## The Light Returns

A place this large does not vanish just because its purpose does. Rather than sell, the government kept the facilities and pressed them into other service through the lean years, sheltering digital startups and, during the pandemic, operating as a mass COVID vaccination center, its film stages filled with people in line instead of crews on set. The reprieve came in March 2022, when the European Union at last lifted its restrictions and cleared the studio to host film shoots again from that July. After a decade dark, the City of Light switched back on, courting productions once more and floating plans to house operations as significant as a hub for the broadcaster Euronews.

## Flight Context

Ciudad de la Luz sits at 38.309°N, 0.529°W on the coastal plain just south of the city of Alicante, close to the shoreline of the Costa Blanca. From the air, look for the distinctive cluster of large, pale, rectangular soundstage buildings and backlot yards set apart on open ground near the sea, an unusually geometric complex against the surrounding coast. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-6,000 ft AGL. The studio lies remarkably close to its own airport: Alicante-Elche (LEAL) is only a few kilometers away, so the site is often visible on approach or departure. Región de Murcia International (LEMI) lies farther south. Alicante's celebrated clear light means frequent excellent visibility, fitting for a place named for it.
