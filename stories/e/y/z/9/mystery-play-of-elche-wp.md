# Mystery Play of Elche

For more than five centuries, the city of Elche has staged a sung medieval drama of the Virgin's death and assumption inside its basilica, complete with angels who descend from the dome on flying machines.

**Location:** 38.2669, -0.6983

**Categories:** Culture, Religion, Music, History, World Heritage, Spain

## Introduction

Every August, before the show can go on, someone in Elche has to find children who do not get dizzy. The reason is unusual: this play sends them flying. In the ritual called la prova de l'àngel, the test of the angel, organizers gather children inside the Basilica of Santa María and take them aloft on the play's aerial machinery, watching for those who can be lifted toward the dome and lowered again without fear or nausea. Only the steadiest are cast. The Misteri d'Elx, the Mystery Play of Elche, is a sung liturgical drama from the Middle Ages, still performed in the same church on the same two days each summer, and it remains one of the last living examples of a tradition most of Europe abandoned centuries ago.

## The Whole Church Becomes a Stage

There is no separate theater. The Misteri turns the entire Basilica into its set. A great sheet of linen stretched across the base of the dome serves as Heaven, both painting the sky and hiding the winches and ropes behind it, and at its center a square opening, the Door to Heaven, is thrown open three times during the performance to let the flying machines pass. Down the main aisle runs a long ramp, the Corridor, the single element that binds the whole building into one dramatic frame, and beneath the dome sits the Cadafal, the wooden platform that stands for Mary's own house and tomb. Selecting the singers has its own ceremony too, la prova de veus, the test of voices, held not in the church but in the civic council chamber, a quiet reminder that the city, not the Church, pays for and owns this play.

## Machines from Heaven

The flying devices are the drama's astonishment. First comes la Magrana, the pomegranate, a segmented globe that opens as it descends, carrying an angel who brings Mary a golden palm frond and shows her the road to Heaven, a fitting emblem in a city that lives among palms. Later the Araceli descends, a cloud-like structure bearing five angels who gather up the Virgin's soul, shown as a small statuette, and lift it heavenward. In the finale, the image of Mary herself rises through the Door to Heaven and is crowned by the Holy Trinity halfway up her ascent. Some of this machinery is astonishingly old: the great linen is referenced in stage directions as early as 1530, and the pomegranate in its present form dates to the second half of the sixteenth century.

## Two Days in August

The play unfolds in two acts on the eve and feast of the Assumption. On 14 August comes La Vesprà, the Vigil, which tells of the Virgin's final hours and her Dormition, her soul carried up while the grieving Apostles gather around her body. The next day, 15 August, brings La Festa, the Feast, covering her burial, her Assumption, and her Coronation. It opens at ten in the morning with El Soterrar, the Interment, a procession that spills out of the church into the streets, the image of the dead Virgin borne through Elche while bands play the melodies of the drama. The music, preserved in a manuscript called the Consueta, has been handed down through generations of the city's own voices, the role of Mary traditionally sung by a boy.

## How Old Is the Mystery?

Elche loves to date its play to the very moment of its founding as a Christian town. Legend holds that in May 1266, not long after James I the Conqueror took the city, an ark washed ashore on the local beach carrying both an image of the Virgin of the Assumption and the script of the play itself. It is a beautiful story, and almost certainly a later invention; a claimed 1266 authorizing letter was said to exist but has never been found. Most scholars now place the play's composition in the latter half of the fifteenth century. Either way, its endurance is extraordinary. UNESCO recognized the Misteri in 2001 as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity and inscribed it on the Representative List in 2008, a living medieval drama that never stopped being performed.

## An Uncomfortable Inheritance

A tradition this old carries the prejudices of the age that made it. The Misteri includes hostile medieval depictions of Jews, and the play has drawn criticism for keeping that material intact. The scholar David Nirenberg has asked whether Elche should follow the example of the Oberammergau Passion Play in Germany, whose performers revised their most antisemitic passages, and noted that when the Misteri was first streamed on Facebook in 2019 its content ran against the platform's rules on hate speech. It is an honest tension worth naming: a work of genuine beauty and deep local meaning that also preserves a way of portraying Jewish people the modern world has rightly left behind.

## Flight Context

The Mystery Play is performed in the Basilica of Santa María in the heart of Elche, at 38.267°N, 0.698°W, in southern Alicante province. From the air, the basilica's large blue dome is a clear landmark rising from the old center of the city, itself set within the vast green canopy of the Palmeral, Europe's only great date-palm grove. The Vinalopó River threads through the town nearby. Recommended viewing altitude 2,500-5,000 ft AGL to pick out the domed basilica amid the surrounding palms. Alicante-Elche Airport (LEAL) lies just a few kilometers east, so the city and its dome are often visible on approach; Región de Murcia International (LEMI) is to the southwest. The region's clear light offers frequent excellent visibility, especially in the dry heat of the August festival season.
