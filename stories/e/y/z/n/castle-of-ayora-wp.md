# Castle of Ayora

A Muslim-built fortress above a Valencian border town that changed crowns by treaty three times before Philip V's army burned it to ruin.

**Location:** 39.0583, -1.0554

**Categories:** Castles, Reconquista, Province of Valencia, Medieval History, Bien de Interes Cultural, Ruins

## Introduction

Three times in less than a century, the town of Ayora changed rulers not on a battlefield but at a negotiating table. A border sat almost on top of it, the shifting seam between Castile and Aragon, and the great fortress on the rock above the houses was the prize each side wrote into its treaties. The walls that watched all that diplomacy still crown the hill at 552 meters, half-fallen now, their square keep still dominant over the rooftops of a town that learned early how quickly a signature could redraw a life.

## Named by an Arab Geographer

The oldest written trace of the castle comes from the pen of Muhammad al-Idrisi, the Muslim geographer who noted it in passing in the twelfth century, when this was Andalusi ground. What stands today is largely a thirteenth-century rebuild, raised on the older Arabian structure after Aragonese troops recovered the Valle de Cofrentes between 1239 and 1243. The builders did not start from nothing. Among the masonry and cut ashlar of the later fortress, sections of rammed earth still survive from those earlier Muslim walls, the two eras pressed together in the same stone, one civilization's foundations carrying the next.

## Passed by Treaty

Ayora spent the thirteenth century being traded between crowns like a disputed inheritance. The Treaty of Almizra in 1244, signed between Castile and Aragon, handed the town to the Castilians. In 1281 the Treaty of Campillo, agreed by Alfonso X the Wise of Castile and Peter III the Great of Aragon, returned it to Aragon as war reparation. Finally, in 1305, James II the Just folded Ayora back into the Kingdom of Valencia by the Treaty of Elche. Centuries later a harsher decree fell on the valley: the expulsion of the Moriscos in 1609, which drove out the region's Muslim-descended population after they rose against the crown and left the countryside all but empty of the people who had farmed it.

## The Marchioness and Her False Door

For much of the late Middle Ages the fortress was a feudal manor, held by the Aragonese admiral Bernardo de Sarria. In 1492 it was bought by Rodrigo Diaz de Vivar y Mendoza, son of Cardinal Mendoza and the first Marquis of Cenete. His daughter, Mencia de Mendoza, Marchioness of Cenete, inherited it; a Renaissance patron born in Jadraque in 1508 and dead in Valencia in 1554, she left her mark on the walls. It was she who commissioned the Puerta Falsa, the so-called False Door, a graceful entrance of reddish limestone ashlars framed by an alfiz and once crowned with her coat of arms. When she died without children the manor passed to her sister Maria and, through marriage, to the Dukes of the Infantado, who held it until feudal titles were abolished in 1837.

## Assault and Ruin

The castle's long decline began violently. In 1707, during the War of the Spanish Succession, the troops of Philip V under the Count of Pinto took Ayora by assault, looting and burning both the town and the fortress until its people abandoned it. The wreck never recovered. In 1797 the botanist Antonio Jose Cavanilles, touring the Kingdom of Valencia, recorded it simply as a ruined and destroyed place. What little remained standing was finished off in 1812, when French forces occupied the valley during the Napoleonic wars and reduced the last defensible stone. Since 2006 the site has carried official protection as a Bien de Interes Cultural, guarded now against neglect rather than armies.

## Reading the Stones

Even ruined, the plan is legible. A four-sided keep rises from the middle of the compound, deliberately placed to command the land in every direction. Two enclosures survive: the larger Plaza de Armas, paved with pebbles laid in geometric patterns, and a smaller southern courtyard thought to belong to the earlier palace of the Marchioness. Ramparts once ran roughly 900 meters around the whole, standing six meters high, studded with square towers and one semicircular bastion. Above the False Door, two stone corbels still jut where a machicolation once let defenders drop stones on anyone attacking the gate, a small, practical detail of a fortress built for a border that no longer exists.

## Flight Context

The Castle of Ayora sits at 39.058 N, 1.055 W, crowning a rocky hill at 552 m in the centre of Ayora town, deep in the interior Valencian highlands west of the coastal plain. Look for the elongated ruin and its square central keep dominating the townscape, with the surrounding sierra of the Valle de Ayora-Cofrentes as backdrop. Recommended viewing altitude 3,500 to 5,500 ft AGL for the fortress profile against the town grid. Valencia (LEVC) lies roughly 90 km east-northeast; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) and Murcia (LEMI) are farther to the southeast. This inland basin offers strong visibility in dry weather but can develop haze and thermal turbulence over the sierra by midday.
