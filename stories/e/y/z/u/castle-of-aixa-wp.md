# Castle of Aixa

A ruined Andalusi hilltop castle above the Vall de Pop, where almost nothing survives but a commanding view that explains why anyone built here at all.

**Location:** 38.7645, -0.0011

**Categories:** Castles & Fortifications, History, Al-Andalus, Archaeology, Alicante

## Introduction

There is almost nothing left of the Castle of Aixa — a few courses of masonry, the stumps of two toppled towers, a geodesic marker planted where the walls once stood. Yet climb to the summit of the Serra del Castell de la Solana, some 600 meters up, and the reason for the whole thing becomes obvious in a single turn of the head. The entire Vall de Pop lies open below you: the valley floor, the scattered villages, the passes in and out. Whoever held this ridge held the valley. The stones are gone. The logic of the place remains.

## The Hisn on the Heights

The castle was built in the early 12th century, in the Andalusi era, when Muslim rule shaped these mountains and the communities within them. It was a hisn — the Arabic term for a fortified refuge-castle — and it served as the administrative head of the district. Beneath its protection lay a cluster of alquerías, the small farming hamlets that worked the terraced slopes. From this summit its garrison watched over what are now the towns of Alcalalí, Llíber and Xaló. Even the name declares its origins: Aixa is a Valencian echo of the Arabic Aisha, one of countless place-names in this comarca that still carry the sound of Al-Andalus.

## Older Than the Castle

The Muslims who raised these walls were not the first to grasp the value of the peak. The same rocks, the same commanding crest, had drawn the Iberians long before — the pre-Roman people who ranged across eastern Spain and who chose high, defensible ground for the same reasons any later builder would. A hilltop that controls a valley controls it in every century. Aixa is really a single idea, occupied and reoccupied: whoever wants the Vall de Pop must first take the ridge that looks down on it.

## Water on a Waterless Summit

What survives of the fortress is spare but telling. Its rectangular tower measured roughly twelve meters by four, its masonry reinforced with cut ashlar at the corners. Most striking are three shallow pools and a fountain worked into the ruins. On an exposed summit hundreds of meters above the nearest stream, water was the difference between a castle that could hold out and one that could not. Those pools were not decoration. They were survival — the means to shelter people and provisions through a siege, or to give refuge to the valley's farmers when raiders came through the passes below.

## The Valley Changes Hands

The Christian conquest reached these mountains in the 13th century. The armies of James I of Aragon took the district, and in 1268 Alcalalí was granted to a noblewoman, doña Berenguela. As across much of the Valencian interior, the Muslim inhabitants were largely allowed to stay and keep working their land, their world continuing beneath new lords. But a hilltop hisn built to administer an Andalusi valley had lost its purpose. Slowly the towers came down, the roofs fell in, and the garrison's careful waterworks filled with rubble and rain.

## What the Ridge Sees Now

Today the ruins sit almost erased, near the geodesic marker at the highest point of the range. The name still causes confusion — the ancient Castell d'Aixa, also called Castell de la Solana, shares its title with a modern country house down in Alcalalí. But the view has only grown richer. The Vall de Pop below is now celebrated wine country, its floor quilted with the muscatel vines and old terraces of the Xaló valley. Where a Muslim garrison once scanned the passes for threats, visitors now climb simply to stand where the whole valley opens at their feet.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 38.7645°N, 0.0011°W, atop the Serra del Castell de la Solana in the Marina Alta of Alicante province, inland from the Costa Blanca. The summit sits near 600 m (about 2,000 ft), so fly ridge-level at 3,000–4,500 ft to read the valley layout. Navigate by the Vall de Pop itself — the villages of Alcalalí, Xaló and Llíber ring the valley floor, with the Mediterranean roughly 15 km east near Dénia and Xàbia. Nearest major airport: Alicante–Elche (LEAL) to the south; Valencia (LEVC) lies farther north. Coastal haze can soften the inland ridges on humid summer afternoons.
