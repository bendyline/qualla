# Biar

A small, remarkably intact medieval town in northern Alicante where two Christian kingdoms once drew the border between them, its name a memory of the wells that made life here possible.

**Location:** 38.6331, -0.7667

**Categories:** Towns in Spain, Medieval towns, Valencian Community, Alicante Province, Historic sites in Spain, Reconquista

## Introduction

In 1244 two rulers sat down at nearby Almizra and drew a line across a peninsula they were still in the middle of conquering, and the line started here. The Treaty of Almizra, signed by James I of Aragon and the Castilian prince who would become Alfonso X, fixed the southern boundary of the young Kingdom of Valencia along a frontier that ran from Biar toward the coast. Everything on one side would be Aragonese and Valencian; everything on the other, Castilian. For a small town wrapped around a hill in what is now northern Alicante province, it was a strange kind of fame: to be the point where a map was decided.

## The Line Began Here

Biar's importance was geographic before it was anything else. When the Christian reconquista swept through in the 13th century, the town and its castle became a border post on the frontier between the crowns of Castile and of Aragon and Valencia, two Christian powers that had just spent decades fighting Muslim rulers and now turned to the awkward business of dividing the spoils between themselves. The Almizra settlement drew that dividing line through this exact ground, running from Biar to the sea. To stand at Biar was to stand at the edge of two kingdoms at once, and the town spent generations as a watchful frontier outpost rather than a place anyone simply passed through.

## A Town Named for Water

The name itself reaches back further than the border. Biar most likely comes from the Arabic word bi'ar, meaning wells, the plain, essential fact of water in a dry land. People had lived on this spot since prehistoric times, but the town you walk through today took its shape during the Moorish period, and that inheritance shows in the tight, climbing weave of its streets. It is counted among the better-preserved medieval towns not just of Alicante province but of the whole Valencian Community, a place where the layout of centuries ago is still legible underfoot rather than buried under later rebuilding.

## Under the Castle's Eye

Crowning the town is its monumental Arabic castle, the reason many visitors come and the feature that anchors Biar's medieval character. From the fortress the eye takes in the whole compact settlement below and the country beyond, the same commanding view that made this hill worth fortifying and worth fighting over when the border ran past its walls. The castle and the town grew up together, defender and defended, and their survival side by side is what gives Biar its unusual completeness, a frontier stronghold and the community it sheltered, both still standing.

## Olives and Quiet

The wars are long over, and modern Biar earns its living mostly from olive oil, with tourism a growing second. It remains genuinely small, the kind of town best explored entirely on foot. Getting here still takes a little effort, which is part of its charm: a single weekday bus links it to Villena, or a short ten-minute taxi ride covers the same distance for a handful of euros. Once you arrive, though, the appeal is exactly that quiet, a medieval town that history made briefly momentous and then left, mercifully, to its olive groves and its wells.

## Flight Context

Biar sits at 38.63 degrees N, 0.77 degrees W, on a hill in the northwest of Alicante province, just east of Villena. The clearest landmark from the air is the Arabic castle crowning the compact town, with olive groves and the ridges of the surrounding sierras framing it and the inland corridor that once formed the Castile-Aragon frontier running nearby. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 4,000 feet above the terrain shows the town, its hilltop castle, and the pass-country around it together. The nearest major airport is Alicante-Elche (LEAL) to the southeast; Valencia (LEVC) lies to the north. The dry inland climate keeps skies clear and visibility long for much of the year.
