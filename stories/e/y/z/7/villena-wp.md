# Villena

A crossroads city in the hills of inland Alicante where a single archaeologist unearthed two Bronze Age gold hoards in one astonishing year.

**Location:** 38.6350, -0.8658

**Categories:** Cities and Towns, Archaeology, History, Castles, Festivals, Spain

## Introduction

In 1963, a postal worker turned self-taught archaeologist named José María Soler García had two of the best years of luck an Iberian digger has ever recorded, and both fell inside the same twelve months. That spring, his team uncovered a hoard of golden jewelry on a hill outside Villena. That December, five kilometers east of town in a dry ravine, they found something far larger: a trove of ancient gold so significant that, on the entire continent, only the royal graves of Mycenae in Greece surpass it. Both treasures now sit in a museum that bears his name, in a hill town most travelers speed past on the road between Madrid and the sea.

## The Baker's Ravine

The Treasure of Villena did not come from a tomb or a temple. It came from the Rambla del Panadero, the "baker's ravine," a seasonal watercourse in the flatlands east of town, where its ancient owners buried it and never returned. When Soler's excavation brought it into the light in December 1963, it became the most important ancient treasure ever found on the Iberian Peninsula, and the second-largest in Europe. Archaeologists believe the people who hid it lived at Cabezo Redondo, the Bronze Age settlement on a nearby hill, judging by how closely it resembles the smaller gold hoard Soler had already found there that spring. Both collections are the crown of the Archaeological Museum "José María Soler" today, the reason a specialist will travel to Villena on purpose.

## The Hill That Worked in Gold

Two kilometers from the modern town rises Cabezo Redondo, a hill that was a regional center of the Bronze Age between roughly 1500 and 1100 BC. Its inhabitants, probably tied to the Argaric culture that spread across southeastern Iberia, were among the early masters of metallurgy on the peninsula. The 1963 spring hoard found there held thirty-five pieces of jewelry, a tiara among them, along with finger rings, bracelets, and pendants. Long before Rome laid a single stone in Spain, then, this corner of the interior was a place where people smelted, hammered, and hoarded precious metal, and where enough wealth accumulated that some of it had to be hidden and, in the end, lost.

## A Corridor Between Worlds

Villena owes its long importance to geography. The town sits in a natural gap in the hills, the Vinalopó corridor, where the river of that name threads between the coastal plains of Valencia and the high central Meseta. The Roman Via Augusta ran through here on its way inland, and for centuries this was the funnel through which armies, merchants, and pilgrims passed. Under Muslim rule the town was called Medina Bilyana and counted among the seven cities named in the treaty that Theodemir, a Visigothic lord, signed with his Arab conquerors in the eighth century. Whoever held Villena held a door between kingdoms, which is exactly why Castile and Aragon spent the thirteenth century arguing over it and signing treaty after treaty to settle who owned the pass.

## Castle, Marquis, and Revolt

Above the old quarter stands the Castle of la Atalaya, raised by Muslim builders in the eleventh century and layered over by later lords. After the Christian conquest, Villena became the seat of a domain that climbed the whole ladder of feudal titles, from seigneury to duchy to principality to marquisate, its most famous early lord being the writer-prince Don Juan Manuel. Power like that eventually chafes. Encouraged by Ferdinand and Isabella, the townspeople rose against their own marquis, and in 1525 the emperor Charles V rewarded Villena with the formal rank of City. The prosperity of those years still shows in the stone churches, palaces, and squares that fill the historic center, including the late-Gothic Church of Santiago.

## Drums, Guitars, and Aces

Villena still knows how to fill its streets. Its Moors and Christians festival, held each September, is the most heavily attended in all of Spain, thousands marching in the costumes of two faiths that once fought over this very ground. Come August, the town swings the other way and hosts Leyendas del Rock, one of the country's largest heavy-metal gatherings. And in the quieter world of sport, this is the home of Juan Carlos Ferrero, a former world number-one, whose tennis academy on the edge of town has trained a generation of champions, the young Carlos Alcaraz among them. Composer Ruperto Chapí, who gave Spanish operetta some of its best-loved music, was a son of Villena too.

## Flight Context

Villena lies at 38.635°N, 0.866°W, in the inland hills of northwest Alicante province. From the air, look for the town wrapped around a low hill crowned by the square keep of Atalaya Castle, with the ruined Salvatierra Castle on the Sierra de la Villa ridge to the north and the broad, dry basin of the old Villena lagoon on the plain below. The Vinalopó corridor cuts a clear diagonal gap between mountain ranges, carrying the A-31 motorway and the high-speed rail line toward the coast. Recommended viewing altitude 6,000-9,000 ft AGL for the town-and-castle composition. Nearest major airport is Alicante-Elche (LEAL), about 55 km southeast; Región de Murcia International (LEMI) lies southwest and Valencia (LEVC) to the northeast. The dry interior climate offers frequent clear visibility, best in the raking light of early morning or late afternoon.
