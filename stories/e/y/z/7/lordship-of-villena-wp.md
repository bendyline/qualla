# Lordship of Villena

A medieval feudal realm that grew so powerful its lord minted his own coins and kept a thousand knights, ruling a private buffer state wedged between the crowns of Castile and Aragon.

**Location:** 38.6350, -0.8658

**Categories:** History of Spain, Medieval history, Kingdom of Castile, Feudal states, Villena, Nobility of Spain

## Introduction

He minted his own money and kept a personal army of more than a thousand knights. Don Juan Manuel, lord of Villena, was never a king, yet by the mid-14th century he ruled his sprawling domain with an autonomy that rivaled the actual kingdoms around it, playing the great crowns of Castile and Aragon off each other from the ground caught precisely between them. The Lordship of Villena was, in effect, a small state that behaved like a large one, and for a few remarkable decades one ambitious nobleman made it almost impossible to tell the difference.

## A Realm Between Two Crowns

Geography made Villena important before any lord did. The feudal state sat inside the kingdom of Castile, bordered to the north by Cuenca and to the south by the city of Murcia, and from its beginnings it functioned as a buffer between the Crown of Castile and the Crown of Aragon. It began modestly, gathering in the coastal country around Elche and the whole valley of the Vinalopo River, then swelled until it covered good portions of what are now five Spanish provinces: Alicante, Albacete, Murcia, Valencia, and Cuenca. In time it organized itself around two political centers, the Land of Alarcon in the north and the Land of Chinchilla in the south. Villena itself, oddly, sat near the edge of its own lordship, lending its name to a territory whose heart lay elsewhere.

## The Land of Don Manuel

The lordship had a double origin, and both threads ran through one family. A cluster of towns, Jorquera, Hellin, Ves, Tobarra, Almansa, Yecla, Sax, and Villena, belonged to the Infante Manuel of Castile, a king's son, and so the region came to be called simply the Land of don Manuel. The other thread, the Land of Alarcon and Iniesta, had been crown territory in the 12th and 13th centuries. It was Manuel's son, Juan Manuel, who wove the two together. Wielding an unusually wide jurisdiction, he absorbed the older crown lands into his own and set about turning a lordship into something grander, a nobleman who also happened to be one of the most celebrated prose writers of medieval Castile, author of the story collection El Conde Lucanor.

## Duke, Prince, and Almost King

Under Juan Manuel the domain reached its height. He inherited it as second lord, then pushed its status upward, first to a Duchy and finally to a lifelong Principality, gathering to himself an independence that few subjects of any king ever managed. The private coinage and the standing retinue of a thousand knights were the outward signs of a deeper reality: here was a man exploiting his border position so shrewdly that his power approached that of the peninsular monarchs themselves. He could bargain with Castile using the threat of Aragon, and with Aragon using the threat of Castile, and the awkward middle ground where his lands lay became the very source of his strength.

## How a Realm Dissolves

Such concentrated power rarely survives its founder, and Villena's slowly came apart. The Duchy reverted to the Crown in 1366 when Juan Manuel's line ran out of male heirs after his son Fernando Manuel. The title of Marquess of Villena then passed to Alfonso I, Duke of Gandia, only to return to the Crown again in 1385 after his son Pedro was killed at the Battle of Aljubarrota. Through the 15th century the dukedom was granted and revoked several times until it fell back to the Crown in 1445. In that same year the marquessate was recreated for Juan Pacheco, and this time it endured: the title of Marquess of Villena still exists today, a faint aristocratic echo of the near-kingdom that once filled the map between two crowns.

## Flight Context

The Lordship of Villena was a historical territory rather than a single site, but its nominal seat lies at 38.63 degrees N, 0.87 degrees W, at the town of Villena in the northwest of Alicante province. The clearest visual anchor is Villena's hilltop Atalaya castle above the town, set in the broad inland corridor of the Vinalopo valley that once formed the spine of the lordship and the frontier between Castile and Aragon. At its greatest extent the domain sprawled north toward Cuenca and south toward Murcia, across country best appreciated from a viewing altitude of 3,000 to 6,000 feet, where the valleys, sierras, and scattered fortified towns of the old buffer state come into view together. The nearest major airport is Alicante-Elche (LEAL) to the southeast; Valencia (LEVC) lies to the north and Region de Murcia International (LEMI) to the south. The dry interior air keeps visibility long for much of the year.
