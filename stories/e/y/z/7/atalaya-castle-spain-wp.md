# Atalaya Castle (Spain)

A rammed-earth fortress above Villena that shrugged off three sieges, changed faiths and dynasties, and still guards a protective hand carved into its stairway.

**Location:** 38.6319, -0.8608

**Categories:** Castles in the Valencian Community, Castles in Spain, Medieval architecture, Historic sites in Spain, Reconquista, Villena

## Introduction

Climb the stairway toward the keep of the Atalaya Castle and you pass a hand carved into the stone, a khamsa, the five-fingered charm that Muslim builders scratched into walls to turn away the evil eye. The one on the stair today is a reproduction; the original sits in Villena's museum, protected now the way it once promised to protect. That small human gesture, left by hands that expected to hold this hill forever, is a fitting emblem for a fortress that would change owners, religions, and dynasties again and again while its rammed-earth core simply refused to fall.

## A Hand Against the Evil Eye

No one knows exactly when the castle first rose, only that it existed by the 12th century, because Arab sources name it in 1172. It stands on a spur of the Sierra de la Villa in the northwest of Alicante province, and its bones are Almohad. The keep, taller than everything around it, was raised in Almohad times using rammed earth, layer packed on layer. Its small doorway opens into a hall seven meters square, wrapped in walls that average three and a half meters thick, roofed by an Almohad ceiling from the 12th century that survives overhead. The whole fortress follows a concentric plan, a lower barbican shielding an inner square of walls studded with round corner towers, one of which the keep itself occupies. It was built not for show but to endure, and endure it did.

## Three Sieges and a Conqueror

This was a frontier stronghold on the northern edge of Muslim Iberia, and its strength was no legend. James I of Aragon threw three separate sieges against it before he could take it, finally succeeding in 1240 with an army of Calatrava knights and almogavar mercenaries under Ruy Perez Ponce de Leon, Commander of Alcaniz. There was a complication: seizing Villena violated the Treaty of Cazola, which had assigned the town to Castile's zone of conquest. The dispute was settled four years later by the Treaty of Almizra in 1244, which placed the castle firmly in Castilian hands. This corner of the peninsula was exactly where two Christian kingdoms, Castile and Aragon, drew and redrew the line between them, and the Atalaya sat squarely on it.

## A Writer's Castle

Handed first to the Order of Calatrava, the castle soon passed to Infante Manuel when he became lord of Villena, and then to his son, the nobleman Juan Manuel, Prince of Villena, one of the most important prose writers of medieval Castile and author of the celebrated story collection El Conde Lucanor. He treated the fortress as a home worth strengthening, reinforcing its defenses to protect his fiancee, the princess Constanza of Aragon. In the 14th century the line of lords gave way to Alfonso of Aragon and Foix, the first Marquis of Villena. The marquesses did not hold the affection of the townspeople forever. In 1476, encouraged by the Catholic Monarchs, the citizens of Villena rose against them, and afterward the castle ceased to be a marquis's residence and became, with the city itself, a possession of the crown.

## The Last Wars It Fought

Even as its lordly days ended, the castle kept finding itself in the path of history. During the Revolt of the Brotherhoods between 1519 and 1523, the viceroy Diego Mendoza fled here after his defeat at Gandia, and the troops that restored royal authority marched out from these walls. In the War of the Spanish Succession, fifty Bourbon loyalists held the fortress for eight days against the bulk of the Austrian army before it fell. The final blow came in the early 19th century, when French Marshal Suchet's forces looted it during the Peninsular War and left it abandoned. Restoration began in 1958, and today the castle stands in good repair, declared a Historical-Artistic Monument back in 1931 and serving as the beating heart of Villena's annual Moros y Cristianos festival, a yearly reenactment of the very conflict its stones once witnessed for real.

## Flight Context

The Atalaya Castle crowns a rocky spur of the Sierra de la Villa at 38.63 degrees N, 0.86 degrees W, directly above the town of Villena in the northwest of Alicante province. From the air it reads unmistakably as a compact concentric fortress: a tall keep rising above a ring of round corner towers, set on high ground with the whitewashed town spread below and the broad inland corridor of the upper Vinalopo valley, the old frontier between Castile and Aragon, running past it. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 4,000 feet above the terrain frames the castle against the sierra. The nearest major airport is Alicante-Elche (LEAL), roughly 50 km to the southeast; Valencia (LEVC) lies to the north. The dry interior climate offers long stretches of clear, high-visibility flying weather.
