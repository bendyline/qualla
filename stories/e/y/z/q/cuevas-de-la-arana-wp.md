# Cuevas de la Araña

A cluster of caves near Bicorp holding an 8,000-year-old painting of a human reaching into a swarm of bees, the oldest known image of honey-gathering.

**Location:** 39.1099, -0.8641

**Categories:** Archaeology, Rock art, Prehistoric sites, World Heritage Sites, Caves, Valencian Community

## Introduction

Some eight thousand years ago, on a rock wall in the hills above the Escalona river, someone painted a small human figure clinging to a rope, one arm plunged into a cluster of bees. Around the climber the artist added dark specks, the insects themselves, drawn in flight. It is the oldest known picture of a person gathering honey, and one of the earliest depictions of bees anywhere on Earth. The caves that hold it are named, aptly, the Spider Caves.

## The Oldest Sweet Tooth

The honey-gatherer is the reason the Coves de l'Aranya, Cuevas de la Arana in Spanish, matter to the whole story of our species. The painting is the earliest surviving evidence that Homo sapiens sought out and ate honey, and the earliest clear image of bees. Look closely and the scene tells itself: a figure suspended above a hollow, reaching in while the swarm boils around them, a container perhaps slung nearby for the harvest. Anyone who has ever wanted something sweet enough to risk a sting understands the impulse instantly. Eight millennia collapse into a single, recognizable gesture. The precise dating is debated, but the honey scene is generally placed in the Epipaleolithic, roughly 8,000 years old.

## A Teacher's Find

The caves sit in the municipality of Bicorp, in the interior of the Valencia region, tucked into the valley of the Escalona river where dry limestone hills fold down toward the water. They came to wider attention in the early twentieth century thanks to a local schoolteacher, Jaume Garí i Poch, who recognized what the painted walls were. It is a fitting origin. The images were made by people who lived off this exact landscape, and it took someone rooted in the same valley to notice that their ancestors had left a record on the stone.

## Hunt and Harvest

Honey was not the only story worth painting here. Another scene shows a goat hunt rendered with bows and arrows, hunters and prey caught mid-action in the lively, stick-limbed style typical of the rock art scattered across eastern Spain. Together the paintings sketch a way of life: chasing game across the hills, robbing wild hives of their sugar, reading the seasons closely enough to know when and where to find both. These were not crude marks. They were made by people watching their world with real attention, and choosing which moments were worth fixing forever on a wall. The figures are small and reddish, painted in the schematic, energetic manner that runs through the rock art of eastern Spain, where a few confident strokes are enough to set a whole body in motion. Look long enough and the hills around Bicorp start to feel populated again, not by the sparse handful of people who live there now, but by the hunters and gatherers who once read every ridge and hollow of this valley for what it could give them.

## Painted for the Ages

The Spider Caves are part of a much larger treasure. They belong to the Rock Art of the Iberian Mediterranean Basin, a UNESCO World Heritage Site spanning hundreds of shelters and cave walls along Spain's eastern flank, collectively one of the richest records of prehistoric life in Europe. Standing before the honey-gatherer, the distance between then and now feels thin. The pigment has held its color across eighty centuries. The bees still swarm. The climber still reaches. And the small, human wish behind the whole scene, to reach a little farther for something sweet, has not changed at all.

## Flight Context

The Cuevas de la Arana lie at 39.110 degrees north, 0.864 degrees west, in the hills of Bicorp in Valencia's rugged interior, within the valley of the Escalona river. Best viewed from 2,500 to 4,500 feet, where the limestone ridges and dry river valleys of the Canal de Navarres country stand out against the coastal plain to the east. The nearest major airport is Valencia (LEVC), roughly 60 to 70 km to the east-northeast; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies farther south and Murcia (LEMI) beyond that. Inland visibility is generally clear, though the folded terrain can trap morning valley mist before the sun burns it off.
