# Palace of Milà i Aragó

A frontier fortress in Albaida that a Borgia cardinal turned into a palace, and that now shares its grandest rooms with an international museum of puppets.

**Location:** 38.8419, -0.5192

**Categories:** Palaces, Castles, Historic architecture, Renaissance history, Museums, Valencian Community

## Introduction

Two popes, an emperor's cousin, and a Renaissance cardinal all hover in the family tree of the people who owned this building. Today, behind the same three towers, one of the rooms is filled with marionettes. The Palace of Mila i Arago is the most emblematic structure in Albaida, and its long life reads like a compressed history of the Valencian aristocracy: fortress, palace, ruin, and finally, improbably, a home for puppets.

## A Borgia Address

When the crown seized the barony of Albaida over its owners' debts and put it up for auction, the buyer was Cardinal Lluis Joan del Mila, a man wired directly into the most notorious family of the age. He was a nephew of Pope Callixtus III and a first cousin of the man who would become Pope Alexander VI, both of them Borgias, and the palace sits today on the tourist trail known as the Route of the Borgias. The cardinal acquired the properties around 1471, taking up residence there by 1477. His son Jaume del Mila then married Elionor d'Arago, a niece of King Ferdinand the Catholic and a first cousin of the future Emperor Charles V. That match, sealed in 1477, fused the two family names into Mila i Arago and brought the title of count to whoever held the land. A provincial town palace had married into the royalty of Europe.

## Fortress into Palace

Long before the cardinal, this was a defensive strongpoint. An earlier lord's palace, possibly ordered by the admiral Conrad Llanca in the late 1200s, was built to be protected from every angle. It passed to the Vilaragut family, who held it until 1471 and who occasionally rode in to defend the town, as in 1429 when the baroness Carroca de Vilaragut's husband fought the Castilians here. The oldest surviving core was raised right on the city wall, beside the town's new gate, anchored by a heavy western tower. Then peace did what sieges could not. Between 1525 and 1550 a new wing closed the courtyard into a square, a well was sunk in the middle, and galleries of Roman arches opened toward the main square. Balconies were cut into walls that had once been solid defense. Stone by stone, a fortress was talked out of its armor and into becoming a home.

## Painted Rooms and a Private Pew

Inside, the palace divided itself by season. Thick-walled mezzanine rooms with small windows kept the summer heat at bay, while the upper floor, opening to the square through balconies, held the winter rooms where the important events happened. In the 1690s the painter Bertomeu Albert covered the finest chambers in color coded by function: reds and ochres for the summer rooms, blues and greens and indigo for the winter. His cycle in the throne room, the grandest chamber, is a riot of baroque invention, all stylized flowers, angels and demons, animals, monsters, and centaurs. The building was even joined to the neighboring church of Santa Maria, so that a door from the marquis's bedroom led to a private grandstand overlooking the altar. Faith, for the family that lived here, was something you could attend without leaving home.

## Fire, Collapse, and Puppets

The centuries were not gentle. During the Spanish Civil War the palace sheltered a military recruitment office that had fled besieged Valencia, and afterward served briefly as a regional prison. Earlier, its documentary archive, the written memory of the whole marquisate, had been carted off and pulped by a paper mill in Alcoi. Then, on the night of 26 December 1986, fire tore through the oldest western wing. With no money for emergency repairs, the rain finished the job, and in November 1987 that side of the palace simply collapsed, leaving only its outer walls. Restoration has continued patiently since 1994. The building that hosted cardinals and counts now holds the town's tourist office and the International Puppet Museum of Albaida, its throne room and painted galleries given over to figures on strings. It is a soft landing for a hard history, and somehow a fitting one.

## Flight Context

The Palace of Mila i Arago stands at 38.842 degrees north, 0.519 degrees west, in the town of Albaida in Valencia's southern interior, its three towers and long facade fronting the main square beside the church of Santa Maria. From 2,000 to 4,000 feet the palace-church complex and the compact old town read clearly against the surrounding Albaida valley farmland. The nearest major airport is Valencia (LEVC), roughly 85 km to the north-northwest; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies to the south and Murcia (LEMI) farther still. Inland visibility is usually good, with occasional afternoon haze over the valley in high summer.
