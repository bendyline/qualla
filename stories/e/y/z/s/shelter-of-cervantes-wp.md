# Shelter of Cervantes

A Spanish Civil War air raid shelter beneath Alcoy, dug to hold more than a thousand people against Italian bombers and now a museum where survivors' own voices tell what it was like below ground.

**Location:** 38.6952, -0.4801

**Categories:** History and heritage, Spanish Civil War, Museums, Alcoy, Valencian Community, Spain

## Introduction

The words are still on the wall, painted in the blunt practicality of wartime: es peligroso permanecer aqui, it is dangerous to stay here. It is a jarring thing to read at the mouth of a bomb shelter, a place people ran to precisely because everywhere above was more dangerous still. But the warning was honest. It marked the entrance corridor, where a bomb's shock wave could funnel down and catch anyone lingering near the doorway, and it told the citizens of Alcoy what they already feared as they hurried past it into the dark: even here, deep in the earth, no one was entirely safe.

## The Warning at the Door

That surviving sign is the emotional heart of the Shelter of Cervantes, next to the park that gives it its name. Whoever painted it was not trying to frighten people but to save them, urging them to move quickly past the vulnerable entrance and deep into the protected galleries beyond. Read today, in the calm of a museum, the words carry a weight their author never intended. They are a message from ordinary people to their own children and neighbours, a plea scrawled on stone in the middle of a war, still doing its quiet work of protection long after the danger passed.

## When the Bombers Came

Alcoy was a textile city, a working town, and that made it a target. Between 20 September 1938 and 11 January 1939, in the final winter of the Spanish Civil War, it was bombed repeatedly by Savoia-Marchetti SM.79 aircraft of the Aviazione Legionaria, the Italian expeditionary air force fighting on the Nationalist side. There was little the people below could do but dig. More than twenty-five shelters were carved beneath the city, a whole hidden Alcoy of tunnels and galleries created for one purpose: to keep flesh and blood alive while the sky turned hostile.

## Into the Earth

The Shelter of Cervantes was among the largest. It runs more than a hundred metres underground, a warren of eight galleries reached through two corridors, covering some 292 square metres and built to hold an extraordinary 1,166 people. Those numbers do not describe comfort. They describe survival at its most basic, bodies pressed together in lamplit tunnels. There were four latrines, two washbasins, and a first aid room, the minimum a community needs to endure hours below ground and still call itself a community. Everything about the design assumes crowding, fear, and the long wait for the all-clear.

## The People in the Dark

It is easy to reduce a shelter to its dimensions and forget who filled it. These galleries held families, the young and the old, workers pulled from their looms and children pulled from their beds when the sirens sounded. They came down out of the daylight not knowing whether their houses, or their relatives above, would still be standing when they climbed back out. The washbasins and the first aid room were answers to real needs, real injuries, real hours of dread. Whatever the raids destroyed, the shelter's true purpose was to make sure the people of Alcoy survived to rebuild it.

## A Museum of Memory

The shelter fell silent when the war ended, and for decades it simply waited. On 12 April 2006 it reopened, restored and turned into a museum, each gallery given over to a different aspect of the story. Visitors can study aerial photographs and data from the Italian bombing runs, handle military objects through an interactive display, and, most movingly, watch and listen to filmed testimonies from people who lived through the raids. That last choice matters. It insists that the shelter be remembered not as a piece of engineering but as a refuge for human beings, told in their own voices.

## Flight Context

The Shelter of Cervantes lies beneath Alcoy at roughly 38.695N, 0.480W, in the mountains of inland Alicante province, beside Cervantes Park in the city centre. Being underground it is not visible from the air; navigate instead to the dense hillside old town of Alcoy, set among deep river ravines and their landmark bridges. Recommended overflight altitude 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL in clear conditions, with care for terrain rising above 1,000 metres nearby. Alicante-Elche (LEAL) is the nearest major airport, about 60 km to the southeast; Valencia (LEVC) is roughly 90 km to the north.
