# Jesuset del Miracle

A beloved Alcoy procession honors a statue said to have pointed out a 1568 theft — a tender tradition rooted in a foreigner's confession and a brutal public execution.

**Location:** 38.6991, -0.4740

**Categories:** Festivals in Spain, Alcoy, Spain, Catholic processions, History of the Valencian Community, Religion in Spain

## Introduction

Every year, on the thirty-first of January, the people of Alcoy carry a small statue of the infant Jesus in solemn procession through their streets. It is a tender scene: candles, prayers, an old mountain town wrapped around a cherished image. But the tradition it honors, the Jesuset del Miracle — the "Little Jesus of the Miracle" — begins somewhere much darker. It begins with a robbery, a confession wrung from a frightened outsider, a body torn apart and denied a grave, and a claim, not recorded until nearly sixty years later, that the statue itself had bent down to point out the crime.

## The Theft

On 29 January 1568, a cloth-shearer named Joan Prats slipped into his parish church in Alcoy and carried off a silver ciborium holding forty pieces of consecrated bread, along with a pyx and a reliquary. He took them home and hid them beneath a pile of stones under the stairs that climbed from his stable to his living rooms. Later he ate the sacramental bread, broke open the reliquary, and moved everything into an empty chest. Then a colder worry set in — the objects were too easily found. He wrapped the ciborium and reliquary in cloth, buried them in the floor of his stable, and waited to see whether the town would come looking.

## A Foreigner Under Suspicion

The next morning the priest discovered the theft and set the bells ringing. A reward of thirty pounds was posted; the Justice of the Peace raised the hue and cry and alerted the Viceroy, and searchers fanned out as far as Xàtiva and Gandia. Suspicion soon settled on Prats, and the reason his neighbors gave was blunt: he was French. He had come back to Alcoy not long before, after years away in a France torn apart by religious war, and the belief that he might be a Protestant fleeing Catholic persecution hardened the town's distrust. To a frightened community steeped in the xenophobia of the age, an outsider from a land of heretics was easy to cast as the thief. A first search of his house found nothing. He was arrested anyway — less on evidence than on the mood of an agitated crowd.

## Judgment in Alcoy

At midday on 31 January, a farm labourer named Joan Esteve won permission to search the house once more. He quickly turned up a dish the priest recognised, and then the bundle of wrapped vessels. Confronted, Prats confessed to everything. The case passed to a lawyer, Jaume Margarit; an executioner was summoned from Gandia; and Prats was taken to Valencia to be tried by the Inquisition for the crime of sacrilege. Margarit pressed for the sentence to be carried out back in Alcoy, so the town might take the lesson to heart. Five days later Prats was beheaded and dismembered. His head was hung from the door of the monastery of Saint Augustine, his right hand — the one thought to have done the deed — was set in the square beside the priests' houses, and the rest of him was scattered, unburied, along the roads leading out of town. He had committed the theft; the ferocity of his end belonged to its century.

## The Statue That Pointed

The miracle came later — much later. Tradition holds that during the frantic search, a small statue of the Baby Jesus in the house of a neighbor, Señora Miralles, bent forward at the waist and turned its raised, two-fingered hand toward the vessels' hiding place. Yet no written account of this appears until 1627, nearly sixty years after the theft, when town officials gathered sworn statements from residents who claimed to remember it; the longest came from a ninety-five-year-old widow, Jerónima Vilaplana. By then the story had taken on a life of its own. Prats's house was bought and consecrated, Señora Miralles gave up her statue to be venerated, and Archbishop Juan de Ribera had the building remade into a convent, opened in 1597. So a grim winter's crime became, over the generations, a festival of light — the little Jesus of Alcoy, still carried faithfully through the streets each January.

## Flight Context

Alcoy (Alcoi) sits at roughly 38.699°N, 0.474°W, in a mountain basin of northern Alicante province, ringed by the Serra de Mariola and neighboring ridges. From the air, the city is recognizable by the deep ravines that cut through it, spanned by unusually tall bridges and viaducts — Alcoy is often called the city of bridges. Best viewed from 3,000–5,000 ft in clear light, with the surrounding sierras rising green and gray around the urban bowl. Nearest airport is Alicante–Elche (LEAL), about 45 km to the south-southeast; Valencia (LEVC) lies to the north. Mountain cloud can gather over the ranges on winter mornings.
