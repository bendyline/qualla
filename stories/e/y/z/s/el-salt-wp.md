# El Salt

At a limestone shelter near Alcoy, layers of ancient campfires and the oldest known human excrement reveal how Neanderthals lived, cooked, and finally vanished from Iberia.

**Location:** 38.6872, -0.5089

**Categories:** Archaeology, Prehistory, Neanderthals, Alcoy, Natural sites, Spain

## Introduction

Sometime around fifty thousand years ago, a Neanderthal relieved themselves on the cooling ashes of a campfire beside a limestone wall near what is now Alcoy. That waste hardened, mineralized, and waited. When scientists analyzed it in 2014, it was the oldest human excrement ever studied, and it carried a quiet revolution inside it. The chemistry showed fats from meat, as expected, but also unmistakable traces of plants. The cartoon of the club-swinging, all-meat caveman took a serious blow. The people who sheltered at El Salt ate a varied diet, and they left behind a record honest enough to prove it.

## The Campfire's Confession

The coprolite, as archaeologists call fossilized dung, had settled onto the remains of a hearth, and that context made it priceless. It preserved a snapshot of a single Neanderthal's last meals: fats associated with meat, and biomarkers pointing to eaten plants. For a species long imagined as pure carnivores, this was evidence of something more flexible and more human. They foraged as well as hunted. They knew their landscape well enough to gather what it offered, and they cooked over fires whose ashes still hold the story.

## Fires That Never Went Out

El Salt is, above all, a place of fire. One archaeological layer, known as unit X and dated to roughly fifty thousand years ago, is packed with the residue of campfires, in places up to thirty-five centimeters thick. That density means the shelter was used again and again, by group after group returning across the generations, each lighting fires in the same protected spot. The layers even record the weather: one darker, clay-rich band suggests the climate then was wetter than the dry hills of today. To read this ground is to watch countless small acts of warmth stacked on top of one another.

## A Wall of Stone and Time

The shelter sits at the foot of a limestone wall some thirty-eight meters high, its face crusted with tufa and travertine laid down by mineral-rich water. It is an open-air spot, roughly seven hundred meters above sea level, near where the Polop and Barxell streams meet on their way to the Serpis river. That combination of high cliff, running water, and open sky made it a natural place to stop, sheltered but not enclosed, with the landscape and its game visible in every direction. Beneath it lies a stratified deposit more than six meters thick, an archive built one thin layer at a time. El Salt was not an isolated camp but one node in a network, evidence that its people ranged widely across the plains and hills of the Valencian interior, mobile and knowing in their movements.

## The Last of Their Kind

Among the finds are six Neanderthal teeth, intimate remains of the individuals who passed through. Dating techniques place the deposits between roughly 61,000 and 45,000 years old, and it is that younger boundary that gives El Salt its poignancy. A study published in 2014 argued that Neanderthals may have disappeared from the Iberian Peninsula around 45,000 years ago, perhaps five thousand years before they vanished from the rest of Europe. If that reading holds, this quiet shelter near Alcoy sits close to the ending of a human story that had run for hundreds of thousands of years.

## Reading the Layers

Making sense of all this is painstaking work. Excavators first opened the site in the 1960s, but the modern investigation has been led since 1986 by a research team from the University of La Laguna under Professor Bertila Galván, funded by Spanish and Valencian science programs. They divide the deposit into thirteen fine geological units and sift each for tools, teeth, and traces of fire. Flint implements, hearths, and the coprolite all emerge from this careful reading of the ground, where a few centimeters of sediment can represent centuries of lives lived beneath the limestone wall.

## Flight Context

El Salt lies at 38.6872°N, 0.5089°W, in rugged limestone country just outside Alcoy in the interior of Alicante province, roughly seven hundred meters above sea level. From the air, orient on the town of Alcoy nestled in the valley of the Serpis, ringed by the Serra de Mariola and the Font Roja massif; the site sits amid steep terrain northwest of the town. Because the shelter is small and set against a cliff, a lower viewing altitude of 2,500 to 4,500 feet gives the best read of the folded mountain landscape. Nearest airport is Alicante-Elche (LEAL), about 55 km south; Valencia (LEVC) lies to the north. Mountain haze and afternoon cloud can build over these ridges in summer.
