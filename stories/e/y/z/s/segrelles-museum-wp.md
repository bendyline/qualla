# Segrelles Museum

The house-museum where illustrator Josep Segrelles, who painted Dante's hell, Poe's nightmares and the depths of space, came home to a small Valencian town to live and work until his death.

**Location:** 38.8424, -0.5199

**Categories:** Museums, Art and artists, Albaida, Valencian Community, Illustration, Spain

## Introduction

He drew the circles of Dante's hell and the hidden interior of the human body, the far reaches of the cosmos and the fever-dreams of Edgar Allan Poe. In New York, admirers so loved the deep, glowing blue of his skies that they gave it his name: the Segrelles Blue. And then, at the height of a career that had carried him from Valencia to Barcelona to Manhattan, Josep Segrelles went home to a small town in the Valencian hills and built himself a house he never wanted to leave. That house, in Albaida, is now his museum.

## From Albaida to Manhattan

Segrelles was born in Albaida in 1885 and trained in the studios of Valencia and at Barcelona's Escola de la Llotja, where he learned his craft under masters of the age. A Barcelona exhibition in 1918 announced his arrival, and commissions followed, among them illustrations for the novels of the great Valencian writer Blasco Ibanez. By 1928 his reputation had crossed the Atlantic. He settled on New York's Upper West Side and worked for the glossy magazines of the era, painting fantastical worlds for readers who had never heard of the quiet town where he was born. Few illustrators of his generation travelled so far from their roots.

## The Segrelles Blue

What set his work apart was light. Segrelles built his scenes around a saturated, otherworldly blue, and Americans admired it so much that they named the colour after him. His imagination ran to the epic and the strange: gods and genies, ruined temples, storms of angels, cities that never existed. He was not painting the world as it looked but as it might feel in a dream or a myth. That instinct made him one of the most sought-after illustrators of the fantastic, a painter equally at home rendering a passage of scripture and a scene from science fiction.

## The House He Designed for Himself

In 1932 he came back to Albaida for good. Then, working from designs he had drawn up during his years in the United States, he began in 1949 to build the house that would become his museum. Here he lived and painted until his death on 3 March 1969, a fortnight short of his eighty-fourth birthday. The building is not a neutral gallery but the artist's own creation, shaped to his eye and his habits, so that walking through it is less like visiting a collection than stepping inside the mind that made it.

## A Cathedral of the Imagination

The collection sweeps across a lifetime. Early family portraits hang near his last, posthumous canvas, an oil called The Pentecost. Between them lies the astonishing range of his illustration: scenes for Dante's Comedy, for the Thousand and One Nights, for Cervantes, for Poe and H. G. Wells, and even visual responses to passages in Beethoven's fifth and ninth symphonies. He turned his brush to the inside of the human body and to what he called the sidereal, the realm of stars and astronomy, alongside deeply felt religious themes. Few painters attempted so many worlds; fewer still gathered them all under one roof.

## Home Ground

Albaida sits in the fertile valley country between Valencia and Alicante, a landscape of vineyards, olive groves and pale limestone hills. It is not a place that shouts for attention, and that seems to be exactly why Segrelles chose it. The man who had illustrated infernos and galaxies wanted, in the end, the ordinary light of his birthplace. The museum keeps that choice visible. It is a stop on the Route of the Valencian Classics, but its deeper subject is simpler and more human: an artist who saw everything, and came home.

## Flight Context

The Segrelles Museum sits in Albaida at roughly 38.842N, 0.520W, in the valley country of southern Valencia province. The town is small, so navigate by the broad agricultural valley between the Serra Mariola and the Benicadell ridge rather than by the building itself. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000 to 4,000 feet AGL in clear weather; summer haze can soften the valley. Valencia (LEVC) lies about 75 km to the north-northwest and Alicante-Elche (LEAL) roughly 65 km to the south, making either a practical base for a visit.
