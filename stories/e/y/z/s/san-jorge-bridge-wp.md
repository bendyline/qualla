# San Jorge Bridge

Dreamed up in 1876 and not finished until 1931, Alcoy's colossal Art Deco bridge leaps a deep river gorge and became the city's defining image.

**Location:** 38.7000, -0.4752

**Categories:** Bridges, Art Deco architecture, Alcoy, Valencian Community, Engineering landmarks, Spain

## Introduction

The idea appeared on paper in 1876, sketched into Alcoy's plans for a city that did not yet have room to become itself. A viaduct, the drawings promised, would leap the deep gorge of the Riquer and stitch the cramped medieval core to the open ground waiting on the far side. Then nothing happened. Half a century passed, a whole generation of textile workers born, grown, and gone, before anyone poured the first concrete. When the San Jorge Bridge finally opened on 26 March 1931, it had taken fifty-five years to travel from wish to reality, and it arrived as one of the most colossal works of Art Deco anywhere in Spain.

## A City on the Edge of Its Own Ravines

Alcoy is a town that had to earn its flat ground. It sits high in the mountains of inland Alicante, at a point where rivers have carved the land into steep gorges, and for centuries those ravines penned the old quarter in. To grow, the city had to build across the void. The San Jorge Bridge answered that need where the Serpis runs, spanning the Riquer more than forty-five metres above the riverbed. On one side lay the ancient core, tight and vertical; on the other, the Horta Major, the expansion the planners had been promising since the nineteenth century. The bridge did not just cross a river. It opened a future the city had been unable to reach.

## The Measure of It

The numbers still impress. The deck runs 245 metres from bank to bank, carried 45.4 metres above the water on four principal supports, their props built up in ashlar stone and masonry. Reinforced concrete, then a relatively young material, did the structural heavy lifting, allowing spans and heights that older stone arches could never have reached. Work began in 1925 and took six years, a long haul for a single crossing, but the terrain gave no shortcuts. When crews finally struck the falsework and stood back, Alcoy had a bridge scaled not to its modest population but to the sheer depth of the chasm it had to conquer.

## The Shape of an Era

What sets the San Jorge apart is not only its size but its style. This is Art Deco rendered in concrete on a monumental scale, all clean verticals and confident geometry, the visual language of the 1920s and 30s made permanent in a mountain town. It is counted among the most representative Art Deco works in the whole Valencian Community, and it has long since become the single most recognisable image of Alcoy. Look at a postcard of the city, an old tourism poster, a modern photograph, and the odds are the bridge is in it, striding across the gorge with the roofs of the old town clustered beyond.

## The Names on the Blueprint

The bridge was the work of the architect Victor Eusa, joined by engineers Carmelo Monzon Reparaz and Vicente Redon, with the civil engineer Alfonso Pena Boeuf collaborating on its execution. The construction concession went to the Navarrese firm of Erroz y San Martin, and the whole undertaking was financed by the municipality of Alcoy itself, out of a budget of 1,336,824 pesetas. That figure meant something for a provincial town in the 1920s. It was a wager that the city would keep growing, and that the far bank of the Riquer was worth reaching at almost any cost.

## The Bridge and the Festival

The name is no accident. San Jorge, Saint George, is Alcoy's patron, celebrated each spring in the Moros i Cristians, the Moors and Christians festival that fills these streets with drums, gunpowder smoke, and armies in fantastical costume. To name the bridge for him was to hand the newest, boldest structure in town to its oldest story. Today the crossing carries ordinary traffic over the gorge as if it were nothing, but stand beneath it and look up, and the scale returns: a concrete colossus, half a century in the imagining, that finally let a mountain city step across the emptiness it had lived beside for so long.

## Flight Context

The San Jorge Bridge stands at roughly 38.700N, 0.475W, in the mountain city of Alcoy in inland Alicante province. Best appreciated from lower altitudes, 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, in the clear light of a Mediterranean morning, though the surrounding terrain rises well above 1,000 metres, so mind the ridgelines. From the air the bridge reads as a bold concrete span crossing a deep river gorge on the edge of a dense hillside old town. The nearest major airport is Alicante-Elche (LEAL), about 60 km to the southeast; Valencia (LEVC) lies roughly 90 km to the north.
