# Palace of the Counts of Cocentaina

A medieval fortress in the mountains of inland Alicante, once held by the Crown of Aragon's greatest admiral, that the Corella counts transformed into a palace crowned by a gilded hall of painted ancestors.

**Location:** 38.7433, -0.4408

**Categories:** Bien de Interés Cultural landmarks in the Province of Alicante, Palaces in the Valencian Community, History, Medieval, Architecture, Alicante

## Introduction

One of the most celebrated admirals of the medieval Mediterranean held a prize that sat far from any coast. Roger de Lluria, who commanded the fleets of the Crown of Aragon, was lord of this fortified house at Cocentaina, tucked among the mountains of inland Alicante. Over the centuries that followed, its owners turned a soldier's stronghold into a nobleman's palace, trading arrow-slits for altarpieces and battlements for a room of gold.

## A Fortress in the Mountains

The building began as a stronghold, raised over the remains of an earlier Andalusi fortress in a valley that had passed from Muslim to Christian hands during the long Reconquista. Its medieval bones are still legible in the plan: four great halls and four towers rising on solid keeps, each once crowned with merlons, the toothed battlements from which defenders could watch the roads. Cocentaina sits in the valley of the Serpis river, ringed by the peaks of El Comtat, a strategic pinch-point between the coast and the interior. A castle guarding such a place was not decoration. It was the reason the town survived. Control of this narrow corridor meant control of who and what moved between the Mediterranean shore and the high tablelands beyond, and for generations the fortress at Cocentaina held that gate.

## The Count Who Paid in Gold

Everything changed in 1448, when Ximen Perez de Corella took possession of Cocentaina. Corella had served King Alfonso V, the Magnanimous, in his campaigns to conquer territories in Italy, and his reward was a title: Count of Cocentaina, a rank for which he is said to have paid the crown 80,000 gold florins. It was an enormous sum, and it bought more than status. Under the Corella family, the grim fortress was steadily rebuilt into a seigneurial residence, its defensive lines softened by Gothic-Valencian stonework and, later, the graceful proportions of the Renaissance. The house that had repelled enemies now welcomed guests.

## The Golden Room

The palace's crowning glory hides on an upper floor of the Tower of Homage. There, beneath a vault carved with stars, spreads the Sala Daurada, the Golden Room, painted in the early 1600s by Jeronimo Rodriguez de Espinosa and his son. Across its walls unfurls the family tree of the Corella counts, a sweep of genealogical murals reckoned among the most important in the whole Valencian Community. To stand in the room is to watch a dynasty insist on its own permanence, generation after painted generation looking down. It is one thing to inherit a castle; it is another to paint your ancestors onto its ceiling so no visitor could forget who built it.

## Saints and Scripture

The palace also guards a small but rich gallery of sacred art. Among its treasures are a Gothic altarpiece devoted to Saint Barbara, an altarpiece of Saint Anthony by the Valencian master Nicolas Borras, and a hand-copied Bible dating to the 15th century, its pages older than the Golden Room by more than a hundred years. The Corella devotion left a further mark on Cocentaina too: in the 17th century the family founded a convent of Poor Clare nuns attached to the palace itself, folding cloister and court into a single complex where prayer and lordship lived side by side.

## What the Stones Remember

Today the Palau Comtal is protected as a Bien de Interes Cultural, Spain's highest heritage designation, and it anchors the historic old quarter of Cocentaina today as both monument and museum. Walk its halls and the whole arc of the place reveals itself: an Andalusi foundation, a Christian fortress, a count's ambition, a workshop's brushwork, a convent's quiet. Few buildings hold so many centuries so plainly. Visitors climb the same stairs the counts climbed, pass beneath the same painted vault, and step out onto floors that have carried soldiers, servants, nuns and nobles in turn. The towers that once scanned the valley for raiders now watch over a mountain town at peace, keeping the memory of everyone who ever claimed this corner of Alicante as their own.

## Flight Context

The Palace of the Counts of Cocentaina lies at 38.74 degrees N, 0.44 degrees W, in the town of Cocentaina in the Serpis valley of inland Alicante. From the air, look for the square, crenellated towers of the palace within the old town, set at the foot of the Serra de Mariola with the peak of Montcabrer rising to the west and the medieval Castell de Cocentaina perched on the hill above. Best viewed from 2,000 to 4,000 feet in clear conditions, when the tower shadows sharpen the outline. Alicante-Elche (LEAL) is roughly 55 km to the south; Valencia (LEVC) about 90 km to the north. Morning light off the mountains gives the cleanest view.
