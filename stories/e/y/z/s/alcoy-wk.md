# Alcoy

An industrial mountain city where velvet-clad armies refight a 13th-century siege each spring, and where factory workers once invented a coffee liqueur to keep warm.

**Location:** 38.6983, -0.4736

**Categories:** cities, history, festivals, industrial heritage, food and drink, mountains

## Introduction

Once a year, the people of Alcoy divide themselves into two armies. Over several April days honoring Saint George, thousands parade through the old streets in embroidered robes and burnished breastplates, arquebuses cracking against the stone, refighting a war that ended more than seven centuries ago. The Moors and Christians festival is not a costume party but a civic memory made flesh, a reenactment of the last Muslim revolt against the new Christian town, suppressed in 1276. In Alcoy, history is something you march to.

## Gunpowder and Velvet

The Moros i Cristians is the reason most outsiders know Alcoy at all, and one visit explains why. Rival companies of 'Moors' and 'Christians' fill the center with drums, brocade, and the sulfur bite of black powder, a spectacle staged where the modern town first took hold. It commemorates the suppression of the last Arab revolt in 1276, and it is bound up with Saint George, the city's patron. The processions may look like pageantry, but they carry a real weight: generations of families inherit their roles, their music, and their place in the ranks, so that the whole town rehearses its own founding year after year.

## A Name No One Can Agree On

Alcoy has been inhabited since prehistoric times, and even its name resists a tidy story. Some trace it to Arabic, al-Qawi, meaning the fort or the strong place; others to Latin, the Arabic article al fused to cola, the cultivator or settler. The town in its present form was established after the Christian reconquista in 1256, then became the setting for repeated Muslim uprisings. That friction, ending in 1276, is precisely what the spring festival keeps alive. Few cities wear the ambiguity of their origins so openly, and fewer still turn a contested past into an annual celebration rather than a buried grievance.

## The City the Factories Built

Walk the historic center and the medieval street plan is still there beneath your feet, but almost everything you see above it dates from the 19th century. When the Industrial Revolution arrived in the mid-1800s, Alcoy remade itself into a manufacturing town, and its architects rebuilt in the ornate Modernist style then sweeping across the region. Elaborate facades, ironwork, and monuments like the Llotja de Sant Jordi rose during those decades of textile and paper wealth. The result is a mountain town with the confident, decorated look of an industrial boomtown, its prosperity written across the stone in flourishes.

## Coffee With a Kick

Alcoy and neighboring Cocentaina share a signature drink, café licor, a coffee liqueur not to be confused with the sweeter licor café of Murcia and Galicia. Its roots reach back to the early 13th century, but it truly took hold during the industrial boom, when factory workers spiked the coffee in their thermoses to fortify long shifts. Today it carries the Spanish denomination of origin for Traditional Spirits and turns up everywhere: drunk neat, or cut with orxata, soda water, or lemon. It is a small, stubborn tradition, a working-class habit that outlasted the factories that gave rise to it.

## Between Two Mountains

Alcoy sits cradled among ravines in the highlands where two natural parks, the Serra Mariola and the Font Roja, press in on either side. Deep gorges like the Barranc del Cint slice through the terrain, and the town threads its bridges across them. The reminders of harder years are here too: the Cervantes air-raid shelter, dug to protect civilians during the Spanish Civil War, still tunnels beneath the streets. Around it, hiking trails climb into limestone ridges and cool forests, the same mountains that once isolated the town and now draw walkers to its edges.

## Flight Context

Alcoy lies at 38.698 N, 0.474 W, tucked into the mountains of interior Alicante province at roughly 560 meters elevation. From the air the giveaway is the terrain: a compact, densely built city wedged into a bowl and split by deep ravines, its bridges leaping the gorges, ringed by the forested ridges of the Serra Mariola and Font Roja. Nearest major airport is Alicante-Elche (LEAL), about 50 km to the south-southeast; Valencia (LEVC) lies roughly 85 km north. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-4,000 ft AGL for the ravine-and-bridge cityscape. Mountain haze and afternoon cloud can build over the sierras; clearest light is morning.
