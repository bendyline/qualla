# Battle of Castalla (1812)

In 1812, a Spanish general ignored Wellington's advice and attacked the French near Castalla - and a regiment of dragoons that was not supposed to be there turned his plan into a rout.

**Location:** 38.6000, -0.6667

**Categories:** Peninsular War, Napoleonic Wars, History of Alicante, Military history, Battles, Valencian Community

## Introduction

The dragoons were not supposed to be there. On 21 July 1812, in the hills near Castalla northwest of Alicante, a Spanish army under Joseph O'Donnell was carefully probing a French position when the French 24th Dragoon Regiment came thundering onto the field from a direction no one had accounted for. In minutes a cautious advance became a catastrophe. O'Donnell had been asked only to hold the enemy in place; instead he had gambled on a complicated attack, and the gamble came apart under the hooves of cavalry he never expected to face.

## A Holding Action Ignored

The summer of 1812 was a dangerous one for eastern Spain. Marshal Louis Gabriel Suchet's French army had taken Valencia and overrun much of the province, and only to the south did resistance still gather, where the Spanish Army of Murcia regrouped around its base at Alicante. That base had already been tested - in January the Spanish had thrown back an attempt by General Montbrun and 5,500 French soldiers to seize it. Now, with an Anglo-Sicilian expedition under General Thomas Maitland due to land at Alicante, the Duke of Wellington asked the Spanish commander Joseph O'Donnell to do one simple thing: conduct a holding operation, keep the French occupied, and wait. O'Donnell had other ideas.

## Three Columns and a Ridge

Ignoring Wellington's advice, O'Donnell formed his eleven thousand men into three separate attack columns, meaning to swing them wide and envelop a single French brigade under General Harispe. It was an ambitious scheme that asked a great deal of coordination and timing. On the French side, Colonel Delort read the ground shrewdly. He gave up the town of Castalla itself and drew his soldiers up on a nearby ridge, a strong natural position, and waited to see what the three probing Spanish columns in the centre would do. The plan depended on the wings arriving in time. They did not.

## The Dragoons Arrive

As O'Donnell's centre brigades felt their way toward the ridge, the French 24th Dragoons appeared without warning and fell on them with a series of crushing charges. Cavalry loose among infantry that is not braced for it is a nightmare, and this was one. French horse and foot together killed or wounded a thousand Spanish soldiers and rounded up 2,135 prisoners. The two flanking columns that were supposed to close the trap had marched such wide circuits around the battlefield that the fighting was finished before either could bring its weight to bear. O'Donnell's army did not so much lose the battle as watch it end before it had properly begun. Behind the round numbers were more than three thousand ordinary men - killed, wounded, or marched off into captivity - who had trusted their lives to a plan that asked the impossible of them. They were among the many Spanish soldiers of the Peninsular War who paid, in full, for decisions made above their heads.

## Overshadowed

When Maitland's expedition finally came ashore, he found the Army of Murcia wrecked and unfit to campaign for months. Yet the defeat at Castalla was almost immediately buried under better news. The very next day, far to the west, Wellington shattered a French army at the Battle of Salamanca and threatened to break Napoleon's whole grip on Spain. Beside that triumph, one bungled Spanish attack in the hills behind Alicante scarcely registered. History has a small consolation for the town, though. Less than a year later, on almost the same ground, a second Battle of Castalla would be fought - and that one, with a British-led army holding the ridge above the town, would end very differently. The valley that saw a Spanish plan unravel in 1812 would witness a hard-won Allied victory in 1813.

## Flight Context

The battle was fought near Castalla at roughly 38.6 degrees N, 0.667 degrees W, about 32 km northwest of Alicante, in the inland basin known as the Foia de Castalla - a broad agricultural valley ringed by hills and watched over by a castle on a rocky outcrop above the town. From the air the open valley floor and its encircling ridges stand out against the drier heights around them. Alicante-Elche Airport (LEAL) is the nearest major field, about 35 km southeast; Valencia (LEVC) lies further north. Skies over the interior are typically clear and dry.
