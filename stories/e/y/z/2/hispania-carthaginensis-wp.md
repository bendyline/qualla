# Hispania Carthaginensis

A Roman province carved from Spain by the emperor Diocletian, named for the great enemy Rome once destroyed, and fought over by Alans, Suebi, and Visigoths as the empire collapsed around it.

**Location:** 38.0000, -1.0000

**Categories:** Ancient Rome, Roman provinces, Late antiquity, History of Cartagena, Ancient history

## Introduction

It is one of history's quieter ironies that a Roman province should carry the name of Rome's oldest enemy. Hispania Carthaginensis, Carthaginian Spain, took its name from its capital, Carthago Nova, the New Carthage founded by the very people Rome had fought three wars to destroy. The city survives today as Cartagena on Spain's southeastern coast, and the province that answered to it once stretched from the Mediterranean shore deep into the heart of Iberia. Its name was a fossil of a vanished rivalry, worn by a province that would outlive the empire that created it only by a little.

## A Province Named for an Enemy

Carthago Nova was a Carthaginian foundation, planted on the Iberian coast during the long struggle between Carthage and Rome. When Rome finally prevailed and absorbed the peninsula, the name endured, and centuries later it lent itself to an entire province. Hispania Carthaginensis covered the central Mediterranean coast around the city and reached inland across central Iberia, a broad territory anchored on a superb natural harbor. To govern from Carthago Nova was to rule from a place whose very name recalled the enemy Rome had erased, a reminder built into the map that even conquerors inherit their rivals' geography.

## Diocletian's New Map

The province was a deliberate creation, not an accident of settlement. In the year 298, the emperor Diocletian carved Hispania Carthaginensis out of the older and larger province of Hispania Tarraconensis as part of his sweeping reorganization of the empire's administration. Diocletian was a systematizer, a ruler who tried to save a fraying empire by dividing it into smaller, more governable pieces. Splitting the vast Iberian provinces into more manageable units was exactly the kind of reform he pursued everywhere. With a stroke of imperial policy, a new Roman province came into being, its borders drawn from a distant capital to serve the needs of a strained and shrinking state.

## The Century of Invasions

For a time the province was peaceful. Then the fifth century arrived, and with it the great migrations that would dismember the western empire. From 410 to 418 the region fell under the temporary rule of the Alans, a nomadic people of Iranian origin, until the emperor Honorius and his general Constantius III sponsored a Gothic campaign that broke their hold. The province went quiet for two decades before the Suebi, riding the conquests of their king Rechila, seized it briefly in the 440s and 450s. A Visigothic expedition restored Roman authority in 456, only for the same Goths to take it back a decade later under their ambitious king Euric. Rule changed hands like a passed torch.

## What Rome Left

Behind the churn of kings and armies, the deeper Roman imprint endured. Roads, cities, language, and law had taken root during centuries of Romanization, and they did not vanish when the legions did. Cartagena, the old Carthago Nova, remained a Mediterranean crossroads long after the province that bore its name dissolved into the kingdoms of a post-Roman world. The story of Hispania Carthaginensis is the story of the western empire in miniature: a confident administrative creation, a stretch of peace, and then a slow unraveling as new peoples claimed the land. What remained was not the province but the civilization it had carried. The rulers who followed, Alan, Sueve, and Goth, did not sweep the Roman world away so much as settle into it, adopting its cities, its coins, and eventually its faith. For the farmers and merchants of the southeastern coast, the name of the distant emperor mattered less than the roads that carried their goods and the harbor that received their ships. Empires ended; the harbor endured. That continuity, more than any battle, is the real legacy of Hispania Carthaginensis.
