# Benidorm

A mayor's mid-century dream to build upward turned a small Alicante fishing village into Spain's skyscraper-lined beach city, where a Manhattan skyline meets four kilometres of Blue Flag sand.

**Location:** 38.5342, -0.1314

**Categories:** Coastal, Beach Resort, Spain, Costa Blanca, Tourism, Urban

## Introduction

In 1952, the mayor of a small Alicante fishing village climbed onto a Vespa and rode nine hours to Madrid. Pedro Zaragoza Orts had a problem that only a dictator could solve. He had permitted women to wear the new two-piece bikini on Benidorm's beach, Catholic bishops were threatening to excommunicate him, and he needed Francisco Franco to overrule the church. Zaragoza got his audience, got his backing, and rode home to build the most improbable skyline in Spain.

## The Man Who Dreamed Upward

Zaragoza served as mayor from 1950 to 1967, and his 1956 development plan set the town on a course no Spanish resort had taken before. Instead of sprawling low along the coast, Benidorm would grow toward the sky. His logic was simple and slightly poetic: build tall, and more people can see the beaches and feel the sea air. The village obliged with enthusiasm. Today the town bristles with hotels and holiday apartment blocks, many soaring past twenty-five floors, packed so densely that visitors have nicknamed the place the Manhattan of the Mediterranean. The Gran Hotel Bali, finished in 2002, rises 186 metres and was Spain's tallest building until Madrid's Torre Espacio overtook it in 2007. Cranes still turn at the western edge as the concrete forest spreads slowly inland.

## Four Kilometres of Blue Flag

Beneath the towers lies the reason they were built. Benidorm's great asset is four kilometres of safe, sandy beach, including two Blue Flag stretches, split between the lively Levante and the quieter Poniente by a rocky headland. With more than 41,000 hotel beds across some 240 establishments, the town runs at full tilt in July and August, when the population can swell toward 200,000. Yet the beaches rarely empty. The climate is so mild that daytime temperatures seldom drop below 18 degrees even in winter, and northern Europeans arrive all 365 days of the year to trade grey skies for reliable sun. When the sand palls, the resort supplies its own alternatives: theme parks like Terra Mitica and the wildlife park of Terra Natura draw families up into the hills behind the beach, and a thousand restaurants and hundreds of bars keep the town awake long after dark.

## The Village Underneath

For all the neon and high-rise glass, an older Benidorm survives. The compact old town, gathered around its hilltop church, remembers the fishing village that stood here before the boom. Between the two beaches, the Balcon del Mediterraneo juts out over blue water on a small promontory once fortified against raiders. Wander the narrow lanes and you find Calle Santo Domingo, the tapas alley, where the food turns properly Spanish: cured jamon and the excellent wines of the nearby Jalon valley. It is a reminder that the resort was grafted onto a real place, not conjured from empty sand.

## Escape to the Real Coast

The mountains begin almost at the city limits. Above the La Cala beach, an uphill footpath climbs to an old watchtower that once served as a lookout for pirates and smugglers, with a picnic spot at the summit and benches to catch your breath along the way. To the north, the Serra Gelada natural park drops in dramatic cliffs to the sea, crowned by the white Albir lighthouse, while the great cross of La Cruz de Benidorm surveys the whole bay. Boats leave for the tiny inhabited island of Tabarca off Alicante. Inland, within a short drive, wait the stone village of Guadalest, the almond groves and vineyards toward Jalon, and the Algar waterfalls near Polop.

## Loved, Mocked, Unbothered

Few places divide opinion like Benidorm. Purists sneer at the package-holiday crowds, the English breakfasts, the karaoke bars and the eight hundred places serving cheap beer. But the town has never pretended to be anything other than what Zaragoza designed: a democratic pleasure machine where a retired couple from Yorkshire and a Spanish family from Madrid can afford the same slice of Mediterranean sun. That honesty is its own kind of charm. Benidorm made a bet, decades ago, that ordinary people deserved a beach holiday, and it has been cashing the winnings ever since.

## Flight Context

Benidorm sits at 38.53 degrees N, 0.13 degrees W on the Costa Blanca. From the air it is unmistakable: a tight cluster of skyscrapers rising from a curving bay, the slab of the Gran Hotel Bali standing above the pack, with the Serra Gelada headland and Albir lighthouse to the north and the twin-peaked Puig Campana mountain looming just inland. Best appreciated from 3,000 to 6,000 feet on a clear day. Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies roughly 45 km to the southwest; Valencia (LEVC) is about 130 km up the coast to the north. Coastal haze can soften the skyline on humid summer afternoons; mornings offer the sharpest views.
