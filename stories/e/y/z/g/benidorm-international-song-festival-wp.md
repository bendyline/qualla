# Benidorm International Song Festival

The seaside contest that launched Julio Iglesias and gave Spain its answer to Sanremo, before returning decades later as the country's road to Eurovision.

**Location:** 38.5342, -0.1314

**Categories:** Music festivals, Song contests, Culture of the Valencian Community, Benidorm, Spanish popular music, History of Spain

## Introduction

In July 1968, a young man named Julio Iglesias walked onto a stage in Benidorm and sang a ballad called "La vida sigue igual" — "Life Goes On the Same." The title could not have been more wrong. In a single night he took first prize, best vocalist, and best lyrics, and the life he had known was over. Within a few years he would be one of the best-selling recording artists on the planet, his voice familiar from Madrid to Manila. It all began here, on a stretch of Mediterranean coast, at a summer song contest built to put a fishing town on the map.

## Spain's Answer to Sanremo

The idea took shape in 1958 around a seaside kiosk called El Tío Quico. The town's mayor, Pedro Zaragoza, sat with the writer and journalist Carlos Villacorta and a handful of others, and looked to Italy for inspiration. Since 1951 the Ligurian resort of Sanremo had drawn the world's eyes with an annual song festival, and Benidorm wanted the same trick to work for itself. In July 1959 the first Festival Español de la Canción de Benidorm opened in the city's Manila Park, organised by the state radio network known as REM. When a song called "Un telegrama" won and then swept across Spain, its success settled the matter — the festival would stay in Benidorm, even as rival cities in the south schemed to take it away. The town had wagered that a song could do the work of a thousand advertisements, and the wager paid off.

## A Springboard for Stars

For its first dozen years, singers performed each entry twice, in competing versions — a quirk borrowed straight from Sanremo. The formula produced hits that lodged themselves in the national memory: "Comunicando," "Quisiera ser," "Tu loca juventud," "Amor amargo." More than songs, it produced careers. The duo Dúo Dinámico, the balladeer Raphael, the singer Karina, and of course Julio Iglesias all crossed the Benidorm stage. Some arrived unknown and left famous. Others were already climbing and used the contest to climb faster. For a country still living under Franco, the festival offered something rare — a bright, televised summer evening of pop glamour, beamed into living rooms from the Pyrenees to Andalusia. To win here was to be introduced to a nation all at once. The double-version format meant two singers might chase the same melody on the same night, and that rivalry only sharpened the drama for viewers at home.

## Fade and Encore

Nothing stays golden forever. The festival went dark for stretches — absent in 1979, in 1984, and for six long years from 1986 through 1992 — before returning in 1993 for a third act. In 2004 it added the word "International" to its name and began handing winners a Golden Mermaid Trophy, the Sirenita de Oro, along with 36,000 euros to record an album. Silver and bronze mermaids went to the runners-up. But the magic had thinned. Television had changed, tastes had moved on, and the crowds drifted elsewhere. After its thirty-ninth edition in 2006, the contest quietly closed, its final years passing with barely a ripple in the press that had once celebrated it.

## The Second Life

A place that built itself on reinvention does not stay silent for long. In the summer of 2019, an exhibition marking the festival's sixtieth anniversary drew more than ten thousand visitors in its first month alone — proof that the old songs still stirred something in people who had grown up with them. Then, in 2021, the Spanish public broadcaster RTVE and the regional Generalitat Valenciana announced a full revival. Reborn as Benidorm Fest, the contest returned in 2022 with a new purpose: choosing Spain's entry for the Eurovision Song Contest. That mission has since outgrown itself: when RTVE opted out of the 2026 Eurovision Song Contest, Benidorm Fest carried on anyway, an independent showcase for Spanish pop with no contest in Europe left to feed. The mermaid trophy was gone, but the ambition that had started it all — a coastal town singing to catch the world's attention — had come the whole way back around. For a resort that had spent decades reinventing its beaches and its skyline, reinventing its festival was only fitting, proof that in Benidorm even the past is something you can stage again.

## Flight Context

The former festival grounds sit within Benidorm at roughly 38.534°N, 0.131°W. From the air, Benidorm is unmistakable: a dense cluster of high-rise towers rising from the coastal plain between two crescent beaches, Levante and Poniente, earning the resort its nickname as the Manhattan of the Mediterranean. Best viewed from 3,000–6,000 ft on a clear day, with the headland of the Balcón del Mediterráneo dividing the two bays. Nearest airport is Alicante–Elche (LEAL), about 50 km southwest; Valencia (LEVC) lies farther north up the coast. Coastal haze can soften the skyline on summer afternoons.
