# German submarine U-573

The U-boat that limped into neutral Cartagena to die, was sold to Spain instead, and sailed on as the submarine G-7 until 1970, outliving the Third Reich by a quarter of a century.

**Location:** 37.0000, -1.0000

**Categories:** World War II, Submarines, Kriegsmarine, Spanish Navy, Naval history, Cartagena

## Introduction

Most of the U-boats that went to war for Nazi Germany ended up on the seabed. This one ended up in a Spanish naval yard, wearing a new name and a new flag, and it outlived the Third Reich by a quarter of a century. U-573 fired her torpedoes for Germany, was crippled by British aircraft, and crawled into the neutral harbour of Cartagena to die. Except she did not die. She was sold, rebuilt, and sailed on as a Spanish submarine until 1970.

## The Landeck Boat

U-573 was a Type VIIC, the workhorse design of the German submarine fleet, laid down at the Blohm & Voss yard in Hamburg in June 1940 and commissioned a year later under Kapitänleutnant Heinrich Heinsohn. She carried an unusual honorary name. In 1941 Heinsohn arranged for the Alpine town of Landeck, in the Austrian Tyrol, to adopt the submarine under a popular sponsorship scheme, and the townspeople sent gifts and organised holidays for her crew. From then on she was "U-573 Landeck": a warship of the deep ocean, godchild to a mountain town that lay about as far from any sea as a place in Europe can. It is a small, strange kindness to set beside what these boats were built to do.

## One Ship in Four Patrols

For all her menace, U-573 was not a lucky hunter. She left Kiel in September 1941, crossed into the Atlantic through the gap between Iceland and the Faroes, and ranged almost to the coast of Labrador before turning for occupied Saint-Nazaire. Her second patrol was her only real success: she slipped past the heavily defended Strait of Gibraltar into the Mediterranean, torpedoed the Norwegian steamer Hellen of 5,289 tons on 21 December 1941, then made for Pola on the Adriatic. A third sortie from Pola in early 1942 passed without incident. In four war patrols under the German flag she sank exactly one ship. Whatever the recruiting posters promised, most of her war was long grey days of empty horizon and the constant grinding fear of being found.

## Cornered at Cartagena

Being found is what ended her German career. On 29 April 1942, Lockheed Hudsons of the RAF's No. 233 Squadron caught her on the surface northwest of Algiers and worked her over with depth charges. Badly damaged, she limped north and slid into Cartagena on 2 May. International law gave a warship twenty-four hours in a neutral port to make emergency repairs before internment; Spain, quietly sympathetic to Berlin, granted three months, and the British Embassy in Madrid protested hard. Heinsohn flew to Berlin to argue for his boat, then returned by train through the French border town of Hendaye. In the end the damage was beyond three months of work, and the Kriegsmarine simply sold her to Spain, one day before the deadline expired. The crew, who had lost not a single man in combat, were interned and released in twos and threes. Heinsohn left with the last five in February 1943, took command of another boat, and was dead within months, lost with all his new crew.

## A Second Life as G-7

Spain renamed her G-7 and set about the repairs, which took four years rather than months. The British bombs had done more harm than anyone first realised, German parts were nearly impossible to obtain in the last years of the war, and Spain's own economy was still hollow after its civil war. She was finally recommissioned in November 1947, and despite being an already outdated design she was the most modern submarine Spain owned; her fleetmates dated from the early 1930s. She had no radar and no snorkel. In 1958 a film company rented her to play a U-boat in a war picture. She served on, renumbered again, until she was decommissioned in 1970 after twenty-three years and sold at auction for about $26,500. There was talk of keeping her as a museum. Instead she was broken up for scrap, the last Type VII of her kind to sail.

## Flight Context

Plotted at 37.0°N, 1.0°W, just off the Murcian coast near Cartagena, the great naval harbour where U-573 was interned. From altitude the deep natural anchorage is unmistakable, ringed by defensive hills and reaching inland to the shipyards where G-7 was rebuilt. Nearest airfields are Región de Murcia (LEMI), the former San Javier air base on the Mar Menor (LELC), and Alicante–Elche (LEAL). Visibility along this coast is usually excellent, the sea calm and clear, and the fortified naval city itself remains one of the most historic ports on the whole Spanish seaboard.
