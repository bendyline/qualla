# Battle of Cape Palos (1758)

A night-long duel off Cape Palos in which a Spanish squadron deliberately spared the hull of an Algerian corsair flagship to capture her, and freed fifty-three enslaved Europeans locked in her hold.

**Location:** 36.9186, -0.5273

**Categories:** Naval history, 18th century, Spain, Barbary corsairs, Military history, Cartagena

## Introduction

In the small hours of 10 June 1758, off the rocky headland of Cape Palos, Spanish gunners faced a problem most victors would envy. They were trying not to sink the ship they were pounding. For hours they had hammered the Algerian flagship from a careful distance, aiming to break her masts and rigging while sparing her hull, because they wanted to take her whole. When she finally struck her colours and her survivors were lifted off, the Spaniards found fifty-three chained captives below, men who had no idea that rescue had been coming all night.

## Two Centuries of Raiding

For more than two hundred years the corsairs of the Barbary coast had been the terror of the Christian Mediterranean. Sailing from Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli, semi-independent vassals of the Ottoman sultan, they seized ships and raided coastal villages, and the people they carried off, sold into slavery across North Africa, were a pillar of those states' economies. For Spain, whose southern shore and eastern Levante lay directly in their path, the corsairs of Algiers were a wound that would not close. After the peace of 1748 left the Spanish Navy idle, the Marquis of Ensenada turned it against this old enemy, sending divisions to harry the Barbary coast. In 1751, off Cape St Vincent, one such patrol destroyed the Algerian flagship Danzik. Her consort escaped and took her place as the new flagship of Algiers. Her name was Castillo Nuevo, and seven years later she would sail straight into a Spanish squadron once more.

## The Chase off Cape Palos

On 2 June 1758, three Spanish 68-gun ships of the line, Soberano, Vencedor, and Héctor, stood out from Cartagena under Captain Isidoro García del Postigo to sweep the coast clear of raiders. A week later, at eight in the morning on 9 June, they sighted three suspicious sail near Cape Palos and gave chase. One of the three, a captured prize ship, broke away and ran to the south-southeast, while the two Algerian warships turned to cover her escape: the 60-gun Castillo Nuevo, flagship under Arráez Mahamud Rais, and the 40-gun frigate Caravela, commanded by a European renegade named Achí Mustafá. The Spaniards ran up British colours to close the distance, then hauled them down, raised their own, and opened fire in the late afternoon. Héctor peeled away after the fleeing frigate; the other two bore down on the flagship.

## A Battle Fought at Arm's Length

What followed was less a brawl than a slow, deliberate demolition. Soberano and Vencedor held off at a distance, using the longer reach and heavier weight of their guns to batter the Castillo Nuevo while staying beyond her best reply. It was maddeningly patient work: at that range the shot lost much of its force, and the Algerian crew fought on with stubborn courage hour after hour. The Spaniards switched from solid shot to shrapnel, sparing the hull so they might carry the ship off as a prize. By eleven at night the flagship was a dismasted hulk, her rigging shot away; at midnight the exhausted attackers broke off to rest. At one in the morning Mahamud Rais finally surrendered. As the light came up, Spanish boats pulled alongside the sinking ship to take off whoever still lived.

## The Ledger of the Dawn

The reckoning was stark. Aboard the Castillo Nuevo more than a hundred Algerian sailors had been killed and forty-four drowned; three hundred and six were taken prisoner, Mahamud Rais among them. And in her hold were fifty-three enslaved Europeans, Dutchmen, Germans, and one Irishman, now suddenly free. Spanish losses across both ships came to two dead and eleven wounded, a margin so lopsided that one Spanish historian put it down to the corsairs mishandling their own guns. The frigate Caravela, run down against the African coast by Héctor, was lost when a squall drove the two ships apart; a wreck found days later near Alhucemas was taken for her. The prize that had started the whole affair proved to be a richly laden merchantman out of Hamburg, which is why the corsairs had thrown away two warships to save her. It was the third Algiers flagship Spain had destroyed in that century, and though the raids would not end for another generation, this was one of the blows that began their long decline.

## Flight Context

Plotted at 36.92°N, 0.53°W, this point lies in open water off Cape Palos, the sharp headland southeast of the Mar Menor whose lighthouse and the low reef of the Islas Hormigas just beyond are the clearest landmarks from the air. The coast here bends from the lagoon of the Mar Menor down toward Cartagena's harbour. Nearest airfields are the old San Javier field on the Mar Menor (LELC), Región de Murcia (LEMI), and Alicante–Elche (LEAL). Summer skies are clear and the sea a hard blue; the same waters that swallowed the Castillo Nuevo now draw divers to the marine reserve around the Hormigas reef.
