# La Manga

A ribbon of sand roughly 21 kilometers long, La Manga holds two seas apart — the open Mediterranean on one side, the warm salt lagoon of the Mar Menor on the other.

**Location:** 37.6544, -0.7208

**Categories:** Beaches, Coastal, Resorts, Watersports, Mar Menor, Region of Murcia

## Introduction

Stand in the middle of La Manga and you have a sea on each side. To the east, the open Mediterranean rolls in against the beach. To the west, barely a stone's throw away, lie the still, warm, shallow waters of the Mar Menor. Between them runs a ribbon of sand about twenty-one kilometers long and, in places, only a hundred meters wide — a natural breakwater so slender that a single road carries the whole strip. The name says it plainly: la manga means 'the sleeve,' and the lagoon it wraps is the Mar Menor, the 'Lesser Sea.' On a clear morning the two waters read as different colors entirely, pale turquoise on the lagoon side and deep blue on the other.

## Two Seas, One Sleeve

The Mar Menor is one of Europe's largest saltwater lagoons, all but sealed off from the Mediterranean by the thin bar of La Manga and dotted with five small islands. Because the lagoon is shallow and enclosed, its water warms fast and stays calm, clear, and noticeably saltier than the open sea. The result is a gentler, tamer body of water sitting right beside a wilder one. That contrast is the entire appeal of the place. On one side you can ride Mediterranean swell; a few steps away you can wade far out into shallows as warm and still as a bath, the sandy bottom visible beneath you. Few coastlines in the world let you pick which sea you want simply by crossing the road.

## A Lagoon Made for Sailing

That calm, protected water has made the Mar Menor one of the Mediterranean's great outdoor classrooms. Sailing schools, windsurfing and kitesurfing outfits, catamaran and canoe rentals, and water-skiing courses ring the lagoon, running lessons throughout the year. Beginners love it: the water is shallow and forgiving, the wind is dependable, and the waves are not there to punish a mistake. Miles of well-kept sandy beach line both the lagoon and the Mediterranean shore, and when the wind drops there is tennis to play or a round of football golf, a hybrid of soccer and the fairway, to fill an afternoon. Between the reliable breeze, the flat water, and the year-round sun, La Manga has earned a fixed place on the international watersports circuit, drawing sailors and boarders from across Europe.

## The Bridge That Opens on a Schedule

There is only one way for a yacht to sail from the open Mediterranean into the Mar Menor, and it runs on a timetable. At the Tomás Maestre Marina, boats thread a channel dredged to four meters, crossed by the Puente del Estacio, a swing bridge standing seven meters above the water. To let masts through, the bridge swings open for fifteen minutes once every two hours, from eight in the morning until ten at night. Sailors learn the rhythm quickly: miss the window, and you wait. It is a small, human ritual that governs all the traffic between the two seas, a reminder that the sleeve of sand is a barrier first and a beach resort second.

## Winter Home of Footballers

La Manga's warmth and sunshine draw more than sailors. Just inland at Los Belones, La Manga Stadium fills each winter with football clubs from across Europe, escaping the cold of their home leagues to train under a Murcian sun. The La Manga Cup has been played at the resort's La Manga Club every year since 1999, and the ground hosted matches of the 2000–01 Nordic Football Championship as well as part of the Copa del Sol tournaments in 2011, 2012, and 2013. Beyond the pitches, the resort spreads across golf courses and some fifteen hundred acres of walking and riding trails, all beneath a sky that stays warm and bright for most of the year.

## Flight Context

La Manga runs north–south along 37.65°N, 0.72°W, and from the air it is one of the most legible landforms on the Spanish coast: a pencil-thin bar of sand separating the near-circular Mar Menor lagoon, pale and shallow, from the deeper blue of the open Mediterranean. Fly at 3,000–5,000 ft to take in the whole sleeve, the five lagoon islands, and the Estacio channel where the two seas meet. The closest airport is Murcia–San Javier (LELC), a military air base on the lagoon's southwestern shore just a few kilometers away; Región de Murcia International (Corvera, LEMI) and Alicante–Elche (LEAL) lie further inland and to the north. Skies here stay clear and sunny for most of the year.
