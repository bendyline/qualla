# Isla Grosa

A tiny volcanic island off La Manga that has been a ship-killing reef, a Phoenician graveyard, a pirate refuge, a navy dive school, and now one of Europe's most important seabird sanctuaries.

**Location:** 37.7278, -0.7075

**Categories:** Islands of Spain, Region of Murcia, Extinct volcanoes, Protected areas of Spain, Mediterranean islands, Underwater archaeology

## Introduction

A few meters below the surface off Isla Grosa lies a rock that sailors feared for centuries: the Bajo de la Campana, a hidden ledge that has torn open the hulls of ships since the Iron Age. When archaeologists finally worked the wrecks scattered across its seabed, they pulled up something astonishing from the cargo of a Phoenician vessel that went down in the seventh century BC - a set of African elephant tusks, incised with Phoenician letters, hauled across the sea toward a market that has long since vanished. For an island of barely seventeen hectares, Isla Grosa has been the stage for a remarkable number of lives.

## The Rock That Sank Ships

The Bajo de la Campana sits just east of the island, close enough to the surface to gut a keel and deep enough to stay unseen. Phoenician and Roman wrecks litter the bottom around it, but the seventh-century-BC ship is the prize. Alongside the elephant ivory, divers recovered ingots of tin and lead ore, wooden combs, and the small personal belongings of the men who sailed her - the ordinary gear of a working crew who never made port. The whole assemblage, along with the other cargoes lifted from these waters, is now displayed at the National Museum of Subaquatic Archaeology in Cartagena, where a single reef's worth of disasters becomes a window onto ancient Mediterranean trade.

## Born of Fire

Isla Grosa is what remains of a volcano. During the Miocene, between roughly 7.2 and 6.6 million years ago, eruptions built cones across the Campo de Cartagena, and this island and its small companion islet, El Farallón, are among the survivors. Time and weather have worn the cone down, but its origins are still written in the stone: soils of andesite, and along the cliffs on the southern face, elegant columns of rock formed as molten lava cooled and cracked on its way to the surface. From the sea it reads as a simple dark hump rising more than ninety meters out of the water, an easy landmark that gives no hint of the fire that made it.

## A Sanctuary on the Wing

What the island lacks in size it makes up for in birds. A large colony of yellow-legged gulls shares the rock with European storm petrels, shags, and peregrine falcons - and, most importantly, with Audouin's gull, an elegant, red-billed seabird once among the rarest in the world. Isla Grosa hosts the third-largest breeding colony of the species anywhere on Earth, a bird catalogued as of high conservation concern in Europe. That single fact has shaped the island's fate. It is protected as a natural park, a Special Protection Area for birds, and a Site of Community Importance, its cliffs and scrub left to the creatures that need them. Below the birds, the island's low vegetation - much of it a hardy shrub called oroval - shelters the Iberian skink, a small, secretive lizard that finds on this guarded rock exactly the undisturbed ground it needs.

## Pirates, Divers, and Peace

People have always found uses for this rock. Into the eighteenth century it sheltered Barbary corsairs, a hideout within sight of the coast they raided. Much later the navy claimed it: until the year 2000 Isla Grosa was a military zone, a training ground where combat divers and explosives-disposal teams from the fleet's diving school practiced their exacting, dangerous work. Then, in 2000, the island was handed to the regional government for conservation. Today a pier and a handful of old military buildings are the only marks of human occupation, left to weather among the gulls. An island that spent millennia as a hazard and a hideaway has finally been allowed, simply, to be a refuge.

## Flight Context

Isla Grosa rises at 37.728 N, 0.708 W, about 2.5 km off the northern tip of the La Manga sandbar. Standing more than 90 m high, this eroded volcanic cone and its smaller companion islet El Farallón make an excellent coastal waypoint, easily picked out against open water from 1,500-3,000 ft, with the thread of La Manga and the pale Mar Menor lagoon just inshore. Nearest airport is the military field at San Javier (ICAO LELC) on the Mar Menor; civilian traffic uses Región de Murcia International (Corvera, LEMI) to the west and Alicante-Elche (LEAL) to the north. Note the protected seabird colony below - keep altitude to avoid disturbing nesting gulls.
