# Marchamalo salt flats

At the built-up tip of La Manga, a surviving fragment of salt flats and dunes shelters flamingos, an endemic fish, and a Copper Age fishing village older than the resorts around it.

**Location:** 37.6350, -0.7200

**Categories:** Nature Reserves, Wetlands, Wildlife, Archaeology, Region of Murcia, Coastal Spain

## Introduction

La Manga del Mar Menor is a wall now, a thin sandbar built almost end to end with resort towers dividing the Mediterranean from the lagoon it encloses. But at its southern tip, near Cabo de Palos, a fragment of the coast those towers replaced still holds on. The Marchamalo salt flats, covering 197.8 hectares, and the neighboring Las Amoladeras Beach, another 116, together preserve barely 300 hectares of dune, marsh, and shallow salt pan — a small, stubborn remnant of the wild strip that once ran the entire length of La Manga before the developers arrived. Stand at the water's edge here and the contrast is almost absurd: bird-loud saltmarsh in the foreground, a skyline of holiday apartments behind.

## The Last of the Old Coast

What survives here is a rescue, not a relic. Las Amoladeras protects a small patch of the dune vegetation that once blanketed all of La Manga, and the two areas together carry the weight of several protections at once, holding the status of natural park along with the European labels of Site of Community Importance and Special Protection Area. In 2010 restoration crews went to work, pulling out invasive acacias and agaves and replanting the coast's own vanished species: dune juniper, gone from the area for ages, and mastic shrub, set back into the sand where they had once belonged. It was an act of deliberate memory, coaxing an old landscape back from the edge of forgetting.

## Plants That Drink Salt

Life here has learned to tolerate what would kill most plants. Along the dunes grow sea trefoil, marram grass binding the sand, yellow everlasting, the pale trumpet of the sea daffodil, and spiky sea holly. Down in the saline flats around Marchamalo thrive the halophytes, the salt lovers: glasswort, sea lavender, shrubby seablight. Rarest of all is the Mar Menor asparagus, an endemic of the lagoon area that is critically endangered, clinging to existence in a handful of specimens. Not every story here ends well. The saltmarsh plant almarjo, recorded near Cabo de Palos in the early twentieth century, has since vanished from the region entirely.

## Flamingos and the Fartet

The salt flats are a magnet for birds, and the sight that stops visitors is the greater flamingo, wading pink against the white pans, its reflection doubled in the still, shallow water. It shares the flats with the Audouin's gull, the little egret, the black-winged stilt on its improbable red legs, the pied avocet sweeping its upturned bill through the shallows, and the Kentish plover scurrying at the tideline. Beneath the surface swims a survivor few ever see: the fartet, a small endemic fish native to Spain's southeast and now classed as endangered. In a lagoon system under constant pressure, this modest refuge keeps a foothold for creatures that have almost nowhere else to go. What looks, from the resort towers, like empty ground is in fact one of the busiest addresses on the whole sandbar.

## Four Thousand Years of Fishing

People understood the value of this coast long before anyone thought to protect it. At Las Amoladeras, archaeologists have excavated a prehistoric settlement dating to the close of the Neolithic and the Copper Age, between roughly 2500 and 1800 BC. According to Professor Garcia del Toro, it was a village of circular huts ringed by a fortified wall, akin to one found at Mazarron, home to a community of hunters and gatherers who lived by fishing and gathering shellfish from these same waters. Some of what they left behind now rests in Cartagena's Archaeological Museum, and since 1987 the site has been protected as a place of cultural interest. The lagoon has been feeding people from this shore for four thousand years, and its dwindling wildness is a reason it still can.

## Flight Context

The Marchamalo salt flats lie at 37.635 N, 0.720 W, at the southern end of the La Manga sandbar where it meets the mainland near Cabo de Palos, in the municipality of Cartagena. From the air the shallow rectangular salt pans and marsh read as a pale mosaic pinched between the Mediterranean and the enclosed Mar Menor lagoon, with the long wall of La Manga's resort towers stretching north and the Cabo de Palos headland and lighthouse just to the south as unmistakable markers. Best viewed at 1,500 to 3,500 feet in the clear coastal air; the flamingos are most visible over the bright salt pans. The nearest airport is San Javier (LELC) at the north end of the Mar Menor; Region de Murcia International at Corvera (LEMI) lies inland to the west and Alicante-Elche (LEAL) to the north.
