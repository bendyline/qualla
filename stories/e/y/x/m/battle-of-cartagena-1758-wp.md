# Battle of Cartagena (1758)

In one night off a neutral Spanish port, a British squadron scattered a French relief fleet and quietly sealed the fate of a fortress an ocean away.

**Location:** 37.6000, -0.9833

**Categories:** Naval history, Seven Years' War, Military history of the Mediterranean, Cartagena, 18th century, Spain

## Introduction

Captain Arthur Gardiner already carried a stain he could not wash out. Two years earlier he had commanded Admiral John Byng's flagship at the Battle of Minorca, and when Byng was court-martialed and shot for "failing to do his utmost," the whisper of cowardice clung to every officer who had sailed under him. On the night of 28 February 1758, in the dark water off the Spanish port of Cartagena, Gardiner found his chance to answer it. His ship, the Monmouth, was chasing a French flagship far heavier than herself, and he had told his crew he did not intend to let it escape.

## A Fleet Bottled in a Neutral Port

The chase was the last act of a long, patient siege at sea. In 1756 the French had sailed from Toulon and taken Minorca, and for eighteen months afterward their ships stayed home while British squadrons, working out of Gibraltar, corked the mouth of the Mediterranean. When France resolved in 1758 to send fresh strength across the Atlantic to defend Louisbourg in North America, Admiral La Clue slipped out of Toulon in November 1757 with fifteen ships. A storm on 30 November wrecked the plan, driving him to shelter in Cartagena, a port of neutral Spain. There the British admiral Henry Osborn pinned him in place. Osborn's orders were simple and unglamorous: keep the French from reaching the open ocean. When word came that Michel-Ange Duquesne de Menneville was sailing from Toulon with three ships of the line to reinforce La Clue, Osborn waited offshore for him.

## The Scatter and the Chase

Duquesne came on and saw, too late, how badly the odds ran against him. Rather than fight, he ordered his ships to scatter. Osborn kept the bulk of his force between the harbor mouth and La Clue, making certain the trapped fleet could not sortie to help, then loosed his fastest ships after the fleeing French. The Orphee was run down and overwhelmed by three pursuers. The Oriflamme was deliberately driven aground, wrecked by her own crew rather than surrendered. That left the flagship Foudroyant, Duquesne aboard, running for open water with the Monmouth on her heels. The pursuit stretched into the night. When the Monmouth finally closed and opened fire, Gardiner was killed in the exchange. His crew fought on, and by morning the Foudroyant had struck her colors. Duquesne was taken prisoner, and the battle was over.

## A Fortress Lost an Ocean Away

The fighting off Cartagena lasted a single night, yet its consequences reached across the Atlantic. Duquesne's squadron had been the reinforcement meant to strengthen French defenses around Louisbourg, the great stone fortress guarding the approach to the St. Lawrence in North America. With that relief scattered and captured, only thin help would ever reach it. Osborn judged by July that the season had grown too late for the French to attempt the crossing at all, and he pulled back to resupply. La Clue, reaching the same bleak conclusion, gave up the idea entirely and sailed home to Toulon rather than force the Strait of Gibraltar. Louisbourg fell to British siege on 26 July 1758. A struggle decided in Mediterranean darkness had helped decide the fate of a citadel thousands of miles away.

## The Redemption of a Marked Man

The victory did quiet work on the Royal Navy's wounded pride. The loss of Minorca in 1756 had ended with Byng dead before a firing squad and the service's reputation badly bruised. Cartagena helped restore it. For Arthur Gardiner the redemption was personal and complete: the man who had carried Byng's disgrace died attacking a stronger enemy, and his death answered, once and for all, the charge of cowardice that had shadowed him. The campaign also demonstrated the "tight blockade" the British would press even harder the following year, a relentless style of pressure made possible by new methods of keeping ships supplied while they held their stations at sea.

## Flight Context

The action was fought at roughly 37.60°N, 0.98°W, in the coastal waters off Cartagena, southeastern Spain. From 3,000-5,000 ft the harbor's defining features stand out clearly: the deep, mountain-ringed natural anchorage that made Cartagena a prize for every Mediterranean power, flanked by the castle-topped hills that guarded it. The nearest airports are Región de Murcia International (LEMI) about 30 km northwest and Murcia-San Javier (LELC) on the Mar Menor to the north; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies farther up the coast. Clear, dry conditions are common, giving long views out over the Mediterranean where the fleets once maneuvered.
