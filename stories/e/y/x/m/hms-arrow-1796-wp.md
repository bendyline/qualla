# HMS Arrow (1796)

A sloop so oddly built that enemies could name her from the horizon, HMS Arrow fought from the Dutch coast to Copenhagen to the Ottoman capital before making her last stand off Cape Palos.

**Location:** 37.5833, -0.7167

**Categories:** Naval history, Royal Navy ships, Napoleonic Wars, Shipwrecks of the Mediterranean, Maritime history, Cartagena

## Introduction

Most warships try to blend into the haze until they are close enough to strike. HMS Arrow could not. Built in Southampton in 1796 to an unusual design, she carried a silhouette so distinctive that an enemy lookout could recognize her from far off, and by 1803 that had become a liability serious enough for her captain to take her out of service for a time. It is a strange trait for a fighting ship, this inability to hide. Yet over nine years it never stopped her from being where the fighting was, from the sandbanks of the Dutch coast to a headland on the Spanish shore where her luck finally ran out.

## The Ship at the Vlie

Arrow's war began in earnest in the shallow, treacherous waters off the Netherlands. In August 1799 she shared in the mass surrender of the Dutch fleet at the Vlieter Incident, and the next month she and the brig-sloop Wolverine were sent to clear enemy vessels anchored under the Vlie, at the mouth of the Texel. It went almost bloodlessly. Wolverine anchored within sixty yards of a Batavian brig and fired a single gun before the brig hauled down her colours; Arrow then traded broadsides with the ship Draak, of twenty-four guns, which gave up when Wolverine came alongside. Two schooners, four small schuyts, and 380 prisoners were taken in the operation. Nearly fifty years later, in 1847, the Admiralty would strike a medal clasp for the men who had been there.

## The Quarrel with Denmark

The next year Arrow found herself in a fight that turned into a diplomatic crisis. In July 1800 she was part of a squadron that stopped the Danish frigate Freja, which was escorting a convoy under the flag of neutral Denmark and refused to allow the British to search it. When a British boat pulled toward the convoy, the Danes opened fire on it, and Freja and the British ships traded broadsides for some twenty-five minutes before Freja, badly battered, struck her colours. Arrow lost two men killed and several wounded in the exchange; Freja lost eight. The confrontation ended with Freja and her charges brought into the Downs, and it strained relations with Copenhagen so badly that Britain rushed a special envoy north to head off war. The peace held for only about a year. When it broke, Arrow was there too, present at the Battle of Copenhagen in 1801. She came through without a single casualty, though her sister ship Dart lost two men, and afterward she was chosen to carry the dispatches home.

## A Gift from the Kapudan Pasha

Under Commander Richard Budd Vincent, Arrow sailed for the Mediterranean in 1803, undermanned but busy, threading convoys through the Adriatic and the Aegean and calling at Gibraltar, Malta, Naples, Corfu, Venice, Smyrna (now Izmir), and dozens of smaller ports. In Constantinople (now Istanbul), Vincent toured the Ottoman Imperial Arsenal and met the Kapudan Pasha, commander of the sultan's fleet, who presented him with an elegant sabre; Vincent returned the courtesy with a fine pair of pistols. Not every encounter was so genial. In June 1804, boats from Arrow tried to cut out the French privateer Actif from beneath the cliffs of Fano, a neutral island near Corfu. The raid cost two of Arrow's men their lives, one of them mortally wounded, and prompted a careful letter from Lord Nelson himself, weighing the right to strike an enemy against the law of neutral waters.

## The Last Stand off Cape Palos

By late 1804 Arrow was worn out. Her water tanks, built into her very hull, had failed beyond what the Malta yard could mend, and Nelson ordered her home as escort to a convoy. In February 1805, off Cape Palos on the Spanish coast, she and the bomb-vessel Acheron met two French frigates that badly outgunned them. Arrow turned to fight anyway. Her hold flooded, four of her guns were torn loose, her boats were smashed, and she took heavy casualties before she struck her colours and rolled over, spilling men into the sea. French boats pulled the survivors out. Their sacrifice let almost the whole convoy escape. Vincent was carried into captivity at Cartagena, where a French officer confiscated the sabre the Kapudan Pasha had given him, one small final theft at the end of a long, distinctive ship's life.

## Flight Context

Arrow's final action took place off Cape Palos, near 37.58 degrees N, 0.72 degrees W, in the open Mediterranean southeast of Cartagena on Spain's Costa Calida. Navigate by Cabo de Palos, a prominent rocky point with a tall lighthouse, and by the pale bar of the Mar Menor lagoon just to the north. A viewing altitude of 3,000 to 5,000 feet gives a clear read of the coastline and the offshore waters. The nearest airports are Region de Murcia International (LEMI), about 40 km northwest, and Alicante-Elche (LEAL), roughly 70 km north-northeast. The coast is usually clear and bright, but winter gales like the one that scattered Arrow's convoy still sweep through.
