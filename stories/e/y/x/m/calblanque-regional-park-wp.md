# Calblanque Regional Park

A stretch of golden dune-backed coves and ancient mining hills southeast of Cartagena, Calblanque is one of the last wild pieces of the Spanish Mediterranean shore.

**Location:** 37.6075, -0.7500

**Categories:** Nature, Parks and reserves, Coastal, Geology, Wildlife, Region of Murcia

## Introduction

Drive twenty minutes east from the apartment towers of the Mar Menor and the coastline abruptly forgets the twentieth century. The road narrows, the crowds thin, and then the land opens onto pale gold sand backed by low dunes, dark rocky headlands, and hills that hold the oldest stone in the whole Region of Murcia. This is Calblanque, whose full name, Calblanque, Monte de las Cenizas y Pena del Aguila, is longer than most of its beaches and just as layered.

## A Coast That Escaped the Concrete

Almost everywhere else along this shore, resorts won. Calblanque did not. It survives as one of the best-preserved stretches of the Mediterranean littoral, a place where you can still walk a beach that looks roughly the way beaches here looked before the tour operators arrived. The protection is deliberate, and in summer it is enforced: access is limited so the dunes and coves are not loved to death. The reward for the effort of getting here is a run of small, wild beaches with names that sound like a fisherman's litany, Negrete, Las Canas, Parreno, and the golden Cala de los Dentoles that locals also call Cala Dorada. Between them, rock gives way to sand and back again, shaped by wind and salt into arches, hollows, and the honeycomb pitting geologists call tafoni.

## Old Rock, New Sand

Calblanque is a lesson in deep time compressed into a short coastline. Its hills belong to the Sierra Minera, part of the Baetic mountain system, and they carry two very different kinds of stone: metamorphic rock counted among the most ancient in the whole region, and loose Quaternary sediments that are among the youngest. Weather works constantly on both. The same erosion that gnawed natural arches out of the headlands also ground stone into the sand underfoot, so that a single view can hold the region's oldest geology and its newest, side by side. Slopes, patches of forest, and drifting sand each shelter their own community of life, packed into a compact space between mountain and sea.

## Wings, Scales, and Salt

For its size, Calblanque is generous with wildlife. Along the water's edge and its salt-touched margins you might spot little egrets stalking the shallows, black-winged stilts and elegant avocets picking through the mud, and Kentish plovers skittering across the sand. Overhead the raptors patrol: peregrine falcons, Bonelli's eagles, and the great Eurasian eagle-owl. In the scrub and rock live ocellated lizards, jewel-backed and among Europe's largest, alongside Bedriaga's skink and the Montpellier snake. Badgers and beech martens move at dusk, and in the coastal waters swim the small, threatened Spanish toothcarp. The plant life is just as particular, including the critically rare Cistus heterophyllus, a rockrose clinging to survival, and Asparagus macrorrhizus, a wild asparagus found almost nowhere else.

## The Shadow of the Mines

The name Sierra Minera is not decorative. These hills were mined for lead, silver, and zinc for more than two thousand years, from Phoenician and Roman diggings through an industrial frenzy that reshaped nearby La Union. That history left scars, tailings, and altered ground across the range, which makes Calblanque's survival all the more striking: a working, wounded landscape and a protected wild one lie almost against each other. Standing on a headland here, you see the tension plainly. On one side, the open Mediterranean and a beach that could belong to any century. Behind you, hills that generations of miners tunneled for their wealth. Calblanque is what is left when the sea wins back the coast the industry let go.

## Flight Context

Centered near 37.61 N, 0.75 W, on the coast between Cabo de Palos and Cartagena. Best appreciated from low level, 1,500-3,000 ft, where the golden dune-backed coves stand out sharply against the darker rock of the Monte de las Cenizas and Pena del Aguila hills. The contrast between the wild green-brown headlands and the scarred, pale spoil heaps of the Sierra Minera to the west is the key visual marker. Nearest airport is Murcia-San Javier LELC, only about 15 km northeast on the Mar Menor; Region de Murcia (Corvera) LEMI lies about 40 km northwest and Alicante-Elche LEAL up the coast. Clear, calm mornings give the truest color to the sand and water.
