# Palacio de Aguirre

A mining magnate turned a fortune dug from the hills into a corner palace of glazed tile, ceramic bees, and painted ceilings, one of the jewels of Cartagena's Art Nouveau age.

**Location:** 37.6029, -0.9808

**Categories:** Architecture, Modernisme, Cartagena, Art museums, Historic buildings, Art Nouveau

## Introduction

Look up at the corner of the Palacio de Aguirre and you will find bees. They are worked into the facade in sculpture, small and deliberate, and they are not decoration for its own sake. In the visual language of turn-of-the-century design, the bee stood for prosperity earned through labor, and the man who paid for this building had earned his exactly that way. Camilo Aguirre made his fortune in the mines of the hills behind Cartagena, and here he set it in stone, tile, and glass on one of the city's busiest corners.

## Mining Money Made Beautiful

In the late 19th century, the mineral-laced sierra between Cartagena and La Union poured out silver, lead, and zinc, and a class of newly rich entrepreneurs went looking for ways to display their success. They found it in Modernismo, the Spanish strain of Art Nouveau then sweeping the country in the wake of architects like Gaudi. Aguirre commissioned the palace in 1898 and it was finished in 1901, a compact but lavish statement of what ore could buy. It stands as proof that Cartagena's boom did not only leave scarred hillsides and slag heaps behind. It left beauty, too, concentrated in the streets of the city the mines enriched.

## The Architect Who Shaped a City

The palace came from the drawing board of Victor Beltri, a Catalan architect who did more than anyone to give Cartagena its Modernista face. Across the city and neighboring La Union he left a whole portfolio of the style, from the Gran Hotel and the Casa Maestre to the Casa Llagostera and the public market of La Union, all of it financed by mining money. The Aguirre commission let him work at full stretch. He gave the corner a rounded presence rather than a hard angle, crowned it with a ceramic dome, and set glazed figures of angels along the top. Into the design he folded a mirador, the projecting glassed-in bay window that Cartagena had long loved, letting the owner look out over the street from a warm pocket of light. Every element was chosen to catch the eye of a passerby and hold it.

## Inside the Palace

The interior was made for a life of receptions and display, and several of its finest rooms survive intact. The ballroom is dressed in neo-rococo ornament, and across its ceiling the painter Cecilio Pla spread an allegory of spring, all bloom and lightness above the dancers below. A grand imperial staircase carries visitors upward in a single sweeping gesture, the kind of entrance meant to impress a guest before a word is spoken. There is an office tucked behind the bay window, positioned to command both the desk and the street, and a small oratory built in the neo-Gothic style for private worship. Together they sketch the whole world of a Cartagena magnate at the turn of the century: business, ceremony, and faith arranged under one richly worked roof, with the bees on the facade quietly reminding everyone how it had all been paid for.

## From Palace to Museum

The house of a single family has become the property of the public. Today the Palacio de Aguirre serves as the seat of the Region of Murcia's offices in Cartagena, and since 2009 it has also housed part of the Regional Museum of Modern Art. The building that once broadcast one man's private fortune now shelters art meant for everyone, and its own facade remains the first exhibit, a lesson in ceramic and glass on how a mining city chose to dress itself at the height of its wealth. It is a fitting second life for a house built on the produce of the hills. The ore that paid for the angels and the bees is long gone, but the beauty it bought endures, still stopping people in their tracks on a Cartagena street corner more than a century later.

## Flight Context

The palace stands in central Cartagena at 37.603 degrees N, 0.981 degrees W, a short walk inland from the harbor. From the air, orient on the fortress-ringed bay of Cartagena and its old town; the palace sits within the dense historic core near the Roman theater and the Concepcion Castle hill. Its ceramic dome and rounded corner make it hard to single out from altitude, so this is a landmark best appreciated from the ground. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 3,000 feet is suitable for taking in the old town as a whole. Nearest airports are Region de Murcia International (LEMI), about 35 km northwest, and Alicante-Elche (LEAL), roughly 75 km northeast. Coastal visibility is usually excellent, clearest in the morning.
