# Cartagena Naval Base

For nearly three centuries the Arsenal of Cartagena has built, armed, and repaired the ships of Spain, and its eighteenth-century dry docks still cradle the navy's submarines today.

**Location:** 37.6003, -0.9886

**Categories:** Military history, Maritime, Naval, History, Cartagena, Architecture

## Introduction

The best natural harbor on Spain's Mediterranean coast is nearly landlocked, a deep bay shielded by hills with only a narrow mouth to the sea. Sailors have prized it for three thousand years, but it was an eighteenth-century king who turned it into a machine. Behind long stone walls at the western end of Cartagena's waterfront lies the Arsenal, one of the oldest naval bases in Spain and still the fleet's principal home in the Mediterranean.

## A King's Shipyard

The Carthaginians founded the port, and for most of its long life it stayed a commercial harbor. That changed under Philip V, who chose Cartagena as one of the great arsenals of a rebuilt Spanish Navy. Construction of the complex began in late 1731 and was not finished until 1782, in the reign of Charles III, at the enormous cost of 112 million reales. What rose was not a fort but a factory. By the second half of the eighteenth century the Arsenal was a full industrial city of the sea: slipways and workshops for carpentry, rigging, and blacksmithing, and even fine-arts studios to carve and gild the ornament that made a warship a statement of royal power. Several thousand people worked here.

## Ships Beyond Counting

The output was prodigious. In the latter half of the eighteenth century the yard launched 21 ships of the line and 17 frigates, along with more than fifty brigs, xebecs, hulks, and galleys, and a great swarm of smaller craft. Each of those hulls represented months of coordinated labor: timber cut and steamed and pinned, hemp spun into rope, iron beaten into fittings, canvas stitched into sail. The words on the roster, xebec and hulk and galley, are the vocabulary of a lost age of fighting sail, and every one of them once slid down a Cartagena slipway into the sheltered bay to be fitted out beneath these same hills.

## Iron, Steam, and Electricity

The Arsenal kept pace as the age of sail gave way to iron and steam. It was enlarged under Isabella II in 1849. In 1866 the Cartagena Iron Drydock entered service, a floating dry dock that could lift ships onto three new horizontal slipways. Electricity arrived in 1889. Then, in 1918, came the change that still defines the place: the moats of the old dry docks built by the engineer Feringan were adapted into submarine pens. More than a century later, they remain in that role. It is a rare continuity, eighteenth-century masonry doing twenty-first-century work, the same stone basins that once held galleys now cradling the hulls of the modern silent service.

## The Fleet Today

Cartagena is still a working base, and its resident ships trace the shape of a modern navy. The submarine Galerna, last in active service of the three Galerna-class boats once based here, now shares those historic pens with the new Isaac Peral, lead boat of Spain's next-generation S-80 class. Segura-class minehunters and patrol vessels from Toralla to Furor tie up along the quays. The base even sends ships far beyond the Mediterranean: the polar research vessel Hesperides sails from here toward the ice at both ends of the Earth. Standing on the waterfront, you can read almost three centuries in a single glance, ornamented eighteenth-century stone in front, gray steel hulls alongside, and the same protecting hills that convinced a king to build here in the first place.

## Flight Context

Centered near 37.60 N, 0.99 W, at the western end of Cartagena's harbor. From 2,000-4,000 ft the walled arsenal is unmistakable: rectangular basins, dry docks, and covered slipways set behind the old town, with gray naval hulls moored along the quays and the sheltered bay opening south through its narrow mouth to the sea. Note active military airspace and restrictions over the base. Nearest airports are Region de Murcia (Corvera) LEMI about 30 km northwest, Murcia-San Javier LELC to the northeast, and Alicante-Elche LEAL up the coast. Visibility is usually good; summer afternoons can bring coastal haze.
