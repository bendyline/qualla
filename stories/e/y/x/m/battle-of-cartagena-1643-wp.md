# Battle of Cartagena (1643)

One fair-wind dawn in 1643, a French fleet under Richelieu's young nephew gutted a Spanish squadron off Cartagena and sealed its harbor shut for more than a year.

**Location:** 37.6000, -0.9833

**Categories:** Naval history, Thirty Years' War, Military history, Cartagena, 17th century, Mediterranean Sea

## Introduction

At seven in the morning on 3 September 1643, the wind came from precisely the right quarter, and a French fleet used it. Sails full and guns run out, it fell on the ships of the Spanish crown off Cartagena before they could close into a fighting line. Within hours the sea was strewn with wreckage. Two Spanish galleons had gone to the bottom and two more had been taken as prizes, while the rest crowded for the safety of Cartagena's harbor. It was one of the last bright flourishes of a French navy that had spent two years mastering the western Mediterranean, and the admiral who commanded it was barely past twenty.

## Seven O'Clock, Fair Winds

The attack turned on timing and weather. Coming down with the wind behind him, the French commander Jean Armand de Maillé-Brézé caught the Spanish squadrons still scattered and scattered them further. Ships that cannot form a line cannot support one another, and a fleet that cannot support itself is simply a crowd of targets. Two galleons were sunk outright and two captured. The survivors broke off and ran for Cartagena, whose deep natural harbor had sheltered fleets since Punic times. Getting there did not save them. Eight more vessels were so badly mauled that they were beyond repair, and they settled at their moorings inside the port. In a single morning, with no long battle of attrition, the Spanish Mediterranean squadron had been gutted.

## A Fleet Under Many Flags

The force Maillé-Brézé shattered was a patchwork, as Habsburg fleets often were. It brought together Dunkirkers under Joos Petersen — hard-bitten privateer captains from Dunkirk, in the Spanish Netherlands, whose commerce-raiding had terrorized northern shipping for decades — alongside ships sent up from Naples and a squadron of the Mar Oceano fleet under Martín Carlos de Mencos. On paper it was formidable. In practice, vessels drawn from different ports, commands, and traditions of seamanship were hard to weld into one disciplined line of battle. The Spanish crown ruled a Mediterranean empire that stretched from Iberia to Sicily, and its navy reflected that sprawl: cosmopolitan, far-flung, and difficult to concentrate at the decisive moment.

## The Cardinal's Nephew

The young admiral was no ordinary officer. Jean Armand de Maillé-Brézé was a nephew of Cardinal Richelieu, the architect of French power, and he had risen at a speed only such connections allowed — general of the galleys at twenty, master of the fleet not long after. France's confidence in these waters had been building since victories in 1641 and 1642, and with much of Catalonia in French hands after the Catalan Revolt, Spanish ships scarcely dared show themselves along that coast. Maillé-Brézé had sailed south precisely to hunt. His luck did not hold for long. Three years later, at the Battle of Orbetello off the Tuscan coast, a cannonball carried away his head at the opening of the fight. He was twenty-six.

## A Harbor Sealed Shut

Victory at sea meant little unless it could be pressed, and the French pressed it. With the Spanish survivors bottled up, the harbor mouth was closed off under the Duke of Fernandina, and for more than a year not a single Spanish ship dared leave. The consequences reached far beyond Cartagena. Habsburg Spain governed Naples, Sicily, and Milan, and it depended on the sea lanes to move money, troops, and grain between Iberia and its Italian possessions. With Cartagena shut and French squadrons loose in the western basin, that link went dead. Commerce between Spain and Italy became, for a season, impossible — a quiet strangling worth more than the ships sunk in the opening hour.

## The Tide Turns Back

And yet the triumph proved fragile. The cardinal who had willed this navy into being was already gone — Richelieu had died the previous December, in 1642 — and without his relentless hand the French fleet soon lost its edge. Money dried up, ambition cooled, and the Mediterranean that France had briefly dominated slipped back toward Spanish control. The blockade lifted. The sea lanes reopened. The wider war ground on for years yet, indifferent to a single September morning. What remained was the memory of one dawn attack, executed by a very young man in fair winds, when for a few hours the balance of an ocean tilted the other way.

## Flight Context

The action unfolded in the waters off Cartagena, roughly 37.60°N, 0.98°W, where one of the Mediterranean's finest natural harbors bites deep into the coast behind a screen of hills. From the air the port is unmistakable — a narrow, well-defended mouth guarded by old coastal batteries such as the Castillo de Galeras and the Fuerte de Navidad, opening into sheltered water that still hosts a major Spanish naval base. Fly the coast at 3,000–5,000 ft to trace the harbor entrance and the headlands the fleets fought among; Cabo de Palos and its lighthouse mark the coast to the northeast. Nearest airports are Región de Murcia International at Corvera (LEMI, ~30 km northwest), San Javier on the Mar Menor (LELC, ~25 km northeast), and Alicante–Elche (LEAL, ~75 km north). The region enjoys more than 300 clear days a year, though afternoon sea haze can soften the horizon.
