# Carthago Nova

The ancient city beneath modern Cartagena was Hannibal's springboard toward Rome, a silver mine that made empires rich, and a capital that outlasted the Roman world itself.

**Location:** 37.6000, -0.9833

**Categories:** Ancient history, Roman history, Archaeology, History, Cartagena, Region of Murcia

## Introduction

Sometime in 218 BC, a young Carthaginian general led an army and a column of war elephants out through the gates of a city on the southeast coast of Spain, bound for the Alps and for Italy. His name was Hannibal, and the city he left behind was Qart Hadasht, which the Romans would soon call Carthago Nova. Beneath the streets of modern Cartagena, that ancient city is still being unearthed, layer by astonishing layer.

## Where Hannibal Began

The coast here had traded with Phoenician sailors since at least the eighth century BC, and old accounts link the site to a place called Mastia, home of the Tartessians. But the city proper was founded around 227 BC by the Carthaginian general Hasdrubal the Fair, and it quickly became the greatest Carthaginian city in the peninsula, prized for its strong position, its walls, its harbors, and the silver in the hills behind it. It was, above all, a family project. After Carthage lost Sicily in the First Punic War, the general Hamilcar Barca came to Spain to build a personal dominion for his Barcid clan, far from the meddling of the Senate back home. From this base his sons drew the wealth and the men for the next war. From here Hannibal marched.

## The Silver Mountain

What drew Rome was not glory but metal. The mountains around Cartagena and Mazarron held lead, silver, and zinc, and the first Roman interest in Spain was frankly about profit. Scipio stormed the city in 209 BC and renamed it Carthago Nova; under Julius Caesar it gained Latin rights, and in 44 BC it became a full Roman colony. Its prosperity rested on those mines, and it is here the story turns dark. As many as 40,000 enslaved people are said to have labored in the workings that fed Roman wealth, men and women worked underground in heat and darkness so that ingots of lead and silver could flow out to the empire. The gleam of the city was quarried out of their suffering, and the plain lead ingots that survive in local museums are their unsigned monument.

## A City of Marble

Under Augustus, Carthago Nova was rebuilt as a showpiece. An ambitious program of construction gave it a grand forum and a magnificent Roman theatre, one of the largest in all of Roman Spain, carved into the slope of Concepcion hill. An older amphitheatre lay nearby, its remains still buried beneath the city's later bullring. The city grew important enough to become the seat of a judicial district, and in 298 the emperor Diocletian made it the capital of an entire new province he named Carthaginensis. That Roman theatre, so central to the city's identity, vanished so completely that it was only rediscovered in 1988, hidden under centuries of later building, market stalls, and streets.

## Fish Sauce and Falling Empires

From the second century the city slowly contracted. The mines that had made it were running dry, the eastern districts were abandoned, and the old Augustan forum fell into disuse. Cartagena adapted, as ports do, and turned to garum, the pungent fermented fish sauce Romans loved; the coast filled with vats to make it. Then the empires began to fail in earnest. The Vandals sacked the city around 425 on their way to North Africa. In 461 the emperor Majorian gathered a fleet of 45 ships in the harbor to win that province back, only to see his navy destroyed. Visigoths came, and then the Byzantines under Justinian, who made the city capital of their Spanish province and renamed it Carthago Spartaria. Few cities have outlived so many masters, or left so much of each of them still in the ground.

## Flight Context

Centered near 37.60 N, 0.98 W, over the old town of Cartagena, which sits directly atop the ancient city. From 1,500-3,500 ft the key monument is the semicircular Roman theatre on the seaward slope of Concepcion hill, beside the roofless old cathedral; the circular bullring nearby covers the buried amphitheatre. The deep harbor that drew Carthaginians and Romans alike still frames the whole scene to the south.
