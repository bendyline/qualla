# Port of Cartagena

One of the Mediterranean's finest natural harbors, the Port of Cartagena has drawn Carthaginians, Romans, and modern container fleets to the same sheltered bay for more than two thousand years.

**Location:** 37.5927, -0.9808

**Categories:** Ports and harbours, Cartagena, Maritime history, Mediterranean Sea, Roman archaeology, Transportation

## Introduction

The sea slips into Cartagena through a narrow gap between the hills and opens into a deep, almost fully enclosed bay, screened from the wind and guarded at its mouth by rocky heights. Harbors this good are rare, and this one has never gone unused. Carthaginian galleys, Roman grain ships, Spanish men-of-war, and today's container vessels have all found the same shelter here. Empires have risen and fallen around this water, but the reason they came has stayed exactly the same.

## Carthage's New City

Around 227 BC the Carthaginian general Hasdrubal chose this bay as the base for Carthage's grip on Iberia, founding a stronghold he called Qart Hadasht, the New City. It did not stay Carthaginian for long. During the Second Punic War, in 209 BC, the young Roman commander Scipio, later surnamed Africanus, stormed the city in a swift and daring assault, and it passed into the Roman world as Carthago Nova. For both powers the prize was the same: a harbor that could shelter a fleet and a hinterland veined with silver. The ore dug from the surrounding mountains helped finance the wars that decided who would rule the western Mediterranean.

## Silver in the Hills, Grass on the Slopes

The bay's value was never only the water. The mountains behind it held argentiferous galena, a lead ore threaded with silver, and ancient writers marveled at the wealth the mines produced. Around them grew esparto, the tough Mediterranean grass that people twisted into rope, sandals, baskets, and paper, and that shipped out of Cartagena in bulk for centuries. Harbor, metal, and fiber formed a single economy. Ships arrived to carry away what the land gave up, and the port grew rich as the middleman between the mines in the hills and the markets across the sea. That role repeated itself age after age. Roman galleys, Renaissance galleons, and 19th-century steamers all loaded the same kinds of cargo from the same sheltered water, and even today the ore of the Sierra Minera is part of the port's long memory.

## Two Harbors in One

The modern port has split its labor across two separate basins that sit about five kilometers apart by road, though barely more than a mile by sea. The Cartagena dock, tucked beneath the old town, welcomes the cruise ships and the visitors; more than forty percent of the tourists who come to Cartagena arrive by sea, and among Spanish ports Cartagena ranks eighth for cruise traffic. A few kilometers away lies Escombreras, an industrial harbor built for oil and bulk cargo on a scale the picturesque old quay could never handle. Together they make Cartagena the fourth-busiest freight port in Spain, behind only Algeciras, Valencia, and Barcelona, still trading on its old position astride the great east-west shipping route between the Strait of Gibraltar and the Suez Canal. When the painter Mariano Ramon Sanchez set up his easel here in 1793, he captured a harbor already old in the service of ships; the vessels have simply grown larger since.

## The Argument over El Gorguel

Success brings its own pressures. In 2006 the port authority proposed a vast new container terminal at El Gorguel, an inlet east of the city, at an estimated cost of well over a billion and a half euros. The plan promised to lift Cartagena into the top tier of Mediterranean container ports, but it ran straight into opposition from environmental groups, including the Fundacion Sierra Minera, who warned of the damage a terminal of that size would do to a stretch of unspoiled coast. The debate captures the port's eternal tension in modern form: a harbor is a machine for moving the world's goods, and also a piece of a living coastline that people are unwilling to lose.

## Flight Context

The port centers on the bay of Cartagena at 37.593 degrees N, 0.981 degrees W, on Spain's Costa Calida. From the air it is unmistakable: a deep, nearly landlocked bay reached through a narrow entrance flanked by fortified headlands, with the industrial harbor of Escombreras a few kilometers to the east and the small island of Escombreras offshore. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 4,000 feet gives the best sense of the harbor geometry and the two separate basins. Nearest airports are Region de Murcia International (LEMI), about 35 km northwest, and Alicante-Elche (LEAL), roughly 75 km northeast. Coastal visibility is generally very good; morning light gives the cleanest view of the bay before summer haze thickens over the water.
