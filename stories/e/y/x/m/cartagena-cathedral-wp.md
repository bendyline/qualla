# Cartagena Cathedral

The roofless shell of Santa Maria la Vieja on Concepcion hill has stood in ruins since Civil War shells found it in 1939, a cathedral that lost first its rank and then its roof.

**Location:** 37.5992, -0.9846

**Categories:** History, Churches and cathedrals, Ruins, Spanish Civil War, Cartagena, Architecture

## Introduction

On the hill of La Concepcion, above the old town of Cartagena and beside the honey-colored tiers of the excavated Roman theatre, stands a church with no roof. Broken arches frame open sky, and grass grows where a congregation once knelt. This is Santa Maria la Vieja, the old cathedral of Cartagena, and it has been a ruin since 1939, when the shells of the Spanish Civil War came down on it during the final agonies of the fighting.

## A See Worth Fighting Over

Cartagena's church claim runs deep. The city held an important bishopric long before the Muslim conquest of the eighth century, though no trace of that earliest cathedral has ever been found. When Alfonso X of Castile took Murcia in the thirteenth century, he petitioned Pope Innocent IV to restore the Diocese of Cartagena, and in 1250 it was granted. For decades the church here was known simply as the Iglesia Mayor, the main church, and only later generations would call it the Old Cathedral. There is even a tradition that Alfonso's short-lived naval order, the Military Order of Santa Maria de Espana, set up around 1270 in a Cistercian monastery in the city, may have been based here.

## The See That Moved

Cartagena's problem was Murcia. After Bishop Pedro Gallego died, his successor, an elected leader named Garcia Martinez who was never actually consecrated, sought to move the seat inland to Murcia while keeping the old name of the diocese. The church on Concepcion hill lost its cathedral rank and became an ordinary parish. In 1291, under Bishop Diego Martinez Magaz and with the consent of King Sancho IV, the transfer was formalized, and the bishop's chair and chapter went to Murcia for good, even as the diocese kept the title Carthaginensis. The city council never accepted it. For centuries afterward, Cartagena petitioned the Vatican to bring the see home, a grievance so durable that the demand is still occasionally heard today.

## Faith Around a Statue

Even without its rank, the old cathedral remained woven into the life of the port. It was tied to one of the four brotherhoods that carry Cartagena's famous Holy Week processions, the Cofradia del Socorro, which gathered around a figure of Christ Crucified known as the Christ of Succour. The story of the brotherhood's founding is intimate rather than grand. In 1691 an admiral of the Spanish Navy, Pedro Manuel Colon de Portugal, established it as an aristocratic confraternity after one of his sons had, two years earlier, been cured in a way the family could only call miraculous, a recovery they credited to that very statue. Devotion here was measured not in the size of the building but in the weight of such personal debts.

## Ruins on the Hill

The building that stands broken today was never Cartagena's grandest church by design; by the eighteenth century a larger new church was rising nearby to press the city's cathedral ambitions. But it was the old one on Concepcion hill that the twentieth century destroyed. In 1939, as the Civil War ground to its end, Nationalist shelling wrecked Santa Maria la Vieja, and it has stood open to the weather ever since. Now it shares its hilltop with the Roman theatre unearthed beside it, two ruins from opposite ends of the city's story, one from the age of empire and one from a war within living memory. Together they make Concepcion hill a place where Cartagena's centuries lie exposed to the sky.

## Flight Context

Centered near 37.60 N, 0.98 W, on La Concepcion hill overlooking the old harbor of Cartagena. Best seen from low level, 1,500-3,000 ft: look for the roofless masonry shell immediately beside the semicircular tiers of the restored Roman theatre, with the hilltop Castillo de la Concepcion just above. The harbor and cruise dock lie to the south. Nearest airports are Region de Murcia (Corvera) LEMI about 30 km northwest, Murcia-San Javier LELC to the northeast, and Alicante-Elche LEAL up the coast. The open ruin reads best in raking morning or late-afternoon light.
