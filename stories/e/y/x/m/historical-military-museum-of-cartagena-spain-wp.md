# Historical Military Museum of Cartagena

Inside an 18th-century artillery park, Cartagena keeps Spain's largest gun collection, an unexploded shell from a forgotten rebellion, and the staircase where a colonel fell.

**Location:** 37.6046, -0.9854

**Categories:** Military and war museums, History museums, Cartagena, Spanish Army history, Artillery, Historic buildings

## Introduction

In the central courtyard of the museum sits a shell that never went off. It was fired during the Cantonal Uprising of 1873, when Cartagena declared itself an independent state and the Spanish government besieged its own city to bring it back. The projectile struck, buried itself, and simply waited. More than a century later it is a museum piece, cleaned and labeled, resting a few meters from the arches where it landed. It is a fitting object to greet visitors, because this whole building has spent two and a half centuries absorbing the wars of Spain and refusing to be destroyed by them.

## Vodopich's Fortress of Iron

The architect Mateo Vodopich raised this building between 1777 and 1786, in the reign when Cartagena was becoming one of the Mediterranean's great naval arsenals. It was designed not as a museum but as a working machine: the Royal Artillery Park, where cannon were cast, stored, and repaired for the fleet and the fortifications above the harbor. The original plan was a strict rectangle of 17,302 square meters, four pavilions around a central block that split it into two bodies, each with its own courtyard. Only the Patio of Weapons, also called the Ironworks, survives whole today. Twenty-four round arches enclose it, and the entire ground floor still revolves around that paved square, exactly as the artillerymen who worked here would have known it.

## A Staircase Where a Colonel Fell

Read the list of conflicts this building lived through and you read the turbulent modern history of Spain: the war against Napoleon, three separate sieges of the city in the 19th century, and finally the Spanish Civil War. The war left its sharpest mark on an ordinary flight of stairs. On March 7, 1939, in the final convulsions of that war, the 206th Mixed Brigade of the Republican People's Army stormed the position. Colonel Gerardo Armentia Palacios, commander of the artillery regiment, died on the first landing of the interior staircase. Visitors climb those same steps today to reach the upper floor. The museum does not dramatize the spot. It simply tells you what happened there, and lets the worn stone do the rest.

## Santa Barbara and Her Guns

Presiding over the collection is Santa Barbara, patron saint of artillerymen, in an 18th-century image the museum attributes to the sculptor Roque Lopez, a disciple of the great Murcian master Francisco Salzillo. Around her stand the guns she is meant to protect. This is the largest collection of 20th-century artillery in Spain and the largest ammunition collection in the country, drawn from Italian, French, German, Russian, Spanish, and British origins. Nearby sit the busts of Luis Daoiz and Pedro Velarde, the artillery officers who led the doomed resistance at Madrid on the second of May, 1808, and the old regimental mortar nicknamed El Jilguero, the goldfinch. In a quieter case, a German Enigma cipher machine waits, a reminder that not every weapon of war was made to be fired.

## The Friends Who Rebuilt the Guns

A museum of heavy iron is only as good as the hands that keep it. Since 1996 the Association of Friends of the Museum has done exactly that, and its work has an international flavor that suits a Mediterranean port. When a StuG III assault gun arrived in 2002, British volunteers helped restore it to running condition, and today people of many nationalities donate labor, expertise, and money to keep the pieces intact. Upstairs, a separate obsession sprawls across its own rooms: a miniature collection donated by the Cartagena native Francisco J. Sanchez Abril, holding thousands of figures and dioramas. It has earned two Guinness World Records, including one for the largest collection of its kind on Earth.

## The Plaza Fuerte, Explained

The museum opened to the public on June 11, 1997, a delegated section of the Historical Military Museum of Seville, spreading about 3,520 square meters of exhibits across two floors. Its most useful gift to a first-time visitor is the permanent exhibition Cartagena Plaza Fuerte 1503-1996, which uses dioramas, models, and projections to explain how the batteries, walls, and forts once ringed the naval base below. Walk out afterward and the abstractions become concrete: the same headlands the models depict still guard the harbor, and the city you see is the one those guns were built to defend.

## Flight Context

The museum stands at 37.605 degrees N, 0.985 degrees W, in the old town of Cartagena just inland from the naval harbor. The best visual anchor is the deep, sheltered bay of Cartagena itself, flanked by fortified headlands, with the Roman theater and the Concepcion Castle hill immediately adjacent to the museum. A viewing altitude of 2,500 to 4,000 feet gives a clear read of the harbor geometry the museum's exhibits explain. Nearest airports are Region de Murcia International (LEMI), roughly 35 km to the northwest, and Alicante-Elche (LEAL), about 75 km to the northeast. Coastal haze is common on summer afternoons; clearest light and visibility over the port come in the morning.
