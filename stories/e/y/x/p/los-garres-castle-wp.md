# Los Garres Castle

A weathered hilltop fort above the Murcian plain that may have guarded the last western frontier of an empire ruled from Constantinople.

**Location:** 37.9508, -1.0891

**Categories:** Archaeological sites, Castles in Spain, Byzantine Empire, Late antiquity, Region of Murcia, Roman Hispania

## Introduction

The empire that likely fortified this hill above the Murcian plain answered to an emperor in Constantinople. In the sixth century, while most of the Iberian Peninsula belonged to the Visigoths, a ribbon of its southern coast was governed from the far end of the Mediterranean: the province of Spania, the last western foothold of the Eastern Roman Empire. Los Garres Castle stands on the ground where that world met its rivals, a modest late-Roman stronghold on the northern slopes of the Cresta del Gallo, watching the pass that led down toward the sea and the Byzantine coast beyond.

## A Hill Worth Holding

People had found reasons to climb this hill long before anyone raised a wall on it. Fragments of terra sigillata, the glossy red tableware that traveled everywhere Rome did, place the first known occupation here in the 2nd and 3rd centuries AD. In the second half of the 4th century, builders cut a series of reservoirs into the site to hold water through the dry Murcian summers. Only later did the hill become a true fortress. When the walls of the acropolis went up, they sliced straight through one of those older reservoirs, destroying it and leaving two more stranded outside the new defensive line. The result is a place layered on top of itself, each generation reworking what the last had left behind.

## The Frontier of Spania

The story turns on geography. Between 552 and 624 AD, the Eastern Roman Empire held a strip of southern Iberia as the province of Spania, ruling it from coastal cities including nearby Cartagena, which the Byzantines renamed Carthago Spartaria. Those fortified towns were bulwarks against a Visigothic kingdom that could ravage the open countryside almost at will but proved inept at sieges, so the walled centers stayed safe seats of Roman administration while the fields around them burned. Los Garres, archaeologists suggest, was a Byzantine fortress woven into that defensive web around the Campo de Cartagena, positioned to command the nearby Garruchal Pass and the traffic that moved through it. The shape of its gateway offers a clue to its age: it resembles the gate at Begastri, another late-antique site in the region, pointing to a construction date in the 6th century, precisely when the frontier mattered most.

## What the Diggers Found

The finds are small, but they speak. Excavations have turned up objects of Visigothic date, though not necessarily of Visigothic making: a single glass necklace bead, fragments of carved latticework and columns, and two small jugs recovered in 1940. Together they show the hill was still lived on into the 7th century, long after the reservoirs were first dug. Not every scholar reads the ruins the same way. An alternative interpretation ties the site less to the Byzantine defensive line and more to the scatter of late Roman and Visigothic settlements in the foothills nearby, among them the early Christian basilica excavated at Llano del Olivar. Whichever is true, the hill sat at a seam where two civilizations pressed against each other.

## The Ground It Still Commands

Today the fortress is a quiet archaeological site in the pedanía of Los Garres, in the district known as Quijal de La Vega, on the flanks of a rugged limestone ridge the locals call the Cresta del Gallo, the cockscomb. It was studied in detail by the archaeologist Gonzalo Matilla Seiquer, whose 1988 monograph for the University of Murcia framed it as a late fortress guarding the vega. Its importance faded as the frontier it once guarded dissolved into history, and its walls survive as low, sun-bleached lines of stone rather than towering ramparts. Yet the vantage remains what it always was. From here the land falls away toward Murcia's broad green vega, and the eye follows the same corridor that once decided who held this coast and who merely raided it.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 37.95°N, 1.09°W, on the northern slopes of the Cresta del Gallo range just south of the city of Murcia. From 3,000-4,500 ft the ridge reads as a sharp limestone spine dividing the fertile Segura river vega to the north from the drier Campo de Cartagena to the south, with the Garruchal Pass threading between them. The nearest airport is Región de Murcia International (LEMI), roughly 20 km southwest; Murcia-San Javier (LELC) lies near the Mar Menor coast, and Alicante-Elche (LEAL) is farther northeast. The region's clear, arid skies make the terraced hillsides and pale rock of the sierra easy to pick out.
