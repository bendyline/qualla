# Mauritania

A land where the Sahara meets the Atlantic, home to the world's longest train, ancient caravan cities, and one of the great bird sanctuaries on Earth.

**Location:** 21.0000, -11.0000

**Categories:** Countries in Africa, Sahara, Islamic Republics, Travel guides

## Introduction

Picture a train so long you cannot see both ends of it, hauling iron ore across the open Sahara, with travelers perched on top of the cargo, scarves wrapped against the dust. That train is Mauritania in a single image: vast, harsh, improbable, and quietly magnificent. Here the great desert runs straight into the Atlantic, dunes spilling onto beaches where millions of birds gather. Between the two lies a country of ancient stone cities and one of the world's last truly open horizons.

## Desert and Ocean

Mauritania is a land defined by two extremes meeting. Inland, the Adrar and Tagant plateaus deliver exactly the Sahara of the imagination: endless ergs of dune and regs of rocky desert, broken by flat-topped mountains and hidden oases that have watered caravans for centuries. On the coast, the Banc d'Arguin National Park is something stranger and rarer, a stretch where the dunes end in the sea and vast mudflats host more than two million migrating shorebirds from northern Europe, Siberia, and Greenland, alongside breeding flamingos, pelicans, and terns. Off this same coast, at Nouamghar, fishermen famously work with wild dolphins, signaling them to herd fish into the shallows.

## Cities of the Caravan

Long before borders, this was a crossroads of the medieval world. Southeastern Mauritania held the heart of the Ghana Empire, one of the earliest urbanized civilizations in western Africa, with its capital at Koumbi Saleh. The trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt built a string of desert cities, four of which are now a single UNESCO World Heritage Site: Ouadane, Chinguetti, Tichitt, and Oualata, founded between the 11th and 12th centuries. Chinguetti remains a place of pilgrimage and scholarship, its architecture little changed in nearly a thousand years, its libraries still holding manuscripts that crossed the desert by camel. To walk these towns is to walk the routes that once carried half of Africa's gold to the Mediterranean.

## The Longest Train on Earth

No experience captures Mauritania like the iron ore train. Stretching up to two and a half kilometers and sometimes more, it is the longest and among the heaviest trains in the world, running on a single line between the mines of Zouerat, deep in the Sahara, and the port of Nouadhibou on the coast. Built in the 1960s, it exists to move ore, but Mauritanians and a growing trickle of travelers ride it too, in a lone passenger carriage or, more dramatically, perched in the open iron hoppers. The full journey runs around twenty hours through scorching, wind-scoured desert. It is uncomfortable, dusty, and unforgettable, the closest thing the modern Sahara has to a public adventure.

## The People of the Sand

Mauritania is an Islamic Republic, one of only a handful in the world and the only one in Africa, and Islam shapes nearly every part of daily life. Its people reflect the meeting of desert and Sahel: the Bidhan or Moors of the north, the Haratin who form the largest single group, and the Fula, Soninke, Bambara, and Wolof communities of the greener south. The vernacular is Hassaniya Arabic, rich with Berber loanwords, and French lingers from the colonial era. Hospitality is a serious matter here. Tea is poured in three rounds, slowly, and to share it in a family's home, sitting on a mat as the desert cools outside, is to glimpse the country at its warmest. Travelers should plan carefully, as parts of the interior carry real security risks, but the welcome in the south is genuine and unforced.

## Flight Context

Mauritania spans a vast swath of northwest Africa; its geographic center sits near 21 degrees N, 11 degrees W. Principal gateways are Nouakchott-Oumtounsy International (GQNO, with GQNN also serving the capital) on the Atlantic coast and Nouadhibou International to the north, terminus of the iron ore railway. Inland fields include Atar (for the Adrar ksour), Tidjikja (GQNT) in the Tagant, and Néma (GQNF) in the southeast. From altitude the country reads as endless dune and rock, with the green smudge of the Senegal River valley along the southern border and the pale coastal mudflats of Banc d'Arguin between Nouakchott and Nouadhibou. Dust from the harmattan can sharply reduce visibility in the long dry season.
