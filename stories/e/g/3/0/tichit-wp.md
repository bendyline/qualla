# Tichit

A stone caravan town at the foot of the Tagant Plateau, slowly disappearing beneath the dunes that once made it rich.

**Location:** 18.4404, -9.4941

**Categories:** Communes of Mauritania, World Heritage Sites in Mauritania, Archaeological sites in Mauritania, Ksars, History of the Sahara

## Introduction

The sand is winning. Walk the lanes of Tichit today and you pass doorways half-swallowed by dunes, rooms with sand drifted to the lintel, whole quarters that families abandoned when the desert finally reached their thresholds. Yet the walls themselves refuse to vanish. Built from the pale stone of the Tagant cliffs and laid in patterns that no other town in the Sahara repeats, the houses of Tichit hold their geometry even in ruin. This is a place that has been outlasting its own ending for a very long time.

## A Door on the Caravan Road

Tichit was founded in the 12th century as a stop on one of the great arteries of the medieval world: the trans-Saharan trade route that linked Oualata in the south to Chinguetti in the north, and beyond them to Sijilmasa at the desert's far Moroccan edge. Camels carried salt south and gold north, and towns like Tichit lived on the margin between them, offering water, shade, dates, and a night's safety. For centuries the Azer dialect of Soninke was the language of its streets, spoken here from the era of the Wagadou Empire until the early 20th century. To arrive at Tichit was to find the desert briefly tamed, a grid of cool stone rooms set against an ocean of dune and rock.

## The Decorated Stone

What sets Tichit apart is the masonry. Its builders dressed the local sandstone into thin, careful blocks and arranged them into triangles, diamonds, and bands set directly into the facades, so that the decoration is the wall rather than a coat applied over it. Doorways are framed with the same patient stonework, and a handful of carved and studded wooden doors survive as the town's quiet treasures. It is a vernacular architecture in the truest sense: a style invented on the spot, from what the cliffs provided, by people solving the same problems of heat and sand their descendants still face. UNESCO recognized that achievement, listing Tichit in 1996 among the four Ancient Ksour of Mauritania.

## Treasures Against the Wind

Tichit was self-sufficient and home to several thousand people in the early 20th century, with a small museum and libraries holding manuscripts that traveled the same routes the salt and gold once did. Then the desert pressed in. Decades of drought and advancing sand emptied house after house, and in 1999 torrential rains, rare and violent here, flooded the town and damaged an estimated eighty percent of its structures. The people who remain farm dates in the palm groves below the cliffs and shore up what they can. Tichit has been called the living ghost of yesterday's glory, but the phrase undersells it: ghosts do not still grow dates, mend walls, or keep the museum's doors open.

## Deep Time Below

The cliffs that shelter Tichit also hold something far older. Just beyond the modern village lie the Neolithic sites of Dhar Tichitt, drystone settlements raised around 2000 BC by the agropastoral communities of the Tichitt tradition. They are the oldest surviving archaeological settlements in West Africa and the oldest stone-built sites south of the Sahara, thought to be the work of proto-Soninke people and a possible precursor to the Ghana Empire. To stand in Tichit is to stand at a hinge: a 12th-century town built in stone, beside ruins where the very idea of building in stone south of the Sahara may have begun, four thousand years before.

## Flight Context

Tichit sits at 18.44 degrees N, 9.49 degrees W, at the foot of the Tagant Plateau in central Mauritania. The town's two unpaved runways (Tichitt Airport) lie about 1 km southeast of the village, marking it against an expanse of dune and sandstone cliff. The nearest sizeable airfields are Tidjikja (GQNT) to the west and Néma (GQNF) to the southeast; Nouakchott (GQNO/GQNN) lies on the Atlantic coast far to the west. Recommended viewing altitude is roughly 3,000 to 5,000 feet AGL for the pale stone town against the dunes; the long northern cliff of the Hodh depression is the dominant landmark. Visibility is best outside the dust-laden harmattan season.
