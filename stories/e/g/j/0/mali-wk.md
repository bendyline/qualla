# Mali

Landlocked at the edge of the Sahara, Mali holds the gold-and-salt legacy of medieval empires, the mud mosques of Djenné and Timbuktu, and a musical tradition that rivals any on Earth.

**Location:** 17.0000, -4.0000

**Categories:** Countries of Africa, West Africa, Sahel, Sahara, Mali

## Introduction

The richest person who ever lived was born here. Mansa Musa, the 14th-century emperor of Mali, ruled a kingdom so flush with gold that when he passed through Cairo on pilgrimage, he gave away so much of it that he reportedly destabilized the region's economy for years. That wealth came from this land - from gold mined in the south and salt cut from the northern desert, traded weight for weight across the Sahara. Today Mali is one of the poorest countries on Earth. Between those two facts lies one of the most remarkable stories in West Africa.

## Empires of Gold and Salt

Three great empires rose on this ground, each controlling the trans-Saharan trade in gold, salt, and other commodities. The Ghana Empire came first, dominant until 1078. Then the Mali Empire formed along the upper Niger and reached its height in the 14th century under Mansa Musa, whose fortune funded mosques that still stand. As Mali declined, the Songhai Empire rose to replace it, until a Moroccan invasion shattered it in 1591. These were not kingdoms of rigid borders or single ethnic identities but fluid Sahelian states bound by trade and faith. Under their rule, the cities of Djenné and Timbuktu became centers not just of commerce but of Islamic scholarship - places where, for a time, books were worth more than gold.

## Cities of Mud and Memory

Mali's architecture is made of earth, and it is breathtaking. In Djenné stands the Great Mosque, rebuilt in 1907 entirely of mud, with five stories and three towers - the largest mud-brick building in the world. Each spring, the whole community gathers to replaster it by hand, an act of renewal that has become a festival in itself. Seen from a rooftop, Djenné is all soft texture and rounded lines, melancholic and strange. Northward lies Timbuktu, the legendary city of trans-Saharan trade and learning, its name a byword in the West for the ends of the earth. Mali holds four UNESCO World Heritage sites, including the cliff-side villages of the Bandiagara Escarpment in Dogon Country, where a whole way of life clings to the rock.

## The Music Capital of West Africa

If Mali is poor in money, it is extravagantly rich in music. Its capital, Bamako - among the fastest-growing cities in Africa - has a fair claim to be the music capital of the entire region. From here come the desert blues of the Tuareg, the river-soaked guitar of Ali Farka Touré, the powerful voice of Oumou Sangaré, and a tradition of griots - hereditary musicians and oral historians who have kept the stories of families and kingdoms alive for centuries. The kora, the ngoni, the djembe, the balafon: these instruments and the masters who play them have carried Malian sound to stages around the world. To learn drumming in Mali is to study at the source.

## The River and the Desert

Mali's life is written by water and its absence. The Niger River curves through the country in a great arc, and each rainy season it floods to create the Inner Niger Delta - a giant inland lake blooming on the edge of the Sahara, where the river splits into a maze of channels across a vast floodplain. People travel it by pirogue, the slender wooden canoe, and by pinasse, its larger motorized cousin. North of the river, the land hardens into Sahel and then into true desert, where droughts are frequent and the dunes of the Sahara take over. Between them lies a band of savanna. The country's climate is a gradient from green to gold to bare sand, and Malian life follows it.

## A Nation Under Strain

Mali's recent history has been painful, and it deserves to be told plainly. The Sudanese Republic gained independence from France in 1960 and, after Senegal withdrew from a brief federation, became Mali that September. Decades of dictatorship gave way to democracy in 1992 - but in 2012, civil war split the country in two. Islamist militants seized the north, banning music and ordering the destruction of revered shrines in a region that had safeguarded them for centuries. Further coups followed. For Malians, this has meant displacement, danger, and the silencing of cultural traditions that define them. The country today is largely unsafe for travel. Yet the empires, the mosques, the river, and above all the music endure - the inheritance of a people who have weathered far more than this, and who carry their history in song.

## Flight Context

Mali is a landlocked Sahelian nation centered near 17.00°N, 4.00°W, sprawling from savanna in the south to deep Sahara in the north. The Niger River is the dominant landmark, arcing northeast from Bamako through Mopti and toward Timbuktu before bending southeast to Gao - and forming the seasonal Inner Niger Delta, a vast braided wetland visible from altitude in the wet season. Principal airports include Bamako (GABS) in the southwest, Timbuktu (GATB), and Gao (GAGO) in the north. The terrain shifts from green river floodplain to ochre Sahel to bare desert as you fly north. Visibility is generally excellent but harmattan dust can blanket the country in the dry season. Note that much of Mali, especially north and east of Mopti, is an active conflict zone unsafe for travel.
