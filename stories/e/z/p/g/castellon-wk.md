# Castellón

A calm, flat provincial capital on the Coast of Orange Blossom, where a lone bell tower presides over a bilingual city you can cross by bicycle.

**Location:** 39.9831, -0.0331

**Categories:** Cities, Coastal, Travel, Spain

## Introduction

In spring, the scent arrives before the city does. Castellón de la Plana sits at the centre of the Costa del Azahar - the Coast of Orange Blossom - and for a few weeks each year the orchards ringing the town fill the air with a sweetness you can catch from the road. This is a calm, flat, unhurried place of roughly 184,000 people, a provincial capital that has never traded its ease for spectacle. The Mediterranean is a short ride east; the mountains rise close behind. In between lies a city content to be pleasant.

## A City You Can Cross by Bike

Castellón is so small and so level that the best way to see it is on two wheels. The city runs a bike-sharing scheme, Bicicas, that costs about two euros for a day, six for a week, or ten for a month, and the flat grid of the old centre makes pedalling effortless. The name says as much: de la Plana means "of the plain," and the town sprawls comfortably across it. Streets open onto the arcaded Plaça Major, the market hall, and the town hall, all within an easy loop. There are no hills to conquer here, only distance to enjoy at whatever pace suits the afternoon.

## El Fadrí

The landmark that fixes Castellón in the mind is El Fadrí, the octagonal bell tower standing in the heart of the old town. Its name means "the bachelor," a nod to the way it stands apart - detached from the co-cathedral it serves, rising alone above the rooftops. Free-standing bell towers are unusual, and Castellón's has become the city's signature, the point every skyline photograph returns to. During the city's great spring festival, the tower is lit in a pyrotechnic display that Castellón calls the Enfarolà del Campanar, turning the lone bachelor into a beacon over the plain.

## Two Tongues, One Plain

Castellón keeps two official languages, Valencian and Spanish, and both are woven through daily life - on street signs, in shops, across conversations that switch tongues mid-sentence without anyone breaking stride. The setting helps explain the city's temperament. It lies far enough north that summers never turn punishing, and far enough south that winters stay gentle, and the sea keeps the whole plain mild. It is the kind of climate that produces easygoing places, and Castellón obliges: a bilingual, temperate city that seems in no hurry to be anywhere else. Even the way out reflects that unhurried temper: the A-7 motorway ties Castellón to Valencia in one direction and to Tarragona in the other, keeping the bigger cities within reach without ever quite pulling the town into their orbit.

## Where the Palms Meet the Sea

Castellón is hemmed by two very different landscapes. To the north rises the oddly named Desert de les Palmes - a range of low, wooded hills rather than any true desert, its "desert" recalling the hermits who once sought solitude on its slopes. To the east, past the orange groves, the city meets the water at its port district, the Grau, where beaches and quiet harbours front the Mediterranean. You can stand in the same city and choose between pine-shaded ridges and open sand, with the perfume of orange blossom drifting over both. That easy reach - mountains one way, sea the other - is Castellón's quiet luxury.

## The Orange City

The oranges are more than scenery. Castellón grew prosperous on citrus, and the fruit still shapes the province's landscape and economy, the orchards stitching town to town across the plain and lending the whole coast its fragrant name. Down at the Grau, the port keeps the city's working relationship with the sea, and from there the beaches run north and south along the Mediterranean. It is an unpretentious kind of wealth - not the glamour of Spain's flashier coasts, but a steady, green, well-fed comfort. Visitors who arrive expecting a resort tend to find something better: a real city that happens to sit between mountains, sea, and a million orange trees, going about its business at its own agreeable pace.

## Flight Context

Castellón de la Plana lies on the coast at 39.98°N, 0.03°W. From the air the flat grid of the city stands out against the orange groves of the plain, with the octagonal El Fadrí tower marking the centre and the Grau port district on the shoreline to the east. The wooded Desert de les Palmes range rises just to the north. Castellón-Costa Azahar (LECN) is the local airport a short distance north; Valencia's LEVC lies roughly 65 km south, with Alicante-Elche LEAL farther down the coast. A viewing altitude of 2,500-4,000 ft AGL frames the city between mountains and sea. The coastal setting keeps weather mild and visibility generally clear.
