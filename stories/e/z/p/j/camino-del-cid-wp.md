# Camino del Cid

A 1,500-kilometre route across Spain that follows a medieval warlord into exile - not by history exactly, but by the epic poem that immortalized him.

**Location:** 40.3446, -1.1083

**Categories:** History, Hiking, Cultural Tourism, Medieval, Spain

## Introduction

You can carry a medieval safe-conduct pass across modern Spain and have it stamped town by town, exactly as a traveller would have done eight hundred years ago. It is the credential of the Camino del Cid, a route of roughly 1,500 kilometres that follows a warrior into exile - not the real route, exactly, but the one an anonymous poet imagined for him around the year 1200. Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar, the eleventh-century knight the world remembers as El Cid, rode east from Castile more than nine hundred years ago. His legend has been drawing travellers along this diagonal ever since.

## The Man and the Poem

El Cid was both a real person and a literary giant, and the Camino leans on both. The historical Rodrigo Díaz was a Castilian noble driven into exile, surviving by his sword in the shifting borderlands between Christian and Muslim Spain. The legendary Cid belongs to the Cantar de mio Cid, the great Hispanic epic composed at the turn of the thirteenth century, which follows him from banishment to hard-won glory. The route is inspired by that poem rather than by strict fact. Its anonymous author blended history with invention, tracing a path along old Roman roads and trade routes - some of which travellers still walk today.

## How the Trail Was Found

The idea of following the Cid's footsteps is younger than it feels. Toward the end of the nineteenth century, the American scholar Archer Milton Huntington - founder of the Hispanic Society of America - travelled these paths deliberately, reading the landscape against the poem. Not long after, the great philologist Ramón Menéndez Pidal and his wife, María Goyri, went hunting for the Cid's traces through the Castilian and Aragonese countryside. It was their scholarship that turned scattered place-names and old tracks into a recoverable itinerary, laying the groundwork for the signposted long-distance route that carries travellers across the peninsula now.

## Eight Provinces, One Diagonal

The Way of El Cid runs from the northwest to the southeast, from the plains of Castile to the Mediterranean, crossing eight provinces and four autonomous communities. Along the way it strings together an extraordinary necklace of places - the Gothic cathedral of Burgos, the Mudéjar towers of Aragon, the silk market of Valencia, and thirty-nine villages formally designated as historic sites, among them Covarrubias, Sigüenza, Albarracín, Morella, and Xàtiva. Much of the route follows old borderland, which is why it is so thick with castles and watchtowers; through the rugged Teruel highlands especially, the trail threads river valleys beneath ridges still topped by medieval stone.

## The Letter of Safe Conduct

The route's cleverest touch is its credential, the Letter of Safe Conduct - modelled on the medieval document that once guaranteed a traveller free and protected passage. Collect it free at any of the ninety-eight tourist offices along the way, and you can have it stamped in each town you pass, every stamp recalling a historic or legendary event tied to that place. Gather enough of them - four stamps from each of the eight provinces - and you earn the Camino's certificate. You can travel the whole thing on foot along the waymarked GR 160, by mountain bike, by road bike, or by car, moving at whatever pace an old poem deserves.

## Four Ways to Follow a Legend

The Camino is really several routes sharing one story. Walkers follow the waymarked GR 160 across nearly 1,500 kilometres of plains, valleys, and hill country; mountain bikers take a parallel network that mostly shadows the walking path but detours around its most technical sections; road cyclists and drivers have their own longer itineraries along quiet minor roads. None of it demands special skill beyond stamina and a sense of direction. Whichever way you choose, the reward is the same: to move through a landscape at something close to the speed of the people who first crossed it, watching the country shift from Castilian meseta to Aragonese highland to Mediterranean shore, with an eight-hundred-year-old poem for a guidebook and a stamped pass to prove you were there.

## Flight Context

This coordinate, near 40.34°N, 1.11°W, marks the Camino del Cid where it crosses the highlands of Teruel province, a rugged stretch of the long diagonal route between Castile and the Mediterranean. From the air the country here is all folded ridges, river valleys, and castle-topped hills - classic frontier terrain, with the Sierra de Albarracín and Maestrazgo country to the south and west. Elevations run high, so keep well above the ridgelines and expect mountain weather. Valencia's LEVC lies to the southeast; Alicante-Elche LEAL is far to the south. The route itself is linear and hundreds of kilometres long; clear, stable days give the best views of its valleys and strongholds.
