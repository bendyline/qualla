# Torre de San Martín

A 14th-century Teruel bell tower that doubles as a city gate - built on the plan of a Muslim minaret, sheathed in glazed tile, with a street still running straight through its base.

**Location:** 40.3443, -1.1093

**Categories:** Towers in Spain, Buildings and structures in Teruel, Mudéjar architecture, World Heritage Sites in Spain, History, Tourist attractions in Teruel

## Introduction

Walk the old streets of Teruel and one of them ducks straight beneath a tower. The Torre de San Martín stands astride the road, letting people pass through an ogival arch cut into its base - a bell tower and a city gate fused into a single structure. It has held that pose for seven centuries, its brick face sheathed in glazed ceramic, one of the images that defines the city. Look closely at its lower courses, though, and the tower tells a quieter story of survival against an enemy no wall could keep out.

## Built to Be Walked Through

The tower went up in 1315 and 1316, and like several of Teruel's landmarks it was conceived as a gate-tower - a passage in the city fabric as much as a place to hang bells. The road still threads through the ogival arch at its foot. Every visible surface is worked in glazed ceramic, so the whole shaft glints as the light moves across it. It takes its name from the church of St Martin attached alongside, though that church is a much younger, Baroque affair - meaning the tower, in a sense, is older than the saint whose name it carries.

## A Tower Inside a Tower

The design is a direct inheritance from Islamic architecture. San Martín is built as two concentric square towers, one nested inside the other, with the staircase climbing the narrow gap between them. The inner tower rises through three floors roofed with cross vaults. It is precisely the scheme used in the great Almohad minarets of al-Andalus, repurposed here to carry Christian bells. To climb it is to spiral upward between two walls, through a space first devised centuries earlier, in another faith, to call the faithful to a different prayer.

## Rotting From the Ground Up

The tower nearly lost its footing. Over the generations, damp crept into its base and gnawed at the brick - humidity, of all the threats a fortress-town might have feared, proving the most patient and the most dangerous. By 1550 the lower section had decayed so badly that it had to be rebuilt. That 16th-century repair is the reason the tower still stands today: the ornament above is medieval, but the ground it rests on was renewed at the height of the Spanish Renaissance. Here, preservation has always been a slow war against water and time.

## A Place on the World's List

In 1986 UNESCO added the Torre de San Martín, together with the other Mudéjar structures of Teruel, to its World Heritage List. The listing set the tower among the finest surviving examples of a craft born of coexistence - Muslim skill in the service of Christian worship, brick and colored glaze standing in for carved stone. And the street still runs beneath it, as it has since the Middle Ages, so that the everyday passage of Teruel goes on through a monument the whole world has agreed to protect.

## Flight Context

The Torre de San Martín stands in Teruel's old town at about 40.34°N, 1.11°W, only a few hundred meters from the tower of San Pedro. Its tall, tile-clad Mudéjar profile reads clearly from a low pass over the terracotta rooftops in good light. Teruel Airport (LETL) lies just west of the city; Valencia (LEVC) is roughly 130 km southeast and Alicante-Elche (LEAL) farther south along the coast. At over 900 m elevation the surrounding plateau offers crisp visibility much of the year, with occasional winter haze or low stratus.
