# Turia Garden

Valencia moved an entire river out of its city center, then turned the empty riverbed into an eight-kilometer green ribbon that curves through the heart of town.

**Location:** 39.4801, -0.3861

**Categories:** Parks and Gardens, Urban Planning, Valencia, Spain, Rivers and Floods, Public Spaces

## Introduction

Follow the green curve that loops around Valencia's old town and you are walking on the bed of a river that is no longer there. The Turia once flowed here, straight through the city, until Valencia did something almost no city ever does: it picked up its river and moved it. What the water left behind is now the Turia Garden, a sunken ribbon of orange groves, pine forest, fountains, and football pitches that runs nearly eight and a half kilometers from the western edge of town to the sea. Nine hundred years of bridges still arch over it. Underneath them, where boats and floodwater once ran, joggers now trace the old current in the cool of the morning.

## The Night the River Rose

On 14 October 1957, the Turia broke its banks and poured into Valencia. At least eighty-one people died as water surged through streets and ground-floor rooms, and the city woke to a disaster it resolved never to repeat. The engineers' verdict was blunt: a river could no longer be allowed to run through the center of a growing city. In 1958, under a scheme called the Plan Sur, Valencia decided to dig the Turia a new channel that swung wide around the city's southern flank, steering the water away from the old town entirely. It was one of the largest civil works Spain had attempted. When it was done, the river was gone from the heart of Valencia, and a wide, dry scar curved where it used to be.

## The Riverbed Is Ours

An empty riverbed running through a crowded city is prime real estate, and the plans that followed treated it that way. The Spanish government and the city council sketched a motorway down the vacant channel, a fast concrete artery threading the old town. Valencians pushed back with a slogan that became a rallying cry: "El llit del Túria és nostre i el volem verd" - "The Turia riverbed is ours, and we want it green." The campaign won. Instead of asphalt, the channel was given over to a park, with the through-traffic pushed to the edges and buried in tunnels where it crossed. Founded in 1986, the Turia Garden was born of a rare civic argument that the trees, not the cars, deserved to win.

## A Garden Built in Sections

Because it is so long, the garden was designed piece by piece, each stretch given its own character. Near the Palau de la Música, the architect Ricardo Bofill laid out a formal quarter of orange and palm trees, a nod to the citrus country that surrounds Valencia. Farther upstream, the Department of Agriculture planted an "Urban Forest" of thousands of pines. Sports courts, fountains, and a running track fill the wider reaches. The most beloved stretch belongs to children: the Gulliver garden, where a giant reclining figure of Jonathan Swift's marooned traveler lies pinned to the ground, his coat and hair reworked into ramps and slides that small climbers swarm like the Lilliputians of the story. Spanning roughly 136 hectares, it is one of the largest purely urban gardens in Spain.

## Bridges Over Dry Ground

The strangest sight in the Turia Garden is overhead. Bridges still span the channel, some more than five centuries old, built and rebuilt across generations of floods - now arching over lawns and cyclists instead of water. The Puente del Mar, the historic road to the port, was raised in 1591 after a flood swept its predecessor away in 1589; its ten low arches carry images of the Virgin and grand staircases added in the 1930s. At the seaward end, the sculptor of a different age took over. Santiago Calatrava, Valencia's own, built his first bridge here in 1989 and returned to add the white, harp-like span of l'Assut d'Or, its cables leaning toward the towers of his City of Arts and Sciences just beyond.

## Life in the Old Channel

Today the riverbed is the city's living room. More than three million people visit each year, making it the most-visited park in Spain, and on any given morning the running track fills with thousands tracing the vanished current. The garden strings together landmarks that grew up along its banks: the Bioparc zoo at the western headwaters, opened in 2008; the Oceanogràfic aquarium near the sea, opened in 2003; and Calatrava's gleaming complex rising where the water once emptied toward the Mediterranean. A city that lost eighty-one lives to its river answered by taming it, then by refusing to pave the space it left - keeping the memory green.

## Flight Context

The Turia Garden lies at roughly 39.480°N, 0.386°W, an unmistakable green crescent curving through central Valencia along the dry former riverbed - trace it from the Bioparc in the west to the City of Arts and Sciences near the coast. Best viewed from 1,500-3,000 ft AGL in the clear light typical of the Valencian coast. Valencia Airport (LEVC) sits about 8 km west; Alicante (LEAL) is roughly 130 km south. The white sails of Calatrava's l'Assut d'Or bridge and the City of Arts and Sciences make the seaward end easy to spot.
