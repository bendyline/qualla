# Battle of Cuarte

In October 1094, El Cid broke an Almoravid siege of Valencia with a trap built from rumor and a feigned dawn raid, winning perhaps the greatest victory of his career.

**Location:** 39.4806, -0.4306

**Categories:** El Cid, Battles of the Reconquista, Military history of Valencia, Medieval Spain, Al-Andalus, 11th century in Spain

## Introduction

At dawn on 21 October 1094, a small band of horsemen clattered out of Valencia's western gate — the one the Arabs called Bab al-Hanax, the Snake Gate — and rode straight at the enormous Almoravid army camped on the plain. It looked like a nuisance, the kind of skirmish that broke the tedium of a siege. It was bait. Somewhere in the dark behind the enemy, El Cid waited with the bulk of his cavalry, and a trap he had built out of whispers and misdirection was about to spring shut.

## A City Worth an Empire

Valencia had fallen to Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar — El Cid — on 17 June 1094, the end of a long and grinding siege. The prize was worth the effort: a walled city of roughly fifteen thousand people, rich orchards fed by the Turia, and a Mediterranean port. It was also a Christian island in a Muslim sea, and the Almoravids, the austere Berber dynasty that had surged up from North Africa, meant to take it back. From Ceuta, the emir Yusuf ibn Tashfin raised an army — as many as ten thousand men, with a hard core of elite cavalry that included enslaved soldiers pressed into the imperial guard — and gave the command to his nephew Muhammad ibn Tashfin. The host crossed the strait in stages, marched some 750 kilometres through Málaga, Granada and Murcia, and by mid-September had pitched camp between Quart de Poblet and Mislata, a few kilometres west of the walls.

## A War of Whispers

El Cid could not win by numbers, so he fought first with the mind. Inside the walls he confiscated weapons and iron, and expelled anyone he suspected of Almoravid sympathies — a harsh measure in a city with a large pro-Almoravid faction. Then he turned his attention to the besiegers. He was famous for reading omens in the flight of birds, and he let it be known that the omens promised victory. More potent still was a rumor he seeded through the enemy camp: that Alfonso VI of León and Castile was riding to relieve the city. Whether or not the king had truly been summoned, the story did its work. Through the whole of Ramadan the siege sat idle. Doubt spread among the Almoravid contingents, and desertions thinned their ranks. By the time the fasting ended and the drums and horns of battle finally sounded, the army outside the walls was already fraying.

## The Feint at the Snake Gate

The whole plan turned on a single deception. In the darkness before dawn, El Cid led the main body of his cavalry out through the southern Boatella gate and swung wide around the plain, circling unseen to a position behind the Almoravid camp. At first light, the decoy rode from the Snake Gate and goaded the enemy vanguard into a chase. As the Almoravid horse streamed off in pursuit, El Cid struck the camp from the rear. To men already jittery with rumor, an assault from behind could mean only one thing: Alfonso VI had come. The rearguard broke and scattered in every direction. When the vanguard grasped that a large force was behind them, it too dissolved into confusion. By midday it was over — a swift, nearly bloodless victory that a later church document would remember as a triumph won without loss against an enormous host.

## Prince Rodrigo, the Battle-Master

The spoils were staggering: horses, weapons, and the wealth of the Almoravid royal camp. What mattered more was what the victory secured. It was the first serious defeat the Almoravids had suffered on the Iberian Peninsula, and it froze their advance in the Levante for years. Taifas began paying El Cid tribute. Within four years he had taken the strongholds of Almenara and Murviedro — present-day Sagunto — and when a new cathedral rose on the site of Valencia's main mosque, consecrated to Santa María, he endowed it in 1098 with a signature that revealed everything about his ambitions: princeps Rodericus Campidoctor, Prince Rodrigo the Battle-Master. He had no royal blood, and he governed as though he needed none.

## After the Campeador

El Cid died in mid-1099, and the principality he had carved out did not long survive him. His widow, Jimena Díaz, held Valencia for nearly three more years with the help of her son-in-law Ramon Berenguer III of Barcelona. But a lone Christian city that deep in Muslim territory could not be sustained. In May 1102, Alfonso VI — the very king whose imagined arrival had won the day at Cuarte — ordered Valencia abandoned. The Almoravids reoccupied it, and it stayed in Muslim hands until James I of Aragon took it for good in 1238. Out on the plain where a clever trap once broke an empire's army, the villages kept their quiet farming names: Mislata, Quart de Poblet.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lay on the plain between Quart de Poblet and Mislata, near 39.481°N, 0.431°W, three to six kilometres west of Valencia's medieval walls. Today the ground is dense western suburbs threaded by the old Turia bed; Valencia Airport (Manises, ICAO LEVC) sits almost on top of the historic site, so the plain is best appreciated from 2,000–4,000 ft on approach or departure. Look east to pick out Valencia's cathedral bell tower, El Miguelete, marking the medieval city El Cid held. Alicante–Elche (ICAO LEAL) lies roughly 130 km south. The coastal plain offers clear visibility for most of the year, with occasional summer haze drifting in off the Mediterranean.
