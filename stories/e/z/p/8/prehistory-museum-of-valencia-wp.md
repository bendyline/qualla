# Prehistory Museum of Valencia

Housed in a former almshouse, Valencia's Prehistory Museum traces the region's people from Paleolithic hunters to the Visigoths, gathering a century of local excavation.

**Location:** 39.4784, -0.3831

**Categories:** Museums, Archaeology, History of Valencia, Tourist attractions, Prehistory

## Introduction

The building went up in 1841 to shelter the poor of Valencia, a House of Charity organized around a set of quiet courtyards with a Byzantine-style chapel added later. Today it shelters something else entirely: the deep past of the land itself. Walk into the old almshouse now and you enter the Prehistory Museum of Valencia, where the story runs from the first stone tools chipped in local caves all the way to the Visigoths, and where the chapel where the destitute once prayed has become the museum's hall.

## From Almshouse to Archive

The House of Charity, raised in 1841, was a working institution of nineteenth-century Valencia, its church built in 1881 in a Byzantine style. The museum took over the building in 1982, and a full restoration followed in 1995 under the architect Rafael Rivera. The layout keeps its old bones: a ground floor and two stories arranged around five courtyards. The consecrated church became a lecture and exhibition hall, and the workaday rooms filled with laboratories, workshops, and galleries. There is a rightness to it, a building meant to care for the living now given over to remembering the dead of forty thousand years.

## Deep Time, Room by Room

The collection is arranged as a climb through time. The first floor holds the Paleolithic, Neolithic, and Bronze Age, the long ages of stone and early metal. The second is given to the Iberian culture and the Roman world that followed. It is a complete arc, the whole human occupation of this corner of the Mediterranean laid out in sequence. The oldest chapters come from places like the Cueva de Bolomor at Tavernes de la Valldigna, where excavators uncovered the oldest human remains ever found in Valencian lands, evidence of people who hunted and sheltered here before the region had any name at all.

## The Warrior and the Mosaic

A few objects hold the eye longer than the rest. Among the most celebrated is the small bronze Warrior of Moixent, a compact Iberian figure that has become a kind of emblem of the pre-Roman peoples who once ruled these hills. From the Roman centuries comes the Font de Mussa mosaic, one of the museum's highlighted pieces. Neither is grand in scale, but both carry an outsized charge: the warrior with his ancient poise, the mosaic with its patient tesserae, each a message from a Valencia that existed long before the cathedral or the city walls.

## A Century of Digging

Behind the display cases lies a working institution. The Prehistory Research Service, known by its Spanish initials SIP, was founded in 1927 at the urging of Isidro Ballester Tormo, and the museum grew directly from its fieldwork. Some of Spain's most notable archaeologists passed through, among them Lluís Pericot, Domingo Fletcher, Enrique Pla, and Carmen Aranegui. Their excavations reach across the whole province: the Iberian settlement of Bastida de les Alcusses at Moixent, the Cova Negra at Xàtiva, the Cova del Parpalló at Gandia, the Tossal de Sant Miquel at Llíria. The finds from those digs became the collection you now walk through.

## The Weight of the Collection

A century of careful excavation has made the SIP and its museum one of the most important institutions of its kind in Spain. Its specialized library, begun alongside the museum in 1927, and its long-running journal, the Archivo de Prehistoria Levantina, kept the scholarship flowing even through lean years, when economic hardship once stretched sixteen years between the first volume and the second. What accumulates here is more than artifacts. It is a continuous account of the people who have lived on this land, told patiently, trowel by trowel, across generations of hands.

## Flight Context

The Prehistory Museum occupies the former Casa de Beneficencia in central Valencia at about 39.4784°N, 0.3831°W, in the Ciutat Vella just west of the cathedral quarter. From the air it reads as part of the dense old-town fabric; the nearby Torres de Quart and the green Turia gardens help fix its position. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL frames the museum within the medieval core. Valencia Airport (LEVC) lies roughly 7 km west; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) is the secondary field about 130 km south. Visibility over the coastal plain is generally good, clearest in the morning.
