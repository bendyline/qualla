# Torres de Serranos

Valencia's grand ceremonial gate, built in the 1390s, once became a padded bunker for the Prado's masterpieces and now opens the city's Fallas festival each spring.

**Location:** 39.4793, -0.3759

**Categories:** History of Valencia, Gothic architecture, Medieval architecture, City gates, Spanish culture

## Introduction

In December 1936, with Spain at war with itself and bombs falling on its cities, the safest place for the nation's greatest paintings turned out to be a medieval gate in Valencia. Workers packed the Torres de Serranos with a fortress of protection: a layer of reinforced concrete nearly a meter thick, then rice husk to cushion any blow, then soil piled deep, then sandbags across the terrace. Behind those improvised defenses, masterpieces evacuated from the Prado Museum in Madrid waited out the fighting inside a tower built five and a half centuries earlier.

## A Gate That Guarded the Prado

The scheme was meticulous. The reinforced concrete went onto the first floor above the stored art, cushioned by a meter of rice husk and a meter of soil; another meter of soil covered the second floor, and the terrace disappeared beneath sandbags. An automatic system even controlled humidity and temperature to keep the canvases from rotting. The work was directed by José Lino Vaamonde, architect of the wartime board charged with saving Spain's artistic treasures. For a few desperate years, one of the finest picture collections on earth sheltered inside a Valencian city gate, its Gothic stones pressed into service as a bomb-proof vault.

## The Main Door of the City

The gate was built to be seen. Commissioned by the Valencian government and raised between 1392 and 1398 by the architect Pere Balaguer, it belonged to a wall of twelve gates and drew its polygonal Gothic form from models like the Porta de Sant Miquel at Morella and the royal gate of the Poblet Monastery, with a touch of Genoese influence. Though its walls are solid fortification stone, Balaguer clad them in fine limestone from nearby Alginet to lend the building a distinguished air. This was the northern gate, the ceremonial main entrance to the city, and when it became clear in 1397 that grand arrivals needed a grander approach, a monumental staircase was added before the works were finished the following spring.

## Prison of the Nobility

For centuries the gate did the ordinary work of defense and ceremony, welcoming ambassadors and kings. Then, after one of Valencia's main prisons burned down in 1586, the towers were converted to hold prisoners of a particular class: knights and nobles, confined here until 1887 when they were moved to a monastery. It makes a telling pair with the nearby Torres de Quart, which around the same era held the city's poorest women. Two surviving gates, two prisons, the powerful locked in one and the powerless in the other, the medieval city's social order preserved even in how it jailed.

## The Wall That Vanished

The great ring of walls that the Serranos gate once anchored is almost entirely gone. In 1865 the provincial governor Cirilio Amorós ordered the medieval wall pulled down to let the growing city breathe, and it fell. Only a handful of fragments escaped the demolition: the Serranos towers, the Quart towers, and scattered ruins like the old Jewish Gate. The Serranos survived largely because its use as a prison had kept it standing, though the conversions had walled up its great arches and stripped away its battlements. A careful restoration between 1893 and 1914, led by the sculptor José Aixá, gave the towers back much of their form, and a cleaning of the stone in 2000 brought them to their present appearance.

## Where the Fallas Begin

The gate is no relic. Each year the Torres de Serranos becomes the stage for the crida, the ceremony that opens Valencia's exuberant Fallas festival. On the last Sunday of February, the Fallera Mayor, the festival's leading figure, stands on a platform before the floodlit towers and declares the Fallas begun, and the crowd answers with the anthem of the Valencian Community and the Marcha Real. Six hundred years after Pere Balaguer's masons set the first stones, the city's main door still marks its most important arrivals, now the arrival of spring and fire and celebration.

## Flight Context

The Torres de Serranos stands at the northern edge of Valencia's old town at about 39.4793°N, 0.3759°W, beside the old bed of the Turia river, now a green park. From the air, its twin polygonal towers mark where a bridge crosses the Turia gardens into the medieval core; the Miguelete tower rises a short distance south, and the Torres de Quart guard the western edge. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL frames the gate, the riverbed park, and the historic center together. Valencia Airport (LEVC) lies roughly 8 km west; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) is the secondary field about 130 km south. Coastal visibility is usually excellent, sharpest in the morning.
