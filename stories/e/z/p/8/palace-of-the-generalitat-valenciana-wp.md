# Palace of the Generalitat Valenciana

Begun in 1421 to house a medieval tax commission, this Gothic palace is one of the rare European buildings still governed by the institution that built it.

**Location:** 39.4726, -0.3747

**Categories:** History of Valencia, Gothic architecture, Government buildings, Palaces, Spanish history

## Introduction

In 1421, a committee of tax collectors rented two rooms from a notary on a narrow Valencia street to hold their periodic meetings. Six hundred years later, the government of an entire region still works on that same ground. The Palace of the Generalitat Valenciana grew room by room from that modest beginning into one of the finest works of Valencian civil architecture, and it belongs to a very small club: buildings of medieval origin in Europe that remain the seat of the very institution that raised them.

## A Tax Office Becomes a Palace

The institution came first, the palace second. The Diputació del General, or Generalitat, was created in 1362 as a delegated commission of the Valencian Corts, charged with collecting and managing the taxes owed to the Crown of Aragon by the kingdom's three estates: the church, the nobility, and the towns. As its powers grew, so did its need for a permanent home. In 1421 the deputies rented, and the following year bought, a house on Carrer dels Cavallers, close to both the cathedral and the old city hall, the twin seats of spiritual and temporal power. From that cramped meeting room the building expanded across a century and more, swallowing neighboring houses as the institution's importance swelled.

## Built from the Kingdom

The palace is, in a sense, made of Valencia itself. Its fabric was drawn from the land around the city: stone quarried at Godella and Rocafort, glazed tiles fired at Manises and Paterna, marble from the Serra del Buixcarró, and timber carved from native forests. Generations of master craftsmen shaped it, from the stonemasons Pere Compte and Joan Corbera to the woodcarvers and gilders who lined its ceilings. The result is a building that moves through styles as it moves through time, from Mediterranean Gothic with its open courtyard and grand staircase to Renaissance windows and a tower crowned in the manner of El Escorial. In 1931 it was declared a monument of cultural interest.

## The Golden Rooms

Climb the stone staircase and you reach the palace's showpieces, the great gilded halls built through the sixteenth century. The Sala Daurada and the soaring Sala Nova are crowned with intricately coffered wooden ceilings, carved and painted by families of craftsmen who passed the work from father to son. The Sala Nova holds something rarer still. In 1591, after consulting the kingdom's finest painters, the deputies decided to line its walls with collective portraits of themselves, painted from life under the direction of Juan Sariñena. The men who governed here still watch the room, ranked by estate, a parliament frozen on the walls.

## The Emblem of Three Powers

Look closely and you find an emblem repeated throughout the palace, a compact statement of how this kingdom understood itself. Three figures stand for the three arms of the realm: Saint George for the military and the nobility, the Virgin Mary for the church, and the Guardian Angel for the civic life of the royal cities and towns. It is a picture of shared power, of a government answerable to distinct communities rather than to a single crown. That emblem now belongs to the Valencian Corts, while the Generalitat itself uses the heraldry of King Peter the Ceremonious, crowned and topped with a winged dragon.

## Interrupted and Restored

The continuity was not unbroken. After the War of the Spanish Succession, King Philip V abolished the Valencian fueros in 1707 and disbanded the Generalitat, leaving the palace without its purpose. For nearly two centuries it served other masters, housing the Real Audiencia court from around 1750 and later the Provincial Council. Only with the return of democracy and Valencian self-government did the building come full circle. Since 1982 it has once again been the seat of the Generalitat Valenciana, the institution restored to the rooms it commissioned, a rare thread of governance unbroken, mended, and running still.

## Flight Context

The Palace of the Generalitat stands in the La Seu quarter of Valencia's old town at about 39.4726°N, 0.3747°W, between the Plaça de Manises and the cathedral. Its twin towers rise among the tight lanes of the Ciutat Vella, best identified from the air by their proximity to the Miguelete bell tower a short block east. Circle at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL to frame the palace within the medieval core and the curving Turia gardens. Valencia Airport (LEVC) lies about 8 km west; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) sits roughly 130 km south as a secondary field. Visibility over the coastal plain is typically good year-round.
