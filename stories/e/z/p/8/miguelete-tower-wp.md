# Miguelete Tower

Valencia's octagonal Gothic bell tower, as wide around as it is tall, took its name from the great bell that has tolled the hours over the old city for centuries.

**Location:** 39.4750, -0.3755

**Categories:** History of Valencia, Gothic architecture, Bell towers, Religious landmarks, Medieval architecture

## Introduction

Valencians rarely call it the cathedral's bell tower. They call it El Micalet, after a bell. The great bell that has struck the hours since the fifteenth century grew so beloved that its name climbed the stonework and swallowed the whole structure, until the octagonal Gothic tower itself became the Miguelete. There is something fitting in a building named not for its architect or its saint, but for the sound it makes over the rooftops of the old city.

## As Wide Around as It Is Tall

Masons began the tower in 1381 and finished the stonework in 1429, passing the plans from hand to hand across nearly fifty years and several master builders. What they raised is an octagonal prism of Valencian Gothic, unusually pure in its geometry. Measure the tower around its base and you get 50.85 metres, precisely its height to the terrace. Diagonal buttresses climb the corners, and fine stone moldings mark each floor like the rings of a tree. Inside, 207 steps wind up a spiral that tightens as it rises. A soaring spire was once designed by the architect Antonio Dalmau, but it was never built. His drawings survive in the city's historical museum, a monument to an ending that never came. The belfry crowning the tower today was a later addition, built between 1660 and 1736.

## The House of the Bell-Ringer

For centuries the tower was inhabited. Its second level, a vaulted chamber with a single window, once served as the cathedral's prison and place of asylum. The third, larger and lit by two windows, was known simply as the Home of the Bell-Ringer. Someone lived up here, among the ropes and the great bronze voices, keeping time for a whole city. The last to do so was Mariano Folch, who tended the bells for more than sixty years and died around 1905. Imagine the life: the cold stone, the long climb home, the deafening music that was also your daily labor. When Folch pulled his ropes, the sound reached farmers in their fields and merchants at the port, marking prayers, deaths, storms, and the nightly closing of the city gates.

## Catherine, 1305

The bells themselves are a chronicle in bronze. The oldest, named Catherine, was cast in 1305 and still rings, the oldest working bell in all the former Crown of Aragon. Around her hang generations of companions: Ursula from 1438, Paul from 1489, Narcissus, Barbara, María, and the great Jaime of 1429. Together they form one of the most numerous sets of Gothic bells in Spain. The bell that gave the tower its name, the mighty Miguelete reserved for striking the hours, was recast again and again, in 1465, 1484, 1521, and finally 1539, as if the city could not quite settle its signature voice until it had tried five times.

## The Silence After Electrification

For most of its history the tower rang with a living art. The peals sounded no fewer than two hundred times a year, an elaborate vocabulary of rhythms for feast days, funerals, alarms, and the ordinary turning of the hours. Electrification in the twentieth century broke that tradition almost completely. The old choral peals faded from memory, replaced by mechanisms that announced little more than Mass. But the craft did not die. The Bell Ringers of the Cathedral of Valencia now climb the tower to swing the great bells by rope again, reviving the stopping and flipping techniques their predecessors had perfected, so that on the right mornings the old music still pours down over the Plaza de la Reina.

## The Climb to the Terrace

Originally the Miguelete stood apart, a free-standing tower beside the cathedral until the church's central nave was extended to meet it at the end of the fifteenth century. Today anyone can buy a ticket and make the ascent, passing the barred doors of the bell chambers before emerging onto the open terrace. From there the whole of Valencia unfolds: the tiled domes, the tight medieval lanes, and beyond them the green ribbon of the old Turia riverbed curving toward the sea.

## Flight Context

The Miguelete rises from the heart of Valencia's old town at roughly 39.4750°N, 0.3755°W, its octagonal Gothic silhouette and the taller adjoining cathedral dome forming an unmistakable pair over the tight medieval core. For sightseeing, circle at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, where the tower and the curving green Turia gardens frame the historic center clearly. Valencia Airport (LEVC) lies about 8 km west; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) is the secondary field roughly 130 km south. Clear Mediterranean light is common, though summer haze can soften the skyline by afternoon.
