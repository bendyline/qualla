# Siege of Valencia (1092–1094)

El Cid's nineteen-month siege of Muslim Valencia, born of a palace coup and won by a daring night raid, made him lord of a city he would hold only briefly.

**Location:** 39.4667, -0.3667

**Categories:** History of Valencia, Military history, Medieval history, Reconquista, El Cid

## Introduction

In October 1092, while the warlord known as El Cid was away, the people of Valencia gathered in one man's house and decided to overthrow their own king. It was a fateful meeting. Out of that plot came a coup, a murder, a change of masters, and finally one of the most famous sieges of the medieval Iberian world, at the end of which a Castilian adventurer named Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar would rule a Muslim city on the Mediterranean coast.

## A City Turns on Its King

The plotters met at the house of Ibn Jahhaf and resolved to depose the ruling king, Yahya al-Qadir, calling in troops to enforce their will. Al-Qadir barricaded himself and sent an urgent plea to El Cid, but the plea came too late. When the rebel soldiers entered the city, the king fled his own palace disguised as a woman and took shelter in a poor house. He was found there. Stripped of his throne and his disguise, the deposed ruler was sentenced to death, and on the twenty-eighth of October 1092, Ibn Jahhaf proclaimed himself governor of Valencia over the body of the man he had unseated.

## The Bargain and the Betrayal

El Cid returned to find the city changed. He struck a bargain with the new governor, confirming Ibn Jahhaf as ruler on one condition: that Valencia would never be handed to the Almoravids, the militant dynasty pressing north from Africa. It was a promise made to be broken. Secret messages went out to the Almoravid sultan Yusuf ibn Tashfin, begging him to drive El Cid off. An Almoravid army did march, then thought better of it and withdrew without a fight, leaving the Valencians to face the consequences of their appeal alone. El Cid drew his lines around the city.

## Nineteen Months

The siege settled into a grinding test of endurance, and it lasted nineteen months. Inside the walls, the people were torn. Some argued for holding out in defense of Islam, certain the Almoravids would return to relieve them. Others, gathered around Ibn Jahhaf, pushed to negotiate with the Castilian at the gates. Hunger and fear did their slow work on both factions. Secret agreements were made and left unclosed, hopes raised and dashed, until in May 1094 the city finally fell, nominally to the Christians. For the ordinary Valencians who had lived through nearly two years of blockade, the change of ruler came at a bitter price.

## The Night Raid at Quart

The Almoravids were not finished. Yusuf ibn Tashfin ordered Valencia retaken and handed the task to his nephew, Muhammad ibn Tashfin, whose great army camped at Quart de Poblet a few kilometers from the walls. Seeing that host, the Muslims of the city believed deliverance was finally at hand. But the besieging general grew complacent, and when word spread that El Cid had called on King Alfonso VI of León for aid, desertions began to thin the camp. Rather than wait for help, El Cid rode out at night with a body of cavalry. On the morning of the twenty-first of October 1094 he struck the unguarded camp, feigned a retreat to draw off the enemy horsemen, and let hidden soldiers fall on the tents. The Almoravids broke and fled.

## The Lord of Valencia

The victory confirmed El Cid as lord of Valencia and checked the Almoravid advance, at least for a time. He ruled the city as his own dominion, a rare Christian island in a Muslim coast, holding what he had won by force and cunning. But the hold was fragile. The Lordship of Valencia was attacked again and again, and within a few years the balance tipped back. In 1102 the city fell into Almoravid hands after all, closing a brief and dramatic chapter that would nonetheless echo for centuries in the legend of El Cid.

## Flight Context

The events unfolded at and around the old walled city of Valencia, near 39.4667°N, 0.3667°W, with the decisive night raid at Quart de Poblet a few kilometers west of the historic center. From the air, the medieval core is marked today by the cathedral and the Miguelete tower; the flat, canal-laced huerta plain that surrounds the city is the ground the armies crossed. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 4,000 feet AGL takes in the old city and its western approaches together.
