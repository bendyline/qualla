# Palace of the Marqués de Dos Aguas

A Rococo palace whose famous alabaster doorway carves the marquis's title in stone, and which today guards Spain's national collection of ceramics.

**Location:** 39.4726, -0.3747

**Categories:** History of Valencia, Palaces, Museums, Rococo architecture, Art and culture

## Introduction

The marquisate is called Dos Aguas, two waters, and the family carved the pun in stone for all of Valencia to read. Over the palace's alabaster entrance, two muscular figures recline and pour water from urns, personifications of the Turia and the Júcar, the region's two great rivers. Around them writhe crocodiles, a snake twined through ivy, a reclining lion, a single palm. It is one of the most exuberant doorways in Spain, and it announces exactly what the building behind it was built to broadcast: the wealth and lineage of the men who lived here.

## The Doorway of Two Waters

The entrance was cut in 1745 from alabaster, the work of the Valencian sculptor Ignacio Vergara after a design by the painter Hipólito Rovira. Their collaboration is signed into the stone itself. The two river figures give the marquisate its visual motto, water spilling endlessly from their vessels beneath a swirl of Rococo ornament. High in a niche above sits the Virgin of the Rosary, flanked by kneeling matrons who represent Agriculture and Justice, and crowned by an angel with a trumpet, La Fama, proclaiming the greatness of the house. Every inch overflows. Restraint was never the point.

## The Rabassa Fortune

The money behind all this splendor came from trade and taxes. The Rabassa family rose from merchants to grandees, enriched from around the year 1500, first through commerce and then through the lucrative business of leasing the tax rights of the Generalitat Valenciana, the very contracts that funded the region's government. They bought the barony of Dosaigües in 1496, and in 1699 King Charles II of Spain raised the title to a marquisate. Through careful marriages into other noble families, the Rabassa de Perellós accumulated estates and standing until, for centuries, their house was spoken of in Valencia as a byword for nobility and opulence.

## A Gothic House Made Rococo

The palace did not begin this way. Around the year 1400 it stood as a sober Gothic building of three wings around a courtyard, with an embattled tower and a tiled roof. Its transformation came in 1740, when the third Marquis, Ginés Rabassa de Perellós y Lanuza, ordered a radical renovation as a deliberate display of his power and bloodline. He assembled a formidable team: Rovira to design and decorate, Vergara to sculpt, and Luis Domingo to handle the interiors. The old Gothic bones vanished beneath frescoed facades, undulating balconies, and pavements of glazed tile painted with mythological scenes.

## The Hidden Virgin

One detail reveals the mind of the household better than any coat of arms. The niche holding the Virgin of the Rosary, the family's chosen patron, was fitted with a lid. When the marquises were away from the palace, the image was concealed. When they were in residence, it appeared in full view to the people below, a private devotion turned into public signal. The ground beneath the palace held older secrets too. On the ninth of September, 1743, workers digging in one of the courtyards uncovered the remains of a Roman necropolis, the city's dead from seventeen centuries earlier resting beneath the marquis's floors.

## From Mansion to Museum

The house of Dos Aguas no longer belongs to a marquis. It is owned today by the Spanish State and holds the González Martí National Museum of Ceramics and Decorative Arts, its lavish rooms preserved as galleries. Visitors move through halls that once hosted famous dances, singers and musicians brought specially to Valencia: an oriental tearoom, a smoking hall hung with Flemish tapestry, a dining room whose ceiling shows the four corners of the known world. The palace that was built to proclaim one family's grandeur now safeguards the craft of an entire region, its overflowing doorway still stopping passersby in the street.

## Flight Context

The Palace of the Marqués de Dos Aguas stands in central Valencia near the Plaça de l'Ajuntament at about 39.4726°N, 0.3747°W, deep in the dense old-town grid. From the air, look for it just south of the cathedral quarter and the Miguelete tower, within the compact Ciutat Vella. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL captures the palace and the surrounding historic center. Valencia Airport (LEVC) lies roughly 8 km west, with Alicante-Elche (LEAL) about 130 km south as a secondary field. Coastal skies are usually clear, with best clarity in the morning before afternoon haze.
