# Torres de Quart

One of two surviving gates of Valencia's medieval wall, the Torres de Quart still carries the pockmarks of French cannon fire, preserved on purpose as a memory of 1808.

**Location:** 39.4757, -0.3840

**Categories:** History of Valencia, Gothic architecture, Military history, Medieval architecture, City gates

## Introduction

Run your eye over the western face of the Torres de Quart and you can read a battle in the stone. The gate is cratered with pockmarks, and they are not the wear of ordinary time. On the twenty-eighth of June 1808, French cannonballs and musket fire struck this gate during the fighting for Valencia, and when the wounds were later restored, the city chose to leave the holes exactly where they were. The scars were kept on purpose, a monument in negative, so that no one passing beneath would forget what had happened here.

## Scars Kept on Purpose

The damage came during the Peninsular War, when Napoleon's armies pressed against the old walls. That the marks survive at all is a deliberate act of memory. Restorers could have smoothed the facade clean, as restorers usually do, but Valencia treated the scars as part of the gate's meaning. The stone became a witness. Later violence added to it, cannon fire in 1873 during the Cantonal rebellion leaving its own traces, so that the tower now records not one conflict but the layered troubles of the nineteenth century, written across a wall built for the fifteenth.

## The Western Gate

The Torres de Quart is one of only two gates left from Valencia's old city wall. It rose between 1441 and 1493 as the western gate, facing the road that ran toward the kingdom of Castile and the town of Quart de Poblet, which gave it its name. In the medieval scheme it was one of four principal gates, each pointed at a different world: Serranos north toward Aragon and Catalonia, El Mar east toward the port and the sea, San Vicente south, and Quart west. The site was older still. An Islamic gate called Alcántara had stood here under the taifa rulers of the eleventh century, a cubic tower open on one side, long before the Christian city rebuilt in stone.

## Fifty-Two Years in the Making

Where the neighboring Torres de Serranos went up in a brisk six years, Quart took fifty-two. Its construction reads like a chronicle of medieval misfortune. A succession of master builders passed the work along, among them the stonemason Baldomar, then Jaime Pérez, the great Pere Compte, and finally Pere Bonfill. The foundations were laid by Jaime Gallén and his son. Progress stalled when the city market burned in 1447 and again when plague swept through in 1450. Bit by bit the walls of lime mortar and ashlar rose, the vaults were closed, the projecting machicolations built out for dropping stones on attackers, and in 1476 the spiral staircases were cut. New doors were finally hung in 1493.

## A Prison Behind the Gate

Once the wars of the Middle Ages faded and Valencia was no longer besieged, the towers found other work. In the seventeenth century the gate became a prison, and its inmates were women, many of them sex workers whom the city swept off its streets and locked behind the fortress walls. Their names went largely unrecorded, but they lived their confinement inside this stone, part of the building's long human history. The tower's design served the city even here: its back was left open toward the town, so that if enemies ever seized the gate it could never be turned as a stronghold against Valencia's own people.

## A Monument at Last

The gate served as a military prison again from 1813, its rooms holding soldiers where they had once held the poor. Time and disrepair wore at it, and the wall it had guarded was gone, torn down as the growing city outgrew its medieval belt. Yet the Torres de Quart endured, and in 1931 it was declared a national monument, its most recent restoration completed in 2007. Today it stands with the Serranos towers as one of just two survivors of a vanished ring of walls, its cannon-scarred face still keeping watch over the western approach.

## Flight Context

The Torres de Quart stands on the western edge of Valencia's old town at about 39.4757°N, 0.3840°W, where the medieval wall once ran. From the air, its two rounded towers mark the boundary between the dense Ciutat Vella and the newer districts to the west; the Torres de Serranos lie a short distance northeast along the old wall line, and the Miguelete rises to the east. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL frames the gate within the historic core. Valencia Airport (LEVC) lies roughly 7 km west; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) is the secondary field about 130 km south. Visibility over the coast is usually good, best in morning light.
