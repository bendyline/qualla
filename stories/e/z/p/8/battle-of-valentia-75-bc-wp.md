# Battle of Valentia (75 BC)

In 75 BC, Pompey the Great crushed the rebel legates of Sertorius in the narrow ground beside Valentia's walls, then razed the young veteran colony for backing the losing side.

**Location:** 39.4667, -0.3750

**Categories:** Roman Republic, Sertorian War, Pompey, Military history of Valencia, Ancient history of Spain, 1st century BC

## Introduction

Its name meant valour. Barely sixty years old in 75 BC, Valentia was a modest colony of Roman veterans planted where the Turia spilled onto the coastal plain, named by its founders for the courage of the soldiers who had earned their land there. That name would turn bitter. In the autumn of that year, the town watched two Roman armies form up in the strip of ground between its walls and the river — and before the day was out, Roman soldiers had put Roman-founded Valentia to the sword.

## A Colony of Old Soldiers

Valentia was young when Pompey came. The colony had been founded in 138 BC by the consul Decimus Junius Brutus, who settled Roman and allied veterans on the banks of the Turia and gave the place a name that honoured their valour. It was a frontier town of retired fighting men, still finding its footing on the eastern edge of Hispania. None of that would spare it. In the tangled civil wars that convulsed the late Republic, geography and loyalty could be a death sentence, and Valentia had thrown in with the wrong Roman.

## Rome at War with Itself

The trouble began far away, in 88 BC, when Lucius Cornelius Sulla marched his legions on Rome and opened a generation of civil war. Quintus Sertorius, a gifted commander loyal to the faction of Gaius Marius, ended up on the losing side and fled to Hispania, then was driven out entirely. He regrouped in North Africa, took the city of Tingis, and was invited back by the Lusitanians to lead their fight against the Sullans. Landing near the Pillars of Hercules in 80 BC, Sertorius proved so brilliant a guerrilla general that Rome sent army after army against him. First came Quintus Caecilius Metellus Pius, whom Sertorius wore down to exhaustion. Then, in 76 BC, Rome dispatched a rising young star with an even larger force: Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus — Pompey the Great.

## The Line of the Turia

In 75 BC, Sertorius decided to hunt Metellus himself and left the battered Pompey to two of his lieutenants, Marcus Perpenna Vento and Gaius Herennius. Pompey marched south across the plain of Valentia and met no serious resistance until he reached the town, where he found the rebels drawn up along the line of the river Turia. Perpenna and Herennius chose to fight, apparently confident they could beat Pompey in the open. They squeezed their battle into the narrow space between the river and the city walls — ground that favoured neither side, so the fight became a raw contest of strength, nerve, and endurance. Pompey's hardened veterans simply outclassed them. Herennius fell among some ten thousand casualties. Valentia was taken and put to the sack.

## The Price of the Wrong Side

Pompey did not merely defeat the town; he razed it to the ground, punishment for the citizens' support of Sertorius. The plain around it stayed hazardous. Not long after, Metellus shattered another Sertorian army at Italica, and at the river Sucro, Pompey nearly lost both his army and his life to Sertorius himself. The war ground on for three more years. In the end, no Roman army finished Sertorius — a handful of his own men plotted against him and murdered him, and the rebellion collapsed with its leader. Valentia would not rise again for roughly half a century, rebuilt around 25 BC into the flourishing Roman city whose bones still lie beneath the streets of Valencia.

## The City Beneath the City

Ancient Valentia never entirely vanished. When the town was rebuilt around 25 BC, it rose over the same ground, and the layers of that history still lie beneath Valencia's old quarter. At the archaeological centre of La Almoina, beside the cathedral, visitors can look down onto excavated Roman streets and structures — the physical memory of the colony Pompey's veterans destroyed. There is a grim symmetry to the whole affair: a settlement named for the valour of Roman soldiers, unmade by other Roman soldiers, then patiently rebuilt into one of the Mediterranean's enduring cities. The battle beside the Turia was a catastrophe for the people who lived through it. But Valentia's story, unlike that of the fallen Herennius, did not end on that autumn day.

## Flight Context

The battle was fought beside the Roman town at about 39.467°N, 0.375°W, in the narrow ground between the city walls and the river Turia. The heart of ancient Valentia lies under Valencia's old quarter, around the cathedral and the Plaça de l'Almoina, where Roman remains are on display. The Turia's original course through the city was diverted south after the 1957 flood, and its old bed is now the green ribbon of the Turia Gardens — a useful line to trace the river the legions once fought along. Valencia Airport (Manises, ICAO LEVC) is about 8 km west; Alicante–Elche (ICAO LEAL) lies roughly 130 km south. View the compact old town from 2,500–4,000 ft; visibility is generally excellent, with light sea haze possible in summer.
