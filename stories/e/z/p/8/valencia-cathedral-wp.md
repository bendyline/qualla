# Valencia Cathedral

A Gothic cathedral raised over a mosque that itself replaced a Visigothic church and Roman streets, guarding an agate cup that millions revere as the Holy Grail.

**Location:** 39.4756, -0.3751

**Categories:** Cathedrals and Churches, Medieval History, Valencia, Spain, Gothic Architecture, Religious Sites

## Introduction

For nearly a quarter-century after Valencia changed hands, the city's grandest building was still a mosque. When James I the Conqueror took the city in 1238 and had it consecrated as a Christian cathedral, the structure itself did not change overnight. The old mosque simply kept standing - its walls still carrying verses of the Koran - while the new rulers used it as their church. Not until 22 June 1262 did Bishop Andreu d'Albalat resolve to pull it down and raise a Gothic cathedral in its place. What rose over the next centuries is one of Spain's great layered monuments, a stone palimpsest where four civilizations left their mark on a single patch of ground.

## Down Through the Floor

Dig beneath Valencia Cathedral and you fall backward through time. Excavations at the neighboring Almoina site have uncovered the ancient Visigothic cathedral that stood here before Islam arrived, and beneath that, the remains of Roman buildings and streets. When the Moors ruled the city, the Visigothic church became a mosque. Then the mosque became a cathedral again. Architects believe the geometry survived the conversions: the old mosque may correspond to the cathedral's present transept, its main entrance to what is now the Apostles' Gate, and its mihrab - the niche pointing toward Mecca - to the Almoina door. The same walls that once framed Christian, then Muslim, then Christian worship still stand, rebuilt but never quite erased.

## Built to Plant a Flag

Valencia Cathedral is unusually plain for a building of its stature, and the reason is telling. It was not a king's vanity project but a civic one, paid for by the local bourgeoisie and thrown up quickly to mark newly Christian ground. Speed and sobriety went together. The masons worked stone from quarries at Burjassot and Godella, hauling other blocks by boat down the coast from Benidorm and Xàbia. Begun between 1270 and 1300 as the mosque came down around it, the cathedral rose first as an ambulatory ringed by eight radiating chapels, then a crossing, then nave by nave. Gothic dominates, but centuries of work left Romanesque, Renaissance, Baroque, and Neoclassical fingerprints all through it.

## El Micalet and the Growing Church

For decades the cathedral was a patchwork of separate parts. The octagonal bell tower - El Miguelete in Castilian, El Micalet in Valencian - and the old chapter house stood apart from the main body until 1459, when the architects Francesc Baldomar and Pere Compte stitched everything together with a new bay called the Arcada Nova. The finished church runs 94 meters long and just under 54 meters wide, crowned by an eight-sided lantern tower, the cimbori, that pours light down onto the crossing. Later ages kept adding: in 1703 the German sculptor Konrad Rudolf designed the great Baroque main door, its concave face bending the eye - though the War of the Spanish Succession forced him to leave it for the Vergara brothers to finish.

## The Cup They Call the Grail

In a hushed side chapel sits a small agate cup, and around it clings one of Christianity's most enduring legends. This is the Santo Cáliz, the Holy Chalice that many believe to be the true Holy Grail - the vessel of the Last Supper. King Alfonso V of Aragon gave it to the cathedral in 1436, and its stone bowl may indeed date to the first century. Whatever its origins, its ceremonial life is real: it has served as the official chalice of popes, and Benedict XVI drank from it here on 9 July 2006. Pilgrims still queue to stand before an object that may be exactly what it claims, or may simply have carried the hope of that claim for six hundred years.

## A Borgia's Cathedral

Valencia gave the Church one of its most notorious families, and the cathedral bears their touch. Rodrigo Borgia - the Valencian who became Pope Alexander VI - lifted the local diocese to the rank of a metropolitan see, a promotion granted in 1492, the same year Columbus sailed. As a patron he brought Italian Renaissance art north: the fresco painters Francesco Pagano and Paolo da San Leocadio worked here alongside the local master Jacomart, their fifteenth-century panels among the cathedral's treasures. Some of those high-altar paintings vanished behind later plaster and were only rediscovered in 1874, when workers cleaning the altar found Renaissance color hidden beneath the grime - one more layer, briefly lost, then found again.

## Flight Context

Valencia Cathedral stands at 39.4756°N, 0.3751°W in the heart of the old town, its octagonal El Micalet bell tower the clearest visual marker - look for the lantern tower rising just north of the Plaza de la Reina. Best appreciated from 1,000-2,500 ft AGL in clear coastal light. Valencia Airport (LEVC) lies about 9 km west-southwest; Alicante (LEAL) is roughly 130 km south. The cathedral sits inside the green loop of the Turia Garden, a helpful orientation landmark from above.
