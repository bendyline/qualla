# 2024 Valencia Residential Complex Fire

In February 2024 a high-rise in Valencia's Campanar district burned in minutes, its cladding spreading flame like a fuse - the city's deadliest fire in nearly six centuries.

**Location:** 39.4825, -0.4022

**Categories:** disasters, Valencia, modern history, urban safety, 2024, Spain

## Introduction

It was about half past five on a February evening when one of the building's concierges realised the tower was on fire and did the only thing that could save lives: he ran through the corridors pounding on doors, shouting the word every resident dreads - 'Fire!' - and driving people out into the stairwells. There was almost no time. Within roughly ten minutes, witnesses said, flames had swallowed the entire fourteen-storey building and were leaping to a second one beside it. In a disaster measured in minutes, the seconds that doorman bought were the difference between life and death for many of his neighbours.

## Ten Minutes

The fire broke out on 22 February 2024 in the Campanar district of Valencia, in a residential complex built during the housing boom of the late 2000s. What made it so terrifying was the speed. Strong winds that day gusted up to 100 kilometres per hour, fanning the flames and blowing firefighters' water jets uselessly away from the walls. As the blaze climbed, crews could reach only as high as the twelfth floor, though they managed to pull some residents to safety. In one scene shown across Spanish television, a firefighter leapt from a seventh-floor balcony onto a rescue mat. Later, drones were sent up to search the gutted shell for those who had not made it out.

## A Fridge and a Façade

The investigation traced the tragedy to something ordinary: a leak in the refrigerant system of a fridge in an eighth-floor apartment. The escaping gas, isobutane, is highly flammable and can ignite at room temperature; a spark of static or a small electrical fault was likely all it took. An apartment fire, though, should not consume a whole tower. This one did because of the building's skin. The exterior was clad in aluminium composite panels with a polyethylene core - materials known to be flammable, yet legal when they were installed. Once the fire reached them, the façade behaved like a fuse. Temperatures topped 1,000 degrees Celsius in places, the panels melted and dripped burning material down the walls, and alarms, sprinklers and smoke detectors failed to do their job, possibly because the power that ran the pumps had been cut.

## The Ten Who Died

Ten people lost their lives - nine Spanish nationals and a Russian citizen - and it matters to remember who they were, not merely how many. Among the dead were four members of a single family, including two small children; a fourteen-year-old girl; and an elderly couple. Fifteen more people were injured, among them six firefighters and a child. These were households in the middle of an ordinary Thursday evening - homework, dinner, television - when their building turned against them. It was the deadliest fire Valencia had seen since 1447, when an arson gutted the medieval city centre and claimed ten lives. Nearly six hundred years separated the two disasters, and the same grim number joined them.

## A City in Mourning

Valencia responded the way stricken communities often do - by turning toward the survivors. Residents organised clothing drives for families who had lost everything; taxi drivers offered free rides to the hotels pressed into service as shelters; the city declared three days of official mourning. Condolences came from Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez and from the opposition, and Pope Francis sent a telegram. The football club Valencia CF, calling itself 'devastated,' flew its flags at half mast and postponed its match against Granada, and a minute of silence was held before Spain's national women's team played in Seville. Grief, for a while, took precedence over everything else the city had planned.

## The Grenfell Echo

For many watching, the fire carried a sickening familiarity. The way flame raced up the outside of the building recalled the 2017 Grenfell Tower disaster in London and a similar high-rise fire in Milan - both driven by combustible cladding on residential towers. That parallel was not lost on the bereaved. On 29 February, relatives of the Grenfell and Milan victims travelled to Valencia to lay flowers and meet the emergency crews, drawn by a shared and bitter understanding. Their visit made a quiet, insistent point: that these tragedies keep happening, that the lessons are known, and that the families left behind carry the same wound in whatever language they mourn.

## Flight Context

The fire occurred in the Campanar district on the northwestern side of the city of Valencia, near 39.482°N, 0.402°W, not far from the green ribbon of the former Turia riverbed that curves through the city. From the air the setting is dense urban Valencia, close to the Mediterranean coast. Valencia's own airport (LEVC) at Manises lies just to the west; Alicante–Elche (LEAL) and Murcia (LEMI) are well to the south. This is a place best remembered with restraint rather than sightseeing - a residential neighbourhood where ordinary families lived, and where ten of them died on a windswept February evening in 2024.
