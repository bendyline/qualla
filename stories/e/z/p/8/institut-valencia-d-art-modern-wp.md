# Institut Valencià d'Art Modern

Spain's first museum of modern art, opened in 1989 around a collection of iron sculpture — and built atop a rediscovered stretch of Valencia's medieval wall.

**Location:** 39.4800, -0.3828

**Categories:** Art museums in Spain, Modern art, Museums in Valencia, Culture of the Valencian Community, Tourist attractions in Valencia

## Introduction

Dig the foundations for a modern art museum, and you may strike the Middle Ages. When Valencia broke ground for the Institut Valencià d'Art Modern in the 1980s, the works exposed a long stretch of the city's medieval wall — fortifications pulled down more than a century earlier and all but forgotten. Rather than clear it away, the museum kept it. Visitors now descend to a basement gallery, the Sala de la Muralla, and find raw medieval stone standing as the backdrop for contemporary art: the oldest thing in the building holding up the newest.

## The First of Its Kind

IVAM was a genuine landmark for Spain. Founded in 1986 and opened to the public in February 1989, it was the country's first dedicated centre for modern art, and it quickly grew into one of the more important museums of modern and contemporary art in Spain and Europe. The Ministry of Culture of the Generalitat Valenciana had been assembling the collection even before the building existed, so that the doors opened onto a museum with something to say from day one. That collection now runs to more than ten thousand works, focused on the art of the twentieth century and after.

## A Museum Built Around a Sculptor

Every collection needs a cornerstone, and IVAM's was iron. The museum's starting point was the acquisition of some 118 works by Julio González — 36 sculptures, 10 copper plates, 10 paintings and 62 drawings — a pioneer of welded metal sculpture in modern art, and it honours him in the name of its main building: the Centre Julio González, opened in 1989 to hold the permanent collection alongside temporary shows. Around that core the institution built further, adding more than 700 works — over a hundred paintings and some six hundred drawings — by the Valencian painter Ignacio Pinazo, among many others. The choice of González was itself a statement: in the late 1920s he had helped Pablo Picasso translate ideas into welded metal, opening a new path for twentieth-century sculpture — a fitting foundation for Spain's first museum of modern art. From the beginning, then, IVAM was rooted in artists of international stature with local ties, Valencia claiming a place in the story of modern art rather than merely importing it.

## Stone Beneath the Steel

The museum occupies two very different spaces. Above ground is the modern gallery building, all clean lines and controlled light. Below it lies the Sala de la Muralla, the Hall of the Wall, set into the basement where a substantial run of Valencia's medieval fortification survives. That wall had been demolished over a hundred years ago as the growing city shrugged off its old limits; the excavation for IVAM brought it back into the light. The pairing is deliberate and striking — twentieth-century canvases and sculptures shown against ramparts that once decided where the city ended and the countryside began.

## Sorolla to Start

IVAM announced its ambitions in its very first year. In 1989, working with the San Diego Museum of Art, it mounted a major exhibition devoted to Joaquín Sorolla, the Valencian master of Mediterranean light, drawing paintings from collections scattered across Europe and the United States. It was a statement of intent: a brand-new institution reaching across the Atlantic to reassemble the work of the region's most celebrated painter. For a museum barely opened, borrowing at that scale signalled that Valencia intended to be taken seriously on the international art circuit.

## Directors and Discontents

Running a flagship public museum in a politically charged region has rarely been calm. Consuelo Císcar led IVAM for a decade before being ousted amid complaints from critics, artists and gallery owners about the quality of its programming and its management. José Miguel García Cortés directed the institution from 2014 to 2020, and Nuria Enguita Mayo followed, from September 2020 until she resigned in February 2024, citing a lack of support. In April 2025, Blanca de la Torre took over. The churn at the top is its own kind of portrait — of an institution that matters enough to be fought over, and that has kept its doors open through every change of leadership.

## Flight Context

IVAM stands on the northwestern edge of Valencia's old town, near 39.480°N, 0.383°W, along the line of the vanished medieval wall on Carrer de Guillem de Castro, beside the Turia Gardens — the green former riverbed that loops around the historic centre. From the air, fix on the nearby Torres de Quart, one of the surviving medieval gates, and on El Miguelete, the cathedral bell tower to the southeast. Valencia Airport (Manises, ICAO LEVC) is about 8 km west; Alicante–Elche (ICAO LEAL) lies roughly 130 km south. The compact old quarter is best viewed from 2,000–3,500 ft; visibility over the coast is usually excellent.
