# Siege of Valencia (1812)

In the winter of 1811 to 1812, Marshal Suchet enveloped a Spanish army and a city of 100,000, winning a victory Napoleon rewarded with a title drawn from a Valencian lagoon.

**Location:** 39.4703, -0.3767

**Categories:** History of Valencia, Military history, Napoleonic Wars, Peninsular War, Spanish history

## Introduction

Of all the marshals Napoleon created, only one earned his baton on Spanish soil. The Peninsular War wrecked reputations as often as it made them, yet Louis Gabriel Suchet came out of it a Marshal of France, rewarded for a string of victories in the east of Spain. His greatest prize was Valencia, and to take it he would trap not only a Spanish field army but an entire city of a hundred thousand people through the cold weeks of a Mediterranean winter.

## The Marshal Made in Spain

Suchet received his baton in July 1811, specifically for the brutal capture of Tarragona, a siege he had pressed so ruthlessly that a British squadron bombarded his lines and ferried reinforcements into the city from just offshore, yet the port fell all the same. Emperor Napoleon then ordered his new marshal to take Valencia. Through the summer and fall of 1811 Suchet ground his way south, seizing towns and ports and defeating the Spanish Captain General Joaquín Blake y Joyes at the Battle of Saguntum on the twenty-sixth of October, an action in which Suchet himself took a severe wound to the shoulder. Reinforced, the French pressed on toward the city.

## Blake's Open Flank

Blake gathered some twenty-eight thousand men to defend Valencia, deploying them in a long line that faced generally north, its right anchored on the coast and the city, its left reaching out to the villages of Mislata and Manises. Canals and ditches strengthened much of the front. But the far left flank, held by divisions that had already fought poorly at Saguntum, simply hung in the open air with nothing to protect it. Suchet saw the flaw at once. He resolved to swing wide around that undefended end, and on the night of the twenty-fifth of December he led his main column across the river Túria at Riba-roja.

## The Trap Closes

The battle turned on deception. French attacks along the coast and at Mislata fixed Blake's attention while the main column reached his rear almost unopposed. It was not bloodless. As the French approached the village of Aldaia, the cavalry general André Joseph Boussart rashly charged a far larger Spanish force with a single squadron of hussars. The handful of horsemen were wiped out, and Boussart was cut down and left for dead, his sword and his decorations stripped from his body where he lay. The bulk of the French cavalry soon routed the Spanish troopers. Realizing his army was about to be encircled, General Nicolás de Mahy ordered a retreat, and Blake pulled his best divisions back inside Valencia, where they were promptly sealed in.

## A City That Could Not Hold

With a hundred thousand inhabitants, little food, and obsolete walls, Valencia was in no state to withstand a siege. On the night of the twenty-eighth of December, Blake tried to break out; the attempt failed but for a spearhead of five hundred men who slipped away. Suchet wasted no time, digging his first siege trenches on the first of January and opening fire on the outer defenses three days later. As the bombardment intensified over the trapped soldiers and civilians alike, Blake capitulated. On the ninth of January 1812 he surrendered the city, and with it more than sixteen thousand troops and hundreds of guns. The citizens, who had suffered through it all, blamed him bitterly for his feeble defense.

## Duke of a Valencian Lagoon

Suchet turned his conquest into an important base of operations and was rewarded richly. Napoleon ennobled him with a new title, Duke of Albufera, taken from the name of the great freshwater lagoon just south of the city, so that a French marshal carried a Valencian marsh in his honors for the rest of his life. The triumph, though, marked a high-water mark. As Napoleon stripped troops from Spain for his coming invasion of Russia, Suchet's advance stalled. Blake was held prisoner near Paris until 1814, and Boussart, promoted at last to general of division, died the following year of the many wounds his reckless courage had cost him.

## Flight Context

The 1812 siege ringed the walled city of Valencia, centered near 39.4703°N, 0.3767°W, with the decisive French envelopment sweeping through the western villages of Mislata, Manises, Aldaia, and the Túria crossing at Riba-roja. From the air, the flat huerta plain and the arc of the old Turia riverbed trace the ground the armies fought over; the Albufera lagoon that gave Suchet his title lies about 10 km south. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 4,000 feet AGL frames the city and its western approaches. Valencia Airport (LEVC) sits about 8 km west amid the very villages Suchet used; Alicante-Elche (LEAL) is roughly 130 km south.
