# Conquest of Valencia (1238)

In October 1238, King James I of Aragon took Muslim Balànsia after a five-month siege, ending more than five centuries of Islamic rule and founding the Christian Kingdom of Valencia.

**Location:** 39.4667, -0.3667

**Categories:** Reconquista, History of Valencia, Crown of Aragon, James I of Aragon, Medieval Spain, Al-Andalus

## Introduction

On 28 September 1238, the Muslim defenders of Balànsia climbed the tower of Alí Bufat and, with their own hands, raised a banner that was not their own — the striped flag of Aragon and Catalonia, remembered ever after as the Pennon of the Conquest. The city that had once held El Cid at bay for two full years had endured King James I of Aragon for barely five months. Eleven days later, on a Saturday in October, the king rode through the gates, and more than five centuries of Muslim Valencia came to a close.

## Five Centuries of Balànsia

Valencia had been under Islamic rule since 711, unbroken except for the eight-year Christian interlude when El Cid held it between 1094 and 1102. For generation after generation it was Balànsia, a Mediterranean city of gardens and trade. By 1229, power there had passed to a local strongman, Zayyan ibn Mardanish, who pushed aside Zayd Abu Zayd, the last Almohad governor of the province. Zayd fled north to Aragon and did something that would prove fateful: he became a vassal of King James I. With a deposed Muslim governor sworn to him, James could now frame an invasion not as conquest but as intervention in someone else's civil war — a convenient fiction for a young and ambitious king.

## The Lure of a Rich City

In 1233, two seasoned knights lit the fuse. The Occitan Hug de Follalquer, a master of the Knights Hospitaller, and the Aragonese Blasco d'Alagón — freshly returned from years of exile inside Balànsia itself — spoke to the young king of the riches waiting in the Taifa of Valencia, and urged him to take them. The campaign opened that same year with the capture of Burriana. By the spring of 1236, the king's uncle, Bernat Guillem de Montpeller, had seized and fortified El Puig, a hill commanding the road to the city. When Pope Gregory IX granted a crusade bull in February 1237, knights streamed in from Aragon, Catalonia and Provence, and from as far as Germany, Hungary, Italy and England. By the middle of 1238, as the siege dragged on, the Archbishop of Tarragona, Pere d'Albalat, threw in 5,000 marks of silver and a body of knights, and named his brother Benet commander of the troops.

## A Fleet That Never Landed

Ringed by Christian armies, Zayyan sent desperate appeals to the other Muslim rulers of the Mediterranean. Only one answered. Abu Zakariya Yahya, the King of Tunis — reached by the scholar and poet Ibn al-Abbar, dispatched to plead the city's case — sent a relief fleet of twelve ships. They reached Balànsia on 17 August 1237 and then did nothing. The walls were already under bombardment by the Aragonese, and the crews dared not disembark into the fighting. The ships turned away, and with them went the city's last hope. Inside the walls, food ran short, and Zayyan, out of allies and options, began to negotiate.

## The Queen's Terms

The surrender was shaped as much by diplomacy as by siege engines, and here a woman proved decisive: Violant of Hungary, James I's queen, took a leading hand in setting the conditions. They were unusually humane for the age. Zayyan and any Muslims who wished to leave were free to depart southward, beyond the Júcar river; those who chose to remain could stay safely, though now under Christian rule — the beginning of Valencia's large Mudéjar community. To make the capitulation public, the defenders themselves raised the royal pennon over the tower of Alí Bufat on 28 September. On Saturday 9 October 1238, James I formally took possession and entered the city he had spent five years reaching.

## A New Kingdom

The fall of the capital was not the end of the campaign but the making of a kingdom. From 1239 to 1245, James pressed south: Cullera fell in 1240, Alzira in 1242, Xàtiva in 1244, Biar in 1245. There the advance stopped, because the coastal Taifa of Murcia had already been claimed by Ferdinand III of Castile in 1243. James would return to Murcia in 1266, this time on behalf of his ally Alfonso X, after a Mudéjar revolt. Out of these conquests rose the Kingdom of Valencia, with its own laws, its own institutions, and a population that for centuries after would blend the Muslim families who stayed with the Christian settlers who came — a frontier society built, uneasily, on the terms sworn in 1238.

## Flight Context

James I entered the city near its Islamic core, around 39.467°N, 0.367°W, beneath the tower later crowned by the cathedral's octagonal bell tower, El Miguelete, the standout visual landmark of the old quarter. The tower of Alí Bufat, where the Pennon of the Conquest was raised, stood by the former citadel near today's cathedral precinct. Trace the medieval limits by the surviving gates — the Torres de Serranos and Torres de Quart — at the northern and western edges of the old town. Valencia Airport (Manises, ICAO LEVC) lies about 8 km west; Alicante–Elche (ICAO LEAL) is roughly 130 km south along the coast James pushed down after 1238. Best viewed from 2,500–4,000 ft; visibility is usually excellent over the coastal huerta.
