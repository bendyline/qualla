# Fallas

Every March, Valencia spends a year of craft and satire building towering sculptures, then burns them all in a single roaring night — the UNESCO-listed festival of Fallas.

**Location:** 39.4700, -0.3764

**Categories:** Festivals in Spain, Culture of the Valencian Community, Traditions involving fire, Intangible cultural heritage, Saint Joseph, Tourist attractions in Valencia

## Introduction

For an entire year, the neighbourhoods of Valencia pour their money, wit and craft into towering sculptures — some rising higher than the buildings around them — packed with caricatures of the powerful and the ridiculous. Then, on a single night in March, they set them alight. This is Fallas, and its logic is gloriously backwards: a festival whose masterpieces exist to be destroyed, whose climax is a citywide bonfire, and whose mornings begin at eight o'clock with brass bands and firecrackers flung into the street.

## Built to Burn

Each falla is a monument made for a single night of glory. Neighbourhood associations — the casals fallers, hundreds of them across the city — spend the whole year raising money over paella dinners, then commission artists to build elaborate structures of cardboard, papier-mâché and, today, moulded polystyrene and soft cork. The largest can top thirty metres. Peopling them are the ninots: satirical figures skewering whoever has drawn the city's attention that year. Shrek and George W. Bush turned up in 2005; Barack Obama and Lady Gaga in 2012; Obama and Vladimir Putin, recast as characters from The Nutcracker, in 2015. Since 1903, the Lo Rat Penat cultural association has run a contest for the sharp Valencian verses — printed in booklets called llibrets — that explain each scene to the crowds.

## From a Carpenter's Scrap Fire

The word falla comes from the Latin for torch, and the fire is far older than the sculptures. One account traces the festival to medieval carpenters, who hung their winter candles on wooden frames called parots. When spring returned and the long dark evenings ended, the parots were no longer needed, so they were burned — and the Church folded that bonfire into the feast of Saint Joseph, patron saint of carpenters, on 19 March. In time the carpenters dressed the parots to look like people, sometimes like a recognisable neighbour, and children went door to door singing for an old rug — una estoreta velleta — to feed the flames. Those dressed frames were the first ninots. What began as tall boxes holding a few wax dolls grew, over the centuries, into the vast painted monuments of today.

## The Rhythm of the Days

Fallas runs on a strict clock. The season opens on the last Sunday of February with La Crida, staged at the medieval Torres de Serranos, where the mayor hands the keys of the festivity to the Fallera Major. From then until 19 March, each day detonates at eight in the morning with La Despertà, the wake-up call: brass bands march down every street while fallers throw large firecrackers at their heels. At two in the afternoon comes the Mascletà, a tightly choreographed barrage of gunpowder felt as much as heard — a rhythmic pounding in the chest. Between the explosions there are flower offerings to the Mare de Déu dels Desamparats, Our Lady of the Forsaken, and streets thick with the smell of frying: churros, bunyols and porres, and roasted chestnuts, as the whole city turns into an open-air party.

## La Cremà

It all builds to La Cremà, the Burning, on the night of 19 March. The small children's falles go up first, around ten o'clock; the great neighbourhood monuments follow closer to midnight; and the enormous falla in the Plaça de l'Ajuntament, outside the city hall, is always saved for last, so everyone can watch it fall. Crowds gather hours ahead for a front-row place. Each sculpture is laden with fireworks that are lit first; then the frame itself catches. Falles burn fast and fierce, and the heat drives spectators back even from behind the barriers. In the narrow streets it scorches the buildings, and firefighters hose down façades, blinds and signs to keep them from melting until the blaze cools. A year of labour becomes ash before dawn.

## A Fire That Would Not Be Put Out

The falles have always carried a political charge, and authorities have often wished them gone. In the early twentieth century the monuments grew pointedly anti-clerical and mocked local and national governments, which tried again and again to ban them, and always failed. Under Franco, censorship stripped away much of the satire, yet the falles were among the few public expressions still tolerated, and it was then that customs such as the flower offering took firm root. In all, the festival has been suspended only six times: a protest over fees in 1886, the Spanish-American War in 1896, the three years of the Spanish Civil War from 1937 to 1939, and the pandemic of 2020 and 2021, before returning in 2022. In 2016, UNESCO added Fallas to its list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity — recognition, at last, for a fire the city has never let anyone extinguish.

## Flight Context

Fallas fills the whole of central Valencia, but its heart is the Plaça de l'Ajuntament, near 39.470°N, 0.376°W — the city-hall square where the daily Mascletà is fired at 2 pm and the largest falla is burned last on the night of 19 March. From the air the old town reads as a compact grid pinned by El Miguelete, the cathedral's octagonal bell tower, with the Torres de Serranos marking the northern edge where the festival opens. Valencia Airport (Manises, ICAO LEVC) sits about 8 km west; Alicante–Elche (ICAO LEAL) is roughly 130 km south. In mid-March, expect daytime Mascletà smoke and, after dark on the 19th, the glow of hundreds of fires across the city. Best viewing 2,000–4,000 ft; coastal visibility is generally very good.
