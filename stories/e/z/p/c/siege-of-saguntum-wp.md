# Siege of Saguntum

In 219 BC a Spanish hill town chose Rome as its protector, waited eight months for a rescue that never came, and destroyed itself rather than surrender to Hannibal.

**Location:** 39.6758, -0.2775

**Categories:** History, Ancient Rome, Ancient Carthage, Military History, Spain

## Introduction

For eight months, the people of Saguntum watched the horizon for an army that never appeared. They had chosen Rome as their protector, and when Hannibal's forces closed around their walls in 219 BC, they sent to Rome for aid. The Senate, its attention fixed on a revolt across the Adriatic, sent none. What happened on this Iberian hilltop would go on to trigger one of the most important wars of antiquity - and the townspeople who set it in motion would not live to see it.

## Hannibal's Calculation

Two years earlier, at the age of twenty-six, Hannibal had been made supreme commander of Carthaginian Iberia, and he had spent the time since preparing carefully. Saguntum stood in his way. It was one of the most heavily fortified cities in the region, and leaving such a stronghold at his back, allied to Rome, would have been reckless. There was money in it, too: plunder to pay the mercenaries he had recruited from Africa and across the Iberian Peninsula, and to spend on rivals in the Carthaginian senate at home. Rome, meanwhile, had received ample warning of his intentions and done nothing, distracted by the rebellious Illyrians. By the time the news of the siege reached them, it was already too late to matter.

## The Long Wait

The Saguntines did not fold. Their fortifications and their sheer stubbornness cost Hannibal dearly; at one point a javelin drove into his thigh and stopped the fighting for weeks while he recovered. But his army was patient, dismantling the city's defences one section at a time. Inside the walls, the situation grew desperate. The appeal to Rome went unanswered. Food ran out. Ancient writers describe a population pushed past the limits of endurance - starving, some claimed, to the point of cannibalism in the final weeks. Still the messengers watched the road, and still no legions came. A city had staked its survival on a distant promise, and the promise was not kept.

## The Last Choice

After eight months, the last defences gave way. Hannibal offered terms: the survivors could leave Saguntum unarmed, each carrying two garments, and live. They refused. Rather than hand their city and its wealth to the man who had starved them, many gathered their possessions and set them ablaze. A great pyre was built, and people threw themselves into the flames; others died by the sword, by their own hands. When Carthaginian troops finally broke through, every adult who remained was put to death. These were not numbers in a chronicle. They were farmers and traders and families who had gambled everything on Rome, and who chose to destroy what they had built rather than surrender it to a conqueror.

## The Spark and the Legend

The fall of Saguntum handed Rome its casus belli and lit the Second Punic War. From here Hannibal moved to New Carthage (modern Cartagena), gathered his men, and began the march that would carry him over the Pyrenees, across the Alps, and to within three miles of Rome itself, winning crushing victories at the Trebia, Lake Trasimene, and Cannae. The town he destroyed was not forgotten. At the end of the first century AD the poet Silius Italicus retold the siege at length in his epic Punica, and in 1727 the English dramatist Philip Frowde staged its tragedy on the London stage. Today the ruins crown the hill above modern Sagunto - a quiet acropolis whose stones once decided the fate of the ancient Mediterranean.

## What the Hill Remembers

Walk the castle of Sagunto today and the siege is everywhere and nowhere. The enclosures ringing the crest hold Iberian, Roman, and later stone, layered over the ground where the townspeople made their stand, though little of the doomed city itself survives to be touched. The story outlived the stones. The Roman historian Livy recorded the siege as the opening act of a war that came close to destroying the Republic, and it passed into the language of statecraft: for centuries, to speak of Saguntum was to speak of the ally abandoned and the promise unkept. The tale has been retold in Latin epic, staged as tragedy on the London stage, and even set to music by a modern metal band. Few places so thoroughly lost have been remembered so long.

## Flight Context

The ancient site crowns the castle ridge above modern Sagunto at 39.68°N, 0.28°W, about 25 km north of Valencia. From Valencia's LEVC, look for the long fortified crest running along the hilltop, with the Roman theatre cut into the slope below and the coastal plain - the ground Hannibal's army once covered - spreading toward the sea. A viewing altitude of 2,500-4,000 ft AGL frames the whole acropolis and the Palància river mouth. Alicante-Elche LEAL serves longer southern approaches. Visibility along this coast is usually excellent outside the summer haze.
