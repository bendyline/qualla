# Ahafo Mine

One of the largest gold mines in Ghana, where billions of dollars in extracted wealth have collided with displacement, pollution, and a community of subsistence farmers fighting for their land.

**Location:** 7.0125, -2.3560

**Categories:** Gold mines in Ghana, Brong-Ahafo Region

## Introduction

The earth here is red, laterite-rich, and laced with gold. For centuries, the people of the Brong-Ahafo region farmed this soil, growing cocoa, plantain, and cassava in the rolling hills of central Ghana. Then Newmont Mining arrived, and the arithmetic changed. What had been farmland measured in harvests began to be measured in ounces per ton, and the Ahafo mine -- one of the largest open-pit gold operations in West Africa -- reshaped the landscape, the economy, and the lives of thousands of people who had never asked for any of it.

## Gold Beneath the Farms

The Ahafo mine began commercial production in 2006, sprawling across ten host communities in the Asutifi North and Tano North districts. The operation extracts gold through both surface and underground mining, and by the mid-2020s, the Ahafo South complex alone had produced more than 10 million ounces of gold. Newmont, now the world's largest gold mining company, has estimated the site holds reserves of some 17 million ounces. In 2025, the company expanded with the Ahafo North project, expected to produce between 275,000 and 325,000 ounces annually over a 13-year mine life. The numbers are staggering -- billions of dollars of mineral wealth pulled from beneath land that once fed families.

## The Spill That Confirmed the Fears

On October 8, 2009, an instrument malfunction at the mine's processing plant allowed a pond containing sodium cyanide solution to overflow. The toxic water cascaded into a secondary containment pond already full for scheduled maintenance, and from there into the surrounding waterways. Fish died in large numbers. Newmont initially attributed the die-off to overpopulation and oxygen depletion, but an independent investigation told a different story: cyanide levels in the water were 1,400 percent above World Health Organization standards, with cadmium and arsenic concentrations that far exceeded safe limits. A Ghanaian ministerial panel faulted Newmont for operating multiple ponds simultaneously, delaying notification to downstream communities, and failing to maintain adequate spill-prevention measures. The company was fined 4.9 million dollars -- a fraction of the mine's annual revenue.

## Displacement and Resistance

Environmental group Earthworks has documented a broader pattern of harm. The first phase of the mine displaced roughly 9,500 people, at least 95 percent of whom were subsistence farmers. For these families, land was not an abstraction or an asset class; it was sustenance, identity, and inheritance. Compensation programs and community development funds have been established, but the gap between corporate promises and lived reality has fueled ongoing friction. Women in the affected communities have been particularly outspoken, organizing to demand fair treatment and adequate resettlement. The Environmental Justice Atlas lists the Ahafo mine among its cases of resource extraction conflicts, a catalog of disputes where the wealth underground belongs to shareholders while the cost on the surface belongs to everyone else.

## The View from Above

From the air, the Ahafo mine is unmistakable. The open pits carve geometric terraces into the reddish earth, surrounded by haul roads, waste dumps, and tailings ponds that spread across the landscape like industrial scars. Beyond the mine's perimeter, the green of tropical forest and farmland returns abruptly, marking the boundary between extraction and the life that preceded it. Ghana has been mining gold for centuries -- the country's colonial name, the Gold Coast, speaks to the depth of that history. But the scale of modern industrial mining is something different entirely, and the Ahafo mine stands as both an engine of national revenue and a case study in the costs that rarely appear on a balance sheet.
