# Gao

Once the capital of the largest empire in West African history, Gao still sits where the desert meets the Niger, a city that has been a metropolis, a ruin, and a metropolis again.

**Location:** 16.2667, -0.0500

**Categories:** cities, history, west-africa, mali, trans-saharan-trade

## Introduction

The prime meridian runs straight through Gao, the invisible line of zero longitude that the rest of the world measures itself against. It is a fitting accident of geography for a city that once stood at the center of its own world. Five hundred years ago, Gao was the capital of the Songhai Empire, one of the great cities of West Africa, its markets thick with merchants and its name known from Cairo to Andalusia. Today it is a regional capital of Mali on the eastern bank of the Niger, where the Tilemsi valley opens onto the river and the Sahara begins in earnest just beyond the last houses.

## Where the River Meets the Sand

Gao occupies a precise and demanding spot on the map. It lies 320 kilometres east-southeast of Timbuktu, the largest town in eastern Mali, joined to the distant capital Bamako by 1,200 kilometres of paved road. The climate is uncompromising: hot desert, where May highs average 43 degrees Celsius and the only rain falls between June and September. From October to March the Harmattan blows in from the Sahara, hazing the sky with dust. Yet the Niger redeems it. When the river floods, fed by rains far upstream in Guinea, it reaches its peak at Gao not in summer but in December, and at full flood it spreads four kilometres wide. Nearly all local farming depends on that water. In the dry season the river shrinks until islands rise from its bed.

## A Capital of the Trans-Saharan Trade

Gao's importance was never accidental. Arabic geographers writing in the ninth and tenth centuries already knew it as Kawkaw, a power on the river and a hub of the trans-Saharan trade, where Saharan salt was exchanged for gold from the south. By the late tenth century its ruler was reported to be Muslim. The city was absorbed by the Mali Empire late in the thirteenth century, then broke free in the fifteenth, and under Sonni Ali and the Askia dynasty became the capital of Songhai, the largest empire the region ever produced. When Leo Africanus visited around 1510 he described a wealthy, unwalled town of rich merchants, abundant rice and melons, and crowded markets, including the slave market that was part of the trade's brutal foundation.

## Rise, Ruin, and Return

Empires do not last, and Gao knew the full arc of the fall. After a Moroccan army defeated Songhai in 1591, the invaders moved the seat of power to Timbuktu and Gao began a long decline. When the German explorer Heinrich Barth reached it in 1854, the great capital had withered to a village of about 300 huts made of matting, its people poor, the site of the old city overgrown with thorny bushes. But the river never let Gao die entirely. Through the twentieth century it grew back into a real city: 52,000 people at the 1998 census, nearly 87,000 by 2009. In 2006 the Wabaria bridge finally spanned the Niger here, replacing the old ferry.

## A City in the Crossfire

Gao's recent history has been hard. In 2012, amid a Tuareg rebellion fueled by weapons from the Libyan war, the Malian army withdrew and the city fell, briefly proclaimed the capital of a self-declared state of Azawad before armed Islamist groups seized control and imposed harsh rule. French and Malian forces retook Gao in January 2013, but peace did not follow. In January 2017 a suicide bombing at a military camp near the city killed 77 people, the deadliest terrorist attack in Malian history. Through all of it the people of Gao have endured. Most speak Koyraboro Senni, and the city remains a cultural capital of the Songhay world, home to festivals, river markets, the museum of the Sahel, and the towering Tomb of Askia, a UNESCO World Heritage Site that still anchors the skyline as it has since 1495.

## Flight Context

Gao lies at 16.27 N, 0.05 W on the eastern bank of the Niger River, where the Tilemsi valley meets the water. From altitude the river's broad curve and the surrounding Sahel dominate; the Wabaria bridge, the Tomb of Askia, and La Dune Rose on the opposite bank are useful landmarks. The prime meridian passes through the city. Nearest airport is Gao International (GAGO). Best visibility comes in the dry season, though the Harmattan can haze the sky with dust from October to March.
