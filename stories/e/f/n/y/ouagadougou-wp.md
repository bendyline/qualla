# Ouagadougou

The capital of Burkina Faso is a Sahelian metropolis of over two million where an emperor still holds weekly court - and where, every Friday, a centuries-old ceremony chooses peace over war.

**Location:** 12.3686, -1.5275

**Categories:** Cities, Capitals, Africa, Burkina Faso, Culture

## Introduction

Every Friday morning, in the middle of a city of more than two million people, an emperor prepares for a war he will not fight. The Moro-Naba, supreme ruler of the Mossi, appears at his palace dressed in red, mounted as if to ride into battle. A cannon sounds. His chiefs plead with him to stay. He withdraws - and returns in white, having chosen peace. This fifteen-minute ritual has been performed for centuries in Ouagadougou, and it is the only weekly royal ceremony still conducted by a living African monarch.

## Home of the Wagu

The city's name is older than the country around it. 'Wagadugu' means 'home of the Wagu' - a Soninke people connected to the ancient Ghana Empire, also called Wagadu, from which a diaspora is thought to have helped found a settlement here as early as the eleventh century. 'Ouagadougou' is simply the French spelling of that older name. When the Mossi pushed north in the fourteenth century, they took the town and made it theirs. The Wagadugu Kingdom emerged in the fifteenth century and became the principal seat of the Mossi states. To say the name aloud - WA-ga-DOO-goo - is to pronounce a thousand years of layered history in four syllables.

## The Emperor's City

Ouagadougou did not become the Mossi capital overnight. The tenth Moro-Naba was the first to live here, in the middle of the seventeenth century, and it became the permanent seat of power under the twenty-first Moro-Naba a century later. The court never left. The Friday ceremony reenacts an old legend - a ruler returning from war to find his palace plundered and his favorite wife taken, who is then persuaded by his elders to make peace rather than vengeance. In an age when ancient monarchies survive mostly as tourist pageantry, the Moro-Naba's court remains a genuine institution, woven into the fabric of a modern capital that has grown up around it.

## A Sahelian Metropolis

Today Ouagadougou is the administrative, economic, and cultural heart of Burkina Faso, with a population of around 2.4 million as of 2019. It sits in the Sudano-Sahelian zone, where the climate is hot and semi-arid, the rains fall from May to September, and the dry harmattan wind blows down from the desert. The city has one of West Africa's largest markets - the Grand Marché, which burned in 2003 and reopened with better fireproofing. Its industrial districts of Kossodo and Gounghin draw workers from the countryside, and its university, founded in 1974, anchors the nation's higher education. French is the official language; Moore, the Mossi tongue, fills the streets.

## The Forest of Knowledge

In the heart of the city lies a forest that was sacred long before it was a park. The Bangr-Weoogo once belonged to the Mossi chiefs, who treated it as holy ground - a place for initiations and for refuge. French colonists, indifferent to its meaning, simply declared it a public park in the 1930s. In 2001 the city gave it back its name: Parc Urbain Bangr-Weoogo, 'the urban park of the forest of knowledge.' It is a fitting emblem for Ouagadougou itself - a place where the deep Mossi past was overwritten by colonial rule, and where, decade by decade, the city has been quietly reclaiming its own meaning.

## Flight Context

Ouagadougou sits at 12.369°N, 1.527°W on the flat Sahelian plateau, roughly 300 meters above sea level. Thomas Sankara International Airport (ICAO: DFFD; IATA: OUA) lies immediately southeast of the city center. From the air, look for the grid-patterned street layout, the Place des Nations Unies roundabout, and the Grand Mosque. The clearest flying weather runs November to February; harmattan dust can sharply cut visibility from December through March.
