# Dogon Country

A 150-kilometer wall of sandstone in central Mali, where the Dogon built granaries into a cliff that had already sheltered people for two thousand years.

**Location:** 14.3500, -3.6111

**Categories:** Regions of West Africa, Dogon country, Geography of Mali, Mopti Region, Tourism in Mali, Dogon holy places, Sacred sites in traditional African religions

## Introduction

Look up at the cliff and you see two civilizations stacked on top of each other. Near the base cling the mud-brick villages of the Dogon, their square houses and conical granaries the color of the rock itself. Higher up, lodged in shadowed recesses no ladder seems to reach, sit older structures - the work of people the Dogon found already here when they arrived, and whom they remember in a single word: Tellem, "those who were before us." The Bandiagara Escarpment is not just a landscape. It is an archive carved into stone.

## Three Worlds in One

Pays Dogon - Dogon country - folds into three distinct zones, and you can read all of them in a single sweep of the eye. There is the plateau, a vast sandstone tableland that rises gradually from the Niger River belt. There is the escarpment itself, a near-vertical wall running some 150 kilometers from southwest to northeast, towering as much as 500 meters above the lowlands. And there is the Séno-Gondo plain at its foot, sweeping away toward the border with Burkina Faso. The Dogon settled across all three, but it was the cliff that defined them. Steep, defensible, and laced with springs, it offered shelter from both hostile raiders and a hostile sun.

## Those Who Were Before

Long before the Dogon, the cliffs were home to others. The Tellem occupied the escarpment between roughly the 11th and 16th centuries, building cylindrical granaries and carving burial caves into recesses high on the rock face - places so inaccessible that scholars still debate how they were reached. Before even the Tellem came the Toloy, whose traces reach back further still. The Dogon themselves arrived around the 14th or 15th century, migrating into the region and, according to tradition, holding to their own beliefs rather than convert to Islam. They did not erase what they found. The old granaries remain, weathering quietly above the living villages - a continuity of habitation stretching across two thousand years.

## Amma and the Nommo

At the heart of traditional Dogon religion stands Amma, a single supreme creator - omnipotent, omniscient, present in all things. Beneath Amma move ancestral spirits: the serpent Lebe, and the Nommo, the water spirits central to Dogon accounts of creation. Duality threads through everything. Twins, the pairing of masculine and feminine, the doubling of a life - these are not curiosities but the structure of the cosmos as the Dogon describe it. In the mid-20th century, French anthropologists Marcel Griaule and Germaine Dieterlen published claims that Dogon cosmology encoded secret knowledge of Sirius B, an invisible companion star. Later fieldwork, notably by Walter van Beek, found no such tradition among the Dogon themselves. The episode is better understood as a cautionary tale about how outsiders project meaning onto the cultures they study.

## The Masks Come Out

When someone dies, the Dogon do not simply mourn - they perform. Masked dancers move through the village in rituals that may follow a death immediately or arrive years later, in the great collective ceremonies that draw a generation together. The carved masks, some towering far above the dancer's head, embody ancestors and animals and the ordered relationships between the living and the dead. Weavers work the looms; markets fill the spaces below the cliff with indigo cloth and the murmur of trade. This is a culture that has carried its forms intact across centuries, and it is the reason Dogon country remained, for decades, the most visited region in all of Mali.

## A Difficult Present

The cliffs have always meant safety, and in recent years that meaning has returned with painful force. Since the Mali War that began in the early 2010s, central Mali has suffered waves of intercommunal violence, and parts of Dogon country have been caught in it. Villages have been attacked; some have been abandoned. The tourism that once sustained families along the escarpment has largely vanished. These are not abstractions but homes - people whose ancestors chose this rock precisely because it could be defended. The landscape that UNESCO recognized in 1989 endures. So, with resilience, does the community that gives it meaning. Both deserve to be seen clearly, and whole.

## Flight Context

The Bandiagara Escarpment runs roughly along 14.35°N, 3.61°W, a 150-km sandstone wall oriented southwest to northeast, rising up to ~500 m above the Séno plain - a dramatic linear feature from cruising altitude in clear conditions. Nearest airport is Mopti-Ambodédjo (GAMB / MZI), about 65 km west-northwest near the Niger-Bani confluence. Best viewed in the dry season (November-February) when Harmattan haze is lower; the rains and dust of other months reduce visibility. Recommended viewing altitude 6,000-10,000 ft AGL to take in the full sweep of plateau, cliff, and plain.
