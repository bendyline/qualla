# Mopti

Where the Niger meets the Bani, a town once dismissed as a 'miserable village' grew into the great river port of inland Mali, salt slabs from the Sahara stacked beside fish from the floodplain.

**Location:** 14.4873, -4.1896

**Categories:** Cities, Rivers, Architecture, Trade, Inner Niger Delta

## Introduction

For half the year, the roads simply disappear. When the Niger and the Bani swell between August and December, the floodwater of the Inner Delta rises around Mopti until the town becomes a scatter of islands, its quarters joined by raised causeways and the rivers themselves doing the work of streets. Pinasses, long wooden canoes shaped like the hulls of an older world, slide between the high ground carrying people, sacks of millet, and slabs of salt. The Bozo fishermen who first settled these mounds called the place Sagan. The world now knows it as Mopti, the busiest harbor on the great river of West Africa.

## The Town the Flood Built

Mopti sits on the right bank of the Bani, a few hundred meters above where that river pours into the Niger. The geography is the whole story. Long before any town existed, only a handful of higher mounds stayed dry when the annual flood spread across the Inner Niger Delta, and people built on those mounds because there was nowhere else. A French officer exploring the river by gunboat in the 1890s found a cluster of villages two kilometers apart, mud-brick houses on islands of dry ground, fishermen working cotton nets. Another colonial captain, Lucien Marc, dismissed the place in 1902 as a 'miserable village' of a few huts. He could not have been more wrong about its future.

## An Engineered Lifeline

What made Mopti was a wall of earth. Between 1905 and 1912, French colonial forces raised a twelve-kilometer dyke linking Mopti to neighboring Sevare, so that the town could be reached by road even when the Niger was in flood. That causeway turned a marooned fishing settlement into a place you could actually get to, year-round, and the village expanded with startling speed. Today the same elevated road carries traffic onto Route Nationale 16, the bitumen artery running from Bamako in the west clear to Gao in the east. Unlike the ancient cities of Djenne, Timbuktu, and Gao, Mopti never shaped the medieval history of the Sahel. It became important only in the twentieth century, by being the place where every road and river converged.

## Salt, Fish, and Pottery

The harbor is the soul of the place. Watch the quayside and you watch the trade of half a continent change hands. Slabs of rock salt arrive from Taoudenni, the brutal mine deep in the northern Sahara, hauled south by the same routes that once moved gold and books to Timbuktu. Beside them are towers of dried fish bound for the markets of Djenne, exactly as the early chroniclers described more than a century ago, when the women of the town also sold what one visitor called a 'beautiful kind of pottery' to boats heading downriver. The market is a meeting of peoples. Fula, Bozo, Bambara, Dogon, Songhai, Bobo, and Mossi all trade here, Fula and Bozo the languages most heard above the noise.

## The Mosque of Mud

Rising above the Komoguel quarter stands the Great Mosque, its banco-plastered walls bristling with the wooden beams that brace every building in this style. Built on the site of an earlier mosque from 1908 and modeled on the famous Great Mosque of Djenne, it is a masterwork of Sudano-Sahelian architecture, sun-dried brick sheathed in smooth earth. In 1978 restorers coated the upper walls in cement, a modern fix that backfired badly: rainwater seeped behind the cement and split the mud beneath. Between 2004 and 2006 the Aga Khan Trust for Culture stripped the cement away and returned the building to honest earth. Mopti gave Mali a president too, Amadou Toumani Toure, who was born here.

## Flight Context

Mopti lies at 14.49 N, 4.19 W, at the confluence of the Niger and Bani rivers in central Mali. From the air the town reads as a cluster of higher ground stitched together by the long straight causeway running to Sevare, dramatically obvious during the flood season when surrounding land is underwater. The Great Mosque's twin towers and the busy river harbor are key landmarks. Nearest airport is Mopti-Ambodedjo (GAMB) near Sevare, a few kilometers east. Best viewed at 2,000-4,000 ft AGL; visibility can be poor in the harmattan dust season (December-March).
