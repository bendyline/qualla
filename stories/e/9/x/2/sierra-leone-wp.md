# Sierra Leone

A West African nation founded as a home for freed people, named for its lion-shaped mountains, scarred by a diamond-fueled war, and remade by a fragile, hard-won peace.

**Location:** 8.5000, -12.1000

**Categories:** Countries in Africa, West Africa, Member states of the United Nations, Member states of the Commonwealth of Nations, English-speaking countries and territories

## Introduction

A Portuguese sailor named Pedro de Sintra looked up at the mountains above what is now Freetown in 1462 and thought he heard lions in the thunder rolling off the peaks. He called the range Serra Leoa, the lioness mountains, and the name stuck across five centuries and a dozen mapmakers' spellings. But the deeper story of Sierra Leone is not written in its hills. It is written in the people who were brought, freed, and resettled here, and in the way a country built as a sanctuary for the formerly enslaved would later be torn apart by the trade in the diamonds beneath its soil.

## The Province of Freedom

Sierra Leone began as an experiment in liberty. In 1787, British abolitionists and the Crown founded a settlement they called the "Province of Freedom," a place to resettle Africans freed from slavery. The first attempt nearly died of disease and conflict with the Temne people who already lived there. Then, in 1792, some 1,200 Black Loyalists, formerly enslaved people who had sided with Britain in the American Revolution and resettled in chilly, hostile Nova Scotia, crossed the Atlantic to found Freetown. They were soon joined by Jamaican Maroons and, after Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807, by thousands of "Liberated Africans" freed from intercepted slave ships. Out of these communities a new people and a new language, the Krio, took shape on the peninsula.

## The Athens of Africa

For a time, Freetown shone. The British founded Fourah Bay College in 1827, and for more than a century it stood as the only European-style university in this part of Africa, drawing students from across the West Coast in theology and education. The city earned the nickname "the Athens of Africa" for its schools and its educated Krio class, who filled posts in the colonial administration and dominated trade with the interior. The shine had limits. After the Berlin Conference carved up Africa, Britain pushed inland, declared a Protectorate in 1896, and began squeezing Krio people out of office. A new hut tax sparked the 1898 rebellion led by the Temne chief Bai Bureh, whose fighters held off far stronger British forces for months before he surrendered to spare his people.

## Diamonds and the Long War

Beneath the eastern forests lay a curse disguised as fortune. Sierra Leone sits among the world's top diamond producers, and in 1991 a rebellion by the Revolutionary United Front plunged the country into eleven years of civil war financed by those stones, the "blood diamonds" traded for the weapons that armed the fighting. The conflict was brutal beyond the telling of statistics, marked by coups, child soldiers, and mass atrocity. About 50,000 people were killed, and hundreds of thousands were driven from their homes into Guinea and Liberia. The war finally ended in 2002 after intervention by West African ECOMOG forces and, decisively, the British, whose Operation Palliser helped break the rebellion. To this day many Sierra Leoneans regard Britain's role with gratitude.

## A Country Rebuilding

Peace, when it came, was fragile and is still being earned. A UN-backed war crimes court tried leaders from both sides. Elections in 2007 and after returned the country to multi-party democracy. Then in 2014 the Ebola epidemic swept in, killing thousands and forcing a national state of emergency just as the economy was finding its feet. Today Sierra Leone is a presidential republic of more than eight million people, home to some eighteen ethnic groups led by the Temne and Mende, where Muslims and Christians live alongside one another with a tolerance rare in the region. There are hopeful signs: in 2025 the Gola-Tiwai forest complex, nearly destroyed in the war, became the country's first UNESCO World Heritage Site, a sanctuary for one of the planet's richest concentrations of primates.

## Flight Context

Sierra Leone sits on the West African coast between latitudes 7° and 10°N and longitudes 10° and 14°W, bordered by Guinea to the north and east, Liberia to the southeast, and the Atlantic to the west. The capital, Freetown, occupies a mountainous peninsula around 8.48°N, 13.23°W. The main gateway is Freetown-Lungi International Airport (ICAO GFLL), set across the broad Sierra Leone River estuary from the city. Mount Bintumani, the highest point at 1,948 m, rises in the eastern interior. Navigate by the deeply indented coastline, the third-largest natural harbor in the world at Freetown, and the dense rainforest inland. The dry season (November to April) offers the clearest flying; the July-August rains bring heavy cloud and reduced visibility.
