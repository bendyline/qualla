# Bijagós Islands

Eighty-eight islands built from an ancient river delta, where a fiercely independent people kept their own world - and won UNESCO World Heritage status in 2025.

**Location:** 11.2500, -16.0833

**Categories:** Islands, Guinea-Bissau, Culture, Wildlife, World Heritage Sites, West Africa

## Introduction

In 1535, a Portuguese duke sent a force to conquer the Bijagós, and the islanders destroyed it almost to the last man. The archipelago had a powerful navy of its own, and for years afterward its people refused to trade with Portugal and dealt harshly with shipwrecked sailors. Three centuries later, in 1849, a joint British and French expedition arrived to 'pacify' the islands - and was repulsed. Portugal did not formally annex the Bijagós until 1937. These eighty-eight islands and islets, strung across the Atlantic off Guinea-Bissau, were among the last places in West Africa to bend, and in some ways they never fully did.

## Born of a River

The Bijagós are the ghost of a river. The archipelago formed from the ancient delta of the Geba and Grande de Buba rivers, which once poured silt into the Atlantic and built, over millennia, the maze of islands that exists today - some 88 of them, spread across nearly 13,000 square kilometers of ocean. Twenty are inhabited year-round, the most populous being Bubaque; the colonial-era administrative capital, Bolama, sits on its own island. The result is one of the most varied coastal landscapes in West Africa: mangroves laced with tidal channels, palm forests, dry and semi-dry woodland, coastal savanna, sandbanks, and shifting aquatic zones. In 1996 the whole archipelago was named a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve, and in 2025 UNESCO inscribed it as a World Heritage Site under the name "Coastal and Marine Ecosystems of the Bijagós Archipelago - Omatí Minhô."

## A Crossroads of Wings

Twice a year the islands fill with birds. The Bijagós' intertidal mudflats and mangroves are designated an Important Bird Area, a vital waystation on the great migratory highway between the Arctic and the tropics. Hundreds of thousands of migratory Palaearctic waders stop here to feed, alongside gulls, terns, greater flamingos, and pink-backed pelicans. Beneath the surface, the waters shelter dolphins, manatees, and globally significant numbers of green and leatherback sea turtles - the southern island of Poilão alone hosts one of the most important green-turtle nesting beaches on Earth. The isolation that once repelled invaders has, in the modern age, preserved a richness of life that more accessible coasts have lost.

## The People Who Kept Their World

Distance has been the Bijagó people's great ally. Cut off from mainland Guinea-Bissau by water and by choice, they have retained a striking degree of autonomy and held onto an ancestral culture that outside forces never managed to dissolve. The Bijago language still dominates the countryside, spoken alongside Guinea-Bissau Creole and a Portuguese that, by some accounts, is barely used at all - not even in the schools. Much has been written about whether Bijagó society is matriarchal: in many communities women manage the household, the economy, and matters of law, choose their own husbands and end marriages, and religious authority rests with priestesses known as baloberas. Other scholars push back, arguing the structure is more complex than the label suggests. What is clear is that women hold real and visible power, and that the islanders see no division between the natural and the social - to them, harm to the community, the ancestors, or the spirit world registers as harm to the land itself.

## Masks, Memory, and Hard History

Bijagó art is unmistakable. Carvers produce portable ancestor shrines called iran and bold zoomorphic masks - cattle, sharks, stingrays - made for the fanado coming-of-age ceremonies that structure island life, along with carved stools, basketry, spears, and headdresses whose iconography shifts from island to island. The history these objects carry is not all gentle. After resisting the Portuguese, the islanders themselves became providers of enslaved people, setting aside inter-island rivalries to raid the mainland - a brutal chapter that bound the archipelago into the Atlantic slave trade. One who escaped that trade was Benkos Biohó, taken from these islands to Cartagena in Colombia, who broke free and founded San Basilio de Palenque, a town of self-liberated people that endures to this day. The Bijagós' isolation cuts both ways: it preserved a culture, and it has also, in recent decades, drawn narcotraffickers using the remote islands as transit points - the old independence turned, for a time, to darker use.

## Flight Context

The Bijagós Islands spread across the Atlantic off Guinea-Bissau, centered near 11.25°N, 16.08°W and spanning roughly 13,000 km². From altitude the archipelago appears as a sprawling delta-born scatter of green islands, pale sandbanks, and silt-laden channels - a striking natural waypoint. The mainland gateway is Osvaldo Vieira International Airport in Bissau, ICAO GGOV. Recommended viewing altitude 5,000-9,000 ft to appreciate the full extent of the island chain and the tidal patterns that reveal its river-delta origins; light is best in the dry season (December-May) when haze is lower.
