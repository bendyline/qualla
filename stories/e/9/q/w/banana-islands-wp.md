# Banana Islands

Three small islands off the Freetown Peninsula whose quiet beaches hide a turbulent history of shipwrecks, a self-crowned castaway king, a dynasty of slave-trading chiefs, and the freed people who finally made the islands home.

**Location:** 8.1172, -13.2117

**Categories:** Geography of Freetown, Islands of Sierra Leone, Slave trade history, Caulker family, Coastal

## Introduction

A man washed ashore here and made himself a king. William Clevland was an Anglo-Scot working for the Royal African Company when the slave ship he was aboard wrecked on these islands in the 1730s, and rather than wait for rescue, he declared himself ruler of the place. He sealed the claim by marrying Ndamba, a Kissi woman, and founded a small dynasty on the sand. It is a fitting origin for the Banana Islands - a curve of three little islands off Yawri Bay, just southwest of the Freetown Peninsula, where outsized history has always crowded onto a very small stage.

## Three Islands, One Story

Geographically the Banana Islands are simple. Two of them - Dublin and Ricketts - are joined by a stone causeway, a spit of sand that vanishes beneath the water at high tide and reappears when it falls. Dublin is the island of beaches; Ricketts is the island of forest. The third, Mes-Meheux, is the smallest, uninhabited, and today marketed by an eco-tourism venture as an adventure destination. Cupped by the Freetown peninsula and reachable only by boat, ferry, or helicopter, the islands have always lived from the water. Fishing and, increasingly, tourism are the mainstays of a community of around 900 people clustered in the villages of Dublin and Ricketts.

## Wrecks and Crowns

The sea both isolated these islands and delivered their history. In 1747 the Diemermeer, an East Indiaman of the Dutch East India Company, went down here, one of many ships the reefs have claimed. The Cleveland dynasty that Clevland founded did not rule unchallenged. Through the late 1700s, its rivalry with the powerful Caulker family on the nearby Plantain Islands hardened into open and bloody conflict. The fighting ran into the 1800s before it was settled - and it was the Caulkers who prevailed, finally taking control of both island groups and absorbing the castaway king's little realm into their own.

## The Caulker Shadow

To understand who won is to understand one of West Africa's most remarkable families. The Caulkers traced their line to Thomas Corker, a young Englishman who arrived on the Sherbro coast in 1684 in the service of the Royal African Company and married into the local Sherbro ruling house. Their mixed-heritage descendants - the name softened over generations from Corker to Caulker - became some of the wealthiest people on the coast, and they grew rich largely through the Atlantic slave trade, supplying captives to ships bound for the Americas. By the time they took the Banana Islands, the Caulkers were a hereditary power whose chiefdoms would endure into the twentieth century. The islands' beauty sits atop this harder truth: for a long stretch of their history, they were a link in a chain that carried human beings across the ocean against their will.

## A Refuge, At Last

History on these islands eventually turned back on itself. Portuguese sailors had first visited in the 17th century, perhaps earlier, but the people who would give the modern villages their character arrived later and under very different circumstances. In the late 18th and 19th centuries, the Banana Islands were settled by Recaptives - Africans who had been illegally enslaved, then freed when anti-slavery patrols intercepted the ships carrying them, many of those ships bound from or for the Americas. The descendants of these liberated people make up most of the islands' population today, gathered in the villages of Dublin and Ricketts where the old churches, cemetery, and fort still stand among the palms. There is a quiet justice in that arc: a place once entangled in selling people into bondage became, in the end, a home for those who had been freed from it - and the names on the gravestones now belong to families who chose to stay and build.

## Flight Context

The Banana Islands lie at roughly 8.12 N, 13.21 W off the southern tip of the Freetown Peninsula, about 25 nm south of Lungi International Airport (GFLL). From altitude, look for three small forested islands hugging the coast of Yawri Bay: Dublin and Ricketts linked by a faint causeway, with tiny Mes-Meheux beyond. The peninsula's green mountains rise to the north and the town of Kent marks the nearest mainland point. Best viewed at 3,000-5,000 ft in dry-season clarity (November-April); rainy-season cloud and haze can obscure the bay from May to October.
