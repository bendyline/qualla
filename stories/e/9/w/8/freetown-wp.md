# Freetown

A city founded by the formerly enslaved, where the descendants of London's Black Poor, Black Loyalists from Nova Scotia, Jamaican Maroons, and tens of thousands of Africans freed from slave ships built a home and a language of their own.

**Location:** 8.4872, -13.2356

**Categories:** Cities, Capitals in Africa, Port cities and towns in Africa, History, Repatriated Africans, African heritage

## Introduction

On a March morning in 1792, more than a thousand people who had once been enslaved waded ashore on the Sierra Leone peninsula, cleared the jungle until they reached an enormous cotton tree, and gathered beneath it to sing. "Awake and Sing of Moses and the Lamb," the preachers led, and the land was christened with a single, defiant word: Free Town. Nearly every capital city carries a founding myth. Freetown's is unusual in that it is also literally true. This is a city built, from the ground up, by people fleeing slavery toward the promise of freedom.

## Four Waves of the Free

Freetown's people arrived in waves, each more remarkable than the last. First came the Black Poor of London in 1787 - around four hundred formerly enslaved Africans, Afro-Caribbeans, and Black Britons who founded a precarious settlement called Granville Town, which was burned to the ground within three years. Then, in 1792, came the Nova Scotian Settlers: over eleven hundred Black Loyalists who had won their freedom by siding with the British in the American Revolution, only to find cold and discrimination waiting in Canada. They founded Freetown proper. In 1800 came roughly five hundred and fifty Jamaican Maroons, fierce descendants of escaped slaves deported after their war with the British. And from 1808 onward came the largest group of all - tens of thousands of "recaptives," Africans freed by the Royal Navy from slave ships at sea. Out of these strands, a single people emerged: the Krio.

## A Language Born of Many Tongues

When peoples from across the Atlantic world and the whole West African coast are thrown together in one harbour town, they need a way to speak to one another. What grew in Freetown was Krio - an English-based creole stitched through with words and rhythms from Yoruba, the Caribbean, and the languages of the recaptives. Today Krio is the heartbeat of the city, spoken at home by many and understood by nearly everyone, a lingua franca that binds together a population in which no single ethnic group forms even a third of the whole. The Sierra Leone Creole people who carry the name are a minority nationally, yet their language belongs to all of Freetown. To hear it in the markets of the East End is to hear two centuries of survival made audible.

## The Deep Water Harbour

Geography gave Freetown its reason to exist long after its founders' dream took root. The city wraps around the estuary of the Sierra Leone River, one of the largest natural deep-water harbours in the world and the largest on the African continent. Oceangoing ships have berthed here for centuries - first the Royal Navy's anti-slavery squadron, later the Allied convoys that mustered here during the Second World War before crossing the South Atlantic. The harbour still defines the city's economy, handling Sierra Leone's main exports past the wharves of the East End. Above it, the green Lion Mountains rise to the southeast, the feature that gave the country its Portuguese name, Serra Lyoa - the lion range.

## Festivals of Light and Endurance

Freetown is a city that throws itself into celebration. Each year, at the end of Ramadan, the Lantern Festival fills the streets with enormous illuminated lantans - elaborate sculptures up to twenty feet long, built in the shapes of animals, warships, and mythical beasts, lit from within and hauled through the city on wheeled iron frames to the beat of Sierra Leonean music. Christmas brings a month of neighbourhood street parades. The crowds dance in matching family attire, judges weigh creativity and coordination, and the night glows. For a city that has weathered civil war, an Ebola epidemic, a deadly 2017 mudslide, and a 2021 fuel-tanker explosion, this insistence on spectacle and joy is itself a kind of statement.

## Monuments to Freedom

The traces of Freetown's founding are everywhere, if you know where to look. For more than two centuries the great Cotton Tree stood at the city's heart, the spot where the first settlers gave thanks; it fell at last in a violent storm in May 2023, mourned across the nation. Nearby stands the stone King's Yard Gate, through which freed Africans once passed into liberty, and the Sierra Leone National Museum, guarding the country's deep precolonial past. Fourah Bay College, founded in 1827, is the oldest Western-style university in West Africa, where the first student went on to become the first indigenous Anglican bishop on the continent. Beyond the city stretch white-sand beaches and the rainforest sanctuary of Tacugama, home to rescued chimpanzees. Freetown wears its history openly - a living monument to the idea that a people can name their own home.

## Flight Context

Freetown sits at approximately 8.49°N, 13.24°W on the Sierra Leone peninsula, wrapped around one of the world's largest natural deep-water harbours at the mouth of the Sierra Leone River. From the air, look for the broad estuary, the green Lion Mountains rising to the southeast, and the long white beaches of the Freetown Peninsula curving south toward Lumley and Aberdeen. The city's primary airport is Freetown International (Lungi, ICAO GFLL), located across the estuary to the north - travelers reach the city by ferry, water taxi, or helicopter. Hastings Airport (GFHA) southeast of downtown handles small aircraft. Best viewing in the December-February dry season, when the Harmattan haze clears and visibility over the harbour is sharpest; the May-November rainy season brings heavy thunderstorms.
