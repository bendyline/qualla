# 2009 Guinean protests

On 28 September 2009, tens of thousands gathered in a Conakry stadium for a peaceful pro-democracy rally; soldiers sealed the exits and opened fire, killing more than 150 people in a massacre that took fifteen years to reach a verdict.

## Introduction

They came to the stadium to be counted, not to be killed. On the morning of 28 September 2009, tens of thousands of Guineans, by many accounts around 50,000, filled the Stade du 28 Septembre in Conakry for a peaceful rally. They were demonstrating against the military junta that had seized power nine months earlier, and against its leader's signal that he would break his promise and run for president after all. They sang. They held banners. By the end of the day, more than 150 of them were dead.

## The Promise That Was Broken

The crisis traced back to December 2008. Six hours after the death of long-ruling President Lansana Conté, Captain Moussa Dadis Camara appeared on state radio to announce that the army had taken over, dissolving the government and suspending the constitution. At first many Guineans were willing to give him a chance, weary after Conté's decades of authoritarian rule. Camara had promised a swift return to democracy and vowed he would not stand in the coming election. As the months passed, that promise frayed. By September 2009 it was clear he intended to run, and the opposition called Guineans into the streets. The government had already banned protests. The people came anyway.

## The Stadium

When the crowd gathered in the stadium, security forces moved in and sealed the exits, then opened fire on the trapped demonstrators. The killing was not the whole of it. Soldiers beat people with batons and bayonets, and committed mass rape against women in the open. United Nations investigators would later document that at least 109 women and girls were raped or sexually mutilated that day. These were students and traders, mothers and young men, ordinary citizens who had believed a rally could be safe. The official tally counted at least 157 killed and 1,253 injured. Among the wounded was former Prime Minister Sidya Touré, who hid in a hospital restroom and described the carnage to the BBC by phone.

## The Bodies That Vanished

In the days after, families went looking for their dead and could not find them. The junta first insisted only 56 had died, even as relatives reported that bodies had disappeared from morgues. Witnesses described presidential guards loading corpses into trucks, and human rights groups gathered photographs of victims lined along the streets as evidence that the real toll was far higher than the government would admit. Camara, for his part, told Radio France Internationale that the killers were "uncontrollable elements" he could not command, and denied giving any order to shoot. The denials did not survive scrutiny. The Human Rights Watch and a United Nations panel both concluded the violence likely amounted to crimes against humanity.

## Fifteen Years to a Verdict

Justice came slowly, but it came. On the thirteenth anniversary of the massacre, 28 September 2022, a trial finally opened in Conakry. For nearly two years the court heard from more than a hundred victims, eleven accused, and over a dozen witnesses. On 31 July 2024, the court convicted Moussa Dadis Camara and seven others, reclassifying the charges as crimes against humanity, the first such prosecution in Guinea's history. Camara was sentenced to twenty years. The survivors and the families of the dead had waited a decade and a half to hear a court name the crime. For the people who filled that stadium believing they were safe, the verdict was an acknowledgment, long overdue, that they had been real, that they had mattered, and that what was done to them was a crime against humanity itself.
