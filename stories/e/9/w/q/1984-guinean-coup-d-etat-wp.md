# 1984 Guinean coup d'état

When Guinea's founding president died on an operating table in Ohio, the army moved within a week, seizing power in a bloodless coup just hours before the ruling party could name a successor.

## Introduction

The man who had ruled Guinea for twenty-six years died far from home, on an operating table at the Cleveland Clinic in Ohio. Ahmed Sékou Touré, the firebrand who led Guinea's lonely 1958 break from France, succumbed during emergency heart surgery on 26 March 1984. Back in Conakry, the question of who would rule next was supposed to be settled by the only legal party in the country. The army had other plans. Within a week of Touré's death, the soldiers struck.

## A Coup Against an Empty Chair

Touré's death left Guinea without its center of gravity. Prime Minister Louis Lansana Beavogui, who had held that office since 1972, stepped in as interim president, and the Politburo of the Democratic Party of Guinea, the sole permitted party, prepared to choose a permanent successor. Beavogui was expected to win. He never got the chance. On 3 April 1984, just hours before the Politburo was to vote, Colonel Lansana Conté led the military in a bloodless seizure of power. No leader had to die for the regime to fall; it fell because the men with guns moved faster than the men with ballots. Conté was named president on 5 April, before the country had finished mourning its founder.

## Settling Old Scores

The new junta soon turned on the old one. Power struggles broke out within the ruling council, and Conté's rival Lieutenant Colonel Diarra Traoré, who had briefly served as prime minister, was executed after a failed coup attempt in July 1985. Conté used that attempt as cover to eliminate Sékou Touré's inner circle. Among those executed were Touré's half-brother Ismaël Touré and Siaka Touré, both tied to Camp Boiro, the notorious prison where thousands had disappeared under the old regime. The men who had run the machinery of repression now faced its successor. Guinea's first transfer of power was settled not at the ballot box but at the wall.

## The Long Shadow of Lansana Conté

What began as a sudden seizure became a quarter-century in power. Lansana Conté held the presidency until his death on 22 December 2008, ruling Guinea for more than twenty-four years. His passing, like Touré's before it, triggered an immediate coup: within hours, Captain Moussa Dadis Camara and a military council declared the government dissolved. The pattern set in 1984 had repeated itself almost exactly. In Guinea, the death of a strongman had twice become the starting gun for the army. The 1984 coup was not an aberration but the opening of a script the country would replay across the decades.

## Flight Context

The events of the 1984 coup centered on Conakry, Guinea's capital on the Kaloum Peninsula, roughly 9.52°N, 13.70°W. The government district and presidential palace sit near the peninsula's tip. Ahmed Sékou Touré International Airport (ICAO: GUCY) serves the city. From the air the capital reads as a dense strip along the narrow peninsula, the Atlantic on both sides.
