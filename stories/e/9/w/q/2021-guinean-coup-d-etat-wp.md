# 2021 Guinean coup d'état

A former French Foreign Legionnaire turned special forces commander seized Guinea's presidential palace on 5 September 2021, toppling the leader who had once trusted him and sending world aluminium prices to a decade high.

**Location:** 9.5167, -13.7000

**Categories:** Military coups in Guinea, 2021 in Guinea, 2020s coups d'état, Conakry, History of Guinea

## Introduction

The gunfire started at eight in the morning, echoing across the Kaloum quarter where Guinea keeps its government. By the time it stopped, Mamady Doumbouya, a former French Foreign Legionnaire who had returned home only a few years earlier to command the country's elite Special Forces Group, had taken the president he once served as a hostage. Alpha Condé, the first leader Guineans had ever freely elected, was filmed sitting on a sofa, surrounded by soldiers, refusing to speak. The man he had trusted with his protection had just ended his rule.

## The President Who Overstayed

Condé's story made the fall sharper. After decades of autocrats and army strongmen, he had taken office in 2010 as Guinea's first democratically elected president, a genuine break with the past. He was re-elected in 2015. But the constitution limited him to two terms, so in 2020 he engineered a referendum that 'reset' the clock and let him seek a third. The maneuver ignited the 2019-2020 protests, which security forces crushed, leaving more than thirty people dead. By 2021, critics accused him of drifting into the same authoritarian pattern Guineans had elected him to escape. When the soldiers moved, many in the streets cheered.

## The Morning of 5 September

The takeover unfolded fast. The Republic of Guinea Armed Forces surrounded the Sekhoutoureah Presidential Palace and cut off the surrounding government district, telling residents of Kaloum to stay indoors. After a shootout with forces loyal to Condé, the mutineers seized the president. Doumbouya soon appeared on state television, Radio Télévision Guinéenne, the constitution dissolved, the borders sealed, the institutions of the republic declared void. He announced a transitional council, the CNRD, that he said would steer the country for eighteen months. By that evening the coup leaders declared they held all of Conakry. Almost every foreign government condemned the takeover and demanded Condé's release; on the ground, the reaction was strikingly muted, even celebratory.

## A Coup the World Felt in Metal

Few coups ripple through commodity markets, but this one did. Guinea holds around a quarter of the world's bauxite, the reddish ore from which aluminium is made, and the country had become a critical link in the global supply chain during Condé's mining boom. When news of the coup broke, traders panicked. Aluminium prices on the London Metal Exchange surged to a decade high, touching 2,782 dollars per tonne. The irony ran deep: Guinea sat on extraordinary mineral wealth, yet most of its people had seen little of it, with around 60 percent of the workforce still farming and the communities around the mines poorly compensated. A nation rich in the ground and poor at the table was exactly the grievance the coup claimed to address.

## From Soldier to President

Doumbouya did not hand power back. On 1 October 2021, he was sworn in as interim president, and the eighteen-month transition stretched on. The African Union suspended Guinea's membership and imposed sanctions, lifting them only in 2026 after Doumbouya was elected civilian president in the 2025 vote, completing the journey from the officer who seized power to an elected head of state. Guinea's 2021 coup fit a grim regional pattern: Mali, Chad, and Niger all saw their own military takeovers in the same span, a wave of democratic backsliding across the Sahel and West Africa. For Guinea, it was the latest turn in a cycle that had begun decades before, the army stepping in once again where elections had failed to hold.

## Flight Context

The coup centered on the Kaloum government quarter at the tip of Conakry's peninsula, near 9.52°N, 13.70°W, where the Sekhoutoureah Presidential Palace and ministries cluster. Ahmed Sékou Touré International Airport (ICAO: GUCY) serves the capital; its closure during the coup stranded a visiting Morocco football team. From the air the government district sits at the narrow Atlantic-facing tip of the Kaloum Peninsula, with the dense city stretching back toward the mainland.
