# Nouakchott

A capital conjured almost from nothing on the edge of the Sahara, Nouakchott grew from a fishing village of a few thousand into a sprawling Saharan metropolis of more than a million in a single lifetime.

**Location:** 18.0858, -15.9785

**Categories:** Capitals in Africa, Planned communities, Populated coastal places in Mauritania, Populated places in Mauritania, Cities

## Introduction

In 1957, planners drew a capital onto a stretch of coastal dune where almost nothing stood. Mauritania was approaching independence with a problem no flag could solve: it had no capital of its own, having been governed for decades from Saint-Louis, across the river in Senegal. A small fortified fishing village, a ksar, sat near the Atlantic where the desert meets the sea. The site had little to recommend it except the most important thing of all. It belonged to no one faction. Building there meant the new capital would not sit in territory dominated by the Arab-descended Moors of the north or the Black African communities of the south. On 5 March 1958, workers laid the foundation stone for a city meant to hold 15,000 people. Today more than a million live here.

## A Capital Made by Decision

Most great cities grow where rivers meet, where harbors shelter ships, where roads converge over centuries. Nouakchott was decided. French and Mauritanian engineers laid out a downtown of broad avenues and a grid, and they planned, oddly, for a city where commerce was not expected to happen. When independence was proclaimed in 1960, Nouakchott officially became the capital of the new republic that November. The early city was modest almost to the point of fragility: in 1969, fewer than 20,000 people lived here. Then drought struck the Sahel through the 1970s and 1980s, killing herds and emptying the desert of the nomads who had grazed it for centuries. They came to the city. The planned town of 15,000 swelled past every projection, its edges spreading into improvised neighborhoods faster than any blueprint could follow.

## The Avenue That Splits the City

Run a finger along a map of Nouakchott and you find its spine: Avenue Gamal Abdel Nasser, a tree-lined boulevard cutting northeast from the site of the old airport through the heart of downtown. It divides the city in two. The administrative core holds the headquarters of national banks and companies, the ministries, the open-air markets that cluster downtown. Around the formal grid, the city improvised. New districts like the Cinquième Quartier, the Fifth District, sprouted markets and homes within a few years and soon outgrew the original ksar in importance. By 2014 the city had grown so vast that it was split into three administrative regions, Nouakchott-Nord, Nouakchott-Ouest, and Nouakchott-Sud, each with three departments of its own.

## Where the Catch Comes In

For all its planning, the most vivid place in Nouakchott may be the one the planners never centered: the Port de Pêche, the fishing wharf a few kilometers west of downtown. In the evenings, teams of fishermen haul brightly painted wooden canoes back onto the sand, and the day's catch is sold where it lands, loaded onto donkey carts and old cars to be carried into town. Mauritania's waters are among the richest in West Africa, and this beach is where that abundance meets the city. Nouakchott also serves an economy that runs largely on the informal handshake; by some accounts, the great majority of the city's economic activity takes place outside formal records, in the markets, the wharves, and the streets.

## A City Against the Sand

Nouakchott lives in tension with the desert that surrounds it. The dust-laden harmattan winds sweep in off the Sahara, and dune migration is a permanent threat at the city's edges. The same dust that coats the streets settles on solar panels: the Sheikh Zayed solar plant, completed in 2013 and counted among the largest in Africa when it opened, must contend with grit dulling its photovoltaic cells. To the seaward side, a different danger looms. Built barely above sea level on the Atlantic, Nouakchott is acutely exposed to rising seas, and climate studies project that by mid-century its weather may come to resemble that of present-day Khartoum, hotter and harsher. A capital willed into existence now works to hold its ground between encroaching sand and rising water.

## The Improvised Metropolis

Counting Nouakchott has always been an inexact art, in part because some residents remain semi-nomadic, pitching tents where it suits them and folding them away when it does not. What is certain is the scale of the transformation. Where a couple of decades of drought once filled the city with displaced herders living in shanty districts, redevelopment efforts in the 2000s and 2010s brought improved services and housing to tens of thousands in the slum areas, the World Bank counting access to better services for over 181,000 people. The University of Nouakchott opened in 1981; a Chinese-built deepwater port opened in 1986; new five-star hotels rose in the 2020s. From a fishing ksar to a Saharan metropolis in a single human lifetime, Nouakchott is one of the fastest-built capitals on Earth.

## Flight Context

Nouakchott sits at 18.086°N, 15.979°W on Mauritania's Atlantic coast, where the Sahara meets the sea. The city is served by Nouakchott–Oumtounsy International Airport (ICAO: GQNO), which opened in 2016 to replace the older in-city airport. From altitude, look for the pale grid of the planned downtown laid against the tan of surrounding dunes, the dark line of Avenue Gamal Abdel Nasser running northeast, and the Atlantic surf to the west. The coast is often hazed by airborne Saharan dust, so visibility can drop sharply when the harmattan blows. Recommended viewing altitude: 3,000–6,000 ft for the contrast between ocean, city grid, and advancing sand.
