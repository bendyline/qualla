# Chinguetti

A medieval caravan city in the Mauritanian Sahara whose family-held libraries still guard thousands of centuries-old manuscripts as the dunes press against its walls.

**Location:** 20.4574, -12.3623

**Categories:** World Heritage Sites in Mauritania, Historic Sites, Islamic History, Sahara, Ksars

## Introduction

For centuries, when Arab geographers wanted to name the vast emptiness south of Morocco, they did not say Mauritania. They said Bilad Shinqit, "the land of Chinguetti" - and a whole country took its name from a single desert town. Today that town is half-buried. Walk to the western edge of the old quarter and the street simply stops, swallowed by a sand dune. Behind a few of the flat-roofed stone houses, though, in trunks and on wooden shelves, lie thousands of handwritten books that have outlasted empires.

## The Spring of Horses

Long before the dunes arrived, this was green country. Rock paintings at the nearby Amogjar Pass show giraffes and cattle moving through a landscape no one alive has ever seen here - a broad savannah where agricultural peoples, ancestors of the Soninke, once lived. The town's name carries that older world inside it. Chinguetti means "spring of horses" in Azayr, an extinct dialect of Soninke laced with Berber. The first settlement rose around AD 777. By the 11th century it had become a gathering point for the Sanhaja, a confederation of Berber tribes who merged with the Almoravids and rode out to build an empire reaching from Senegal to Muslim Spain. The town's plain, unadorned architecture still echoes their austere faith.

## City of Caravans

After two centuries of decline, Chinguetti was effectively re-founded in the 13th century as a fortified way-station for the great trans-Saharan caravans - the slow rivers of camels that linked the Mediterranean to the gold and salt of sub-Saharan Africa. A wadi splits the town in two, old quarter on one side, newer settlement on the other. In the old sector, houses rise from reddish dry-stone and mud-brick, their flat roofs timbered with palm. Look closely at the doors: many were hewn by hand from massive acacia trees that vanished from this desert generations ago, the wood now older than the trees that could replace it. Narrow lanes crowd toward the central mosque, whose square minaret, capped with five ostrich-egg finials, has stood since the 13th or 14th century.

## The Library in the Desert

Chinguetti's true treasure is paper. The old quarter holds a cluster of family-owned manuscript libraries without peer in West Africa - five principal private collections guarding thousands of Arabic manuscripts, some dating to the later Middle Ages. Their pages range across Qur'anic study, law, astronomy, mathematics, medicine, and poetry. These were working schools, not museums. Along a lane once called the Rue des Savants, the "street of the learned," scholars gathered to argue the fine points of Islamic law. So central was the town to West African Islam that pilgrims bound for Mecca assembled here, and Chinguetti became known across the Muslim world as a place of learning - sometimes counted, in local and regional tradition, among Islam's holy cities. The manuscripts survive today largely because particular families have kept them, generation after generation, in a climate kind to old paper and unkind to almost everything else.

## Holding Back the Sand

UNESCO inscribed Chinguetti on the World Heritage List in 1996, alongside the sister caravan towns of Ouadane, Tichitt, and Oualata. But recognition has not stopped the desert. High dunes mark the western boundary, and house after house has been abandoned to the encroaching sand. In 2021 a BBC documentary, Life at 50 Degrees C, filmed ordinary residents enduring heat that makes the question of survival literal. The town that once named a nation now fights simply to remain on the map - keepers of the libraries dusting sand from manuscripts older than most European universities, the dunes always returning by morning.

## Flight Context

Chinguetti sits at 20.46°N, 12.36°W on the Adrar Plateau of northern Mauritania, about 75 km east of Atar. The nearest airport is Atar (GQPA). From altitude, look for the wadi dividing the old and new quarters, the square minaret of the Friday Mosque, and the unmistakable line where the western edge of town meets advancing sand dunes. Best viewing is in clear, dry season air; expect haze and reduced visibility during Harmattan dust events.
