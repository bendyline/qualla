# Nouadhibou

Mauritania's iron-ore and fishing capital, built on a desert headland where three-kilometre freight trains meet the Atlantic and the world's largest ship graveyard rusts in the bay.

**Location:** 20.9148, -17.0502

**Categories:** Nouadhibou, Dakhlet Nouadhibou region, Regional capitals in Mauritania, Ship graveyards, Railway stations in Mauritania, Port cities

## Introduction

The train is so long you cannot see both ends of it at once. Three kilometres of open hopper cars, heaped with iron ore the colour of dried blood, snake out of the Sahara and grind to a halt at the edge of the Atlantic. This is Nouadhibou, where the desert delivers its only treasure to the sea. Mauritania's second city sits on a narrow spit of land, hemmed by ocean on one side and disputed border on the other, a place that exists because the rock 670 kilometres inland had to go somewhere.

## Iron Out of the Sand

Everything in Nouadhibou bends toward the ore. The Mauritania Railway runs from the mines at Zouerat, deep in the interior, to the loading terminals here on the coast, and its freight trains are reputed to be the longest in the world: up to three kilometres of wagons hauled by locomotives straining against a load of pure iron. The line also carries passengers, many of whom ride atop the ore itself rather than pay for a seat, wrapped against the grit and the cold desert night. The wharf was built in 1966 to handle 50,000 tonnes a year; lengthened in 1977, it grew to a capacity of 320,000 tonnes. The city took its modern name at independence in 1960, shedding the colonial label Port-Etienne, bestowed in 1907 in honour of a French minister of the colonies.

## The Graveyard in the Bay

Look out across Nouadhibou Bay and you see them: dozens of ships, hundreds over the years, beached and abandoned, listing at angles the sea slowly flattens. For decades the calm waters here became one of the largest ship graveyards on Earth, a free dumping ground where owners ran vessels aground rather than pay to scrap them. Rust-streaked trawlers and freighters sit half-submerged, their hulls colonised by gulls and, beneath the surface, by fish that shelter in the wreckage. Salvage crews have hauled many away in recent years, but the bay still holds a fleet of the dead, a slow-motion museum of every ship that someone decided was no longer worth saving.

## A Coast of Departures

Nouadhibou has become a place people leave from. Since 2006, as Morocco tightened its coastline and Spain fortified its enclaves at Ceuta and Melilla, this stretch of Mauritanian shore opened as a departure point for migrants risking the Atlantic crossing to the Canary Islands. The journey is among the deadliest in the world, undertaken in open fishing boats that the surf can swallow whole. Many who gather here have travelled from Mali and across the Sahel, fleeing conflict and poverty for a sea horizon that may or may not deliver them to Europe. These are not statistics drifting through a port town but families and young men weighing an impossible bet, and the coast that ships iron to the world also carries away its most desperate cargo.

## Strange Trades and a Vanished Sun

For a town at the end of the road, Nouadhibou attracts curious commerce. It is known as a clearing house for meteorites scavenged from the open Sahara, dark stones plucked from pale sand and sold on to collectors and, to the dismay of scientists, sometimes lost to research before anyone can study them. The desert here has drawn watchers of the sky before: on 30 June 1973, during one of the longest total solar eclipses of the twentieth century, researchers launched an Aerobee sounding rocket from Nouadhibou to study the sun the moment the moon swallowed it. The sea, the sand, and the sky all converge on this thin peninsula at the continent's western edge.

## Flight Context

Nouadhibou sits at 20.91 N, 17.05 W on the eastern shore of the Ras Nouadhibou peninsula. Nouadhibou International Airport (GQPP) lies at the city's edge. From altitude, look for the long bay studded with wrecked ships, the rail terminals running inland toward Zouerat, and the narrow spit of land that separates Mauritania from Western Sahara just to the west. Best viewing at 3,000-6,000 ft along the coast; the Atlantic haze can soften the headland, but the ship graveyard is unmistakable in clear afternoon light.
