# Puig Major

Mallorca's highest peak is crowned by a military radar dome and closed to the public, and it once relayed the news that Apollo 11 had landed safely on the Moon.

**Location:** 39.8075, 2.7933

**Categories:** Mountains of Mallorca, Mountains of the Balearic Islands, Highest points of Spanish provinces, Serra de Tramuntana, Cold War sites

## Introduction

You can see the summit of Mallorca from half the island, but you cannot stand on it. Puig Major rises 1,445 meters above the Serra de Tramuntana, the highest point on the island, and its peak has been closed to the public for more than half a century, crowned not by a cairn or a cross but by a great white sphere. Inside that dome sits military radar. The mountain still belongs to the armed forces who took it over during the Cold War, and the closest a hiker can legally get is a shoulder just below the top.

## A Summit Made of Ancient Sea

Puig Major is built from Liassic limestone laid down in the Early Jurassic, when this stretch of the Mediterranean lay beneath a warm, shallow sea. Over tens of millions of years the seabed hardened, buckled, and was thrust skyward as Africa pressed against Europe, raising the whole spine of the Serra de Tramuntana. At 1,445 meters the mountain stands higher than anything else on Mallorca, and its heights host life found in few other places, including Agrostis barceloi, a grass native to these slopes. To look at the pale rock is to read deep time directly: an ancient ocean floor, now catching the last light of Mediterranean sunsets more than a kilometer and a half above the water that made it.

## The Mountain the Cold War Claimed

For most of human history the summit was simply the top of the island, walked by shepherds and pilgrims. That changed in the 1950s. Between 1957 and 1959 the United States built a radar station across roughly six hectares of the summit, part of the web of early-warning installations strung across Cold War Europe. The USAF's 880th Aircraft Control and Warning Radar Squadron operated it, scanning the western Mediterranean sky, and the post stayed active until 1993. The peak lies within the quiet inland municipality of Escorca, but for decades its highest ground answered to Washington and Madrid rather than to the villages below. Even now a military presence remains, which is why the true summit stays fenced and forbidden.

## A Whisper from the Moon

The station's strangest moment had nothing to do with war. In July 1969, as Apollo 11 settled onto the Sea of Tranquility, the signals confirming that the astronauts had landed safely were relayed through the satellite station on Puig Major on their way to Mission Control in Houston. For a few charged hours, a mountaintop on a Mediterranean holiday island became one link in the chain carrying the most famous news of the century back to Earth. Shepherds' country had become part of the nervous system of the space age. It is the kind of history that leaves no visible trace, no plaque on the closed summit, yet it happened here, above the orange groves and the sea.

## Las Bolas on the Skyline

Through the second half of the twentieth century, two orange spheres sat on the summit, protective radomes shielding the radar dishes inside. Mallorcans watched them from across the island and gave them an affectionate name: Las Bolas, or Ses Bolles in the local Catalan, the balls. They became an accidental landmark, a pair of bright dots marking the island's high point in a way no natural feature quite did. In 2005 a technological overhaul swept them away and replaced them with a single, larger white sphere, the one that crowns the peak today. Generations who grew up watching two orange globes on the skyline still call the mountain by the old nickname.

## As Close As You Can Get

Hiking runs deep in Mallorcan life, and Puig Major, for all its restrictions, remains a magnet. Walkers cannot reach the true summit, but they climb the shoulders around it, the Penyal des Migdia to the southwest or the Morro d'en Pelut to the north, for views that sweep from the Cúber and Gorg Blau reservoirs to the open sea. Both routes brush against, and sometimes cross, the military zone, often without formal permission, a quiet negotiation between the island's oldest tradition and its newest boundary. From these edges the great white sphere looms close enough to touch with your eyes, if never your hands: the highest point on Mallorca, seen but not summited.

## Flight Context

Puig Major stands at 39.81°N, 2.79°E, the highest terrain on Mallorca at 1,445 m (about 4,740 ft). Its single white radome is a prime visual landmark, visible across much of the island; the Cúber and Gorg Blau reservoirs sit in the valley just to the south. Expect restricted military airspace over and around the summit, and treat the peak as a hard terrain and airspace boundary rather than a waypoint. The nearest major field is Palma de Mallorca (LEPA), roughly 25 km to the south-southwest, with Son Bonet (LESB) nearby on Palma's northern edge. Mountain weather here can build quickly, with orographic cloud, turbulence, and rapidly changing visibility, so maintain generous terrain clearance.
