# Sóller

A mountain-locked valley of oranges on Mallorca's northwest coast, cut off from its own capital until a narrow-gauge railway threaded the rock in 1912.

**Location:** 39.7663, 2.7153

**Categories:** Towns in Mallorca, Populated places in Mallorca, Serra de Tramuntana, Heritage railways, Port towns

## Introduction

For centuries, the easiest way out of Sóller was by boat. Hemmed in by the steep wall of the Serra de Tramuntana, this fertile valley on Mallorca's northwest coast grew rich on oranges, lemons, and olives, and then faced the problem of getting them to market. Carting fruit over the mountains to Palma was slow and punishing, so Sóller looked seaward instead, shipping its citrus from the port and building deeper trade ties with the south of France than with its own island capital. The valley's isolation ended, spectacularly, in 1912, when engineers threaded a narrow-gauge railway through the rock.

## The Little Orange Train

That railway still runs, and riding it remains the classic way to arrive. The Ferrocarril de Sóller opened in 1912, a three-foot-gauge line of heritage wooden electric carriages that clatters up from Palma's Plaça d'Espanya, through the town of Bunyola, and bores through the mountains to reach the valley in about an hour. At Sóller, passengers change to a tram, opened a year later in 1913, which trundles nearly five kilometers down to the Port de Sóller on the coast. Both are crowded with tourists for much of the year, and both were built not for nostalgia but for oranges: the fastest link this valley had ever known between its groves and the wider world.

## A Square Touched by Gaudí's Shadow

The heart of town is the Plaça de sa Constitució, where trams rumble past cafés beneath the Church of Sant Bartomeu. The church was begun in the early 13th century and reworked in every style since, a mostly baroque interior from around 1700 beneath a neogothic bell tower, but its most striking feature is newer than it looks. The dramatic façade was designed in 1904 by Joan Rubió i Bellver, a follower of Antoni Gaudí, who brought a touch of Barcelona's Modernisme to this mountain valley. Rubió also designed the ornate bank on the same square. The result is a plaza where medieval faith, baroque gold, and Catalan Art Nouveau all crowd companionably together.

## Women, Treacle, and Pirates

Sóller's most beloved festival remembers a genuinely dangerous past. In 1561, Algerian corsairs raided the valley, and the attack was famously beaten back, by, among others, a group of enraged local women wielding whatever came to hand. Each May the town stages Es Firo, re-enacting the battle between Moors and Christians in the streets, and tradition holds that spectators should not wear sunhats, which mark them as Christians and draw the mock-attackers' fire until the women and their treacle sap the assault. Later in summer, during the feast of Sant Bartomeu, the Correfoc fire run fills the streets with costumed demons until a symbolic fire brigade drives them back. These are not manufactured spectacles for visitors but old communal memories, replayed year after year.

## The Port with a Secret

Down at the coast, Port de Sóller wraps around a near-circular bay, guarded since 1842 by a lighthouse on the western headland. The port carries a quiet history of its own: during the Spanish Civil War, when Nationalist forces held Mallorca, it served as a naval base, and its tram depot, improbably, was pressed into service as a submarine base, very likely the only tram shed ever to shelter submarines. Today the waterfront is low-rise and easygoing, lined with restaurants and the terminus of that little tram. From the headland at sunset, with the mountains blackening behind the bay, it is easy to forget how hard-won this valley's connection to the outside world once was.

## Ravines, Villages, and a Coming Eclipse

The valley rewards those who wander its edges. East of town lies Biniaraix, a hamlet whose old stone washrooms recall the days before piped water, and beyond it a karstic ravine, the Barranc, climbs the mountain along a centuries-old pilgrimage trail, the toughest stretch of the long GR221 dry stone route. Higher still sits Fornalutx, a stone village celebrated for its beauty and reachable on foot through the orchards. The Irish actor Colm Meaney has made his home in the area. And on 12 August 2026, Sóller will have a front-row seat to a rare event: a total solar eclipse, the darkened sun setting over the sea beyond the port for about a minute and a half at roughly half past eight in the evening.

## Flight Context

Sóller sits at 39.77°N, 2.72°E in a bowl-shaped valley on Mallorca's northwest coast, ringed by the Serra de Tramuntana with Puig Major (1,445 m) and its white radar dome rising to the northeast. The most distinctive landmark is Port de Sóller's near-circular natural harbor a few kilometers northwest of town, along with the thread of the narrow-gauge railway and tram line linking the two. A viewing altitude of 3,500 to 4,500 ft AGL clears the surrounding ridges while keeping the valley and port in frame. The town lies roughly 24 km north of Palma de Mallorca (LEPA), the nearest major field, with Son Bonet (LESB) on Palma's north side. Terrain and rapidly changing mountain weather warrant generous clearance and a southeast approach.
