# Monument to Antonio Maura

Palma's bronze tribute to its statesman son, five-time prime minister of Spain, once toppled by a falling tree.

**Location:** 39.5712, 2.6489

**Categories:** Monuments and Memorials, Palma de Mallorca, Public Sculpture, Spanish History, Balearic Islands, Mariano Benlliure

## Introduction

In November 2014, a falling fig tree did what a lifetime of political opponents never quite managed: it knocked Antonio Maura clean off his pedestal. The bronze statue of Mallorca's most famous statesman crashed to the ground in the Plaça del Mercat, toppled by the collapse of a nearby ficus. It was an undignified end for a man who had governed Spain, though not a permanent one. The monument would be repaired and restored to its place, as Palma's tributes to its favorite son tend to be.

## The Boy from Palma

Antonio Maura was born in Palma in 1853, and the island never stopped claiming him. He left Mallorca for a career in law and then politics on the mainland, rising to the very summit of Spanish public life. Between 1903 and 1922 he served as prime minister of Spain five separate times, leading the Conservative Party through one of the most turbulent stretches of the nation's history. His politics were rooted in a belief in reform imposed from above, and his long career left a deep mark on the Spain of the early twentieth century. Maura was a formidable orator and a divisive one, admired and opposed in almost equal measure, the kind of politician whose name became a rallying cry for supporters and critics alike. When he died in December 1925, his home city set about honoring him in the most permanent way it knew. That the monument was funded by popular subscription, paid for by ordinary citizens rather than the state, says something about how Palma regarded the boy who had gone on to run the country.

## Cast by a Master

The commission went to Mariano Benlliure, the most celebrated Spanish sculptor of the age, a man whose realistic bronzes stood in plazas across the country. Benlliure conceived the memorial as a sculptural ensemble rather than a simple statue. Atop a stone pedestal he placed Maura in bronze, captured mid-speech in an oratorical attitude, one hand raised as if addressing a crowd, forever the parliamentarian. Below him, carved from white marble, sits an allegorical figure of Truth. She points to a cartouche bearing an inscription that translates as a kind of epitaph in stone: that Antonio Maura equaled thought with his life. Truth's marble hand and Maura's bronze one answer each other across the tiers of the memorial. It is a grand and solemn claim, the sort a nation makes about the dead it wishes to remember well, rendered by a sculptor who knew exactly how to make marble and bronze speak.

## A Date Chosen with Care

The monument was unveiled in the Plaça del Mercat on 13 December 1929. The date was no accident. It fell on the fourth anniversary of Maura's death, folding the ceremony into an act of remembrance. The square, one of Palma's busy central plazas ringed by handsome buildings, gave the statesman a stage in the middle of daily life, where locals and visitors would pass beneath his raised hand without necessarily knowing whose voice had once carried across the Spanish parliament. Benlliure's monument joined a district already rich in Modernista architecture, the bronze orator presiding over one of the city's most photographed corners.

## Toppled by a Tree

For eighty-five years, Maura kept his place. Then, on 11 November 2014, a large ficus growing beside the monument gave way and fell, striking the statue and knocking it from its pedestal. The bronze was damaged, the ensemble broken, and for a time the plaza held only an empty base where the orator had stood. The city did not leave it that way. Restorers repaired the damage, and roughly a year later Maura returned to the Plaça del Mercat, hoisted back into position above his marble allegory of Truth. The episode became a small local legend, the day nature toppled a prime minister and the careful repair that followed. The statue that a tree had felled was standing again, addressing a square that had briefly forgotten the sound of his silence.

## Flight Context

The monument stands in the Plaça del Mercat in central Palma (39.57°N, 2.65°E), only a couple of hundred meters from the Gran Hotel on neighboring Plaza Weyler and a short walk from the cathedral, La Seu, on the waterfront. From the air the surest markers are the cathedral and the curve of the Bay of Palma; the monument itself is a street-level detail within the dense old town grid. Palma de Mallorca Airport (LEPA) lies about 8 km to the east. Best encountered on foot, though the old town's compact layout reads clearly from 1,500 to 3,000 feet.
