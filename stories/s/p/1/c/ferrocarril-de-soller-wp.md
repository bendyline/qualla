# Ferrocarril de Sóller

The wooden electric train that a citrus boom paid for, still tunneling through Mallorca's mountains a century later.

**Location:** 39.7069, 2.6936

**Categories:** Railways, Historic Transport, Mallorca, Serra de Tramuntana, Balearic Islands, Scenic Journeys

## Introduction

In 1911, Sóller's wealth came out of the ground in wooden crates: oranges and lemons, packed tight and shipped to markets across Europe. The citrus trade was booming, and the town made an audacious decision about what to do with the money. It would build a railway, one that tunneled straight through the mountains that had always cut Sóller off from Palma and the rest of Mallorca. More than a century later, the same varnished wooden carriages still make that run on narrow-gauge track, brass fittings gleaming, windows thrown open to the air. Locals and guidebooks came to call it the Orange Express.

## Paid for in Citrus

For centuries, the Serra de Tramuntana kept Sóller in a kind of beautiful isolation. The town sat in a fertile valley of orange and lemon groves, but the only way to move that fruit to Palma was over the mountains by mule track or around the coast by boat. When the citrus trade turned lucrative, the growers had both a problem and the means to solve it. Work on the railway began in 1911, financed by the profits of the very oranges and lemons it was built to carry. The nickname stuck for good reason. This was a train that fruit paid for, and the ride between the capital and the valley has been bound up with that golden harvest ever since. Before the railway, getting a crop to market meant days of hauling over mule paths or loading boats along the coast; the train did in an hour what had taken far longer, and it carried people as readily as it carried fruit.

## Thirteen Tunnels Through the Mountains

The line leaves Palma and runs north across the flat central plain, gathering speed past Son Sardina and Bunyola before the real work begins. Ahead rises the Serra de Tramuntana, the limestone spine of Mallorca's northwest coast, and the engineers chose to go through it rather than around. Thirteen tunnels bore through the rock, threading the train into darkness and then back out to sudden views of terraced hillsides and distant sea. The carriages are electric and narrow-gauge, their wooden interiors and brass fittings unchanged in feel from an earlier century. Riders lean from the windows as the train climbs and curves, the mountains closing in and opening again. What began as freight infrastructure became, almost by accident, one of the most scenic rail journeys in the Mediterranean.

## Two Stations, Two Ages

The line's two ends could hardly be more different. Palma's station is a modest, brown-brick building just off the Plaça d'Espanya, its façade dominated by a large "Ferrocarril de Sóller" sign. Part of it has been given over to a museum, cleverly renovated so that big glass windows reflect the tracks and make the rails appear to run right into the rooms. Outside, a popular park sits atop the underground Estació Intermodal, dotted with old railway buildings and train-themed play structures, each one a carriage named for a town along the line. Sóller's station is grander and stranger, developed from Ca'n Mayol, a fortified house dating to 1606. Inside, a small museum displays works by Picasso and Joan Miró, and from its platforms an equally antique tram rattles down to the harbor at Port de Sóller.

## A Railway Owned by Its Town

For all its charm, the Ferrocarril de Sóller is not a museum piece but a working, privately held company, and an unusually democratic one. Most of its 172,000 shares belong to roughly 800 local people, making the railway something close to communal property. That sense of ownership was tested in 2019, when an anonymous group of investors, operating through a firm called Goros Investments, launched a hostile takeover bid worth 25 million euros. Residents pushed back hard against the idea of outsiders seizing their treasured line. The numbers explain the passion. By 2019 the train was carrying over a million passengers a year, no longer merely a way to reach Sóller but a destination in itself. More than a century after the oranges paid for it, the town still guards the railway as its own.

## Flight Context

The railway threads north from Palma (39.57°N, 2.65°E) through the Serra de Tramuntana toward Sóller (39.76°N, 2.71°E). From the air, trace the line where the flat central plain meets the abrupt limestone wall of the Tramuntana range; Sóller itself sits in a bowl-shaped valley of orange groves ringed by peaks, with the Port de Sóller inlet just beyond to the northwest. Palma de Mallorca Airport (LEPA) lies roughly 20 km to the south of Sóller. Best viewed at 2,500 to 4,000 feet in the calm, clear mornings before the afternoon sea breeze builds haze over the mountains.
