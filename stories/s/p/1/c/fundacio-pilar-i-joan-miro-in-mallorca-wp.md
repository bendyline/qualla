# Fundació Pilar i Joan Miró in Mallorca

The Palma museum built around the studios where Joan Miró spent his last decades, walls still bearing his sketches.

**Location:** 39.5550, 2.6092

**Categories:** Art Museums, Joan Miró, Palma de Mallorca, Modern Art, Balearic Islands, Museums

## Introduction

In a plain Mallorcan farmhouse called Son Boter, some of the drawings on the walls were made by one of the twentieth century's most inventive artists, and none were meant to be sold. Joan Miró sketched directly onto the plaster here, working out ideas for enormous sculptures in charcoal and pencil. The graffiti survives, scrawled across rooms he treated as a giant notebook. Today that farmhouse is one piece of the Fundació Pilar i Joan Miró, the Palma museum built around the studios where the painter spent the last decades of a long and restless life. It is one of the rare museums organized around the place of making, not just the finished art.

## A Son of the Island

Miró's bond with Mallorca ran through his family before it ran through his address. Born in Barcelona in 1893, he had deep island roots on his mother's side, and in 1929 he married a Mallorcan woman, Pilar Juncosa, whose name the foundation still carries. The couple lived on the island between 1940 and 1943, sheltering there during the Second World War, and in 1955 they resolved to settle permanently. They bought land on the outskirts of Palma, then open countryside, since swallowed by the city's growth. For an artist who had spent decades moving between Paris, Barcelona and beyond, the island became the fixed point he returned to for the rest of his life. The move rooted one of modern art's restless innovators in a single Mediterranean place for nearly thirty years.

## The Studio He Always Wanted

In his early sixties, Miró finally built the workspace he had imagined for decades. He turned to a friend, the Catalan architect Josep Lluís Sert, and asked him to design a proper studio. Sert delivered a light-filled workshop, angled to catch the island's strong Mediterranean sun, that Miró used until his death in 1983. Its walls and floor were eventually crowded with canvases, tools and the controlled chaos of a working painter. The couple lived beside it at the Son Abrines estate, which remains private and closed to the public. Photographs of the Sert studio show an old man among vivid, half-finished work, still experimenting in his eighties, still refusing to repeat himself. The building survives much as he left it, a rare chance to stand where an artist actually made things.

## Graffiti on the Walls

In 1959, Miró bought a second, older building nearby: Son Boter, a typical eighteenth-century Mallorcan house. He used it as the place to develop his largest works, and its walls became his sketchbook. Rather than confine his ideas to paper, he drew straight onto the plaster, roughing out forms for monumental sculptures in bold, gestural lines. That graffiti can still be seen, offering an unguarded look at how a finished Miró began, as a scribble on a farmhouse wall. Where the Sert studio shows the painter at work, Son Boter shows the mind behind it, thinking out loud in charcoal.

## A Gift to the City

Miró wanted his island retreat to outlive him as something public. On 7 March 1981, he formally offered the buildings and a body of work to the people of Palma, donating both the studios and a vast collection of paintings, drawings, sculptures and graphic works, along with an archive of his life. He and Pilar Juncosa created the foundation that year, though the full agreement with the city council would not be made public until 1992, when the museum formally opened. To house and rotate the works, the architect Rafael Moneo designed a spare new exhibition building, completed after the artist's death. The collection is staggering in scale: 118 paintings on canvas, more than 1,500 drawings, hundreds of prints and dozens of sculptures, most made from the 1960s on. Around it sits an archive of some 800 letters and thousands of documents, and works by friends and peers including Marc Chagall, Eduardo Chillida and Antoni Tàpies. By its twenty-fifth anniversary in 2017, rebranded as Fundació Miró Mallorca, it was drawing tens of thousands of visitors a year, the great majority from abroad.

## Flight Context

The foundation sits above Cala Major on Palma's western edge (39.56°N, 2.61°E), a few kilometers southwest of the old city and its cathedral. From the air, the low white Moneo building and its sculpture garden stand among dense coastal suburbs, with the wide sweep of the Bay of Palma to the south. Palma de Mallorca Airport (LEPA) lies about 10 km to the east, across the bay. Best appreciated at low level, 1,500 to 2,500 feet, in the clear light that first drew Miró to settle here.
