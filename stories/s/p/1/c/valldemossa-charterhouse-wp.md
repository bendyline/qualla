# Valldemossa Charterhouse

A royal palace turned Carthusian monastery where Chopin spent one bitter, brilliant winter composing his Preludes.

**Location:** 39.7089, 2.6219

**Categories:** Monasteries, Valldemossa, Frédéric Chopin, Mallorca, Serra de Tramuntana, Historic Buildings

## Introduction

Frédéric Chopin came to Valldemossa to get well, and nearly died instead. In the winter of 1838, the composer arrived at the old Carthusian monastery high in Mallorca's mountains with the writer George Sand and her two children, hoping the Mediterranean air would ease his failing lungs. Instead the season turned cold and wet, the damp stone cells offered little comfort, and his tuberculosis worsened. And yet, in those few grim months, Chopin composed some of the most beloved music ever written for the piano. The monastery that nearly broke him became one of the most storied buildings in Spain.

## A Palace for a Prince

Long before the monks or the music, this was a royal address. In the early fourteenth century, King James II of Majorca chose the spot, more than 400 meters up in the Serra de Tramuntana, to build a palace for his son Sancho. It became known simply as the Palace of King Sancho, a mountain retreat with commanding views over the valley and the distant sea. The Kingdom of Majorca was then briefly its own realm, and its rulers wanted a residence to match the drama of the landscape. The Tramuntana range shelters the valley from the north, and the clear light here has drawn artists and pilgrims ever since. Even now, stripped of its court and its crowns, the site keeps the airy, elevated feel of a place built for a prince to survey his island.

## From Kings to Carthusians

In 1399, the royal connection gave way to a religious one. Martin of Aragon handed over all the crown's possessions in Valldemossa to the Carthusian order, and the palace became a charterhouse, a monastery for one of Christianity's most austere and silent brotherhoods. The Carthusians reshaped the complex around their way of life: a cloister, one of the oldest surviving parts of the buildings, an apothecary's pharmacy, a garden, and the rooms of the Prioral Cell, with its chapel, library, audience room and dormitory. Among the island's holy figures honored here is Catherine of Palma, one of Mallorca's patron saints, born in Valldemossa in the sixteenth century. The monks kept their vows in this place for more than four centuries, until 1835, when a nationwide confiscation of church property forced the community out for good.

## The Winter of Chopin and Sand

The monks' departure is what opened the door to Chopin. With the Carthusians gone, the empty cells were rented out to ordinary tenants, and it was one of these, Cell 4, that Chopin and George Sand took for the winter of 1838 to 1839. Their stay was famously miserable. The locals, wary of a visibly sick foreigner, kept their distance; the weather was brutal; and Sand later poured her frustration into a sharp-tongued travel book, Un hiver à Majorque, "A Winter in Majorca." But the same cold cell saw extraordinary work. It was here that Chopin labored over his Preludes, Opus 28, a set of short pieces that reinvented what a miniature for the piano could hold. Among them is the famous "Raindrop" prelude, its repeated note echoing, so the story goes, the water dripping from the monastery roof.

## A Museum of Cells

Today the Valldemossa Charterhouse is a museum, and its greatest draw is the memory of that unhappy, productive winter. Cells 2 and 4 have been preserved as the Chopin and George Sand Museum, displaying personal belongings, manuscripts and one of Chopin's Pleyel pianos, the instrument he waited weeks to have released through Mallorca's customs. Visitors move through the same rooms where he coughed and composed, then out into the cloister, the old pharmacy with its rows of jars, and the garden terraces looking down the valley. Palace, monastery, refuge and shrine to a composer, the building has been all of these in turn. Each summer, recitals of Chopin's music return to the cloister, the notes he wrote here sounding again in the place that shaped them. The valley below fills with travelers climbing to see where a Polish composer spent one hard Mallorcan winter. Few monuments hold so many separate lives so gracefully within a single set of walls.

## Flight Context

The charterhouse crowns the village of Valldemossa in the Serra de Tramuntana (39.71°N, 2.62°E), more than 400 meters above sea level on Mallorca's northwest flank. From the air, look for the village's tight cluster of ochre roofs in a green terraced valley, with the monastery's bell tower the tallest thing in it and steep limestone ridges rising all around. Palma de Mallorca Airport (LEPA) lies roughly 20 km to the southeast, beyond the mountains. Mountain flying rules apply: fly at 3,000 to 5,000 feet, favor calm mornings, and watch for turbulence and cloud clinging to the Tramuntana peaks in the afternoon.
