# Es Baluard

Palma's museum of modern and contemporary art fills the great stone bastion of the city's old sea walls, tracing a century of Balearic art from the painters who chased the island's light to Miró, Picasso, and Barceló.

**Location:** 39.5700, 2.6414

**Categories:** Mallorca, Palma de Mallorca, Art Museums, Contemporary Art, Balearic Islands, Culture

## Introduction

The name gives it away: Es Baluard means 'the bastion.' Palma's museum of modern and contemporary art occupies the great stone bulwark of the city's old sea walls, a Renaissance fortification built to repel cannon fire and now given over to Picasso and Miró. Opened on 30 January 2004, Es Baluard holds more than seven hundred works tied to the Balearic Islands or to international renown, and it wears its setting openly — you climb ramps and cross balconies inside the ramparts, moving between gallery and battlement almost without noticing. Few museums make the leap from fortress to gallery so completely, or so gracefully.

## Art in the Ramparts

The museum was carved into the fortifications in the early 2000s, the work of architects Lluís García-Ruiz, Jaume García-Ruiz, Vicente Tomás, and Angel Sánchez Cantalejo, whose 2003 design threads more than five thousand square metres of building through the old bastion. Half of that space, some 2,500 square metres, is given over to exhibitions. Three storeys step down through the walls, linked to the street outside, to the ramparts, and to one another by ramps, skylights, and generous interior balconies — an arrangement the architects described as an 'indoor street.' The effect is that the visitor never quite leaves the fortress: light falls from above onto contemporary canvases, and a turn along a balcony opens suddenly onto the bay below. It is a rare building where the architecture competes, cheerfully, with the art on its walls.

## How a Collection Comes Together

A museum like this is assembled rather than born. The heart of Es Baluard's holdings arrived as donations and deposits from the Fundació d'Art Serra, joined by works entrusted to it by the city of Palma, the Council of Mallorca, and the regional government of the Balearic Islands. To that civic foundation, more has steadily been added — pieces bought, given, or lent by artists, collectors, and institutions — until the reserve passed seven hundred works. The result is less one collector's vision than a shared inheritance, a portrait of a place built up by many hands and meant to keep growing. That civic authorship suits the building, which was itself a joint undertaking of city, island, and region. A museum stitched into public walls could hardly have been assembled any other way.

## The Light That Drew the Painters

The collection opens where modern Mallorca's art does: in the late nineteenth century, when the island became a magnet for painters chasing its light. Es Baluard traces the renewal of Spanish landscape painting through figures like Joaquim Mir, Santiago Rusiñol, and Joaquín Sorolla, and through Hermen Anglada-Camarasa, whose vivid canvases helped fix Mallorca's place in the story. The island's role in that renewal was not incidental but central. Alongside them hang the works of Antoni Gelabert and Tito Cittadini, and of two women who moved in the same artistic and literary circles, Pilar Montaner de Sureda and Norah Borges. Together they record a moment when a quiet Mediterranean island convinced a generation of artists that its coastline and its clarity were worth crossing the sea for. Anglada-Camarasa in particular made Mallorca central to his art, and the island has kept his work close ever since.

## From the Avant-Garde to Now

From there the galleries march forward through the upheavals of the twentieth century. The avant-garde is present in strength — María Blanchard, Wifredo Lam, Fernand Léger, André Masson, Roberto Matta, Robert Motherwell, and the towering pair of Joan Miró and Picasso, both of whom left deep marks on Spanish art. The postwar decades bring Antoni Tàpies, Antonio Saura, and Juan Genovés, and then a new generation led by Miquel Barceló, the Mallorcan painter of international fame. The story does not stop at the canvas: the contemporary rooms make space for Marina Abramović, Christian Boltanski, and the Mallorcan photographer Toni Catany, among many others. In a single building, the visitor walks from the island's first modern brushstrokes to the restless art of the present day. It is a great deal of history to hold in one fortress, and the walk through it doubles as a walk through the island's own hundred years.

## Flight Context

Es Baluard stands at 39.570°N, 2.641°E, on the western edge of Palma's old town, built into the historic sea walls just above the waterfront. It lies within the immediate control zone of Palma de Mallorca (LEPA), only about 6 km southwest of the airport, so treat it as a landmark to identify from a distance rather than to orbit — the city and its bay sit under a busy, tightly managed approach. The clearest visual references are the bastion walls above the port, the marina and cruise terminals of Palma's harbour, and the great Gothic bulk of Palma Cathedral just to the east. Any transit here belongs at a respectful altitude with full attention on LEPA traffic. Visibility over the Bay of Palma is usually excellent.
