# Miquel Capllonch Rotger

A fisherman's town on Mallorca's northern shore sent one of its sons to the courts of Berlin, and welcomed him home with a bronze bust in the square.

**Location:** 39.9077, 3.0818

**Categories:** Music, Composers, Cultural History, Mallorca, Pollença

## Introduction

On a small square facing the harbor at Port de Pollença, a bronze figure keeps watch, and the town around it carries his name. Miquel Capllonch Rotger was born on the northern shoulder of Mallorca in January 1861, into a Balearic world of fishing boats and stone terraces that rarely sent its children to the palaces of Europe. He would leave for Madrid, then Berlin, and come back with the bearing of a man who had played for kings. Yet the plaza that remembers him is not grand. It is an ordinary seaside square where children chase gulls and old men debate the wind, which is precisely the kind of homecoming his music kept reaching for.

## A Cousin at the Keyboard

His first teacher was family. Joan Rotger, his cousin and the organist of the Pollença parish church, set the boy at the keyboard and gave him his foundations, the way village music has always passed from one pair of hands to the next. Talent outgrew the parish quickly. Capllonch studied next with William Massot, then traveled to the Conservatory of Music in Madrid, where he worked under Tragó, Galiana, Chapí, and Hernando, names that carried real weight in Spanish musical life. In Madrid he also performed the celebrated Polonaise by Carl Maria von Weber, a showpiece for any young pianist. For a young man from a Mallorcan port, the capital was a different planet, loud with ambition and opportunity, and he clearly held his own in it.

## Berlin and a Famous Friend

The Island's Council awarded him a grant to finish his training in Germany, and he enrolled at the Conservatory of Music in Berlin. There he met one of the towering pianists of the age. Anton Rubinstein, the Russian virtuoso and composer whose playing had astonished concert halls across the continent, became not merely a teacher but a genuine friend. Imagine the distance traveled: a boy who learned scales from his cousin the church organist now sitting in the orbit of a man whose name filled European programs. Berlin sharpened him, and the friendships he made there opened doors that a provincial upbringing never could have.

## Courts and Crowns

Talent and connection carried him into rooms most musicians only read about. Capllonch taught music for the royal families of Prussia, the Hohenzollerns, and for the royal house of Saxony, and he gave concerts for European royalty. It was a life of gilded salons and long train journeys, yet he never fully severed the thread back to Mallorca. In 1906 he married Gabriela Miteau, and together they raised four children. In 1912 the family moved to Madrid, and three years later to Barcelona, following the restless geography of a working artist who kept chasing the next audience, the next commission, the next city.

## The Bust by the Sea

He came home to die. Capllonch passed away in Pollença on 21 December 1935, and the town he had left as a boy claimed him as its own. Today a bronze bust stands in the main square of Port de Pollença, and the plaza itself, Plaça de Miquel Capllonch, bears his name. His catalog runs heavily to sacred and choral works, from the Marxa pontifical to a stream of Ave Maries and canticles, the music of a man raised in the shadow of a parish organ. But listen for the two pieces that name his home ground, the Alborada de Pollença and La ximbomba d'Artà, the latter borrowing the growl of the ximbomba, the friction drum that still turns up at Mallorcan festivals. Whatever courts he played, the island kept its claim on him.

## Flight Context

Port de Pollença sits at 39.9077 N, 3.0818 E on the sheltered Bay of Pollença, at the northern tip of Mallorca. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,500 to 4,000 feet, low enough to trace the crescent bay, the marina, and the small waterfront square. The Formentor peninsula juts northeast into the sea, and the jagged wall of the Serra de Tramuntana rises to the west, both unmistakable navigation anchors. Palma de Mallorca airport (LEPA) lies roughly 50 km to the southwest; Menorca airport (LEMH) is across open water to the east. Clear Mediterranean visibility is common outside summer haze and the winter tramuntana blow.
