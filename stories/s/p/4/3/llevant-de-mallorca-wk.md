# Llevant de Mallorca

Mallorca's eastern face in miniature: prehistoric stone towers, wild headlands, red sausage and airy pastries, all packed into a coast where the deep past lies just beneath your feet.

**Location:** 39.6233, 3.3397

**Categories:** Mallorca, Balearic Islands, Travel, Mediterranean, Archaeology, Food and wine

## Introduction

Three thousand years ago, a people whose name is lost to us stacked great slabs of stone into towers across the eastern hills of Mallorca. Later islanders remembered them only as the Masters of Stone, and called their towers talayots — a word that traces back through Catalan and Castilian to the Arabic atalaji, a watchman on guard. The Masters built to last. They built so well that centuries of later rulers simply quarried their monuments for ready-cut blocks, and some of that prehistoric stone was carried off to help raise the great Cathedral of Mallorca in Palma. This is the Llevant, the island's eastern face — a stretch of coast and farmland where the deepest past is never far beneath your feet.

## A Whole Island, Concentrated

The Llevant is often described as a kind of Mallorca concentrate, and the description fits: nowhere else on the island are hills, farmland, cliffs, hidden coves, and long sandy beaches gathered into so small a space. Wind and rain have rounded the limestone into gentle rises, cloaked in the garriga — the low Mediterranean scrub of mastic and gorse, tree heather and wild olive, with lavender, rosemary, and rock roses tucked into the undergrowth. From the headland of Cap Ferrutx at the northeastern tip, the land unspools below you: the Serra de Llevant rising to just over five hundred meters in one direction, and in another the ground dropping out of sight toward the Coves del Drac, whose galleries reach twenty-five meters below the level of the sea.

## The Watchtowers

The talayots still stand, scattered through the fields and scrub. The most important of them is Ses Païsses, a walled prehistoric settlement; another, Capocorb Vell, lies further south; and at Hospitalet Vell, on the road from Manacor toward the coast, a square-planned tower still holds its stone column and its roof of massive slabs, raised somewhere between 1800 and 1500 BC. Who these builders were, and how they lived, is still only partly understood — much of what they made was pulled down and reused before anyone thought to ask. The Masters of Stone left no writing and no names, only the towers themselves and a scattering of walls, standing where they were set three millennia before the first tour bus rolled past.

## Fortresses, Windmills, and a Champion

The centuries that followed left their own marks. Above Cala Ratjada rises the Castle of Capdepera, a fourteenth-century fortress; near Manacor stands the Torre dels Enagistes, a fortified manor from the same era — built after the Catalan conquest of 1229 — that now houses the town museum and its treasures, among them a mosaic salvaged from the early Christian church of Son Peretó. North of Artà, the Sanctuary of Sant Salvador crowns its hill, while in Son Servera the Església Nova stands roofless and unfinished, a grand church abandoned when the money simply ran out. And just outside Manacor sits a shrine of a very modern kind: the tennis academy founded by Rafael Nadal, the town's most celebrated son, where new players are trained on the red clay he made his own.

## The Peninsula They Left Wild

Not all of the Llevant went to the resorts. In the northeast, the Natural Park of the Península de Llevant protects some 21,507 hectares — roughly 16,232 of land and 5,275 of sea — of untouched bays and low mountains where the dwarf palm, Europe's only native palm, sets the tone of the whole landscape. Peregrine falcons and booted eagles ride the updrafts along the cliffs, and coral gulls wheel above the water. Most of Mallorca's remaining tortoises live within these boundaries, moving slowly through the scrub. It is a deliberate emptiness, held in reserve against the crowded beaches a few miles south — a reminder of what the entire coast looked like before the hotels arrived.

## A Table Set from the Island

Everything the Llevant eats, it tends to make itself. Its most famous product is sobrassada, a soft, brick-red sausage of air-dried, seasoned island pork, protected by law and produced by eight makers within the region alone. From its ovens come ensaïmades, the coiled, snail-shaped pastries — 'the lightest, airiest and most delicious' on Mallorca, by local reckoning — turned out by nine certified bakers here. There is olive oil under the Oli de Mallorca seal, and wine from vines whose ancestors the Romans first planted. Strangest of all is the sea fennel, or fonoll marí, that women still gather in baskets along the splash-wet rocks; rich in vitamin C, it once rode in every sailor's kit as a guard against scurvy, seasoning Mallorcan cooking and crossing oceans in the same breath.

## Flight Context

The Llevant occupies the eastern third of Mallorca, centered near 39.62°N, 3.34°E. From 6,000–10,000 feet the whole shape reads clearly: the Serra de Llevant running down the spine, the built-up resort strip of Cala Millor, Sa Coma, and Cala Ratjada tracing the northeast coast, and the wild, empty headlands of the Península de Llevant natural park at the island's northeastern corner near Cap Ferrutx. Inland, the town of Manacor and the Ma-15 highway from Palma make easy waypoints. Nearest airports are Palma de Mallorca (LEPA), about 60 km to the west, and Menorca (LEMH) to the northeast across the channel. Summer skies are clear and blue, best flown in the calm of morning before afternoon heat raises a coastal haze.
