# Siege of Artà

In March 1230, during the conquest of Mallorca, James I of Aragon spent two days besieging sixty defenders trapped in a cave near Artà.

**Location:** 39.6936, 3.3494

**Categories:** History, Medieval History, Reconquista, Military History, Aragon, Mallorca

## Introduction

A message reached the king: sixty armed men were holed up in a cave near Artà, on the far eastern shoulder of Mallorca. It was March 1230, and the war for the island was supposed to be all but over. The Conquest of Majorca had begun the previous autumn, when James I of Aragon — remembered by history as James the Conqueror — landed an army of Catalans and Aragonese on the island's western coast. Palma had fallen that winter, and yet the fighting refused to end. Across the mountains and the coast, the island's Muslim defenders had scattered into caves and hilltop castles, and the young king was learning that taking a city and taking an island were very different things.

## After the City Fell

When Madîna Mayûrqa — the city the conquerors would rename Palma — fell in December 1229, the surrender brought no peace. The island's last Muslim governor, Abu Yahya Muhammad, was killed, and in the violence that followed the city's fall, thousands died. Those who survived did not simply submit. A leader named Abu Hafs ibn Sayrî withdrew into the highlands, where accounts say he gathered some sixteen thousand refugees and fighters. They fortified themselves in the castles of Alaró, Pollença, and Santueri, and in the folds of the Serra de Tramuntana, naming a man called Xuiap de Xivert as their new lord. Most of James's knights, meanwhile, had already sailed home to Catalonia, leaving the king to hold a hostile island with a dangerously thin force. Even leading a small raiding party into the western mountains had become too dangerous to risk.

## The Cave at Artà

So when word came of the sixty defenders near Artà, the king moved with care. On 19 March 1230 two of his commanders, Nuño Sánchez and Hugh of Forcalquier, rode east with him and pitched camp near the coast, at the edge of a river. This was no siege of towers and battlements but of a hole in the rock. The defenders held the mouth of the cave, hurling stones down on anyone who came near the entrance, and for two days the attackers could do little but press against the door. The end came not by sword but by fire. The besiegers burned the barricades, and on 21 March the trapped men surrendered. The whole grim episode lasted barely forty-eight hours.

## The Longer War

The fall of one cave settled nothing lasting. James sailed back to Catalonia in November 1230, and the resistance in the mountains ground on into the following year. In February 1231, Abu Hafs ibn Sayrî died along with some six thousand of his followers, and afterward Xuiap de Xivert surrendered in the Serra de Tramuntana. Even then, roughly two thousand Mallorcan Muslims refused the terms they were offered and shut themselves inside the castle of Pollença. For them, surrender on such conditions meant exile or worse, and many clearly preferred a castle's walls to the victors' mercy. It, too, was besieged. There the Majorcan chief Abu-Ali Umar died, and with him collapsed much of the island's organized defense.

## What the Chronicles Remember

Much of what we know of these months comes from the Llibre dels fets, the remarkable memoir James dictated about his own life and campaigns — a victor's account, told in the victor's voice. Read against the grain, it records a brutal reality: a native population defending its homes cave by cave and castle by castle, and dying in the thousands as it lost. The people who sheltered near Artà were not statistics in a chronicle but families and fighters who chose the rock over surrender. Today Artà is a quiet town of honey-colored stone crowned by a hilltop sanctuary, and little there marks the frantic days of 1230 — but the eastern hills still hold their caves, and the memory of who once hid inside them.

## The Making of a Kingdom

The fall of Artà's cave was a small act in a much larger drama. James I's conquest of Mallorca, begun in 1229, pulled the island out of the Muslim world and into the Christian kingdoms of the western Mediterranean, reshaping its language, faith, and rulers for the eight centuries that followed. The scattered sieges of 1230 and 1231 — cave, mountain, and castle — were the last convulsions of that transfer of power. For the island's defenders, they marked the end of a world; for James, they secured the first great prize of a reign that would later add Valencia to his crown and win him the name history remembers him by.

## Flight Context

The action unfolded near Artà, at 39.6936°N, 3.3494°E on Mallorca's rugged eastern shoulder, where the Llevant hills tumble toward the sea. From 2,500–4,000 ft AGL the town of Artà and its hilltop Sant Salvador sanctuary make the clearest landmark; the cave-riddled eastern coastline lies a few kilometers to the east. Palma's airport (LEPA) is about 60 km to the southwest, and Menorca (LEMH) sits across the channel to the northeast. Expect afternoon sea breezes and some turbulence over the coastal ridges; morning light best reveals the folded terrain that once hid the island's last defenders.
