# Mallorca

The largest of the Balearic Islands, where Chopin once waited for his piano: a mountain wall along one coast, a farming plain at its heart, and a shoreline remade by mass tourism since the 1950s.

**Location:** 39.6167, 2.9833

**Categories:** Islands, Balearic Islands, Mallorca, Mediterranean, Travel, Spain

## Introduction

Chopin's piano was late. When the composer came to Mallorca with the novelist George Sand in November 1838, his Pleyel was still making its way from Paris, so he made do with an instrument built in Palma by Juan Bauza. Hostility from locals who learned the couple were not married pushed them into lodgings in a former Carthusian monastery at Valldemossa. There, his health worsening in the bad weather, Chopin reported that the island's three most celebrated doctors had seen him: "The first said I was dead, the second that I am dying, and the third that I'm going to die." The Pleyel did arrive, he wrote, "in spite of the sea, the bad weather and the Palma customs," and he finished his Preludes on it. In those days Mallorca was the undiscovered Mediterranean. It is not undiscovered now.

## A Mountain Wall and a Farming Plain

Mallorca is the largest of the Balearic Islands, yet it is a compact place to know. A mountain range, the Serra de Tramuntana, runs along the whole northwest coast from Andratx to Pollença and out to the headland of Cap Formentor, where birds work the cliffs. The range's limestone bluffs hold plenty of climbing routes, and the local specialty is psicobloc, climbing above deep water with only the sea below. Walkers take about eight days, and some guidebooks allow ten, to follow the GR221 trail along that same coast, from Andratx to Port de Pollença. Inland, the centre of the island is an agricultural plain, and a lower range of hills follows the southeast shore. Distances stay short: crossing from Palma to Alcúdia is only 55 km. Twenty kilometres to the south lies Cabrera, the largest island of an uninhabited archipelago and a national park, whose protected waters are described as offering the best diving around Mallorca.

## An Army of Cement Mixers

Mass tourism reached the island in the 1950s and boomed through the 1960s and 1970s, and it kept growing after Franco died in 1975 and Spain presented a friendlier, more modern face to the world. The Balearics sat within easy flying range of Britain and Germany, and Mallorca drew a conservative clientele who wanted warmth, bars and beaches on a modest income. Ahead of them, in Wikivoyage's memorable phrase, marched "an army of cement mixers" that lined the coastline with hotel blocks. When the cost of flying dropped from the 1990s, crowds of young party-goers joined them. Counting visitors from mainland Spain, the guide puts the yearly total at over 13 million, and, the guide says, Magaluf has the worst reputation for binge drinking and street rowdiness. The principal tourist areas run along a few strips: 30 km either side of Palma, west to Magaluf and east past the airport to S'Arenal, and again from Alcúdia to Can Picafort. Step inland and the hotels thin out. Visitors there stay in fincas, converted farmhouses on working farms and vineyards.

## Cathedral, Charterhouse and an Unfinished Church

Palma's old town is centred on its cathedral, and stretches of the old city walls still stand beside its narrow streets, though most of the wall was pulled down from the early 1900s. In other towns the church is usually the centrepiece, often 17th-century Baroque. The oddest stands in Sa Servera near Cala Millor: the shell of a Gothic church that was never completed. Valldemossa's charterhouse has lived the most lives. It began as a palace built by King James II of Majorca for his son Sancho. In 1399 Martin of Aragon yielded the royal property to Carthusian monks, who remained until 1835, when the community was forced to leave and the buildings passed into private hands. That is how a composer and a novelist came to take a cell there three years later. The "Chopin cell" is now a museum, and among its exhibits is his Pleyel upright piano.

## Pirates, Treacle and Hellfire

Most of Mallorca's fiestas have religious roots, with great Madonnas teetering in pomp down ancient streets. A few have always been secular, and the town of Sóller keeps two of the strangest. Es Firó, in May, involves pirates, enraged women, treacle and catapults. In August comes the Correfoc, when Sóller is invaded by hellfire demons until the fire brigade douses them. Catalan and Spanish are both official here, and most people speak both, but among themselves islanders converse in Mallorquín, a sub-dialect of the Balearic variety of Catalan. Sóller is also the end of the line for a heritage train from Palma, where passengers change to a wooden tram down to the port. The train has become so crowded with tourists that, the guide reports, locals complain mightily about it, while secretly preferring the bus that hurtles through the Ma-11 tunnel.

## Pork Fat, Almonds and a Revived Vintage

Mallorcan cooking is built on bread, vegetables, olive oil and meat, especially pork. Pa amb oli, bread with olive oil and tomato and perhaps cheese or tuna, makes a simple summer meal. Sobrassada, a sausage of pork, paprika and seasonings, is eaten plain or toasted on bread, and it is normally not spicy. Breakfast belongs to the ensaïmada, an airy spiral bun made with pork fat and sprinkled with powdered sugar, traditionally dipped in hot chocolate. Dessert might be gató, an almond cake served with almond ice cream, while autumn brings bunyols, sweet pastries of boiled potato and flour fried in hot oil. Wine was long the weak point. Quality and quantity were both so low that Mallorca had to import it, until the industry was revitalised in the 1990s. Today there are some 70 vineyards, and two areas, Binissalem and Pla i Llevant, hold Denominació d'Origen status.

## Flight Context

This story is anchored at 39.62°N, 2.98°E in Mallorca's agricultural interior. The island is compact enough to take in whole from 15,000-25,000 feet on a clear day: the Serra de Tramuntana makes an unmistakable ridge along the northwest coast from Andratx to Cap Formentor, the central plain spreads out as farmland, and a lower line of hills follows the southeast shore. Palma, with its cathedral and old walls, sits on the south coast, flanked by resort strips running west to Magaluf and east to S'Arenal; another strip runs from Alcúdia to Can Picafort in the northeast. Palma de Mallorca Airport (LEPA), the island's only commercial airport, lies about 4 nautical miles east of the city and handles heavy traffic in summer. Menorca Airport (LEMH) lies about 70 nautical miles to the east-northeast across the water, and the uninhabited Cabrera archipelago sits roughly 10 nautical miles off Mallorca's south coast. Coastal winds and waves often pick up in the afternoon.
