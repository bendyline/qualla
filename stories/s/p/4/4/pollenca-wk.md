# Pollença

A preserved medieval town in Mallorca's northeastern mountains, famous for the 365 steps that climb to its hilltop Calvary.

**Location:** 39.8772, 3.0164

**Categories:** Towns & Cities, History, Religious Traditions, Serra de Tramuntana, Balearic Islands, Mallorca

## Introduction

Three hundred and sixty-five stone steps climb from the main square of Pollença to a small oratory on the hill above — one for every day of the year, cypresses standing sentinel along the way. On the night of Good Friday, they become the stage for one of Mallorca's most haunting rituals, when black-cloaked figures carry an effigy of Christ down the steps by torchlight, in complete silence. Pollença rewards the uphill effort. It is a tight medieval knot of narrow alleys and tall stone houses in the mountains of the island's northeast, carefully preserved because, for centuries, almost nothing came to disturb it.

## The Town That Inherited a Name

Pollença owes its name to a city that stood ten kilometers away, down on the coast. Roman Pollentia was Mallorca's chief port for trade with Rome and the eastern Mediterranean, while Palma handled traffic with mainland Spain and North Africa. But when the Roman Empire fell, Pollentia suffered attack after attack from the sea, and eventually its people abandoned it and moved inland to safer ground. The old coastal site sat empty, just farmland, until 1229, when the town of Alcúdia was founded there. The refugees' new settlement, meanwhile, kept the ancient name in altered form — and so today the ruins of Pollentia lie beside Alcúdia, while the living town of Pollença carries the name a few valleys inland.

## A Maze Built to Keep the World Out

The new Pollença grew from the thirteenth century, and when it was rebuilt in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, it kept its medieval plan: a grid of narrow alleys running beneath tall blocks of stone houses. Those cramped streets were never friendly to carts, cars, or industry, and that inhospitality turned out to be a kind of preservation. Traffic and factories went elsewhere, and the historic center stayed intact. When mass tourism reached this coast, it settled down by the water at Port de Pollença, leaving the old town to its quiet. The result is a place best explored on foot, where you round a corner and find the centuries still stacked overhead.

## 365 Steps to the Calvary

The town's defining walk is the climb up to El Calvari. The little oratory at the top was built in 1795 on a hill 123 meters high, and it is reached by the famous flight of 365 steps that rises straight from the edge of the main square. Fourteen Stations of the Cross and a series of viewpoints punctuate the ascent. At the bottom lies the Plaça Major, the pedestrianized heart of town, lined with cafes and anchored by the Church of Santa Maria at its western corner. It is here, on Good Friday, that the Davallament — the Descent from the Cross — is re-enacted: an effigy of Jesus is borne in silence down the Calvary steps to the main church by a torchlit procession of black-cloaked figures.

## Rooster Fountains and Hilltop Nuns

Beyond the steps, Pollença keeps unfolding. A short walk north of the Plaça Major lies the Plaça Almoina, the town's original center, where the Font del Gall — the fountain of the rooster — once watered horses. On a hill above town stands the Puig de Maria, a fourteenth-century nunnery abandoned in 1988; it is closed now, but walkers still climb to it for the hilltop views. Each August the town fills with music for the Festival de Pollença, held in the cloister of the Sant Domingo monastery, and the surrounding valleys have taken to growing wine, with vineyards scattered along the roads out of town. For a place that spent most of its history being left alone, Pollença has learned to be quietly rewarding.

## Bridges, Gardens, and the Road to the Sea

There is more to Pollença than its steps. On the north side of town, a small Roman bridge still carries a footpath over a seasonal stream on two weathered arches, a survivor from the deep past. In the center, the Jardins de Joan March offer a leafy pause; their lone tower house is all that remains of a marquis's mansion, and the park itself was once his orchard. Beyond the town, the roads lead outward to pleasures of a different kind — the rocky coves of Cala Sant Vicenç seven kilometers northeast, the sandy beach and marina at Port de Pollença, and past them the wild, cliff-hung Formentor peninsula reaching far into the sea.

## Flight Context

Pollença sits inland at 39.8772°N, 3.0164°E, tucked against the eastern end of the Serra de Tramuntana in northeastern Mallorca. From 3,000–5,000 ft the town's compact old center reads as a dense cluster of tile roofs between two hills — the Calvari to the north and the conical Puig de Maria to the southeast, the most distinctive local landmark. Just northeast, the dramatic Formentor peninsula juts into the sea beyond the Bay of Pollença. Palma's airport (LEPA) is about 54 km to the southwest; Menorca (LEMH) lies east across open water. Mornings bring the steadiest air; afternoon thermals build quickly over the nearby mountains.
