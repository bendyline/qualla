# Puig de Massanella

Mallorca's second-highest peak and its highest accessible summit, crowned by a limestone pit that once fed the island's mountain snow trade.

**Location:** 39.8060, 2.8530

**Categories:** Mountains, Hiking, Nature, Serra de Tramuntana, Geology, Mallorca

## Introduction

Mallorca's highest mountain is off-limits. A cluster of military radar domes crowns Puig Major, so the highest ground that ordinary walkers can actually stand on belongs to its neighbor, Puig de Massanella — the island's second peak at 1,364 meters, and by far the more rewarding to climb. From its summit the whole spine of the Serra de Tramuntana unrolls in both directions, and on a clear day the sea shows on two sides at once. But the strangest feature here is not the view. It is a deep pit near the top, and the ruins scattered around it, that speak of a vanished mountain trade.

## The Peak You're Allowed to Climb

In a range full of dramatic summits, Massanella holds a particular honor: it is the highest point on Mallorca that anyone can freely reach. Puig Major, just to the north, edges it out in sheer altitude at 1,445 meters, but the Spanish military has held its summit for decades, its antennas and domes sealing the true high point behind fences. Massanella, at 1,364 meters, carries no such restriction, and so it has become the mountain that hikers actually stand atop. That distinction, more than any other, explains why it is one of the most popular climbs on the island. On summer weekends its trails fill with walkers making the pilgrimage to that second-place summit — the closest a hiker can get to the roof of Mallorca.

## Routes to the Top

There is more than one way up, and they suit different appetites for risk. The gentlest is a walking route that approaches from the south, a steady ascent most reasonably fit hikers can manage. From a high col to the north, a scatter of scrambling routes climbs the rougher ground, marked by small cairns and dabs of red paint on the rocks for anyone unsure of the way. A slightly easier scramble begins a little lower, on the southwestern side of the same col. None of it requires ropes, but the upper mountain is limestone — sharp, fissured, and unforgiving of a careless step — so walkers trade the crowds of the coast for a landscape that demands full attention.

## The Snow Merchants of the Mountain

Near the summit lies a deep pit, gouged not by human hands but by water: over ages, rain has dissolved the limestone into the hollows and sinkholes that geologists call karst. Yet people found a use for it. This pit, and the ruined stone huts on the col nearby, belonged to Mallorca's old snow trade. Long before refrigeration, mountain crews would pack winter snow into such pits and stone-built 'snow houses,' insulate it against the thaw, and haul the compressed ice down to the towns through the heat of summer, where it chilled drinks, preserved food, and eased fevers. The summit of Massanella was, in its way, a factory — one that ran on cold and altitude.

## A Roof of Ridges and Sea

Standing on Massanella, you understand why the Serra de Tramuntana was named a World Heritage landscape. The range falls away in every direction, a chaos of grey limestone ridges dropping toward the Mediterranean, with the reservoirs of Gorg Blau and Cúber glinting in the folds below and the radar domes of forbidden Puig Major close enough to seem almost touchable. It is a summit that rewards patience and clear weather, and punishes those who ignore the mountains' moods, for cloud can swallow the ridgelines within minutes. Reach the top on a bright, still morning, though, and Mallorca lies spread beneath you like a map — coast, mountain, and sea in a single sweep.

## The Island's Water Tower

Massanella belongs to a range that does more than look majestic. The Serra de Tramuntana is Mallorca's water tower, its high limestone catching the rain and snow that passing storms deliver and funneling them into the valleys below. Just north of the peak, the twin reservoirs of Gorg Blau and Cúber gather that runoff and send drinking water down to Palma and the coastal towns. The same karst that hollowed the pit near the summit riddles the whole massif with caves and sinkholes, so that much of the mountains' water travels unseen, underground, before it surfaces again. To climb Massanella is to stand atop the source of a good deal of the island's life.

## Flight Context

Puig de Massanella rises to 1,364 m at 39.8060°N, 2.8530°E, in the heart of Mallorca's Serra de Tramuntana. The surest way to identify it from the air is by its taller neighbor: Puig Major, just to the north-northwest, is crowned by unmistakable white radar domes, and Massanella is the bare rocky summit immediately southeast of it, above the twin blue reservoirs of Gorg Blau and Cúber. This is the highest terrain on the island — keep well clear, at 7,000 ft or above, and expect sharp mountain turbulence, rotor, and fast-forming cloud. Palma's airport (LEPA) lies about 28 km to the south; Menorca (LEMH) is far to the east. Fly it on a clear, calm morning.
