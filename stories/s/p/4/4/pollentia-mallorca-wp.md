# Pollentia (Mallorca)

A wealthy Roman city on Mallorca whose theater and forum survive at Alcúdia, even as its name migrated ten kilometers inland to Pollença.

**Location:** 39.8493, 3.1224

**Categories:** Archaeological Sites, Roman History, Ancient Cities, Ruins, History, Mallorca

## Introduction

The strangest thing about the Roman city of Pollentia is that its name lives ten kilometers from its ruins. The stones themselves — a theater cut into bedrock, a forum, the foundations of townhouses — lie at the edge of modern Alcúdia. But the name traveled inland with the refugees who fled the coast when Rome fell, and today it belongs to the town of Pollença. Founded around 123 BC, Pollentia was for a time one of the wealthiest cities in the Balearic Islands, and its bones still surface from the fields at Alcúdia's doorstep.

## A Conqueror's City

Pollentia takes its origin from a soldier. It was founded, allegedly in 123 BC, by Quintus Caecilius Metellus Balearicus — the Roman general whose conquest of these islands earned him the very surname Balearicus. The earliest firmly dated remains, though, come from the first half of the first century BC, which has led researchers to suspect the site began as a castra, a military camp, planted by the expedition that would only a generation later blossom into a true city. Like the other towns Metellus founded, Pollentia is thought to have stayed a garrison until around 70 BC, when — judging from the excavated forum — it was reorganized and rebuilt on a grander, more monumental scale.

## Sixteen Hectares of Stone and Trade

At its height, Pollentia was a rich and prosperous place, and the portion of it that survives underground covers some sixteen hectares. The Roman writer Pliny the Elder counted Pollentia, alongside Palma, among the island's privileged towns whose people enjoyed the rights of Roman citizens. Generations of digging have opened three main areas of the ancient city: Sa Portella, a residential district where the outlines of Roman houses can be traced; the forum, the civic and religious heart of any Roman town; and the rows of tabernae, the shops and workshops that lined its commercial streets. Most evocative of all is the theater, carved partly into the living rock, where an audience once sat on stone benches under the Mallorcan sky.

## Fire and Slow Abandonment

Pollentia's decline was gradual rather than sudden. A devastating fire swept the city in the third century AD, but the flames did not end it: people kept living here, and in the fifth century AD they raised a fortification in the old forum, bracing themselves against more dangerous times. Only over the following centuries did the population drift away for good. When it did, the island's medieval Christians resettled a little to the north, at what became Alcúdia, right next to the empty ruins. The ancient name, oddly, skipped past its own rubble and attached itself instead to Pollença, the inland town whose people traced their roots to the same abandoned coast.

## Digging Up the Dead City

Curiosity about Pollentia is old. Excavations reach back to the sixteenth century, though the serious, sustained work began only in the early twentieth. The finds — including many inscriptions carved in stone — are gathered just across from the site in Alcúdia's small but absorbing Museo Monográfico de Pollentia, and a dedicated guidebook to both the ruins and the museum appeared in 2021. The city also lived on in verse: the Mallorcan poet Miquel Costa i Llobera repeatedly exalted these ruins, most famously in his celebrated Ode to Horace and in a poem written directly over the remains of the Roman theater. For him, the broken stones were not an ending but a doorway back into the classical world.

## Voices in the Stone

Much of what gives Pollentia a human face comes from its inscriptions. Over the centuries of digging, excavators have turned up many carved stones — dedications, epitaphs, records of public office — and the finest of them are now displayed just across from the site. Such inscriptions are how a vanished city speaks: they preserve the names of real Pollentines, the gods they honored, and the pride they took in their standing as Romans on a far Mediterranean island. Set beside the foundations of houses and the worn curve of the theater, they turn a bare field of ruins back into something like a community.

## Flight Context

The ruins of Pollentia lie at 39.8493°N, 3.1224°E, immediately south of Alcúdia's walled old town on Mallorca's northeastern headland. From 2,500–4,000 ft the excavation site shows as a patch of exposed foundations and the curved bowl of the Roman theater just outside the medieval walls, set between the Bay of Alcúdia and the Bay of Pollença. Use the compact walled town as your visual anchor. Palma's airport (LEPA) is about 55 km to the southwest and Menorca (LEMH) roughly 40 km east across the water. Low sun in early morning or late afternoon throws the ruins' outlines into sharpest relief.
