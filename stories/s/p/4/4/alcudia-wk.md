# Alcúdia

A walled medieval town at Mallorca's northeastern tip, built on a failed Roman city and now flanked by a fourteen-kilometer beach.

**Location:** 39.8525, 3.1192

**Categories:** Towns & Cities, Coastal, History, Beaches, Balearic Islands, Mallorca

## Introduction

On the evening of 12 August 2026, if the sky over the northern tip of Mallorca stays clear, the sun will go out. For about ninety seconds a total solar eclipse will darken the horizon just as the sun sinks toward the sea, and the hilltops above Alcúdia rank among the finest places on the island to watch it. It is a fittingly dramatic moment for a town that has spent most of its long history being quietly overlooked. Alcúdia sits at Mallorca's northeastern corner, a walled survivor where Rome, medieval Islam, and the crown of Aragon each left their mark long before the first beach hotel went up.

## A Town Built on an Abandoned City

Alcúdia's story begins with a city that failed. When the Roman Empire crumbled, the nearby coastal town of Pollentia came under attack again and again, until its people gave up and moved ten kilometers inland to a safer site — the town now called Pollença. For centuries the only habitation near the ruins was a Moorish farmstead on the hill above, known in Arabic as al-kudja, 'the hill,' the name Alcúdia still carries. In 1229 James I of Aragon seized the territory during his conquest of the island, and from 1298 King Jaume II of Majorca began raising a walled town on the site. The Romans had chosen this corner of the coast for good reason, and the medieval kings simply chose it again.

## Walls That Outlasted Everything

Those walls still stand, and they remain the making of the town. Built between 1298 and 1362, they encircle the compact old quarter and are best preserved along the north and west, where two of the original four city gates survive. The best known of them, the Porta des Moll, still frames the main entrance into the old town with its pair of towers. A free walkway called the Camí de Ronda runs atop the ramparts, open around the clock. A second, outer ring of walls was added in the sixteenth century and later demolished. When the tourism boom finally arrived, the hotels rose along the beach and left the walled town alone — which is why, inside the gates, Alcúdia still feels like the medieval market town it always was.

## Seven Quiet Centuries, Then the Boom

For roughly seven hundred years after its walls went up, remarkably little happened here. Alcúdia stayed a small port, untroubled by industry, while grander dramas played out elsewhere on Mallorca. A trickle of tourists began arriving in the 1920s, but the real transformation came in the 1970s, when hotels spread the length of the fourteen-kilometer sandy beach that curves toward Can Picafort and the old port gained a leisure marina. Even so, Alcúdia never chased the frenetic nightlife of resorts like Magaluf, settling instead into an easygoing rhythm that suits young families and pensioners. Out on a tiny islet 150 meters offshore, the Alcanada lighthouse — first lit in 1861 and automated in 1960 — keeps watch, close enough to reach by a strong swim.

## Saints, Blood, and Barefoot Silence

Faith runs deep here, and it shows in the calendar. Each July the town gives over nine days to Sant Jaume — Saint James the Great, patron of Spain, known across the country as Santiago — with concerts, ceremonies, and fireworks. Rarer and more solemn is the Triennial of the Holy Christ, held only once every three years. According to a tradition reaching back to 1507, an image of Christ in the nearby cave of Sant Martí wept water and blood during a drought, and the townspeople have honored it ever since by walking barefoot and in silence through the streets. The next procession falls in 2028. It is a striking thing to witness: an entire town moving together without a single word.

## Beach, Wetland, and Open Water

Modern Alcúdia lives largely by its coast. The town's great sandy beach runs some fourteen kilometers, curving southwest toward Can Picafort past the resort strip, while just inland the wetlands of the s'Albufera natural park draw birdwatchers to their reed beds and lagoons. Out on the water, boats leave the marina for dolphin-watching trips and sunset cruises, and each May endurance athletes churn through the bay during the island's Ironman triathlon. From the port, a daily ferry crosses to Ciutadella on Menorca in about 75 minutes, close enough that the neighboring island can be a day trip. It is a lot of life for a town that spent seven centuries doing very little.

## Flight Context

Alcúdia occupies Mallorca's northeastern headland at 39.8525°N, 3.1192°E, wedged between two great bays — the Bay of Alcúdia to the south and the Bay of Pollença to the north. From 3,000–5,000 ft the walled old town, the long arc of sandy beach running southwest toward Can Picafort, and the Alcanada lighthouse on its offshore islet all read clearly, with the s'Albufera wetlands glinting inland. Palma's airport (LEPA) lies about 55 km to the southwest; Menorca (LEMH) is roughly 40 km east across open water, and a daily ferry links Alcúdia's port to Ciutadella. Coastal haze is common on summer afternoons; mornings give the sharpest view of the twin bays.
