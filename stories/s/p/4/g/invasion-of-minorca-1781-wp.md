# Invasion of Minorca (1781)

A Franco-Spanish army laid siege to Fort St. Philip for five months, but it was scurvy, not cannon fire, that finally forced the British garrison to surrender Menorca.

**Location:** 39.9708, 4.0794

**Categories:** Military history, Siege warfare, History of Menorca, American Revolutionary War, Balearic Islands, Anglo-Spanish history

## Introduction

The army that came for Fort St. Philip numbered some fourteen thousand men, backed by hundreds of cannon and mortars. In the end, though, it was not the guns that broke the British garrison. It was scurvy. Through the winter of 1781 and into 1782, as a Franco-Spanish force besieged the great fortress guarding Port Mahon on Menorca, the defenders held their walls even as their bodies gave way, felled not by shot but by the simple absence of fresh vegetables. When the island changed hands that February, it did so with a strange and terrible dignity.

## The Harbour Everyone Wanted

At Menorca's eastern end lies Port Mahon, one of the finest deep-water anchorages in the Mediterranean, and its narrow mouth was sealed by Fort St. Philip, known in Spanish as the castillo de San Felipe. For a naval power without a Mediterranean coast, the fort was a jewel worth almost any price, and the British had held the island for most of the eighteenth century. They had good reason to fear losing it. In 1756 Admiral John Byng chose to preserve his fleet rather than relieve the fort, and was later shot on his own quarterdeck to remind other admirals of their duty. Spain, allied to France by the 1779 Treaty of Aranjuez, made Menorca a chief war aim, second only to Gibraltar, not least because the island sheltered British-licensed privateers preying on their ships.

## A Fleet in Disguise

The invasion was entrusted to the Duc de Crillon, a French nobleman in Spanish service. His fleet sailed from Cadiz in July 1781, first steering west to suggest an American destination before slipping past Gibraltar under cover of the feint; Admiral Guichen's French warships joined it at sea. Menorca was sighted on 19 August. The plan depended on the British mistaking a vast convoy for a friendly one, and to a degree it worked. Spanish troops came ashore, and much of the local population, weary of foreign rule, greeted them with cheers. The British had little choice but to abandon the towns and pull back into Fort St. Philip, fixing a chain across the harbour mouth and scuttling small vessels in the channel to seal it against attack by sea.

## Into the Fort

London's newspapers put the garrison at over five thousand, but the true number of fighting men within the walls was closer to three thousand; the rest were militia and civilian workers. Among the island's residents were long-settled North African and Jewish merchant communities, welcomed earlier by the British, and in September the invaders expelled them, uprooting families who had made Menorca their home. The siege itself opened with batteries rising at La Mola and near Georgetown, today's Es Castell. The British did not sit idle: in a sharp October raid they crossed the harbour and carried off eighty prisoners. De Crillon even offered Governor James Murray a fortune and a foreign commission to surrender. Murray answered that his own family tree was as noble as the Duc's, and would not be bought.

## The Great Bombardment

On 11 November the besiegers' mortars finally opened up, though the fort's own gunners gave as good as they got, destroying one battery and sinking supply vessels in the harbour. The decisive push came on 6 January 1782, when a hundred cannon and thirty-five mortars pounded the outer defences so hard that Murray withdrew every man into the inner citadel. Whenever the enemy fire slackened, the defenders roared back with more than two hundred cannon of their own. Then the attackers found the fort's weak point by accident: a well-aimed incendiary grenade set a storehouse ablaze, and much of the garrison's salted meat burned for four days. Inside the citadel, meanwhile, the strain was showing in other ways, as the quarrel between Murray and his deputy Draper broke down completely and Draper was suspended from duty.

## Defeated by Scurvy

The lost meat mattered less than what the fort had never stocked: fresh vegetables, the only sure defence against scurvy. As the weeks passed the disease spread relentlessly, and by early February the hospital was taking in more than fifty men a day. On 3 February, of the roughly 660 men still able to stand, some 560 showed symptoms, and a few died at their posts rather than report themselves unfit. There were no longer enough healthy soldiers to man the walls. On 4 February Murray sought terms, and on the 6th the garrison marched out with drums beating, matches lit, and colours flying, an honour granted for their endurance. Watching the emaciated survivors file past, many of the French and Spanish soldiers are said to have wept. Menorca returned to Spain the following year; a brief British recapture in 1798 ended for good in 1802.

## Flight Context

The siege centred on Fort St. Philip at the mouth of Port Mahon, around 39.97°N, 4.08°E, on Menorca's eastern tip. From 3,000 to 5,000 feet, follow the long, deep harbour cutting inland from the east coast; the fort's ruins sit on the south side of its narrow entrance, with the La Mola peninsula opposite.
