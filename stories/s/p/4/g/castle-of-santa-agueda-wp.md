# Castle of Santa Àgueda

Reached by a Roman road and named for a Christian saint, this ruined Moorish castle was where Muslim Menorca made its last stand.

**Location:** 40.0275, 4.0081

**Categories:** Castles, History of Menorca, Al-Andalus, Medieval history, Balearic Islands, Archaeology

## Introduction

You reach it by a Roman road. You arrive at a fortress the Arabs built. And you find it named for a Christian saint. The Castle of Santa Àgueda, crowning a long plateau above Ferreries in western Menorca, is a single hill that changed hands and languages so many times that its name still carries the imprint of each conqueror. In Arabic it was Sent Agaiz. In Catalan and English it honours Saint Agatha. Its walls rise from the footings of a Roman camp. Few ruins hold so much history in so little standing stone.

## The Road and the Rock

The plateau of Santa Àgueda stands 264 metres above the sea, the third-highest point on Menorca after Monte Toro at 358 metres and S'Enclusa at 275. To climb it is to walk a genuine Roman road, worn smooth and still legible after two thousand years, switchbacking up to the summit. Until recently a small chapel dedicated to Saint Agatha stood beside the ruins, giving the height its Christian name. From up here the whole western half of the island falls away in every direction, which is precisely why soldiers of successive empires kept choosing this rock. Whoever held the plateau could see trouble coming long before it arrived.

## A Fortress of al-Andalus

The castle as it stands was raised by the Arabs over an older Roman castra, during the centuries when Menorca, then called Manûrqa, belonged to the Caliphate of Cordoba. No document records the exact year it went up. We know only that it already existed before 1232, a stronghold of Muslim Menorca watching the island's interior. Cyclopean effort had gone into these heights long before Islam arrived, and the Arab builders simply added their own defensive logic to a summit that had been fortified again and again. The result was one of the island's most formidable positions, and its defenders would need every advantage the rock gave them.

## The Last Stand, 1287

In 1287 King Alfonso III of Aragon invaded Menorca, and Santa Àgueda became the final refuge of the island's Muslim inhabitants. This was where the defenders of a vanishing world gathered to hold out, on the plateau their ancestors had fortified, when the rest of the island had already fallen. Their resistance did not save them; the conquest went through, and Menorca passed into Christian Aragon. The castle itself outlived them by only a few decades. Around 1343 Alfonso's grand-nephew, King Peter IV of Aragon, ordered it destroyed, unwilling to leave so strong a fortress standing for anyone else to use. From then on Santa Àgueda was left to crumble.

## Ruins and Return

For centuries the castle survived only as tumbled walls and the shell of its cistern, a favourite of hikers who climbed the Roman road for the view. In 2004 the island government drew up a special protection plan to stabilise and restore what remained. Then, on 17 January 2007, the Consell Insular de Menorca announced it had bought the ruins from their private owners. The date was no accident. It fell on Menorca's National Day, which marks the very conquest of 1287 that ended Muslim rule here. Seven centuries after Alfonso III took the island, the site of its last resistance came back into public hands on the anniversary of its fall, a quiet circle finally closed.

## What the Climber Finds Today

There is no gleaming reconstruction waiting at the top, and that is much of the appeal. What draws people up Santa Agueda is the journey itself, the paved Roman road climbing through scrub to the crown of the plateau. Along the way the old paving stones surface underfoot, worn by centuries of feet and hooves, one of the best-preserved ancient roads on the island. At the summit the visitor finds fragments of wall, the deep cistern that once caught the rain to slake a besieged garrison, and above all the view: the whole western half of Menorca laid out, coast to coast, exactly the panorama that made the height worth fighting over. To stand here is to occupy the same commanding ground that Romans, Arabs, and Aragonese all judged worth holding, and to see the island as its defenders once did.

## Flight Context

The castle sits at 40.028°N, 4.008°E, above Ferreries in western Menorca. Best seen from 2,500 to 4,000 feet AGL. Look for a distinct elongated plateau standing higher than the surrounding farmland, roughly midway between the island's north and south coasts. It is lower and more inland than Monte Toro, Menorca's obvious central summit, which makes a useful reference to the east. Menorca Airport (LEMH) lies about 25 nm east near Mahon; Palma de Mallorca (LEPA) is roughly 95 nm west. Clear morning light throws the plateau's edges into sharp relief.
