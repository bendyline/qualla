# Eastern Biniac naveta

A Bronze Age tomb near the heart of Menorca that spent centuries as a cattle shed, its ancient dead long since scoured away.

**Location:** 39.9151, 4.1785

**Categories:** Archaeology, Prehistory, Bronze Age, History of Menorca, Balearic Islands, Megalithic monuments

## Introduction

For much of its recent life, a Bronze Age tomb near the middle of Menorca did duty as a barn. Cattle sheltered inside the Eastern Biniac naveta, and in doing so they scraped away almost everything the archaeologists would later hope to find: the bones, the grave goods, the packed sediment of three thousand years. What remains is the building itself, a squat mound of massive stones beside a farm track. It is one of the strange, boat-shaped monuments that make this island unlike anywhere else, a house for the dead that outlasted the memory of who lay within it.

## Off the Me-1

You could drive past it a hundred times. The naveta sits just off the Me-1, the main road running the length of Menorca between Mahon and Ciutadella, near kilometre 8.1, where a turning to the left leads toward a district known as Argentina. It stands right at the entrance to a property called Lloc de Menorca, close enough to the road that it hides in plain sight. There is nothing grand about the approach, no gate or grand avenue, only a low pile of grey stone rising from the fields. Yet its ordinariness is part of the point: on Menorca, the prehistoric past is not cordoned off in a park but woven into the working countryside.

## How It Was Built

The Eastern Biniac naveta has a rounded plan, its outer wall leaning inward as it rises. The builders used the cyclopean technique found across prehistoric Menorca, stacking huge blocks laid horizontally without mortar, their weight alone holding the structure together. The entrance faces south, and at some later date another wall was added against that side, partly masking it. Inside, a long corridor leads to an oval chamber whose rough walls curve inward, the slabs projecting to form a concave hollow, roofed with horizontal stone. The dimensions are modest: about 11 metres long and 10.7 metres wide on the outside, but only 3.9 by 2.3 metres within. Most of the mass is wall, sheltering a surprisingly small inner room.

## An Intermediate Form

Archaeologists place Eastern Biniac among what they call the intermediate navetas, and the label matters. These rounded structures appear to sit halfway between the older megalithic tombs of the island and the later navetas built on a long, elongated plan. In other words, this squat little building may capture a moment of transition, a step in the slow evolution of how Menorcans housed their dead across the Bronze Age. Its whole chronology falls roughly between 1500 and 1000 BC. To read the shape of the walls, then, is to read a chapter in the changing architecture of memory on the island, one design giving way gradually to the next.

## The Missing Dead

For years the chamber served as a cattle shelter, and the animals and their keepers cleared out the sediment that would have held the archaeological record. When the archaeologist Maria Luisa Serra Belabre carried out a brief excavation of its upper part in the 1960s, the interior yielded nothing. The evidence had been swept away. Yet we are not entirely in the dark. Excavations of better-preserved navetas elsewhere on Menorca tell us what this one almost certainly held: the bodies of a family or small community, laid to rest together and accompanied by grave goods chosen for the journey they believed lay ahead. The people who filled this chamber are anonymous now, but they were mourned, and they were meant to be remembered.

## One Tomb Among Many

Eastern Biniac does not stand alone. It is a single note in a landscape crowded with prehistoric building, the same island that in 2023 saw its ancient monuments inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as Talayotic Menorca. Scattered across the countryside are dozens of navetas, along with the taula sanctuaries and towering talayots of the same deep tradition, an unbroken record of how one island's people lived, worshipped, and buried their dead across thousands of years. That is why a plundered, emptied tomb still matters. Read on its own, Eastern Biniac is a puzzle with its answer erased. Read alongside its better-preserved siblings, it becomes another data point in one of the richest prehistoric landscapes anywhere, a humble stone box that helps complete a much larger picture.

## Flight Context

The naveta lies at 39.915°N, 4.179°E, in the interior of eastern Menorca near the Me-1 road. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL the monument itself is too small to pick out, so navigate by the arrow-straight Me-1 highway crossing the island's waist and the patchwork of stone-walled fields around it. Menorca Airport (LEMH) is only about 6 nm east-southeast near Mahon, so this sits close under the airport's traffic pattern. Palma de Mallorca (LEPA) lies roughly 100 nm west. Low sun rakes the field walls into sharp lines on clear mornings.
