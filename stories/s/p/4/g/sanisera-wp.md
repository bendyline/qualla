# Sanisera

A Roman city on Menorca's wild north coast, born as a military camp among the famed Balearic slingers and layered over by Christians, Muslims, and the sea.

**Location:** 40.0708, 4.0881

**Categories:** Archaeology, Roman sites, History of Menorca, Ancient history, Balearic Islands, Geography of Menorca

## Introduction

On this island, the story went, children were not given their bread until they could knock it down with a sling. So wrote the geographer Strabo of the Balearic slingers, whose lethal accuracy made them prized mercenaries across the ancient Mediterranean. When Rome came to conquer the islands in 123 BC, its general is said to have ordered animal hides stretched over his ships' decks to fend off the incoming stones. The Romans landed on Menorca's rugged north coast, at a small sheltered harbour called Sanitja, and there built a camp that would grow, over six centuries, into the city of Sanisera.

## The Slingers and the Conquest

The islanders Rome faced were no easy prey. The Balearic slinger, trained from childhood, was a fixture of Mediterranean warfare, having fought as a mercenary for Carthage against Rome itself. In 123 BC the general Quintus Caecilius Metellus led the conquest of the Balearics, in part to clear the pirates who infested these waters and choked the flow of trade. Within two years the islands were subdued, and Metellus returned to Rome to celebrate a triumph and take the honorific Balearicus, the conqueror of the Balearics. The natural port of Sanitja, tucked beside the Cavalleria headland, gave him exactly what a conqueror needed: a defensible anchorage from which to watch the whole northern coast. Centuries later, the geographer Pliny would list Sanisera among Menorca's Roman towns.

## A Camp Becomes a City

The first Roman settlement here was purely military, a camp set on the high ground at the south end of the port, ringed with defensive towers that controlled the entrance to the harbour. Soldiers drilled, mended their gear, and cast lead sling bullets, while native Balearic slingers trained alongside them as auxiliaries. A garrison, though, draws a crowd. Around the camp gathered the usual followers of any army: traders, craftsmen, tavern-keepers, and veterans, first in a rough huddle of houses and then in something more permanent. By the time the soldiers left, around the middle of the first century BC, a genuine town had taken root. This was the civitas of Sanisera that Pliny named, a busy port city that reached its height between the first and third centuries AD before the wider crisis of the Roman world began to shrink it.

## The Christian Port

Even as Rome faltered, Sanitja kept trading. After the fall of the Western Empire, Menorca passed to the Vandals in the fifth century and the Byzantines in the sixth, and the pottery pulled from the soil shows commerce with North Africa continuing right through. What changed most was faith. Archaeologists have uncovered an early Christian basilica, its three naves ending in a rounded apse, dating from the late fourth or early fifth century, and a whole ecclesiastical complex from the sixth, complete with a second basilica and a large multipurpose building that may have served as a monastery or a guesthouse for pilgrims. For a small port on the edge of a small island, this was a strikingly organised Christian community, clinging on as the classical world dissolved around it.

## The Necropolis

Between 2008 and 2011, excavators opened a cemetery beside the port, and it put faces to the abstraction of history. Necropolis 6 held some 44 tombs and the remains of at least 250 people, laid out in neat rows with their heads to the west in the Christian manner. The bones tell a hard story: a life expectancy of only 31 to 40 years, and mortality that climbed steeply in early childhood, patterns typical of a rural population living with disease and little medicine. One grave, tomb 42, was an amphora, and inside lay a child barely a year old. Small personal things came out of the earth too, earrings, rings, and glass-paste beads, the ordinary keepsakes of people who lived, worked, and grieved on this exposed northern shore.

## Layers on the Cape

History did not stop when Sanisera faded. During the Islamic centuries, from the conquest of 903 to the Christian reconquest of 1287, a small mosque rose here, built from reused Roman stone, the earliest yet found in the Balearic Islands. Later still came a modest tuna-fishing house of 1787 and, in 1800, a circular British watchtower, one of eleven raised to guard the coast. In 1857 a lighthouse was finally built on Cavalleria point, its beam reaching far out over waters that had swallowed more than seven hundred ships across the centuries. Divers still recover Roman amphorae and ancient anchors from the seabed. Few places pack so many worlds, soldier and pilgrim, Muslim and fisherman, into one small windswept harbour.

## Flight Context

Sanisera lies at the port of Sanitja, roughly 40.071°N, 4.088°E, on Menorca's northern coast beside the Cavalleria headland. Best viewed from 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL along the north shore. Look for the narrow inlet cutting inland just south of Cap de Cavalleria and its pale 19th-century lighthouse, with small offshore islets guarding the harbour mouth. Menorca Airport (LEMH) is about 20 nm southeast near Mahon; Palma de Mallorca (LEPA) lies roughly 100 nm west. This exposed coast is often swept by the tramontana wind, bringing turbulence and haze on rough days and crisp, far-reaching visibility when it eases.
