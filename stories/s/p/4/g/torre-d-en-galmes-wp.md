# Torre d'en Galmés

One of the largest prehistoric towns in the Balearics, this Menorcan hilltop yielded a bronze Egyptian god and a water system of startling ingenuity.

**Location:** 39.9025, 4.1149

**Categories:** Archaeology, Prehistory, Talayotic culture, History of Menorca, Balearic Islands, Ancient settlements

## Introduction

Among the objects unearthed on a hilltop in southern Menorca is a small bronze figure that seems to have no business being there: Imhotep, the ancient Egyptian architect who was worshipped as a god, his cult carried across the length of the Mediterranean. That a statuette of an Egyptian deity turned up on a Balearic hill, far from the Nile, tells you almost everything about Torre d'en Galmes. This was no isolated hamlet. It was one of the largest settlements in the Balearic Islands, a town perched to watch the southern coast that endured, in one form or another, for well over two thousand years.

## The Bronze from Egypt

The Imhotep figurine is a small thing with a long reach. Imhotep had been a real architect in Egypt's Old Kingdom, later deified and venerated far beyond his homeland, and the cult that produced this little bronze had somehow touched a hilltop between the towns of Alaior and Son Bou. It is a reminder that the prehistoric and ancient Mediterranean was a connected world, its islands not cut off but wired into currents of trade and belief that ran from the Levant to the Atlantic. The people who lived here traded, borrowed, and absorbed, and one of them ended up cherishing a god from the Nile valley on an island that most Egyptians would never have heard of.

## A Town on a Hill

Torre d'en Galmes commands a low hill, steeper on its southern face, with clear views over a broad sweep of the south coast and, on a fine day, the distant mountains of Majorca. Settlement began around 1400 BC, at the dawn of the Talayotic era, and the town grew and grew until it was among the biggest in the Balearics, its height letting it look down on its neighbours. Three talayots, the great stone towers that give the culture its name, still rise here, their tops offering the finest views around and marking them as watch-points. Between two of them lies an open space that may have been a public square. The town outlived Rome before it was abandoned, then centuries later sheltered Muslim refugees fleeing the Christian reconquest.

## Houses and the Taula

The homes here followed the classic Menorcan pattern: circular, ringed by huge outer stones, divided by radial walls into rooms around an open central patio where the hearth and a cistern sat. One excavated house even folds a natural cave into its plan. Another preserves a Sala Hipostila, a room of columns roofed with heavy slabs, its pillars widening toward the top in the local style, once used to store goods and shelter livestock. At the town's heart stands a taula, the T-shaped stone monument unique to Menorca and almost certainly religious, set within a horseshoe enclosure. Its great capstone, intact through Roman times, has since toppled and now lies below the pillar that once held it aloft.

## Catching the Rain

For all its size, the town sat on a dry hill, and its survival came down to water. The people of Torre d'en Galmes built a genuinely sophisticated system to capture and hold it: a network of underground cisterns linked by channels, fed by rain running down the slope. They even repurposed an older cave, once a chamber tomb from before the Talayotic age, to store water. Most striking of all, they engineered a filter of shallow, pebble-filled basins to strain the soil out of the runoff before it reached the cisterns, delivering cleaner water to the town below. It is the kind of quiet, practical genius that rarely makes the history books but that let a hilltop settlement thrive for millennia.

## A Town That Would Not Die

Few settlements have such a long afterlife. Torre d'en Galmes was founded around 1400 BC and simply kept adapting. When Rome absorbed Menorca, the townspeople reworked their round houses in the new fashion, squaring off rooms and building with smaller, neater masonry. The place was finally abandoned after the Roman period, only to be reoccupied centuries later by Muslim refugees fleeing the Christian reconquest of Spain, who once again altered the ancient stones to suit their needs. Layer upon layer of habitation is written into the ruins, a single hilltop reinhabited by wave after wave of people across more than two thousand years. Today the site is preserved as part of the island's protected prehistoric heritage, one of the great stops on any tour of Talayotic Menorca, its talayots and taula still commanding the same wide view of the southern sea.

## Flight Context

Torre d'en Galmes occupies a hill at 39.902°N, 4.115°E, in southern Menorca between Alaior and the coast at Son Bou. From 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL, look for an isolated rise with clear ground above the surrounding farmland, set back a short distance from the south coast beach and wetlands of Son Bou. Menorca Airport (LEMH) lies about 8 nm east near Mahon, so this sits close to the airport's traffic pattern. Palma de Mallorca (LEPA) is roughly 100 nm west. On clear days the hilltop and the pale scatter of its ruined stonework stand out against the green interior.
