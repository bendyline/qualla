# Capture of Minorca (1798)

In eight days of November 1798, a British force seized Menorca from Spain with barely a shot fired, all to win one magnificent harbour.

**Location:** 39.9500, 4.0500

**Categories:** Military history, Naval history, History of Menorca, Balearic Islands, French Revolutionary Wars, Anglo-Spanish history

## Introduction

The prize was never really the island. It was the water. Port Mahon, on Menorca's eastern shore, is one of the finest deep-water harbours in the Mediterranean, a long sheltered inlet that the Spanish had fitted with a modern dockyard, extensive storehouses, and a purpose-built naval hospital. In the autumn of 1798, with Spain now fighting alongside Revolutionary France and the British fleet starved for a secure base, Admiral the Earl of St Vincent decided he wanted it. What followed was less a battle than a swift seizure, an entire island changing hands in eight days with only a little blood spilled.

## Why a Harbour Was Worth a War

Britain had held Menorca before. The island fell to British forces in 1708 and was formally ceded under the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713, then handed back to Spain in 1783. When the French Revolutionary War began, London and Madrid were allies, but in 1796 Spain switched sides and declared war on Britain. Suddenly the Royal Navy needed to reassert itself in a Mediterranean where it had almost nowhere to anchor. An attempt to hold Corsica had failed. Then Nelson annihilated the French Mediterranean fleet at Aboukir Bay, and St Vincent resolved to restore British dominance for good. For that he needed a harbour that could not be stormed from the land. Port Mahon, known to the British as Minorca's great anchorage, was the obvious choice.

## Eight Days in November

The expedition sailed on 19 October 1798 and arrived off Menorca on 7 November. St Vincent detached a squadron of ships of the line and frigates under Rear Admiral John Thomas Duckworth, carrying a small army led by Colonel Charles Stuart. The troops came ashore at Addaya, overran a Spanish artillery position, and beat back a counterattack. Over the following days they pushed inland. A force of 300 men under Colonel Paget captured Fort Charles, opening the harbour so the fleet could sail in and anchor, while the main army accepted the surrender of one town after another, among them Fornells, which commanded the island's best northern anchorage, and then Mercadal. Town by town, with little fighting, the island was slipping from Spanish hands.

## The Surrender at Ciutadella

On 11 November a Spanish squadron of four frigates tried to disrupt the operation, but Duckworth's ships drove them off in a brisk counterattack. Stuart marched west and by the 14th was pressing Ciutadella, the old capital on the far shore. The Spanish governor, Juan Nepomuceno de Quesada, offered only token resistance before surrendering the town on 16 November, and with it the whole island. Around 4,000 Spanish soldiers passed into British hands, along with quantities of supplies and weapons. The four frigates were captured too: Flora and Proserpina, each of 40 guns, and Casilda and Pomona, of 38. For so complete a conquest, the cost in lives had been strikingly small.

## A Base, Then a Bargaining Chip

Menorca became one of Britain's principal Mediterranean bases almost overnight. Expeditions launched from its harbour, and the celebrated captain Thomas Cochrane used it as a springboard for raids along the Spanish coast. Charles Stuart governed the island until 1800, when Henry Edward Fox took over. Then diplomacy undid the whole enterprise. The Treaty of Amiens in 1802 required Menorca's return to Spain as a gesture toward a lasting European peace. Many British officers were furious, and Horatio Nelson himself rose in the House of Lords to argue against giving it up. He lost. Richard Bickerton oversaw the evacuation. When the peace collapsed soon after, no one moved to retake the island: Britain had by then secured Malta, and one hard-won harbour had made another unnecessary.

## An Island That Kept Changing Hands

For all its swiftness, the 1798 conquest was only the latest turn of a very well-worn wheel. Menorca had been passed back and forth for most of the century. Britain first took it in 1708 and held it under the Treaty of Utrecht, then lost it, regained it, and lost it again through a succession of sieges. The island's very strength was also its curse: a harbour that valuable was always worth attacking, and its landward defences had repeatedly proved they could be overwhelmed, as two hard sieges, in 1756 and 1781, had shown. Against that history, Stuart's eight-day, near-bloodless walkover stands out as an anomaly, a conquest that for once cost almost nothing. The island had simply changed masters so many times that another handover, in either direction, had come to feel like the natural order of things.

## Flight Context

The action centred on Port Mahon and Ciutadella, at roughly 39.95°N, 4.05°E. From 3,000 to 5,000 feet the whole of Menorca reads as a single low, elongated landmass. Trace the deep, finger-shaped harbour cutting into the eastern end near Mahon, the exact prize that drew the British fleet. Menorca Airport (LEMH) sits just southwest of that harbour; Palma de Mallorca (LEPA) lies about 100 nm to the west across open sea. Mediterranean haze can soften the coastline, but on clear days the harbour's shape is unmistakable from altitude.
