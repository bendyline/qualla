# Menorca

The quietest of the Balearics, where the British left gin and a harbour town behind, black horses lead the fiestas, and stone monuments older than any of its conquerors still keep their secrets.

**Location:** 39.9667, 4.0833

**Categories:** Islands, Balearic Islands, Menorca, Prehistory, Mediterranean, Travel

## Introduction

On Menorca there were a great many junipers, and in the harbour lay the British fleet. Out of that meeting came a spirit far removed from the Spanish and Mediterranean traditions, and noticeably different from English gin too. It is one of countless marks left on an island that Romans, Moors, Catalans, Spaniards, the French and the English have all controlled at one time or another. Menorca is the second largest of the Balearic Islands, only around 48 km long and 16 km wide, with a population of a little over 100,000. It is also the least overrun and most tranquil of the group, its nightlife low-key beside Mallorca and Ibiza, and the whole island is a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve.

## Tables of Stone

Head into the interior and the island grows older and stranger. Menorca is extraordinarily rich in prehistoric settlements, and to this day no one is certain what their monuments meant. Taules are T-shaped stone formations thought to have been spiritual sanctuaries; the name comes from the word for table in Menorquí, the island's own dialect of Catalan. Talayots are stone towers that local people believe served as look-out points, and the word itself is believed to come from the Arabic atalaya, meaning "watch tower." There is little evidence to support either theory. Trepucó and Torre d'en Galmés are the most significant sites, and in 2023 Talayotic Menorca, nine areas of settlements, tombs and sanctuaries from the Bronze Age to the Roman occupation, joined the UNESCO World Heritage List; the orientation of certain monuments is taken to show how this island culture related to the sky. Outside Ciutadella stands the Naveta des Tudons, a megalithic chamber tomb that became the final resting place of at least 100 people, buried there between about 1200 and 750 BC.

## The Harbour Everyone Wanted

Every ruler left something, from Sanisera, the archaeological site near the port of Sanitja on the north coast, to the ruins of a 5th-century basilica on the beach at Son Bou. In the 18th century Menorca became a bone of contention among Britain, France and Spain, and the reason was Maó. Its harbour, by the guide's reckoning the finest natural harbour in the Mediterranean, could shelter the largest fleets of the age. Anglo-Dutch forces captured the island in 1708, and Spain formally ceded it to Britain in the 1713 Peace of Utrecht. One governor, Richard Kane of County Antrim in Ireland, is still fondly remembered for supporting the island's farming: he imported new breeds of cattle and built a road across the island between Ciutadella and Maó, and the guide credits him with reservoirs too. The British military engineer Colonel Patrick Mackellar designed and supervised the building of the town now called Es Castell, beside the Sant Felip fortress at the harbour mouth. Two more British periods followed, beginning in 1763 and 1798. Their legacy includes the growth of Maó, which seized the new opportunities for trade, a tolerance for non-Catholic faiths that set the British at odds with the Church, and, the guide adds, traditions of woodworking and boatbuilding.

## Gin From the Grape

They also left Menorca's most popular drink. London gin is made from a cereal spirit, but Menorcan gin starts with a spirit distilled from grapes, as is usual around the Mediterranean. The juniper now comes from the mainland, yet the distilling is still done in old copper stills, and the spirit is stored, not aged, in large oak barrels. It was drunk throughout the British fleet, and it reportedly impressed distinguished visitors: the guide says the historian Vargas Ponce, visiting Menorca in 1781, called it "the best of the spirits found in Europe today." In Maó, the Xoriguer distillery still turns out a distinctively aromatic gin. Islanders drink it neat or mixed, and a gin-and-lemon mix called pomada is the drink of choice at the many fiestas that fill the summer.

## Black Horses on the Horse Path

Those fiestas belong to horses. Menorca has its own breed of black horses, and at every festivity the horses and their riders, the caixers, are the centrepiece. Even the island's great trail is named for them. The Camí de Cavalls, literally the horse path, was once used to defend the coast on horseback; today it circles the whole island for some 185 km as a signposted route in Spain's Gran Recorrido network, open to walkers and cyclists, though sections close from time to time for landslide risk or roadworks. For reaching a nearby beach it is often quicker than driving, because the roads are so simple: one main road, the ME-1, links Ciutadella and Maó, and every other major road branches off it. East of Es Mercadal rises El Toro, the island's highest point, with a panoramic view. The island's traditional sandal, the avarca, was originally designed as a shoe for farmers and is now a fashion icon found around the world.

## Coves Beyond the Resorts

The main resorts line the south coast, from Santo Tomas and Son Bou to Punta Prima and S'Algar. Son Bou has the island's longest beach, with pale sand and space for everyone a few minutes' walk from the crowd. West of Cala Galdana, Cala Macarella is one of the best beaches on Menorca, a natural cove of crystal-clear blue water, and nearby Cala en Turqueta pairs soft sand with the same turquoise sea. North of Maó, Es Grau offers a long, shallow beach beside a nature reserve. Outside Ciutadella, the Líthica sandstone quarry holds gardens and a sandstone maze. In Maó itself, the Museu de Menorca fills a 15th-century former Franciscan monastery with the island's earliest history and its Roman, Byzantine and Muslim eras, while La Mola, a mid-19th-century Spanish fortress, stands over the harbour that made Menorca worth fighting for.

## Flight Context

This story is anchored at 39.97°N, 4.08°E near the middle of Menorca. At around 48 km long and 16 km wide, the whole island fits in view from 10,000-15,000 feet on a clear day. Look for the long natural harbour of Maó at the eastern end, with the La Mola fortress above it, and Ciutadella at the western end; the ME-1 road links the two through the centre of the island, and El Toro, the highest point, rises east of Es Mercadal. Resorts and coves line the south coast, including Son Bou's long beach. Menorca Airport (LEMH) lies about 2.5 nautical miles southwest of Maó. Mallorca, with Palma de Mallorca Airport (LEPA), lies about 67 nautical miles to the west-southwest across the water, and ferries cross from Ciutadella to Alcúdia. Seasonal traffic is heavy from May to October, with flights becoming sparse in winter.
