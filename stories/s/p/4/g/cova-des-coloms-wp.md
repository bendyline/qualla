# Cova des Coloms

Menorcans call this vast limestone chamber the Cathedral, a natural hall where Talaiotic islanders laid their dead more than two thousand years ago.

**Location:** 39.9329, 4.0389

**Categories:** Caves, Archaeology, Prehistory, History of Menorca, Balearic Islands, Natural landmarks

## Introduction

Menorcans call it the Cathedral, and the moment you step inside you understand why. The Cova des Coloms opens into a chamber whose stone ceiling soars some 24 metres overhead, wide enough to swallow a small church and running back more than 100 metres into the hillside. Daylight fades to a cool gloom. Footsteps and voices gather and return as echoes. This is no built sanctuary but a natural one, carved by water through the limestone of the Binigaus gully in southern Menorca, and for the Talaiotic people of Menorca it served a solemn purpose: it was a place to lay their dead.

## The Walk from Es Migjorn Gran

There is no ticket booth and no floodlit path. You reach the cave on foot, walking roughly a mile southeast from the village of Es Migjorn Gran along the route that heads down toward the beach of Binigaus. The nearest place to leave a car is beside the local graveyard, a fitting neighbour for what lies ahead. From there a signed gravel track threads through a barranc, one of the deep, green ravines that cut across Menorca's south, before the ground opens and the cave's wide mouth appears in the rock. Access is free, as it has always been, and the approach itself sets the mood: quiet, unhurried, a little wild.

## A Cathedral of Stone

The name Cova des Coloms means the Cave of the Pigeons, but its size earned it the grander nickname. The entrance alone is about 15 metres wide, and the interior divides into two distinct spaces. First comes the great central hall, running some 50 metres deep, the part that truly feels cathedral-like. Beyond it a long, narrow corridor tapers away into the dark. The proportions are what stay with visitors: a vaulted ceiling of raw limestone, the shift from bright entrance to shadowed depth, the sense of standing inside a space that no architect designed yet somehow resembles one. Little wonder it has been recognised as a Bien de Interes Cultural, a protected cultural landmark.

## A Talaiotic Tomb

This was a burial place during Menorca's Talaiotic period, and the people interred here were not curiosities but a community honouring its own. The French prehistorian Emile Cartailhac explored the cave in the 1890s and, in 1892, published his discovery of ceramics and human bones. A more thorough excavation followed in 1914 and 1915, carried out by Antoni Vives i Escudero with the landowner Bartomeu Sturla. They recovered pottery vessels and two bronze horns, objects of striking craft. Both horns survive today in the Museum of Menorca. From charred wood associated with one of them, radiocarbon dating returned a date of 406 BC, a precise thread reaching back across more than two thousand four hundred years to the hands that placed it there.

## Menorca's Prehistoric Wealth

The Cova des Coloms belongs to a far larger story that the world formally recognised in 2023, when Talayotic Menorca was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. Across this small island rise funerary navetas shaped like upturned boats, circular houses, T-shaped taula sanctuaries, and the tower-like talayots that give the culture its name. Menorca is said to hold the highest density of prehistoric sites per square metre of any island on Earth, a landscape where cyclopean stonework begins around 1600 BCE and runs unbroken until the Roman conquest of 123 BCE. Seen against that backdrop, the Cathedral is not an isolated wonder but one chamber in an island-wide monument to the deep human past.

## The Ravine to the Sea

Part of the cave's power lies in where it sits. The barranc de Binigaus is one of the deep, sheltered ravines that score Menorca's southern flank, green corridors that funnel down toward the coast and end, in this case, at the pale sand of Binigaus beach. Walking the gully to reach the Cathedral, you pass through a slice of the island most visitors never see, cool and shaded where the cliffs press close, opening to bright Mediterranean light near the shore. The people who buried their dead here chose the spot with care. A great natural chamber, hidden within a ravine that leads to the sea, offered both grandeur and seclusion, a threshold between the living world above and whatever they believed lay beyond. Millennia later, the walk still feels like a passage into somewhere set apart.

## Flight Context

The cave lies at 39.933°N, 4.039°E, in the barranc de Binigaus on Menorca's south coast, near Es Migjorn Gran. From 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL, trace the parallel green ravines slicing south toward the sea; the cave sits within one of them, just inland from the beach of Binigaus. The cave mouth itself is invisible from the air, so navigate by the ravine and the coastline. Menorca Airport (LEMH) is about 12 nm east near Mahon; Palma de Mallorca (LEPA) lies roughly 95 nm west. Clear, calm conditions best reveal the ravine network below.
