# Hispania Balearica

The Roman province born from a war on pirates, famed for slingers trained since childhood and wheat so rich it fed an empire.

**Location:** 39.5000, 3.0000

**Categories:** Roman history, Ancient Mediterranean, Balearic Islands, History of Spain, Ancient provinces, Archaeology

## Introduction

On these islands, a boy did not simply sit down to supper. According to the Greek and Roman writers who marveled at the custom, a Balearic child's bread was placed atop a distant post, and he went hungry until he could knock it down with a sling. Hunger made marksmen. By adulthood the slingers of the Balearics ranked among the most feared missile troops in the western Mediterranean, prized as mercenaries by Carthaginians and Romans alike. The archipelago took its very name from them: in Greek, ballo meant 'to throw,' and this was, quite literally, the land of the throwers.

## The Pirates' Last Refuge

Long before Rome arrived, these were contested waters. The Talaiotic people had raised their stone towers here; Phoenician traders followed; and on Minorca the Carthaginians founded Mago, the port that survives today as Mahón. Majorca's two fine harbors, though, drew a rougher crowd. Pirates from Sardinia and southern Gaul used the islands as a lair, and when Roman campaigns swept Transalpine Gaul in 126 BC and Sardinia the year after, the Baleares became the raiders' last hiding place in the western Mediterranean. In 123 BC the consul Quintus Caecilius Metellus put an end to it, subduing the islanders with little difficulty. Rome had reasons beyond piracy: that same year the reformer Gaius Gracchus held the tribunate, and his fortunes were tied to clients across Hispania who stood to gain from a pacified sea. The military victory was modest, but the reward was not. Metellus celebrated a triumph in 121 BC, earned the honorific Balearicus, and planted the colonies of Palma and Pollentia on Majorca — the ancestors of two towns that stand there still.

## Bread Weighed Against Bread

To hold the islands, Balearicus settled some three thousand Romans here — most likely veterans of the Spanish wars — split between Palma, whose broad sheltered harbor lay open to the trade winds off Baetica and North Africa, and Pollentia on the north coast, a port for ships bound to Gaul. Two Roman colonies on a single island said plainly how much the harvest and the harbors were worth. The colonists had landed somewhere rich. When Pliny the Elder sailed out to see the place for himself, he found the wheat unmatched: a single measure of Balearic grain, he reported, yielded thirty-five pounds of bread, against just twenty-two from the best of southern Spain. The islands sent snails and oysters, tunny and mackerel to Roman tables, and became a center of wine the Romans took to eagerly. Island dye-works colored Baetican wool bound for Italy, and the red ochre of the hills was ground into pigment for frescoes.

## The Emperor and the Rabbits

For all its wealth, the province could be humbled by the smallest of enemies. Under Augustus, the islanders were so overrun by a plague of rabbits — burrowing through fields, undermining crops — that they appealed to the emperor himself, who dispatched soldiers to help beat the infestation back. For a long time Balearica governed itself as merely the fourth district of Hispania Tarraconensis, its own council handling local needs while answering directly to Rome. Only under Diocletian, after 284 AD, did the islands finally become a province in their own right, cut loose because they leaned on no mainland for their bread. By then more than thirty thousand people lived here, and in 418 AD they received a bishop of their own.

## When the Sea Changed Hands

The end came, as it so often did, from the water. In 426 the Vandals under King Gunderic seized Carthago Nova on the Spanish coast — the great naval base of the western Mediterranean — and turned their ships toward the Baleares. A generation later, in 455, following the death of the emperor Valentinian III, Gunderic's successor Gaiseric, now ruling from North Africa, folded the islands into his kingdom alongside Corsica and Sardinia, prizes for a sea power hungry for harbors. The slingers' islands would pass through other hands still, held at last by the Emirate of Córdoba from the early tenth century. The Roman province was gone, but the harbors that had made it worth taking never lost their pull.

## Flight Context

Centered near 39.5°N, 3.0°E, the province spanned the whole Balearic archipelago — Mallorca and Menorca (the ancient Gymnesiae) to the northeast, Ibiza and Formentera (the Pityusae) to the southwest. From a cruising altitude of 15,000–20,000 feet in clear Mediterranean air, all four islands can be held in a single view, ringed by the deep blue that once carried Roman grain ships. The Roman colony of Pollentia lies near modern Alcúdia on Mallorca's north coast; Palma's great bay opens to the south. Nearest airports are Palma de Mallorca (LEPA) and Menorca (LEMH). Summer skies here are reliably clear, with the best light and visibility in calm mornings before the afternoon sea breeze builds haze.
