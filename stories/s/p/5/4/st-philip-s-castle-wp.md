# St. Philip's Castle

Once the strongest fortress in the western Mediterranean, guarding Mahón's harbor mouth, St. Philip's Castle was blown apart by its own conquerors and now survives mostly as tunnels beneath the grass.

**Location:** 39.8653, 4.3057

**Categories:** History of Menorca, Military History, Castles and Fortresses, Balearic Islands, Lighthouses, Buildings and Structures

## Introduction

For two centuries this was one of the strongest fortresses in the western Mediterranean, and today you can barely find it. St. Philip's Castle once guarded the narrow mouth of Mahón's harbor with tiers of ramparts, tunnels, and gun batteries, a stronghold so formidable that armies preferred to starve it out rather than storm it. Then, in 1782, the men who finally took it decided the safest fort was no fort at all, and blew most of it into rubble. What remains lies mostly underground, in the municipality of Es Castell, a labyrinth of galleries beneath the grass at the harbor's edge.

## A Fortress Born of Fear

The castle grew out of catastrophe. After Barbarossa's corsairs sacked Mahón in 1535 and carried off hundreds of islanders into slavery, the Spanish crown resolved that the harbor must never again lie open, and around 1554 it raised the first Castell de Sant Felip on the point commanding the entrance. When Britain took Menorca in the eighteenth century, engineers rebuilt and enlarged the fort in the 1760s into a vast defensive complex, its underground galleries burrowing through the rock. The harbor that had tempted invaders for a thousand years finally had teeth, and whoever held the castle held the finest anchorage in the Mediterranean.

## The Admiral Who Was Shot

In 1756, with the Seven Years' War igniting, a French army under the Duke de Richelieu landed on Menorca and laid siege to the fort. A British fleet under Admiral John Byng sailed from Gibraltar to relieve it, fought an indecisive action offshore, and withdrew; the garrison, unaided, eventually surrendered. Byng paid for that failure with his life. Court-martialed for not doing his utmost, he was shot on his own quarterdeck at Portsmouth on March 14, 1757. The execution so struck Voltaire that he skewered it in Candide, where a character observes that in England it is thought wise to kill an admiral now and then 'to encourage the others.'

## The Second Fall

A quarter-century later the fort fell again. In 1781 a Franco-Spanish army under the Duke de Crillon invaded Menorca and besieged St. Philip's Castle for more than five months. The British garrison held through the winter but was steadily ground down, and in February 1782 it surrendered. This time the victors drew a hard conclusion. Rather than leave so powerful a fortress for the British to reclaim in the next war, Crillon ordered it demolished, and by that August the great castle had been reduced to the ruins and buried passages that visitors explore today. A stronghold that had defied two sieges was undone not by cannon but by the shovels of its own captors.

## The Lighthouse in the Line of Fire

The site's strategic value outlived the fortress. In 1852 a lighthouse rose on the Sant Carles point beside the old castle grounds, a sixteen-meter masonry tower atop a keeper's house, warning ships off the harbor mouth. It had one glaring flaw: it stood squarely in the firing line of the artillery battery still stationed at the castle. Keepers were told when gunnery practice was coming, but they grumbled that the shells rattled the building and terrified their families. The stalemate lasted until 1912, when the tower was retired for a movable light on a metal davit, lowered out of harm's way during practice and raised the rest of the time to throw its beam nine and a half nautical miles out to sea.

## What Lies Beneath

For all that was destroyed, the castle refused to disappear. Beneath the fields of Es Castell survives a network of tunnels, magazines, and galleries carved into the rock, enough that guided tours now descend into the dark to trace the outline of the vanished stronghold. Standing at the harbor mouth, it takes an act of imagination to picture the walls that once loomed here, the batteries that once dueled with a lighthouse, the sieges that helped decide which empire held the Mediterranean's finest port. St. Philip's Castle is a ruin, but a ruin with an outsized story, a fortress important enough that its own conquerors could not afford to let it stand.

## Flight Context

St. Philip's Castle guards the south side of the Port of Mahón entrance at 39.87°N, 4.31°E, in Es Castell, about 6 km east of Menorca Airport (LEMH). From 2,000-3,000 feet the harbor mouth and the low, grassed-over castle point are the key features; the Sant Carles lighthouse area and the open Mediterranean lie just to the east. Good year-round visibility, though the ruins read as earthworks rather than standing walls, so use the narrow harbor inlet cutting inland to the west as the primary reference.
