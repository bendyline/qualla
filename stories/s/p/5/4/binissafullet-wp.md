# Binissafullet

A Bronze and Iron Age settlement near Sant Lluis whose fallen T-shaped taula was the only one on Menorca ever lifted back upright, part of the island's 2023 UNESCO World Heritage prehistory.

**Location:** 39.8457, 4.2346

**Categories:** Archaeology, History, Prehistory, Menorca, UNESCO World Heritage

## Introduction

Six of Menorca's great T-shaped taulas have never moved. For roughly three thousand years they have stood exactly where Bronze Age hands set them, upright through empires and invasions and the slow erosion of centuries. Binissafullet holds the seventh, and it is different. This taula fell. Archaeologists found it lying on the ground, worked out how it had once stood, and raised it back into the sky, making it the only monument of its kind on the island to be re-erected in modern times. To stand before it is to look at prehistory that human beings had to reassemble.

## The Stone That Fell and Rose Again

In 1988 the site was cleared of the trees that had grown over it, and in 1990 its taula enclosure was excavated and restored. The taula itself, the central monument of any such enclosure, was discovered flat on the earth, and only then lifted back into its upright position. It is a modest thing as taulas go, unusual for its small dimensions and the narrow breadth of its supporting stone. That slenderness may be why it toppled where its sturdier cousins endured. What it lacks in scale it gains in candour: alone among Menorca's standing taulas, it wears its restoration openly, a prehistoric silhouette that owes its present posture to the twentieth century.

## A Village of the Bronze and Iron

Binissafullet was no lone monument but a living settlement, occupied from the tenth century BC and reaching its height in the fourth and third centuries BC. It carries the full vocabulary of the Talayotic world: a talayot, the cone-shaped tower that gives the culture its name; the taula enclosure at its heart; a hypostyle hall of stone pillars; dwellings; and silos cut for storage. Scattered Islamic materials across the surface hint that people returned to the spot in the medieval period, long after its builders were gone. Layer by layer, the ground here records a place that mattered to more than one civilization.

## Feasts in the Enclosure

What did people do inside the enclosure? The excavations gave an unusually vivid answer. Diggers turned up fragments of Punic amphorae, the two-handled jars that carried wine across the ancient Mediterranean, alongside the bones of sheep and goats and the remains of a hearth. Read together, they describe ritual: gatherings in which the consumption of wine and roasted meat seems to have been the point. These were not idle meals but ceremonies, performed in a stone sanctuary under open sky. Every one of those artifacts now rests in the Museum of Menorca in Mahon, where the debris of ancient feasting has become a catalogued record of belief.

## A Second Life

The settlement's story did not end when its builders died out. Scattered across the surface of the site, archaeologists have found medieval Islamic materials, evidence that people returned to Binissafullet long after the Talayotic world had vanished, during the centuries when Menorca lay under Muslim rule. It is a quiet but telling detail. A place raised in the tenth century BC, thriving in the fourth and third, was still drawing inhabitants more than a thousand years later, its stone shelters and cleared ground useful to a wholly different culture. Few sites lay their layers so plainly. Stand among the ruins and you are looking not at a single lost people but at a spot that human beings kept finding reasons to occupy, age after age, until it finally passed into the keeping of the road and the field.

## Among the Chosen Sites

Binissafullet does not stand alone in significance either. It was one of the sites in the long candidacy of Talayotic Menorca, and in September 2023 that campaign succeeded: the island's prehistoric monuments were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, becoming Spain's fiftieth such site. Getting here asks little of a visitor. The settlement sits beside the Me-10 road linking Sant Lluis with the Binissafuller residential area, close enough to the tarmac that the taula can be seen from the road itself, a three-thousand-year-old ceremony frozen in stone just past the verge.

## Flight Context

Located at 39.8457 N, 4.2346 E in Menorca's flat southeastern interior, near Sant Lluis and roughly 7 km southwest of Port Mahon. Menorca Airport (LEMH) lies about 5 nm to the west-northwest. From low altitude the site appears as a small cluster of pale stone amid cultivated fields, hard to distinguish from farm walls without local knowledge; the nearby Me-10 road is the best visual reference. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,500 ft over the southeastern plain gives context to the dry-stone landscape. Visibility is generally excellent in summer.
