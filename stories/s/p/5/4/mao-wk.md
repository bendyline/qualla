# Maó

The capital of Menorca, wrapped around one of the Mediterranean's finest natural harbours, fought over by Carthage, Britain, France and Spain, and quite possibly the birthplace of mayonnaise.

**Location:** 39.8650, 4.2260

**Categories:** Travel, History, Menorca, Cities, Food and Drink

## Introduction

The city may take its name from a brother of Hannibal. Mago Barca of Carthage left his mark on the western Mediterranean more than two thousand years ago, and Maó, capital of Menorca, is thought to carry an echo of him still. That deep pedigree suits the place, because Maó has spent its whole history being wanted. The reason lies at its feet: a sheltered inlet reaching five kilometres inland, one of the great natural harbours of the Mediterranean, calm and defensible and perfectly placed. Whoever held it could dominate the sea lanes of the western Med, and for centuries a parade of empires tried.

## The Best Harbour in the Sea

Everything about Maó begins with the water. The harbour is not a bay but a long, narrow sea inlet, a natural fjord that lets ships slip far inland to safe anchorage while the town watches over them from the bluffs. As vessels grew larger in early modern times, so did the value of a haven this size and this well protected. The logic was brutally simple: control Maó, and you controlled trade and territory across the western Mediterranean; leave it in a rival's hands, and you handed them the same advantage. A harbour like this was not a convenience. It was a strategic asset that reshaped the fate of the whole island around it.

## The Prize Everyone Fought For

Being coveted has a cost. The harbour was fortified after 1287, when the island's Muslim rulers were deposed, but the defences were not enough. In 1535 an Ottoman raid struck the port and carried off six hundred of its people into slavery, a single day that tore hundreds of Menorcans from their homes and families and scattered them across the sea. The memory of that vulnerability hardened the town's resolve to wall itself against the next attacker. Maó learned early what it meant to sit on a prize: that the same waters which brought wealth and ships could just as easily bring raiders, and that safety on this coast was never something to take for granted.

## The British Century

The British cast the most covetous eye of all, as they strung together imperial stepping stones from Gibraltar toward Malta, Egypt and India. They first occupied Menorca in 1708, and a treaty in 1713 handed them the entire island; they promptly moved the capital here from Ciutadella and reinforced the harbour. When the Seven Years' War broke out in 1756, the French swiftly overran the island, penning the British inside Fort St Philip. Admiral Byng's relief squadron failed to break the siege and withdrew, a failure for which Byng was court-martialled and shot. Menorca then ping-ponged among Britain, France and Spain before being ceded, finally and definitively, to Spain in 1802.

## A City You Can Eat

For all its martial history, Maó's most enduring conquest may be culinary. Mayonnaise takes its name from this town; legend links the sauce to the French victory of 1756, though it is probably older than that convenient story. The island's cooler, wetter climate favours dairy farming over its drier Balearic neighbours, and the result is Mahon cheese, a firm, tangy staple that carries the city's name to tables far beyond Spain. Down on the waterfront, the old Xoriguer distillery still produces the juniper-heavy gin that the British appetite for spirits first established here. Empire came and went; the flavours it left behind stayed on, wearing the town's name.

## Walking the Capital

Modern Maó, home to around thirty thousand people and nearly a third of all Menorcans, is best met on foot in its traffic-restricted old town. An elegant neo-baroque town hall from 1789 stands near an active Franciscan monastery, and a surviving city gate, built in 1359, is the last fragment of the medieval walls. Out in the harbour, boats visit the Lazaretto, the old quarantine station on its own island. Ring the town, and the deep prehistory returns: the taula and prehistoric village of Trepuco and the tower of Torrello stand within easy reach, reminders that people were building in stone on this ground thousands of years before the empires ever arrived.

## Flight Context

Located at 39.865 N, 4.226 E on the eastern side of Menorca, at the head of the long inlet of Port Mahon. Menorca Airport (LEMH) lies about 2.5 nm to the southwest in Sant Lluis. From the air the defining feature is the 5 km fjord-like harbour reaching inland from the sea, with the city clustered on its southern bluff, Illa del Rei island midstream, and the La Mola fortress guarding the seaward mouth. A viewing altitude of 2,000-3,500 ft along the inlet gives the fullest picture of the harbour and old town. Summer visibility is excellent; the eastern approaches are exposed to the northerly Tramuntana wind.
