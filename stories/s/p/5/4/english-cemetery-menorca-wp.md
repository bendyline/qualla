# English Cemetery, Menorca

A small walled burial ground above Port Mahon where American sailors who died far from home in the early 1800s still lie, once forgotten and now tended each Memorial Day.

**Location:** 39.8894, 4.2902

**Categories:** History, Naval History, Menorca, Cemeteries, United States Navy

## Introduction

The name misleads twice over. The English Cemetery at Port Mahon holds few Englishmen, and its most numerous residents are Americans. It earned its name not from nationality but from faith, a plot for the non-Catholic dead in a resolutely Catholic country. Behind its walls lie twenty named sailors of the United States Navy, one Navy wife, and others whose identities the years have swallowed. They came to this Mediterranean island young and in good health, and they never left it. Their headstones, some carefully cut and some improvised, are a quiet record of an American naval story most Americans have never heard.

## Twenty Names Far From Home

There is a particular loneliness to a sailor's grave on a foreign shore, and this ground is full of it. The men buried here were mostly Americans who died of illness or injury during peacetime service, not the drama of battle but the slower attrition of fever and accident that dogged any long deployment. Among them lie occasional sailors of other nations, and at least one Navy wife who crossed an ocean and stayed. Record-keeping was haphazard and the tombstones were often made on the spot, so the cemetery reads less like an official monument than like something the living improvised for the dead, name by name, as men kept dying far from anyone who had known them.

## Why the Americans Came

The story begins with a young republic pushing out into the world. As the United States expanded its commerce and reach after the War of 1812, its navy needed a European base at the eastern end of the Atlantic crossing, and it found one at Gibraltar. But raw feeling lingered from the recent war, and sharing a British port proved awkward. So in 1820 American operations shifted east to Menorca, off the Spanish coast, where a training base needed what every base abroad needs: a medical facility, and beside it, inevitably, a cemetery. The English Cemetery at Port Mahon already existed, its gates open to the Protestant dead, and it began to fill with the sailors of a distant flag.

## The Admiral's Return

The cemetery's proudest moment was a homecoming of sorts. David Farragut, the Union's great naval hero of the Civil War and the first admiral in United States history, was the son of a man born in Ciutadella, Menorca, in 1755. In 1868 Farragut came to the island, and with him came a flush of significance for this small burial ground. It did not last. After his visit Menorca receded from American naval life, and the cemetery slid into neglect. The last recorded burial came in 1890: Lieutenant Karl von Bunsen, of the German family tied to the laboratory burner that bears their name. Then the gate mostly stayed shut, tended only by whatever sailors happened to land.

## The Unnamed and the Remembered

The roll of the dead is only partly legible. Twenty Americans lie here under full names, but others are known only in part, and a small, uncertain number belong to no records at all, sailors of assorted flags whose identities slipped away with the men who might have remembered them. Among the named is a single Navy wife, a woman who followed the fleet across an ocean and was buried beside it. Each stone marks a life cut short far from home by fever or mishap rather than glory, and the roughness of the record is itself a kind of testimony, a reminder that these were ordinary people whose deaths abroad rarely made it into the grand histories of the navies they served.

## Keeping the Graves

Rescuing the place took stubborn affection. In 1965 an American expatriate and amateur historian living on Menorca, Jim Maps, published an article about the cemetery in the naval journal American Neptune, hoping that a portrait of its decay would shame the U.S. Navy into caring for it. The Navy never formally did. But from 1977, memos began directing visiting American ships to tend the graves when they could, and the cemetery passed into the unofficial care of the Madrid Council of the Navy League of the United States. In 2008 the Spanish government carried out a thorough restoration, shoring up the walls and defending the ground against the sea. Each American Memorial Day, the Madrid League still comes to remember the men who never sailed home.

## Flight Context

Located at 39.889 N, 4.290 E on the shore of the long inlet of Port Mahon, on Menorca's eastern side near Es Castell. Menorca Airport (LEMH) lies roughly 5 nm to the west-southwest. The cemetery is a small walled enclosure close to the water and difficult to pick out individually from the air; the dramatic 5 km fjord-like harbour of Mahon is the dominant landmark, with the fortifications of La Mola guarding its mouth. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,500 ft along the harbour gives the best orientation. Visibility is typically excellent in summer.
