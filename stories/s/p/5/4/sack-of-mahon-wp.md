# Sack of Mahón

In 1535 the corsair Hayreddin Barbarossa sailed into Mahón's harbor flying false imperial colors, and turned a homecoming into one of the darkest nights in Menorca's memory.

**Location:** 39.8892, 4.2636

**Categories:** History of Menorca, Military History, Ottoman Empire, 16th Century, Balearic Islands, Piracy

## Introduction

On the night of September 1, 1535, a fleet slipped into the great harbor of Mahón flying imperial colors, and the people of Menorca rejoiced. These were Charles V's ships, surely, home from the emperor's triumph at Tunis. Two Franciscan friars, Bartomeu Genestar and Francesc Coll, rowed out to welcome them. Only as they drew close did the truth arrive like a cold wave: the banners were a disguise, and the man commanding the fleet was Hayreddin Barbarossa, the Ottoman corsair Charles had just driven from Tunis, come to take his revenge on a softer target.

## A Fleet Flying False Colors

Barbarossa had every reason to want an easy victory. Weeks earlier, Charles V had stormed Tunis and scattered the corsair's forces; Barbarossa had fled to Bône, gathered what ships he could, and sailed away with as many as 800 captives bound for the slave markets of Algiers. Menorca lay conveniently on his route home. The friars' warning bought Mahón only moments. Church bells rang, the gates swung shut, and townspeople scrambled to the walls as Barbarossa put 2,500 men ashore and ringed the town. What had looked like a homecoming became a siege in the space of a single night, and the island had no time to muster its strength.

## The Relief Column

Word raced west to Ciutadella, then the island's capital, where the governor gathered his knights and rode hard for Mahón, picking up volunteers as he went. It was courage against arithmetic. On September 3 his column of roughly three hundred met Barbarossa's army on the road and was overwhelmed almost at once. The governor fell there, along with a hundred of his knights. Menorca's best hope died in a single afternoon of one-sided fighting. Behind the walls of Mahón, the defenders understood what the silence from the west meant: the road was open, and no one was left to hold it.

## The Night the City Broke

With the relief column gone and a breach opening in the wall, the town's leaders opened talks. On the evening of September 4 they surrendered, on the promise that their own houses and persons would be spared. The promise meant nothing for everyone else. The night that followed was one of the darkest in the island's memory: the two friars killed, homes burned, women raped, and some six hundred people seized and carried off to be sold into slavery, never heard from again. Behind those numbers were families torn apart in a few hours, neighbors who vanished from the record entirely. The leaders who had bargained for themselves slipped away to shelter at Binimaimut.

## A Reckoning and a Fortress

Justice came for those who had saved themselves. On September 8, by order of Eiximèn Perez de Figuerola, Viceroy of Mallorca, the men who had negotiated the surrender were arrested. Their trial dragged on for more than a year, and on October 24, 1536, the five chief defendants were executed in the Plaça del Born in Ciutadella. The sack also changed how Menorca defended itself. Beginning in 1554 the crown raised St. Philip's Castle to guard the mouth of Mahón's harbor, garrisoned with professional soldiers so that no fleet could ever again sail in wearing a friend's flag. The scars of 1535 would shape the island's defenses for the next three centuries.

## The Harbor That Tempted Empires

Mahón owed its misfortune to its greatest asset: one of the deepest and most sheltered natural harbors in the Mediterranean, a five-kilometer inlet that could swallow entire fleets. What made it priceless in war also made it a prize, and Barbarossa's raid was only the most brutal of many reckonings the port would face. Spanish, then British, then French flags would fly over these shores in the centuries to come, each power grasping for the harbor Barbarossa had exposed as vulnerable. For all its walls and watchtowers, an island can be undone by a single fleet that knows how to lie. The false colors of 1535 taught Menorca that lesson for good. Much of what came after, the castles and garrisons and watchtowers ringing the coast, was really an answer to a single September night.

## Flight Context

Centered on the Port of Mahón at 39.89°N, 4.26°E, the scene of the 1535 raid is the long harbor inlet immediately below Menorca Airport (LEMH), about 4 km to the southwest. View from 2,000-3,000 feet: the deep, narrow harbor is unmistakable, running roughly five kilometers inland, with Es Castell and the St. Philip's Castle point guarding its mouth to the east. Excellent visibility is typical; the harbor water reads deep blue against the pale stone town climbing its northern bank.
