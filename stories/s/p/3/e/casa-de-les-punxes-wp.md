# Casa de les Punxes

A spiked medieval castle dropped into Barcelona's modern grid, built for three sisters and carved with a defiant plea for Catalan freedom.

**Location:** 41.3980, 2.1638

**Categories:** Modernisme architecture in Barcelona, Buildings and structures in Barcelona, Eixample, Catalan architecture, Tourist attractions in Barcelona

## Introduction

Josep Puig i Cadafalch took a triangular corner in Barcelona's rational nineteenth-century grid and built a fairy-tale castle on it. The Casa de les Punxes, or House of Spikes, bristles with six pointed towers crowned by sharp conical spires, and it looks as though it wandered out of medieval northern Europe and lost its way among the tidy blocks of the Eixample. Locals named it for those spikes. Officially it is the Casa Terradas, and behind its stone battlements lies a quieter story: a father's provision for his three daughters, and a message of defiance carved into the wall for anyone who cared to read it.

## A Castle in the Grid

Everything about the building resists its surroundings. Barcelona's Eixample was laid out by Ildefons Cerdà on a strict, egalitarian grid of chamfered octagonal blocks, an emblem of modern urban order. Puig i Cadafalch answered it with romance. Drawing on the Wagnerian, central-European medievalism fashionable at the turn of the century, he blended three houses into what appears to be a single fortified block, its six corner towers rising like the turrets of a castle keep. The irregular, triangular site forced him off the standard grid pattern, and he made a virtue of the constraint. The result is one of the most instantly recognizable silhouettes in the city, a spiked crown of stone standing where the avenues meet.

## A House for Three Sisters

The building began as an act of family care. Bartomeu Terradas i Mont, a textile industrialist born in Figueres, left a fortune divided among his wife and his three daughters, Àngela, Josefa, and Rosa. Their brother managed the money and, around 1903 to 1905, commissioned his friend Puig i Cadafalch to raise a home for each sister within a single grand structure. The architect wove their identities into the stone. A ceramic panel of an angel marks Àngela's house; roses and the initials RTB belong to Rosa; anagrams of the sisters' names run like a frieze around the base of the main tower. What looks like one imposing castle is, read closely, three private homes sharing a wall and a name. The three sisters would die childless, and the building eventually passed to their brother as sole heir, but the personalized ornament remains, a permanent record of the family that gave the house its reason to exist.

## Give Us Back Our Freedom

The most striking decoration is also the most political. On a rear facade, a ceramic panel by the artist Enric Monserdà shows Sant Jordi, Saint George, driving his lance into the dragon, beneath an inscription that reads, in Catalan, "Patron Saint of Catalonia, give us back our freedom." It is a bold nationalist declaration to fix onto a private apartment block. Just below it, Monserdà set a sculpted portrait of a bespectacled man with pouty lips and a receding hairline: Puig i Cadafalch himself, placing his own likeness in the shadow of that plea. Elsewhere the facade carries clover motifs and a ceramic sundial ringed with the zodiac, inscribed with a line from Virgil, Numquam te crastina fallet hora, tomorrow will never fail you.

## The Iron Inside the Fantasy

Beneath the medieval costume, the Casa de les Punxes was quietly modern. Puig i Cadafalch broke with tradition and stripped away the load-bearing walls of the ground floor, replacing them with cast-iron pillars and metal beams to open the space for shops and offices. On the rooftop, where the six towers cluster, he pushed the engineering further still, using hanging steel straps so that upper floors no longer rested on vertical supports in the usual way. Declared a monument of national interest in 1976, the building has since been carefully restored and now houses offices rather than the Terradas sisters. From the tallest tower, at the corner of the Diagonal, the reward is the same one Puig i Cadafalch designed for: a privileged sweep across Barcelona's rooftops, with Gaudí's spires and the distant sea completing the view over a city his generation of Modernista architects reshaped.

## Flight Context

Casa de les Punxes stands at 41.3980°N, 2.1638°E, at the junction of the Avinguda Diagonal with Carrer Rosselló and Carrer Bruc in the Eixample. From altitude, look for the broad slash of the Avinguda Diagonal cutting across the regular grid; the building's six pointed towers sit right where that avenue meets the surrounding octagonal blocks, with Gaudí's Casa Milà a short way south and the Sagrada Família rising to the southeast. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies southwest along the coast, Sabadell (LELL) inland to the north, and Girona (LEGE) up the coast to the northeast. The Eixample's waffle-like pattern of chamfered corners frames the location clearly in good light.
