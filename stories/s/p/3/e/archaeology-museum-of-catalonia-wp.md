# Archaeology Museum of Catalonia

Housed in a fair pavilion that was meant to be demolished, this five-site museum guards Catalonia's deepest past, from cave-dwellers to Greek colonists.

**Location:** 41.3697, 2.1578

**Categories:** Archaeological museums in Catalonia, Museums established in 1932, Museums in Barcelona, Montjuïc, Archaeology of Spain

## Introduction

The building was supposed to be torn down. Raised on the slopes of Montjuïc as the Palace of Graphic Arts for the 1929 Barcelona International Exposition, it was designed to last only as long as the fair. When the crowds went home, though, the architect Josep Gudiol reworked the pavilion instead of demolishing it, and in 1932 the Republican Government of Catalonia filled it with objects far older than any exposition. The Archaeology Museum of Catalonia, or MAC, has been telling the region's oldest stories from that saved building ever since, and its reach now stretches to five separate venues scattered across the region.

## A Pavilion That Refused to Die

There is a fitting irony in housing a museum of deep time inside a structure that was itself nearly lost. The Palace of Graphic Arts belonged to a class of exposition buildings meant to dazzle for a season and then vanish. Gudiol's refit kept it standing, and the Generalitat's decision to place its archaeological collection there in 1932 gave the pavilion a second, longer life. The institution as it exists today was reconstituted under the Museums of Catalonia Act in 1990, but its heart remained on Montjuïc, in that improbable survivor of a world's fair. A building designed to be temporary became the keeper of things that had already endured for millennia.

## Deep Time on Montjuïc Hill

The Barcelona venue, designed by Pelagi Martínez i Patricio, unfolds across roughly 4,000 square meters arranged as a walk through time. Visitors move through five chronological spaces: prehistory, then the protohistoric era of Iberian peoples and the Greek and Phoenician traders who came to their shores, then the Roman Empire, and finally the Visigoths, whose arrival marks the threshold of the medieval world. Among the treasures are the Iberian hoard from Tivissa and a votive crown from the Treasure of Torredonjimeno, delicate objects that carry the weight of vanished cultures. To walk the galleries is to feel how many peoples layered themselves onto this coast, each leaving something behind for later hands to unearth.

## Where Greece Touched Spain

The museum's most extraordinary holding cannot be brought indoors. At Empúries, on a small isthmus near the village of Sant Martí d'Empúries, lie the ruins of a Greco-Roman city. People had settled the spot as early as the 9th century BCE, when the Indigetes lived here; around the 6th century BCE, Greek colonists founded a town, and during the Punic Wars the Romans arrived and made it theirs. Systematic excavation began in 1908, and even now only about a quarter of the site has been uncovered. Standing among its foundations, you are looking at one of the doorways through which the classical Mediterranean first reached the Iberian Peninsula, with three-quarters of the story still buried underfoot.

## A Museum With No Walls

MAC is less a single house than a constellation. At Olèrdola, a hilltop was inhabited continuously from the 8th century BCE until the era of the Reconquista, leaving an Iron Age nucleus, an Iberian settlement, a Roman fortification, medieval churches, and graves cut straight into the living rock; it joined the museum in 1995. In the Baix Empordà, the Iberian town of Ullastret is the largest such settlement yet found in Catalonia, its history reaching back to the 5th century BCE, its ruined walls and cobbled streets still being excavated after digs first began in 1948. A collection in Girona's Benedictine monastery of Sant Pere de Galligants, gathered there in 1857, became part of MAC in 1992.

## The Sea Keeps Its Own Museum

Not all of Catalonia's past lies under soil. Some of it rests on the seabed, in the hulls of wrecked ships and the debris of drowned harbors. To guard that heritage, the Submarine Archaeology Centre of Catalonia, based in Girona, was created in 1992 and folded into MAC in 1996. Its work is quieter than a gallery's: taking inventory of underwater sites, protecting them from looting, conserving what the water has preserved, and supporting outside teams of divers and researchers. Between the hilltop ruins, the Greek city on its isthmus, the saved pavilion on Montjuïc, and the fragile archive beneath the waves, the museum holds the memory of everyone who ever called this coast home.

## Flight Context

The museum's main building sits on Montjuïc at 41.3697°N, 2.1578°E, among the 1929 exposition grounds above Barcelona's port. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet it appears within the green terraces of the hill, near the National Palace (now the MNAC) and the Magic Fountain, with the harbor spreading below. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies just southwest along the shoreline, Sabadell (LELL) inland to the north, and Girona (LEGE) far up the coast to the northeast near two of the museum's own outlying sites. Montjuïc's hilltop landmarks and the ocean edge make the location easy to fix in clear weather.
