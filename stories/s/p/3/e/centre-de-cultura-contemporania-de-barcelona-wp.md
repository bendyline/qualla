# Centre de Cultura Contemporània de Barcelona

A former almshouse that once sheltered Barcelona's poorest children is now the CCCB, a museum devoted to the idea of the city itself, crowned by a glass wall that mirrors the rooftops.

**Location:** 41.3836, 2.1667

**Categories:** Culture in Barcelona, Museums in Barcelona, Arts centres in Catalonia, Architecture, El Raval, Ciutat Vella

## Introduction

For more than a century, the great building on Carrer de Montalegre took in the people Barcelona had no other place for: orphaned children, the elderly, the destitute. It was the Casa de la Caritat, the House of Charity, an almshouse raised in 1802 that sheltered the city's poorest until 1957. Walk into its courtyard today and you will find something the founders could never have imagined. Screens flicker, crowds gather for debates that run past midnight, and a towering wall of tilted glass throws the surrounding rooftops back at the sky. The charity closed, but the building was reborn in 1994 as the Centre de Cultura Contemporània de Barcelona, a place that trades not in alms but in ideas.

## The House of Charity

Before it was a museum, this was a refuge, and not always a gentle one. Almshouses of the nineteenth century offered survival rather than comfort, gathering the abandoned and the aging under one institutional roof at a time when the state provided little else. For over a hundred and fifty years the Casa de la Caritat did exactly that on the edge of El Raval. When the CCCB's designers came to reinvent it, they chose not to erase that past. The monumental original facade still stands, and one of the old nineteenth-century courtyards survives, so that anyone crossing the threshold passes through the bones of the charity that stood here first. It is a quiet act of memory, keeping faith with the thousands who once had nowhere else to go.

## A Wall of Glass That Watches the City

The transformation was the work of architects Helio Piñón and Albert Viaplana, whose reinvention won the FAD and Ciutat de Barcelona architecture prizes in 1993. Their boldest move came at the north wing. Where an old range once stood, they raised a thirty-meter prism of tilted glass and steel, completing the building's original U-shaped plan with something unmistakably of its own era. The glass leans out over the courtyard at the top, and its angled panes catch the jumbled roofline of the old city, turning the facade into a mirror of El Raval and, from the inside, a lookout over Barcelona. The contrast is deliberate and startling: austere historic masonry below, a shard of reflective modernity above, the two held in a single frame.

## A Museum About the City Itself

Most museums collect objects. The CCCB collects questions, and its subject is the city and urban culture, the way people live packed together and what that living makes of them. Across roughly 4,000 square meters of galleries and a full program of exhibitions, festivals, film cycles, concerts, courses, and public debates, it works to link the academic world with artists and with ordinary citizens. The underlying aim is to provoke thought about public space and the shape of contemporary life. It is a center without a permanent collection to defend, which lets it stay restless, reinventing its own halls with each new show and reaching out to co-produce work with institutions far beyond Catalonia.

## Anchor of a Changing Neighborhood

The choice of El Raval was not incidental. For generations this was one of Barcelona's roughest and most crowded districts, dense with immigrants and hard living, tucked between the medieval Gothic quarter and the harbor in the old town of Ciutat Vella. When the city set out to rehabilitate the neighborhood in the late twentieth century, culture became the lever, and the CCCB was among the institutions planted here to pull the area upward. It opened its doors on February 25, 1994. In the decades since, the once-forbidding streets around it have filled with students, galleries, and cafes, the arrival of ideas doing slowly what wealth alone never could.

## The Women's Courtyard

The complex kept growing. In the spring of 2011 the CCCB absorbed the old theatre of the Casa de la Caritat, a hall built in 1912 by the architect Josep Goday i Casals, connecting it to the main building at basement level through a courtyard called the Pati de les Dones, the Women's Courtyard. That project, by the firm Martínez Lapeña-Torres Arquitectos, folded yet another layer of the almshouse's history into the living center, stitching a century-old theatre and a fragment of a nineteenth-century cloister into a place now given wholly to the present. The building has changed its purpose more than once, but it has never stopped serving the city that raised it.

## Flight Context

The CCCB stands at 41.3836°N, 2.1667°E on Carrer de Montalegre in El Raval, in Barcelona's old town just west of La Rambla. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet the tilted glass prism of the north wing can flash in sunlight against the dense low rooftops of Ciutat Vella; the green rectangle of the nearby MACBA plaza and the broad diagonal cut of La Rambla are easier landmarks. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies about 6 nautical miles to the southwest along the coast, Sabadell (LELL) roughly 11 nm inland to the northwest, and Girona (LEGE) some 55 nm to the northeast. Clear Mediterranean light gives the sharpest view of the glass facade's reflections.
