# Barcelona City Hall

On the square where Roman Barcino once argued politics, Barcelona's city hall carries a scarred Gothic door and a medieval hall where a Council of One Hundred once ruled.

**Location:** 41.3822, 2.1775

**Categories:** City and town halls in Spain, Buildings and structures in Barcelona, Gothic architecture in Barcelona, Neoclassical architecture in Barcelona, History of Barcelona

## Introduction

There is a wound in the old doorway. On the quiet side street of Barcelona's City Hall, the medieval Gothic entrance survives, but part of it was cut away, a deliberate mutilation left in plain sight. It is the kind of scar that tells you a building has lived through arguments. This is the seat of the Barcelona City Council, standing on the Plaça Sant Jaume in the heart of the Gothic Quarter, staring across a narrow square at the Palau de la Generalitat, home of the Catalan regional government. Two powers, one plaza, and beneath them both, the buried stones of a Roman forum.

## A Square Where Rome Still Argues

The Plaça Sant Jaume is not just any intersection. In the Roman city of Barcino, this was the site of the forum, the open space where citizens gathered to debate, trade, and decide. Two thousand years later the square performs almost exactly the same function, flanked now by the city's two great political houses facing each other across the paving stones. When Barcelona wants to celebrate, protest, or mourn, it comes here, to the same ground where togaed magistrates once did the same. Few places so compactly stage the continuity of a city's public life. The buildings have changed and the languages have changed, but the instinct to gather at this spot to settle the community's business has never left. Layers of the city stack directly beneath the paving, Roman below medieval below modern, so that the square is not only a stage for politics but a cross-section of time.

## The Council of One Hundred

Inside, the building's grandest room is the Saló de Cent, and its name records an old form of self-rule. Here the Consell de Cent, the Council of One Hundred, governed the medieval city. Master builder Pere Llobet raised the great hall in 1369, and the council held its first session within it on 17 August 1373, during the reign of King Peter the Ceremonious, a date still fixed to a plaque on the wall. For more than three centuries the hundred councillors met beneath its arches to run the affairs of Barcelona, weighing questions of trade, defense, food supply, and public order, until the body was dissolved in 1714. To stand in the Saló de Cent is to occupy a chamber that predates most parliaments, a place where a merchant city practiced governing itself long before the modern state existed. That tradition of self-rule, cut short and later revived, remains a point of fierce local pride, and the hall is still used for the city's most solemn occasions.

## Two Faces, Two Centuries

The city hall wears two facades from two different worlds. Around the corner, on the Carrer de la Ciutat, is the older face: a civil Gothic front designed by the architect Arnau Bargués and built between 1399 and 1402, all pointed restraint and medieval stone. The face that greets the square, by contrast, is coolly classical. When the church of Sant Jaume that had given the plaza its name was demolished in 1823, the architect Josep Mas i Vila was charged with building a new frontage worthy of standing opposite the Renaissance elegance of the Generalitat across the way. Between 1830 and 1847 he raised the neoclassical facade that still faces the square, and the building has presented these mismatched profiles ever since.

## The Wound in the Door

That mismatch nearly cost the city its medieval front entirely. When the neoclassical facade went up, the old Gothic doorway was in the way, and the plan called for the earlier work to be sacrificed to the new. What saved it was scholarship and civic pride: the Reial Acadèmia de Bones Lletres de Barcelona and the Reial Acadèmia Catalana de Belles Arts de Sant Jordi campaigned to preserve the old entrance. They won, but not cleanly. The medieval door remained, though it now bears the visible mutilation where the two eras collided. Rather than a flaw to be hidden, the scar reads as an honest record. It marks the moment a city decided its past was worth keeping, even at the price of leaving the seam on view.

## Flight Context

Barcelona City Hall stands at 41.3822°N, 2.1775°E, deep in the dense street grid of the Gothic Quarter between La Rambla and the harbor. From altitude the individual building is hard to pick out; instead, look for the tight medieval tangle of the Ciutat Vella beside the arrow-straight cut of La Rambla, with the Cathedral's spire nearby and the port to the south. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies southwest along the coast, Sabadell (LELL) inland to the north, and Girona (LEGE) up the coast to the northeast. In clear coastal air the old town's dark, irregular rooftops contrast sharply with the ordered blocks of the surrounding Eixample.
