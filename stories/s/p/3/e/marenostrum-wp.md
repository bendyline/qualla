# MareNostrum

Raised for eighteen years in a glass box inside a deconsecrated Barcelona chapel before outgrowing it, MareNostrum is Spain's most powerful supercomputer, modeling genomes, drugs, climate, and the cosmos.

**Location:** 41.3894, 2.1161

**Categories:** Science and technology, Supercomputers, Computing, Research, Barcelona

## Introduction

Step into the Torre Girona chapel at Barcelona's Polytechnic University and you will not find an altar. Beneath the vaulted ceiling, in a sealed glass enclosure where a congregation once sat, hummed one of the most powerful computers in Spain for eighteen years. This is MareNostrum, the flagship machine of the Barcelona Supercomputing Center, and its former home inside a deconsecrated chapel made it perhaps the most photographed supercomputer on Earth. In December 2023 the newest generation outgrew the chapel and moved into a purpose-built hall next door, while the chapel itself now houses Spain's first quantum computers. The name means our sea, the Roman term for the Mediterranean that laps at the city a few kilometres downhill. In its new hall, the machine works through problems of genomes, medicines, weather, and the deep structure of the universe.

## A Machine in a Chapel

The setting was the whole point of its early fame. Rather than fill a bland warehouse, MareNostrum occupied a former place of worship, its racks standing under stone arches and stained glass in a glass room that kept the machine climate-controlled and the architecture visible. The computer itself was surprisingly compact, taking up about 180 square metres, less than half a basketball court. It first booted up on 12 April 2005 and was rebuilt, generation after generation, inside the same sacred shell for eighteen years, until the fifth generation outgrew the chapel and moved into a new hall built specifically for it next door. The contrast became iconic: centuries-old religious craftsmanship wrapped around some of the fastest calculation on the continent, silence and processing power sharing one room for the better part of two decades.

## What It Thinks About

MareNostrum exists to be used, and the range of its work is vast. Scientists run it to decode the human genome and study proteins, to simulate the birth and death of stars, to forecast weather and model the climate, to map what lies deep beneath the Earth's surface, and to design new drugs molecule by molecule. Other projects simulate earthquakes, model the human heart and lungs, and sift enormous volumes of data. As the most powerful supercomputer in Spain, it anchors the Spanish Supercomputing Network and belongs to the wider European research infrastructure, giving researchers across the country and the continent access to computing they could never build alone.

## A Shared Instrument

It helps to think of MareNostrum less as a computer than as an instrument, closer in spirit to a great telescope or a particle accelerator than to the laptop on your desk. No single lab could afford to build or run it, so it is operated as common ground. As one of a handful of machines in a European supercomputing partnership and a node of the national network, it is opened up to the scientific community across Spain and beyond, its hours parceled out to projects judged worth the enormous resource. A biologist in one city and a climate modeller in another queue for the same racks under the same roof. The building is singular. The machine inside it is deliberately, generously shared.

## Generations of Iron

Each version of MareNostrum has been a different beast beneath the same vaults. MareNostrum 2 relied on 2,560 IBM BladeCenter nodes running PowerPC processors with 20 terabytes of memory. MareNostrum 3 grew to 3,056 IBM nodes and nearly 49,000 Intel cores. MareNostrum 4, dubbed one of the most diverse supercomputers in the world, reached a total speed of 13.7 petaflops, its general-purpose block alone packing 165,888 processors across 3,456 Lenovo nodes. Remarkably, that block ran ten times faster than its predecessor while drawing only about thirty percent more power. Alongside it sat experimental clusters of IBM, NVIDIA, AMD, and ARM chips, a deliberate laboratory for whatever hardware might come next.

## MareNostrum 5

The newest chapter is also a political one. In July 2019 the European EuroHPC initiative chose the Barcelona Supercomputing Center to host one of the continent's pre-exascale supercomputers, part of a push to give Europe computing power that does not depend on machines built elsewhere. MareNostrum 5 was meant to switch on by the end of 2020, but the build slipped, and the system finally arrived in December 2023. It carries a dedicated platform for testing and developing homegrown European supercomputing technology. In a purpose-built hall beside the chapel that once served eternity, the machine now helps a continent plan for the future, one simulation at a time.

## Flight Context

MareNostrum's campus is anchored by the Torre Girona chapel and the new supercomputing hall beside it, on the north campus of the Polytechnic University of Catalonia at 41.389 N, 2.116 E, in the Pedralbes area of western Barcelona, close to the Palau Reial de Pedralbes and the Diagonal avenue and only a short distance from the Camp Nou stadium, the easiest large landmark to spot from the air. A pass at 1,500 to 2,500 feet frames the university district against the green Collserola hills rising behind it. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies about 8 nm southwest along the coast, Sabadell (LELL) roughly 9 nm north, and Girona (LEGE) about 45 nm northeast. Clear mornings after a dry inland wind give the sharpest view over the campus and the city grid below.
