# Casa Batlló

Gaudí's 'House of Bones' on the Passeig de Gràcia, where a dead dragon sprawls across the roof and no straight line survives.

**Location:** 41.3916, 2.1649

**Categories:** Antoni Gaudí buildings, Modernisme architecture in Barcelona, World Heritage Sites in Spain, Passeig de Gràcia, Eixample, Tourist attractions in Barcelona

## Introduction

Barcelonans gave it a nickname before the guidebooks did: Casa dels ossos, the House of Bones. Look up at the lower floors of Antoni Gaudí's building on the Passeig de Gràcia and you can see why. The balconies hang like pale masks or hollow skulls, and the stone columns of the main floor swell and taper like the bones of a leg, complete with what looks like a joint. There is scarcely a straight line anywhere on the facade. Considered one of Gaudí's masterpieces, Casa Batlló is less a house than a creature in stone and glass, an entire building that seems to breathe.

## The House of Bones

The skeletal quality is no accident of the eye. Gaudí set out to banish the straight line entirely, and the ground floor is the proof: irregular oval windows, sinuous tracery, sculpted stonework that ripples like something grown rather than built. The rounded openings edged with lip-like curves have earned the building a second nickname, the house of yawns, as if the whole facade were caught mid-breath. Where other architects imposed order, Gaudí imposed life. The effect can be unsettling up close, a structure that refuses the reassuring geometry of walls and corners, but it is precisely that strangeness that has drawn crowds to this stretch of the Passeig de Gràcia for more than a century.

## A Renovation, Not a Demolition

The building beneath the fantasy is older than its fame. It was raised in 1877 as an unremarkable house, and Josep Batlló bought it in 1903. When Batlló hired Gaudí the following year, his first thought was to tear the whole thing down and start fresh. Gaudí talked him out of it, arguing that a renovation would suffice, and by 1906 the transformation was complete. He gutted the main apartment for the Batlló family, added floors, and expanded the central light well. The house stands on the Illa de la Discòrdia, the Block of Discord, where four buildings by rival Modernista architects compete along a single stretch of street, each trying to outdo the others.

## Slaying the Dragon on the Roof

The roof is the building's boldest stroke. Its arched profile recalls the spine of a great beast, tiled in scales that shift from green at one end through deep blue and violet to red and pink at the other, with a small triangular window standing in for the creature's eye. To the left rises a turret capped with a four-armed cross. The common reading is irresistible: this is the lance of Saint George, the patron of Catalonia, plunged into the back of a slain dragon. Gaudí was working on his Sagrada Família at the same time, and legend held that its spires could once be glimpsed through the roof's eye window, a view partly restored today over the newer buildings between them.

## Light Like the Bottom of the Sea

Gaudí was obsessed with light, and the central atrium is his answer to a hard problem: how to feed daylight to every floor of a deep, narrow building. His solution was color. The light well is lined in blue tiles, richer and darker near the top where the sun is strong, paler toward the bottom, so that illumination reaches the lower floors as evenly as at the summit. He wanted standing at the base of the well to feel like resting on the seabed, gazing up through blue water. Higher still, the loft holds a run of sixty catenary arches, white and rhythmic, that read unmistakably as a ribcage, the interior skeleton of the beast whose spine crowns the roof.

## A Facade Like Water

The front of the house shimmers. Much of it is sheathed in trencadís, mosaic made from broken ceramic tiles, shading from golden orange into greenish blue, while the central section evokes the surface of a lily pond, all ripples and reflection, reminiscent of Monet's Water Lilies. Gaudí studded it with 330 rounds of polychrome pottery and colored glass discs he designed with his collaborator Josep Maria Jujol. When a decorative bulb arrived broken, Gaudí liked the fractured look and kept it. Monograms of Jesus, Mary, and Joseph mark the tower, a sign of his deep faith. In 2005, Casa Batlló was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, its dragon secure forever.

## Flight Context

Casa Batlló sits at 41.3916°N, 2.1649°E on the Passeig de Gràcia in the Eixample, Barcelona's grid-planned nineteenth-century district. From altitude the individual house is small, but the ruler-straight diagonal of the Passeig de Gràcia and the regular octagonal blocks of the Eixample make the neighborhood easy to find, with Gaudí's Casa Milà a few blocks north and the towering Sagrada Família rising to the east. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies southwest along the coast, Sabadell (LELL) inland to the north, and Girona (LEGE) up the coast to the northeast. The Eixample's chamfered corners form a distinctive waffle pattern that stands out clearly in good light.
