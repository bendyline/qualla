# Bombardment of Barcelona (1842)

The day the guns on Montjuïc turned inward, and a government shelled its own rebellious city into submission.

**Location:** 41.4019, 2.1667

**Categories:** Military history of Barcelona, 19th century in Barcelona, Riots and civil disorder in Spain, Labour disputes in Spain, 1842 in Spain, History of Catalonia

## Introduction

The cannons on Montjuïc had always guarded the sea. In the last weeks of 1842, they turned around. On a single day that December, the fortress crowning the hill above Barcelona fired down onto the streets and homes of the very city it was built to protect, hurling more than a thousand shells into a population that had risen in revolt. At least twenty people died and hundreds of buildings were shattered. It was not the work of a foreign enemy. It was the Spanish government answering its own citizens with artillery, one of the darkest episodes in the long, uneasy relationship between Barcelona and the state.

## A City at the Boiling Point

By 1842 Barcelona had become a pressure vessel of grievances. The free-trade policy of the regent, General Baldomero Espartero, was hollowing out the Catalan textile industry and the workers who depended on it, while an unpopular tax on food entering the city sharpened everyday hardship into anger. When the government met the unrest with force, it only widened the revolt. What made the uprising remarkable was its breadth. Weavers and laborers stood alongside figures from the propertied classes, among them the industrialist Joan Güell, father of the future patron of Gaudí, Eusebi Güell, and the Marquis of Llió. Rich and poor, factory owner and factory hand, found themselves briefly united against the same regime.

## The Guns on the Hill

The government's reply came from above. Under the command of Captain General Antonio Van Halen, the batteries of Montjuïc opened an indiscriminate bombardment of the city below. The cannons fired 1,014 projectiles, an artillery barrage aimed not at soldiers on a battlefield but at a dense urban grid full of families, workshops, and homes. Roughly 462 buildings were damaged or destroyed, and at least twenty people were killed. There is a particular cruelty in a fortress designed to defend a city being used to break it instead. For Barcelonans, the sight of shells arcing down from their own hilltop castle became a lasting emblem of how expendable the capital feared it was in the eyes of Madrid. Behind the dry figures were ordinary lives: weavers whose workshops collapsed, families who sheltered in cellars while their homes came apart above them, neighbors who did not survive the day. A bombardment counted in projectiles and ruined buildings was, for the people beneath it, counted in fear and loss.

## Collective Punishment

When the guns fell silent, the reckoning was severe. The militia was disarmed, and several hundred people were arrested. Between seventeen and eighteen members of the popular militia known as the Patuleyas were shot, along with one of their commanders, executed for having taken up the city's cause. The punishment then fell on Barcelona as a whole. The city was ordered to pay an extraordinary sum of twelve million reals to compensate the government's dead and wounded soldiers, and its council was forced to fund the rebuilding of the hated Citadel. In a final blow to organized labor, the government dissolved the Barcelona Weavers Association, the first trade union in Spanish history, and shut every newspaper but a single conservative daily.

## A Habit of Bombardment

The violence of 1842 sat within a troubled era. Isabella II had come to the throne as an infant, her right to rule disputed by the Carlists, who rejected a female sovereign and plunged the country into civil war. Espartero, her regent and three times prime minister, governed a fractured Spain. And Barcelona's ordeal did not end with one bombardment. The very next year, a fresh revolt called the Jamància brought the guns of Montjuïc down on the city again, this time killing 335 people and driving some 40,000 residents to flee. In 1856, the fortress fired on Barcelona once more. Three times in fourteen years, the hill that watched over the city also learned to punish it. The memory hardened into grievance and became part of the long story Catalans tell about their relationship with the central government, a wound that outlasted the rubble by generations.
