# Enriqueta Martí

The 1912 case that Barcelona remembers as "the Vampire of the Raval" is really the story of poor children who vanished while a whole city looked away.

**Location:** 41.3539, 2.1556

**Categories:** History, True crime, Social history, Barcelona, El Raval, Ciutat Vella

## Introduction

Poor children had been vanishing from the streets of Barcelona for years, and the city had learned not to look too hard. A beggar's child gone from the crowded lanes of the Raval rarely troubled the police; those families had no money, no influence, and no one to press for answers. Then, in early 1912, a little girl disappeared who would not stay forgotten, and the search for her tore open one of the darkest episodes in the city's history. The woman at its center, Enriqueta Martí, would be branded "the Vampire of the Raval" in the newspapers. More than a century later, historians are still trying to separate the real horror from the legend the press built on top of it.

## The Children Nobody Counted

Barcelona in the early twentieth century was a city of violent contrasts, immense new wealth pressed up against desperate poverty in the narrow streets of the old town. In that poverty, children were the most vulnerable of all: orphaned, abandoned, or sent out to beg by families who could not feed them. When such a child went missing, the machinery of justice barely stirred. Investigations were minimal precisely because the victims were poor, and for years an uneasy dread spread through the working-class districts that someone was taking their young. These were not statistics. They were sons and daughters whose names the official record scarcely bothered to keep, and whose disappearances were treated as beneath notice.

## A Girl Found Alive

The case broke because one child survived. In 1912, when a small girl vanished, the trail led to a flat in the old city, and there investigators found a living captive who had been kept hidden away. The surviving child described rooms she had been forbidden to enter and the terrible things concealed inside them. What detectives uncovered was enough to horrify a hardened city: bloodstained clothing, a knife, and a sack containing the small bones of children. Forensic experts working from the scant remains they could recover were eventually able to distinguish at least a dozen different victims. Because Martí kept no record of what she had done, the true toll will never be known.

## Medicine, Superstition, and Money

By day Martí dressed as a pauper and moved through the poorest streets; by night, the sources say, she moved among the wealthy at places like the Liceu opera house. She was accused of two intertwined trades, both preying on children and both fed by the appetites of respectable Barcelona. She was said to procure children for clients with monstrous desires, and to peddle quack folk remedies she claimed could cure tuberculosis, the age's most feared disease, or hold off old age. The grotesque ingredients she boasted of, the newspapers reported, came from her victims. Whatever the precise truth, the accusation exposed something ugly: a market in which the bodies of the poor could be sold to the fears and pleasures of those who could pay.

## Justice That Never Came

Martí had brushed against the law before. Arrested in 1909 during the upheaval of Barcelona's Tragic Week and accused of running a brothel that exploited children, she was quietly released, reportedly shielded by her connections among the city's powerful clients. She was never brought to trial for any of it. She died behind bars in 1913, before a court could hear the case, and the wealthy patrons rumored to have used her services were never named or charged. For the families of the vanished children, there was no reckoning and no verdict, only the silence that had surrounded their loss from the beginning.

## The Vampire That May Not Have Been

The press gave Barcelona a monster it could name: the "Vampire," the "Bad Woman," a figure of gothic dread who has since filled novels, a prize-winning musical, and films. But that legend deserves suspicion. In his 2014 investigation, the writer Jordi Corominas found that much of the lurid coverage rested on rough early rumors that no one ever seriously checked, and later historians have argued that Martí was in part a convenient scapegoat. The uncomfortable truth beneath the vampire story may be worse than the myth: a society so unequal that destitute parents could be driven to sell their children, and a city that noticed the danger only when it could no longer look away. The real memorial belongs not to the villain but to the children whose names were never counted.
