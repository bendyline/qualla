# 1977 Barcelona gay pride demonstration

Two years after Franco's death, some five thousand people marched down Barcelona's La Rambla in Spain's first great act of LGBT visibility, met with police charges and rubber bullets, in a country where their very existence was still a crime.

**Location:** 41.3814, 2.1731

**Categories:** LGBTQ history, Civil rights, Barcelona, Spanish history, Social movements, 1977 in Spain

## Introduction

On the afternoon of 26 June 1977, some five thousand people walked down La Rambla in Barcelona and, for the first time in Spain, refused to be invisible. Franco had been dead less than two years, and the country was feeling its way toward democracy, but the laws that criminalized them were still on the books. They marched anyway, behind a banner and a chant that named exactly what the moment was about: Nosaltres no tenim por, nosaltres som. We have no fear, we are. It was the first great act of LGBT visibility the country had ever seen, and it did not pass peacefully.

## A Crime to Exist

To grasp the courage of that afternoon, you have to know what it cost to be seen. Under Franco's dictatorship, homosexuality was not merely frowned upon but prosecuted. For decades it fell under the Law of Vagrants and Thugs, and later under the Law on Dangerousness and Social Rehabilitation, statutes that treated gay people as a danger to be corrected. Those found guilty could be fined, imprisoned, or committed to a psychiatric institution to be cured of who they were. The dictator was gone, but this legal machinery outlived him. The core of that law would not be completely abolished until 1995. Everyone who stepped onto La Rambla that day did so knowing the state still classed them as criminals.

## We Have No Fear, We Are

The march was called by the Front d'Alliberament Gai de Catalunya, the Gay Liberation Front of Catalonia, itself still illegal. At the head of the column walked a group of travestis, trans women and drag performers, who took the most exposed and dangerous position of all, the front, where the cameras and the police would look first. Behind them came the slogans, hand-lettered and defiant: My body is mine and I do with it what I want. Sexual amnesty. We are not dangerous. Down the great tree-lined promenade they went, five thousand strong, in full view of a city that had never been asked to look at them so directly.

## The Charge

The state's answer was violence. Police mounted several charges into the crowd and fired rubber bullets at the demonstrators. At least three people were seriously wounded. A fourth, a doctor named Oriol Martí, was beaten, arrested, and jailed for fifty-six days in La Model prison, accused of attacking and insulting the officers. The peaceful march had been met with force, but the force did not erase it. If anything, the brutality of the response drew the attention of the press and fixed the day in memory as a turning point, the moment the struggle for the rights of gay, lesbian, bisexual and transgender people in Spain became impossible to ignore. The timing sharpened its meaning. Spain was in the thick of its transition to democracy, holding its first free elections in four decades that very month, and here was a group the old order had defined as dangerous, insisting in the open that they were nothing of the kind.

## What the Cameras Kept

Much of what we know of that day survives because a few people were pointing lenses at it. The Catalan photographer Colita captured moments of the demonstration, images that now hang in the collection of the Reina Sofia museum in Madrid. Decades later, a man named Enrique Serrano, from Santa Coloma de Gramenet, brought out a set of photographs he had taken almost by accident when he was seventeen and had wandered into the march. He had kept them for more than forty years. Together, these frozen instants let a later, freer Spain look back at the ordinary, frightened, determined people who walked first, and understand exactly how much that walk had asked of them. Every June since, Pride marches have filled Spanish cities in numbers those five thousand could scarcely have imagined, and much of the machinery of persecution they defied is long gone. But the marches that came later all trace back to this one, and to the ones at the front of it who had the most to lose and stepped forward anyway.

## Flight Context

The demonstration ran down La Rambla in central Barcelona, at 41.3814 N, 2.1731 E, the famous kilometre-long promenade linking Placa de Catalunya to the old harbour and the Columbus monument at Port Vell. From above, La Rambla reads as a green diagonal slash through the dense city grid, with the Gothic Quarter to its east and El Raval to its west, and the sea just beyond its foot. This is dense Barcelona airspace beneath the approaches to Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL), 12 km to the southwest; Sabadell (LELL) lies inland to the north and Girona (LEGE) up the coast. A viewing altitude well clear of the LEBL corridor is essential; city haze can limit visibility on warm days.
