# Siege of Barcelona (801)

A seven-month Carolingian siege wrested Barcelona from eighty years of Muslim rule and created the County of Barcelona, the frontier polity that would one day become the heart of Catalonia.

**Location:** 41.4019, 2.1667

**Categories:** Military history, Medieval history, Sieges, Barcelona, Catalonia, Carolingian Empire

## Introduction

On 4 April 801, the gates of a starving city swung open, and Louis the Pious walked into Barcelona behind a procession of priests singing psalms. The son of Charlemagne had come to give thanks in a church for a victory seven months in the making. Behind him lay a siege of hunger and bombardment; ahead lay the birth of something that would outlast his entire empire. The Carolingians made the conquered city the capital of a new frontier county, and that County of Barcelona, planted here on the dangerous edge of Christendom, would grow across the centuries into the heart of Catalonia.

## Eighty Years Under a Different Banner

Barcelona had not been a Christian city for a long time. Early in the eighth century, when the Muslim armies of the Umayyad Caliphate swept away the Visigothic Kingdom, the city fell to the wali Al-Hurr ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Thaqafi. The tide of conquest rolled on into Gaul until it broke at the battles of Toulouse in 721 and Tours in 732, and Barcelona settled into life as part of the Upper March of al-Andalus, the northern frontier of Muslim Iberia. For roughly eighty years it looked south to Cordoba rather than north to the Franks. That orientation was about to be reversed by force.

## The Franks Push South

From 759 onward the Frankish kingdom drove steadily toward the Pyrenees. Pepin the Short captured Narbonne and brought the border to the mountains, though the Frankish advance was not all triumph: Charlemagne was forced to retreat from Zaragoza and suffered his famous disaster at Roncevaux, ambushed by Basque forces avenging his destruction of Pamplona's walls. The opening came in 785, when the people of Girona threw open their gates to the Franks, clearing the road toward Barcelona. The timing was perfect, for the Emirate of Cordoba had fallen into crisis. The young emir Al-Hakam I, on the throne since 796, was fighting rebellious uncles and a muwallad warlord named Bahlul Ibn Marzuq, who had seized Zaragoza and then Huesca. Coordinating the Frankish effort was William of Gellone, who gathered ambassadors, including envoys of Alfonso II of Asturias, at an assembly in Toulouse.

## Three Armies and a Wall

In August 800 a formidable army mustered under Louis the Pious, whom Charlemagne had made King of Aquitaine as a boy. Drawn from Aquitaine, Gascony, Burgundy, and Septimania and equipped with siege engines, the force split into three corps. Rostaing, count of Girona, led the siege itself at the foot of the walls. William of Gellone and Adhemar of Narbonne took up a blocking position between Lleida and Zaragoza to intercept any relief marching up from Cordoba. Louis himself held the third corps back to guard the Roussillon valley. When Rostaing's troops closed on Barcelona in October 800, the city's wali, Sa'dun al Ruayni, judged that the siege would be long and slipped out to beg help from Cordoba. He never reached it. The Franks captured him and sent him to Charlemagne's court at Aachen, where he was condemned to exile, and a commander named Harun took charge of the defense in his place.

## A City Worn Down

The siege settled into a war of attrition through the winter of 800 to 801. William of Gellone and Adhemar tightened the noose by ravaging Lleida and Huesca, and far to the west a revolt at Pamplona against Muslim rule drew off attention as a useful diversion. No relief force ever broke through. Louis the Pious came south for the final push, reaching Barcelona in February 801. By then the defenders were spent, worn down by hunger, deprivation, and constant assault. On 4 April, Harun accepted terms and surrendered, and the inhabitants opened the gates to the Carolingian army. The long ordeal ended not with a massacre but with a procession, Louis entering in solemn thanksgiving as clergy chanted through the streets.

## The Birth of a County

The conquerors moved quickly to organize their prize. Barcelona became the capital of the new County of Barcelona, folded into the belt of frontier territories the Franks called the Hispanic Marches, its authority shared between a count and a bishop. Bera, a son of William of Gellone, was installed as the first Count of Barcelona. Yet the victory was fragile. The conquered land had exposed flanks and a vulnerable border facing the Muslim strongholds around Zaragoza and Lleida, so the frontier along the Llobregat valley was reinforced, and Carolingian attempts to push farther, against Tortosa and repeatedly against Huesca in 807 and 812, failed. Still, the essential thing had been done. A durable Christian county now held the coast, and from that small, embattled beginning the future of Catalonia would slowly unfold.

## Flight Context

The siege centered on the old walled city of Barcelona, near 41.402N, 2.167E, on the coastal plain between the Collserola hills and the Mediterranean. From 3,000 to 5,000 feet the compact Ciutat Vella marks the site of the Roman and early medieval walls the Carolingians besieged, with the Llobregat river, the reinforced Frankish frontier, running to the southwest past the airport. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) sits about 12 km down the coast in that direction, Sabadell (LELL) lies inland to the north beyond Collserola, and Girona (LEGE), whose people opened the road south in 785, is to the northeast. Clear coastal weather reveals the corridor between mountains and sea along which every army approached the city.
