# Temple of Augustus, Barcelona

Four towering Roman columns, hidden inside a courtyard on a narrow lane in the Gothic Quarter, are all that remains of the temple that crowned Barcelona when it was the Roman colony of Barcino.

**Location:** 41.3834, 2.1772

**Categories:** Roman archaeology, Ancient history, Barcelona, Historic sites, Museums, Catalonia

## Introduction

Step off a narrow medieval lane in the Gothic Quarter, through a doorway that gives no warning, and you find yourself standing beneath ancient Rome. Four fluted columns rise in a small courtyard at Carrer del Paradis number 10, so tall they seem to strain against the surrounding walls. They are the last of the Temple of Augustus, the monument that once crowned Taber Hill at the very heart of Barcino, the Roman colony that became Barcelona. Nearly two thousand years of city have grown up and over the temple, burying it and then forgetting it. Yet here, tucked into a courtyard most visitors walk past, its columns still hold up a piece of the imperial sky.

## Rome at the Top of the Hill

When Rome founded the colony of Barcino, it placed its heart on Taber Hill, the natural high point of the settlement, and there it raised its principal temple. By tradition the building was dedicated to the emperor Augustus, though the dedication has never actually been proven and remains an educated guess. It was a grand structure by the standards of a provincial colony: a peripteral temple, ringed on all sides by columns, measuring some 35 by 17.5 meters and standing on a podium a third the height of the columns themselves. To a Roman colonist climbing the hill, it would have been the unmistakable center of civic and religious life, the place where the power of Rome and the person of the emperor were made visible in stone. Around it spread the forum, the beating administrative heart of Barcino, so that the temple did not merely overlook the colony but anchored it, the fixed point from which the grid of streets and the rhythm of public life radiated outward.

## Lost, Then Found in a Building Site

Empires end, and their temples end with them. If the building was still in use by the fourth century AD, it would have been shut down during the persecution of pagans under the Christian emperors, its gods declared illegal. At some point the temple was demolished outright, and the memory of it faded so completely that the growing medieval city simply built over its remains. The columns came back to light only in the late nineteenth century, when three of them turned up on the construction site of the Centre Excursionista de Catalunya, embedded in a courtyard where no one had expected them. A fourth column, which had been displayed separately at the Placa del Rei, was later brought back and reunited with the others to form the group visible today.

## The Scholars Who Read the Ruins

Recovering the temple was the work of curious minds across a full century. As early as 1830, the architect Antoni Celles produced a complete description and a map of the temple during excavations financed by the Barcelona Chamber of Commerce, an unusually careful piece of early archaeology. Decades later the architect and historian Josep Puig i Cadafalch confirmed Celles's hypothesis that the temple had honored Augustus, and worked out its form in detail: a peripteral plan with eleven columns along each flank, counting the corners, and six across both the front and the rear. Two men, a hundred years apart, each read the same scattered stones and pieced together the shape of a building that had vanished before either was born.

## Hidden in Plain Sight

The temple's fate is a lesson in how cities remember and forget. Barcelona did not preserve its Roman heart in a grand open ruin, the way some cities enshrine their origins. Instead the columns survived by accident, swallowed into the fabric of the Gothic Quarter and rediscovered by chance, and today they stand in a courtyard so unassuming that you can pass its entrance without a glance. Recognized as a Cultural Asset of National Interest in 1931 and now part of the Barcelona City History Museum, the site rewards anyone who steps inside. There is a particular thrill in turning off a shadowed medieval street and finding, without ceremony, the founding stones of the entire city rising silently overhead.

## Flight Context

The Temple of Augustus stands at 41.383N, 2.177E, at the highest point of the Gothic Quarter in Barcelona's Ciutat Vella, a short distance inland from the harbor. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet the site itself is invisible within the dense medieval roofscape, so navigate by the nearby cathedral, the open space of Placa Sant Jaume, and La Rambla's green arc to the west. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies about 12 km down the coast to the southwest, Sabadell (LELL) sits inland to the northwest, and Girona (LEGE) is to the northeast. In clear coastal weather the compact core of the old Roman city is easy to trace as the tightest knot of streets between the port and the modern Eixample grid.
