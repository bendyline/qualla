# Siege of Barcelona (1713-1714)

The thirteen-month siege that ended the War of the Spanish Succession also ended Catalonia's self-rule, and the date of its fall, the eleventh of September, is now the National Day of Catalonia.

**Location:** 41.4019, 2.1667

**Categories:** Military history, Sieges, Barcelona, Catalonia, 18th century, War of the Spanish Succession

## Introduction

Most nations mark their national day with a victory or a birth. Catalonia marks a defeat. Every eleventh of September, Catalans gather to remember the day in 1714 when Barcelona fell after a thirteen-month siege, when the last stronghold of a losing cause was overrun and the Principality of Catalonia lost the institutions that had governed it for centuries. It is a strange thing to build an identity around a surrender. But the men who held Barcelona to the end had chosen to keep fighting a war that everyone else had already abandoned, and it is that choice, more than the loss itself, that the Diada commemorates.

## A War for an Empty Throne

The trouble began with a death. When the childless Charles II of Spain died in 1700, he left his crown to Philip V of the French House of Bourbon, and the great powers went to war over it. The Grand Alliance of Austria, England, and the Dutch Republic backed a rival Habsburg claimant, Archduke Charles, launching the War of the Spanish Succession. Catalonia first accepted Philip V after long negotiations with the Catalan Courts, but the harsh measures of the viceroy Francisco de Velasco and the king's authoritarian decisions, some of them contrary to Catalan law, pushed the principality to switch sides. In 1705 the Archduke's fleet anchored in Barcelona's port, seized the fortress of Montjuic, and bombarded the city into accepting Charles as its monarch.

## Left to Fight Alone

Then the ground shifted beneath Catalonia's feet. By 1713 the exhausted great powers wanted peace. Britain and the Dutch Republic signed the Treaty of Utrecht that April, and the Allied armies simply withdrew, leaving Catalonia and Mallorca as the last realms in Spain still fighting for the Habsburg cause. The Catalans could have surrendered on reasonable terms. Instead, in July 1713, the Junta de Bracos, a parliamentary assembly meeting without a king, voted to remain in the war unilaterally, determined to defend Catalan constitutions, freedoms, and lives against Bourbon punishment. They raised the Army of Catalonia and appealed in vain to Britain and Austria for help. By 25 July the city was ringed by Bourbon forces under the Duke of Popoli, though a shortage of artillery kept his early attacks from breaking through.

## Guns Turned on the City

The stalemate ended when reinforcements came. In the spring of 1714 a fresh army of twenty thousand men arrived under the command of the Duke of Berwick, and with them came French high-caliber cannon. The bombardment that followed was deliberate and pitiless in its logic. Its target was not the walls or the defenders but the houses and the people behind them, an effort to break the city's will by breaking its homes. Through the summer the shells fell on the crowded streets of Barcelona while the outnumbered defenders held their positions and sent troops behind enemy lines in desperate attempts to lift the siege. A first great assault on 30 August failed. The defenders knew a second would come.

## The Eleventh of September

It came before dawn on 11 September 1714. The assault opened at half past four in the morning, and this time the wall gave way in several places. Rafael Casanova, the Conseller en cap and chief commander of the Coronela, the city's urban militia, was wounded carrying the banner into the fighting. In the streets the defense was led by Antoni de Villarroel, general commander of the Army of Catalonia, who was also wounded before the day was done. With the city breaking around them, the Catalan leaders chose to surrender and open negotiations. The talks stretched into the next day, because Philip V wanted to punish the population without any agreement at all, but Berwick, fearing a long and costly street fight, formally agreed to spare the lives of the people of Barcelona.

## A Defeat Made Into Memory

The victors were thorough. Catalonia's independent institutions and laws were abolished and replaced with Castilian ones, and Spain completed its long transformation from a patchwork of realms into a single centralized kingdom ruled from Madrid. In the Ribera quarter, whole streets of houses were demolished to build a citadel; those buried remains lie today beneath the old Mercat del Born, excavated and preserved. The fallen defenders were laid in a cemetery that is now the Fossar de les Moreres, a small square where Catalans still gather each eleventh of September. Out of that ruin grew something the conquerors never intended: a date, a memory, and a national identity built not on triumph but on the stubborn decision to stand and fight when standing was hopeless.

## Flight Context

The siege engulfed the walled city of Barcelona, centered near 41.402N, 2.167E, on the plain between the Collserola hills and the sea. From 3,000 to 5,000 feet the dense Ciutat Vella marks the old fortified core the Bourbons bombarded, with the fortress hill of Montjuic rising to the southwest and the Ribera district, site of the demolished streets and the Fossar de les Moreres, sitting toward the waterfront. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies about 12 km down the coast to the southwest, Sabadell (LELL) is inland to the northwest beyond Collserola, and Girona (LEGE) lies to the northeast. Clear coastal light reveals the natural amphitheater of hills and shoreline that made the city both defensible and, in the end, encircled.
