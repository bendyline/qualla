# Montjuïc

The broad hill over Barcelona's harbour where the city was born, quarried, fortified, executed upon, and finally crowned with Olympic glory.

**Location:** 41.3642, 2.1581

**Categories:** landmarks, history, barcelona, parks and gardens, culture, military history

## Introduction

Barcelona was, in a sense, cut out of this hill. During the Iberian era and especially under Rome, Montjuïc became the city's main quarry, and so much stone was hauled away to raise the streets below that the mountain's very shape was altered. Everything Barcelona later became, its walls and palaces and churches, began as rock carried down these slopes. That is the quiet paradox of Montjuïc: a broad, shallow hill that gave birth to a metropolis and has spent two thousand years being remade by it in return.

## The Hill the City Was Cut From

Montjuïc rises to 177.72 metres southwest of the old centre, a flat-topped mass wedged strategically between the Mediterranean and the Llobregat River, the two channels that made settlement here worth fighting for. Its eastern face drops almost sheer, a natural cliff commanding the harbour immediately below, which is precisely why armies and archaeologists have both found so much to study on its summit. Naturally wooded, the slopes were long used by the people of the neighbouring Ciutat Vella to grow food and graze animals, until the 1890s cleared space for parkland. Stand at the edge today and the whole port unfolds beneath you, container cranes where galleys once anchored.

## A Mountain of Names

The name itself is contested. In medieval Latin and Catalan, Montjuïc means "Jewish Mountain," and the reason lies underfoot: a medieval Jewish cemetery, considered the largest in Europe of its time, whose remains have been found on these slopes. Some sources instead trace the word to the Latin Mons Jovicus, the hill of Jove. In 2007 the Catalan government formally declared Montjuïc an area of Cultural Asset of National Interest, precisely because of that Jewish burial ground, a belated act of recognition for a community whose presence the hill's very name preserves. Few places carry their history so plainly in a single word.

## The Castle and Its Shadows

The fortress crowning Montjuïc dates largely from the 17th century, with 18th-century additions, and its record is grim. In 1842 the garrison, loyal to Madrid, turned its guns on the city and shelled it. For generations the castle served as a prison, often for political prisoners, and as a place of execution. The 1897 Montjuïc trial sent anarchist supporters to their deaths under torture, and for a time the name became a byword for cruelty. During the Spanish Civil War, both Nationalists and Republicans were shot here, each side using the walls when it held them. In 1940 Lluís Companys, the exiled president of the Generalitat de Catalunya, was handed to Franco's government by the Nazis and executed at Montjuïc. Many of the dead lie in unmarked graves nearby.

## Measuring the World

One of the hill's stranger honours belongs to science. In June 1792, the French astronomers Jean Baptiste Joseph Delambre and Pierre Méchain set out to measure the meridian arc from Dunkirk to Barcelona, two cities on nearly the same longitude, in order to define a new universal unit of length. The Montjuïc fortress was chosen as the southern reference point. France and Spain were technically at war, so only after protracted negotiation did Méchain take his measurements here on 16 March 1794. From that survey the metre was born, defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from pole to equator. The definitive platinum bar was presented to the French assembly on 22 June 1799, and it began, in part, on this Catalan hilltop.

## Exposition, Olympics, and Song

The modern Montjuïc was shaped by two great spectacles. The 1929 International Exposition brought the grand Palau Nacional, the Estadi Olímpic, the ornate Magic Fountain, the Poble Espanyol village of Spanish styles, and Mies van der Rohe's spare German pavilion, demolished in 1930 and faithfully rebuilt in 1986. The stadium was meant to host an anti-fascist alternative Olympics in 1936; civil war cancelled it. Vindication came in 1992, when the refurbished Estadi Olímpic Lluís Companys hosted the Summer Games, ringed by the Anella Olímpica, the Palau Sant Jordi arena, and Santiago Calatrava's soaring communications tower. Today the Palau Nacional holds the Museu Nacional d'Art de Catalunya, the Fundació Joan Miró draws crowds nearby, and cable cars and funiculars carry visitors up a hill that has been, by turns, quarry, graveyard, prison, and playground.

## Flight Context

Montjuïc sits at 41.364°N, 2.158°E, immediately southwest of central Barcelona with its cliff face over the harbour. View from 2,500-4,000 ft: the wooded, flat-topped hill is crowned by the Palau Nacional's domes, the Olympic stadium bowl, the white shell of Palau Sant Jordi, and Calatrava's white telecommunications needle, all easy landmarks. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) is about 6 nm southwest down the coast; Sabadell (LELL) lies inland to the north; Girona (LEGE) roughly 55 nm northeast. Coastal haze can soften the view; clear northerly days give the sharpest sightlines from over the sea.
