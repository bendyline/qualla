# Plaza de toros de las Arenas

A grand 1900 Barcelona bullring whose brick shell was jacked into the air and rebuilt as a domed shopping complex after Catalonia turned against the bulls.

**Location:** 41.3763, 2.1493

**Categories:** architecture, barcelona, history, landmarks, culture, adaptive reuse

## Introduction

In the mid-2000s, Barcelonans gathered at Plaça d'Espanya to watch something impossible: their old bullring rising into the air. The entire circular brick facade, all of it dating to 1900, was lifted roughly four metres off the ground on some four hundred hydraulic jacks, held aloft while the arena inside was gutted and the earth beneath it dug out. It was a fitting spectacle for a building at a crossroads. The bulls were already gone, Catalonia had turned against the sport, and Las Arenas was being reborn, quite literally, on new foundations.

## The Bull's Last Afternoon

The arena opened on 29 June 1900, a handsome pile of red brick in the neo-Mudéjar style designed by Augusto Font i Carreras, the architect who also completed the facade of Barcelona's cathedral. At first it staged boxing and free fights rather than bullfighting. That changed in 1927, when the entrepreneur Pedro Balañá bought the building and remodelled it into a proper bullring. For half a century the corridas ran, through the Spanish Civil War, when the structure was occupied by anti-fascist militias, and through the Franco years, when 1960s tourism from across the Atlantic and Europe turned the spectacle into a draw. The last bull fell here on 19 June 1977.

## Not Just Bulls

Las Arenas was never only an arena for killing. Its great oval doubled as one of the city's largest stages, and some memorable nights had nothing to do with the corrida. In 1977, the guitarists Carlos Santana and Paco de Lucía played within its walls. On 30 June 1980, Bob Marley and the Wailers performed here on the European leg of their Uprising Tour, filling the old bullring with reggae less than a decade before its bricks would fall silent. For a building designed around a single brutal ritual, its afterlife as a music venue hinted at what it might yet become.

## A Region Turns Away

Catalonia's relationship with bullfighting had long been uneasy, and in the arena's final decades that unease hardened into law. The first anti-bullfighting campaign in Catalonia was launched in 1989, and support kept building through the 2000s until, in 2010, the Catalan parliament voted to make bullfighting illegal, a ban that took effect in 2012. The story did not end cleanly; Spain's constitutional court overturned the Catalan ban in 2016, reopening a debate that touches on culture, cruelty, and competing ideas of national identity. But Las Arenas itself had already moved on, its fighting days finished long before the legal arguments were settled.

## Lifted Into the Air

The rescue of the building fell to an unlikely team: the British architect Richard Rogers, famous for the Pompidou Centre, working with the local firm Alonso y Balaguer, with Dragados handling construction. Their idea was radical in its respect for the past. Rather than demolish the beloved 1900 facade, they preserved it entirely, lifting the circular brick wall on hydraulic jacks while a new structure and a sunken car park were built beneath. Over the top they set a vast saucer of a dome, some 76 metres across and 27 metres high, braced back to the original walls. The project was dogged by controversy and delays, work halting more than once, but it finally opened in March 2011.

## The Ring With a View

Today the old bullring lives as Arenas de Barcelona, a shopping and entertainment complex layered with stores, restaurants, and cinemas beneath its historic shell. Its finest feature costs nothing at all. A public walkway rings the top of the building, a mirador offering a 360-degree sweep across the city: out over the fountains and twin brick towers of Plaça d'Espanya, up the ceremonial axis toward the Palau Nacional and the wooded heights of Montjuïc, and away to the Mediterranean beyond. Where crowds once looked inward at the sand, they now look outward at Barcelona itself, a fitting reversal for a building that turned its whole meaning inside out. It is one of the few places in the city where the past was not demolished to clear room for the present, but hoisted bodily above it and given something new to hold.

## Flight Context

Las Arenas stands at 41.376°N, 2.149°E on the northwest side of Plaça d'Espanya, at the foot of Montjuïc. View from 2,500-4,000 ft: look for the round, red-brick drum capped by its broad pale dome, directly across the plaza from the twin Venetian-style campaniles and in line with the fountain axis climbing to the domed Palau Nacional on Montjuïc. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies about 6 nm southwest along the coast; Sabadell (LELL) is inland to the north; Girona (LEGE) roughly 55 nm northeast. The Plaça d'Espanya and Montjuïc cluster is easy to identify in clear coastal light.
