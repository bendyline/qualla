# La Mercè

Each September Barcelona hands its streets to giants, fire-breathing devils, human towers, and two million people for La Mercè, a festival born from a vow made during a plague of locusts.

**Location:** 41.3825, 2.1769

**Categories:** Culture, Festivals, History, Catalonia, Barcelona

## Introduction

A child in a helmet scrambles up the backs and shoulders of hundreds of strangers, climbing a swaying tower of human beings toward a point eight or nine bodies above the pavement. Below, a dense ring of people braces inward to absorb the load, while a band plays the melody that tells the climber exactly when to move. This is a castell, a Catalan human tower, and it is one of the sights that fills Barcelona every September during La Mercè, the city's great annual festival. For a few days the streets belong to giants, devils, fireworks, and the everyday marvel of people standing on one another's shoulders.

## A Saint and a Plague of Locusts

The festival honours the Mare de Deu de la Merce, Our Lady of Mercy, patron saint of the archdiocese of Barcelona and, together with Saint Eulalia, co-patroness of the city. In Catalan the word merce carries meanings of service, help, compassion, and mercy. The origins reach back to the Middle Ages, but the decisive moment came in 1687, when a plague of locusts descended on Barcelona. The Consell de Cent, the council that then governed the city, voted to appeal to the Virgin for deliverance, and when the pestilence lifted they named her patroness. Rome formally recognized the title in 1868, and in 1871 the local government organized the first official program of festivities.

## Giants and the Sardana

Much of what defines La Merce today arrived in 1902, when the parades first featured the towering papier-mache figures known as gigantes y cabezudos and the Sardana, the circle dance spreading out of the Emporda region across Catalonia. The most traditional events grow from the popular culture of Catalonia, and the great street processions descend directly from the Corpus Christi parades of centuries past. The scale now is enormous. Close to two million people attend across the week, the metro runs all night to carry them, and each year a Guest City, Vienna in 2013 among them, is invited to share its own artists and traditions with the crowds.

## Fire Over Montjuïc

Then there is the fire. The correfoc, literally the fire-run, sends costumed devils and dragons through the streets trailing showers of sparks, and rather than scatter, the boldest revelers dance directly beneath them. The festival's grand finale is the pyro-musical staged at the foot of the Montjuic hill, a choreographed display in which fireworks, jets of water, and music are synchronized into a single performance. Around these centerpieces cluster newer traditions: a Catalan Wine Fair, a ten-kilometre road race, street theatre, circus, and bands filling squares late into the night. La Merce is less a single event than a city-wide overflow of everything Barcelona likes to celebrate.

## The Human Towers

Of all the festival's spectacles, the castells may be the most purely Catalan. Human towers of this kind emerged at the end of the eighteenth century in Valls, in the province of Tarragona, where rival groups called colles began competing to raise the tallest and most difficult structures. The tradition has never lost that spirit of friendly rivalry. Building a castell demands strength, nerve, and near-total trust: the base packs together to hold the weight, and the lightest, youngest climbers ascend last to crown the tower. When it holds, the crowd roars. When it wobbles and comes down, everyone simply gathers to try again, which is a fair description of the festival as a whole.

## The End of Summer

Every Catalan town holds a festa major in honour of its patron saint, and La Merce is Barcelona's, staged at the scale only a great city can manage. Its timing gives it a second meaning. The feast day falls on 24 September, so the celebration lands at the hinge between summer and autumn, and for Barcelonans it marks the season turning, the beaches emptying, the school year settling in. The events are free and public, spilling across squares, parks, and waterfront, gathering close to two million people over the week. For a few days a huge, modern, cosmopolitan city sets aside its routines to do something very old: parade its giants, raise its towers, light its fires, and remember that it is, before anything else, a community.

## Flight Context

La Merce fills central Barcelona, with its ceremonial heart near the Basilica de la Merce in the Gothic Quarter around 41.383 N, 2.177 E, and its firework climax staged at the foot of Montjuic to the southwest. From the air the old city, the Rambla, and the green bulk of Montjuic with its castle and fountains mark the festival's two poles. A pass at 1,500 to 2,500 feet frames the medieval core against Port Vell and the harbour. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies about 6 nm southwest, Sabadell (LELL) roughly 11 nm northwest, and Girona (LEGE) about 46 nm northeast. Late-September evenings are often clear and mild; note that firework displays impose temporary airspace restrictions over the city during the festival.
