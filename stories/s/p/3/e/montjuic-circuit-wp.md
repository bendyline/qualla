# Montjuïc circuit

A breathtaking street circuit threaded through Barcelona's parkland, remembered for beauty, danger, and the only World Championship points ever scored by a woman.

**Location:** 41.3661, 2.1519

**Categories:** motorsport, history, sports venues, barcelona, formula one, landmarks

## Introduction

Emerson Fittipaldi climbed out of his car before the 1975 Spanish Grand Prix even began. The two-time world champion had walked the guardrails that morning, found bolts missing and barriers poorly secured, and decided no result was worth the risk. He completed a single slow protest lap and withdrew. What happened a few hours later on the slopes of Montjuïc would prove him tragically right, and would close the gates on one of the most beautiful and most treacherous circuits Formula One ever raced.

## A Track Laid Through a Park

The Montjuïc circuit was never purpose-built. Its racing line ran along ordinary public roads winding through wooded parkland on the hill above Barcelona's harbour, past staircases, ornamental gardens, and stone balustrades. Drivers described a course of split personality: one half slow and technical, the other very fast, run anticlockwise so that the rhythm reversed everything muscle memory expected. Setting a car up for both halves at once was nearly impossible, and a compromise on one section meant suffering on the other. The scenery was extraordinary, cars flickering between trees with the Mediterranean glinting below. So was the exposure. Where a modern circuit offers runoff and gravel traps, Montjuïc offered kerbs, lampposts, and crowds pressed close behind fragile Armco.

## From Copa Catalunya to the Penya Rhin

Catalonia had been racing for generations before Formula One arrived. International motorsport ran at the Circuit Baix Penedès with the Copa Catalunya as early as 1908, and in 1923 the first Grand Prix of Spain was held at the purpose-built Sitges Terramar oval nearby. In 1932 a street race started in Montjuïc Park itself, and the following year the eastern loop of that course became the Montjuïc Circuit proper, home to the Penya Rhin Grand Prix. The venue's Formula One era came later. In 1968 Montjuïc was chosen to host the Spanish Grand Prix, taking it from the Jarama circuit outside Madrid, and the inaugural World Championship race there ran on 4 May 1969. Over the next six years the Grand Prix alternated between the two Spanish tracks.

## The Day the Barrier Gave Way

Many drivers arrived in 1975 already uneasy. The guardrails, they felt, had been thrown up carelessly, and Fittipaldi's pre-race withdrawal crystallized a fear that everyone shared. The race went ahead anyway. On lap 26, the Embassy Hill-Lola of Rolf Stommelen left the track at high speed and cleared the barrier. Four people were killed, not drivers but bystanders who had come to watch, a stark reminder of how little separated the spectators from the machines on a course stitched through a public hillside. Officials stopped the race before half distance and awarded half points, recording Jochen Mass as the winner. It was a hollow victory. Formula One never returned to Montjuïc, and the accident became a turning point in the sport's slow, overdue reckoning with safety.

## Half a Point, and a First

Amid the tragedy, the 1975 race produced a milestone that still stands. Italian driver Lella Lombardi finished sixth, and because only half points were awarded, she collected 0.5 of a point toward the World Championship. Small as the number was, it made history: Lombardi became the first and, to this day, the only woman to score points in a Formula One World Championship race. Her name is tied permanently to Montjuïc, a bright thread in a weekend otherwise defined by loss.

## Ghost Lines on Montjuïc

The hill kept racing for a while after Formula One left, hosting the 24 Hours of Montjuïc motorcycle endurance event from 1960 to 1982. Then the roads reverted fully to the park. The old circuit's ground was absorbed into the Anella Olímpica, the ring of venues built for the 1992 Summer Olympics, so that spectators now stroll past the Olympic stadium and Calatrava's soaring communications tower where cars once screamed by. In 2004 the city council decided to mark the layout of the vanished track on the modern streets. And in October 2007, for the circuit's 75th anniversary, engines briefly returned for the Martini Legends: Fittipaldi reappeared in his Lotus 72, and Catalan driver Marc Gené drove a contemporary Ferrari along the ghost lines of a course the sport had chosen never to run again.

## Flight Context

Centered near 41.366°N, 2.152°E on Montjuïc hill, southwest of central Barcelona and overlooking the harbour. Best viewed from 2,500-4,000 ft; the wooded hill is unmistakable, crowned by the Olympic stadium, the white Palau Sant Jordi dome, and Santiago Calatrava's needle-like communications tower. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies about 7 nm to the southwest along the coast; Sabadell (LELL) sits inland to the north, and Girona (LEGE) roughly 55 nm northeast. Clear Mediterranean light makes the hill and harbour easy to pick out from the west.
