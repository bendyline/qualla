# Buenaventura Durruti

The mechanic-turned-anarchist whose Barcelona funeral drew half a million mourners, and whose death on the Madrid front is still argued over today.

**Location:** 41.3540, 2.1502

**Categories:** Spanish anarchists, Spanish Civil War people, Burials at Montjuïc Cemetery, History of Barcelona, 1896 births, 1936 deaths

## Introduction

In late November 1936, half a million people filled the streets of Barcelona to bury a mechanic. They came to walk beside the coffin of Buenaventura Durruti as it moved toward the cemetery on Montjuïc, in one of the largest public gatherings the city had ever seen. He was a divisive man in life, a bank robber and revolutionary as well as a militia leader, but on that day he belonged to the crowd. The turnout was the last great demonstration of anarchist strength during the Spanish Civil War, a river of mourners marking the passing of a figure the movement had already begun to call a martyr.

## Half a Million Mourners

The scale of the funeral is hard to overstate. Durruti's body had been carried across the country from the Madrid front back to Barcelona, the city where he had helped defeat a military coup only months earlier, and the crowds that met the cortege choked the boulevards. Workers, militiamen, and ordinary families poured into the streets to accompany him to Montjuïc Cemetery. The historian Hugh Thomas would later see in that day the end of an era for Spanish anarchism. Whatever one made of Durruti's methods, the grief was genuine and enormous, an outpouring for a man who had come to embody a whole movement's hopes at the moment those hopes were beginning to fail.

## The Mechanic From León

He was born José Buenaventura Durruti Dumange on 14 July 1896, in the Santa Ana neighborhood of León, the second of eight children in a working family. His radicalization did not come from books but from what he saw. When he was six, in 1903, he watched police arrest his father during a tanners' strike; the strike dragged on for nine months before it collapsed, and the family, blacklisted for its part in it, was left destitute. Durruti trained as a mechanic and moved into the workforce at fourteen. Repression of the labor movement did the rest. Each strike broken and each activist jailed pushed him further from moderate socialism and toward the revolutionary anarchism that would define the rest of his life.

## Robber, Exile, Revolutionary

Durruti's path ran through violence, and it does no justice to soften it. In the 1920s he helped lead the anarchist group Los Solidarios, which robbed banks in raids the members called expropriations, using the money to fund their cause, and which carried out killings of figures they held responsible for crushing the workers. Forced into exile, he coordinated militant activity from France and took part in a string of robberies across Latin America. He returned to Spain in 1931 with the proclamation of the Republic and became one of the chief organizers of the insurrectionist wing of the anarchist union, the CNT. Uprising after uprising failed, and he was imprisoned and deported again and again, yet he never abandoned the fight.

## The Barricades of Barcelona

His decisive hour came in July 1936. When the Nationalist military rose against the Republic, Barcelona became a battleground, and Durruti was among the key figures who defeated the coup in the city's streets. In the revolution that followed, he organized and led the Durruti Column, one of the largest and most famous anarchist militias, and marched it to the Aragon front. There, alongside the fighting, he helped put the ideals of libertarian communism into practice across the territory his forces controlled, an attempt to build the stateless society he had spent his life demanding. For a brief window, in a corner of wartime Spain, the anarchist dream was not a slogan but a working experiment, and Durruti was its most recognizable face.

## A Death Still Argued Over

In November 1936, as Nationalist armies closed on Madrid, Durruti led his column to help defend the capital. On 19 November, fighting in the Casa de Campo park, he was struck by a gunshot. His men rushed him to their field hospital in the requisitioned Ritz Hotel, but the wound was mortal, and he died the following day at forty. How exactly he was shot has never been settled. Historians have variously blamed enemy fire, an accident with his own weapon, and the bullets of his own side, and the mystery has only deepened his legend. His grave on Montjuïc became a place of pilgrimage, and his name endured, carried forward by later anarchists who still looked to him as their symbol.

## Flight Context

Durruti rests in Montjuïc Cemetery on the southern flank of the hill, near 41.3540°N, 2.1502°E, above Barcelona's port. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet the cemetery terraces step down the seaward side of Montjuïc, distinct from the fortress and museums on the summit, with the harbor and shipping terminals just below. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies southwest along the coast, Sabadell (LELL) inland to the north, and Girona (LEGE) up the coast to the northeast. Look for the green headland of Montjuïc dropping to the container port; the cemetery occupies its ocean-facing slope, with the dense old city spreading to the northeast.
