# Casa Vicens

Gaudí's first major commission, a riot of marigold-tiled walls and palm-leaf ironwork in Gràcia, born from the wildflowers he found growing on the empty lot.

**Location:** 41.4035, 2.1506

**Categories:** Architecture, Antoni Gaudí buildings, Modernisme architecture in Barcelona, Museums in Barcelona, Gràcia, World Heritage Sites in Catalonia

## Introduction

Before Antoni Gaudí drew a single line, he walked the empty lot in the town of Gràcia and looked down. Yellow marigolds carpeted the ground, and a single exuberant fan palm spread its leaves across the plot. Most architects would have cleared them and started fresh. Gaudí took them as his brief. "When I went to take the measurements of the site," he recalled, "it was totally covered with some yellow flowers, which I used as an ornamental theme for the ceramic. I also found an exuberant palmetto palm, whose leaves fill the grid of the gate of the house." The result, built between 1883 and 1885, was his first major building, and the flowers that once grew here have bloomed in glazed tile ever since.

## A Summer House for a Broker

The commission came from Manuel Vicens i Montaner, a stock and currency broker who wanted a summer residence on land he had inherited from his mother. In 1878 he handed the job to a newly graduated architect with almost nothing built to his name. Gaudí was twenty-six, financing himself by drafting lampposts and shop fittings, and Casa Vicens would launch him. He took the work seriously to the point of obsession. According to those who watched, he supervised the construction from beneath a parasol, occasionally ordering workers to tear down anything he judged shoddy. At the time, Gràcia was not yet part of Barcelona at all. It was an independent town with its own council, a quiet cluster of farmhouses and second homes where the city's bourgeoisie escaped the summer heat. It would be swallowed into Barcelona only in 1897.

## The Orient in a Catalan Town

Casa Vicens belongs to what scholars call Gaudí's orientalist phase, a period when he drew freely on the art of the Near and Far East and on Spain's own Islamic heritage. He had pored over Owen Jones's meticulous studies of the Alhambra and his "Grammar of Ornament," absorbing the geometry of Mudéjar and Nasrid design. The walls climb in alternating bands of rough masonry and checkerboard tile, punctuated by the marigold blossoms of the site. Some of the wooden latticework echoes shitomi, a style of Japanese shutter Gaudí studied at an 1881 exhibition of Japanese architecture in Barcelona. Here the straight line still rules, years before the rippling curves of his maturity, yet the sheer density of invention already marks him as something other than a follower. The house caused a sensation, and not everyone approved.

## Where the Ceiling Grows Stalactites

Step inside and the restraint of the structure gives way to fantasy. The dining room is wrapped in stucco ivy on a gold ground, its beams hung with papier-mâché fruit, its doorframes painted by Francesc Torrescassana with two dozen birds, all shown in flight except the flamingos below. A covered porch once opened to the garden through oriental latticework, a fountain at its center throwing a thin sheet of water that caught the light like a rainbow. Most extraordinary is the smoking room, where the ceiling drips with muqarnas, the stalactite vaulting of Islamic architecture, molded in polychrome plaster into palm fronds and clusters of dates. Along the porch frieze Gaudí lettered snatches of Catalan folk song. "Sol, solet, vinam a veure," reads one, an invitation to the sun to come and see.

## Palm Leaves in Iron

The single most famous element stands at the entrance. Gaudí took the fan palm he had found growing on the lot and translated its fronds into a cast-iron gate, each leaf cast from a plaster mold that the sculptor Llorenç Matamala shaped and the smith Joan Oñós forged. When the property was later carved up and sold, sections of that palmetto fence scattered across the city. Some pieces ended up at the entrance to Park Güell, Gaudí's later masterpiece for his patron Eusebi Güell, closing a quiet circle between the architect's first great work and one of his most beloved. The garden that once held a brick waterfall as tall as the house is mostly gone now, sold off for apartment blocks in the twentieth century, its most theatrical features surviving only in photographs and reconstructions.

## Rescued and Reopened

For most of its life Casa Vicens stayed a private home, passing from the Vicens family to a Barcelona pediatrician who had made his fortune in Cuba, then down through his heirs. Spain declared it a Historic-Artistic Monument in 1969, and UNESCO added it to its World Heritage roster of Gaudí works in 2005. Yet the public could not set foot inside until recently. In 2014 the Andorran bank MoraBanc bought the house and financed a careful restoration, stripping away later additions that had blurred Gaudí's design and recovering the lost porch fountain. On November 16, 2017, Casa Vicens opened its doors as a museum, and visitors could finally stand where the marigolds once grew, surrounded by the tiled echo of a garden that vanished more than a century ago.

## Flight Context

Casa Vicens stands at 41.4035°N, 2.1506°E on Carrer de les Carolines in the Gràcia district, north of Barcelona's Eixample grid. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet the compact, three-storey house is hard to pick out amid dense rooftops, but its tiled corner pavilions and small cupola help; the leafy pattern of Gràcia's tight streets and squares is the better landmark. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies roughly 8 nautical miles to the southwest, Sabadell (LELL) about 10 nm north-northwest, and Girona (LEGE) some 55 nm northeast. Park Güell sits just uphill to the north and the Sagrada Família's spires rise to the southeast, both useful anchors on a clear day.
