# Els Quatre Gats

The café whose name is Catalan slang for "hardly anyone" became the beating heart of Barcelona's Modernisme and hosted a teenage Picasso's first solo show.

**Location:** 41.3858, 2.1737

**Categories:** Culture in Barcelona, Modernisme architecture in Barcelona, History, Ciutat Vella, Art Nouveau, Tourist attractions in Barcelona

## Introduction

The name is a joke at its own expense. Els Quatre Gats means "The Four Cats," a Catalan expression for hardly anyone at all, the phrase you use for a gathering so small and so odd it barely counts. That was the founders' wink at themselves: a handful of misfits, outsiders, bohemians. Yet those four cats and the crowd they drew would reshape Catalan art, and one skinny teenager who hung around the place would go on to become Pablo Picasso. When the café opened its doors on June 12, 1897, Barcelona had no idea it was watching a movement being born over coffee and piano music.

## Four Cats and a Parisian Dream

The idea belonged to Pere Romeu, who had worked at Le Chat Noir, the legendary black-cat cabaret of bohemian Paris, and wanted to bring its spirit home. He borrowed the cat, translated it into Catalan slang, and modeled his café on the French original. Romeu could not have done it alone. Three of the leading modernist artists of the day, Ramon Casas, Santiago Rusiñol, and Miquel Utrillo, put up the money and lent their names. Casas painted a canvas specifically for the walls, a portrait of himself and Romeu pedaling a tandem bicycle with the Barcelona skyline behind them. Its inscription reads, roughly, that to ride a bike you cannot sit with your back straight, a sly manifesto: to get anywhere new, you must break with the old posture.

## Food of the Spirit

The four men wanted more than a place to eat. As Rusiñol put it, the café would serve "food of the spirit," a room where artists could argue over their work and the day's news beneath the piano's steady murmur. It worked almost at once. Painters and sculptors, poets and architects filled the tables; the composers Isaac Albéniz and Enric Granados passed through, Gaudí among the architects, the sculptor Julio González among the makers. Performances, concerts, and exhibitions turned the café into a clearinghouse for every new idea in Barcelona. A magazine followed in 1899, also called Quatre Gats, and though it lasted only fifteen issues, its successors carried the café's voice onward and made it the printed platform of Catalan modernism.

## The Teenager in the Corner

In 1899, a seventeen-year-old named Pablo Picasso began turning up. He was unknown, unformed, and hungry for exactly the kind of company the café offered. The following February, newly eighteen, he mounted his first solo exhibition within its main room, an astonishing thing to be able to say of a bar. He also designed artwork for the establishment, and a Picasso image still marks the corner outside today. For a young artist finding his feet, Els Quatre Gats was less a nightspot than a school, a place where he could measure himself against the best painters in the city and be taken seriously before the world had heard his name.

## A Castle on the Corner

The café occupies the ground floor of Casa Martí, designed in 1896 by the architect Josep Puig i Cadafalch, and the building is half the show. Its facade evokes a medieval castle, more northern European Gothic than Catalan, yet crusted with the ornament of Catalan Art Nouveau: wrought iron by Manuel Ballarín, carved narrative capitals by the sculptor Eusebi Arnau, stained glass set into pointed arches. On the corner stands a figure of Saint Joseph by Josep Llimona, though the one visitors see is a replica; the original was destroyed during the Spanish Civil War. Even the furniture, paid for by Casas, leaned deliberately medieval, so that patrons drank in a fantasy of the past while inventing the future.

## Bankrupt but Immortal

Romeu was a generous host and a hopeless businessman. He let friends run up tabs at a discount or waved off the bill entirely, and in June 1903 the café simply ran out of money and closed. The idea proved harder to kill than the business. Its magazines lived on for years, keeping the flame while the room went dark. Only after the death of the dictator Francisco Franco and the return of democracy did the café come back, reopening to the public in 1978 as part of an effort to restore Catalan culture bruised by decades of repression; Casa Martí itself was returned to its former glory around 1991. Order a coffee there now and you sit where a movement, and a genius, once began.

## Flight Context

Els Quatre Gats sits at 41.3858°N, 2.1737°E on Carrer de Montsió in Barcelona's Barri Gòtic, deep in the medieval old town just east of La Rambla. At street level it is invisible from the air; from 1,500 to 2,500 feet, navigate instead by the dense dark maze of Ciutat Vella set against the ruled grid of the Eixample to the north, with Barcelona Cathedral's towers a short distance southeast. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies roughly 6 nautical miles southwest along the coast, Sabadell (LELL) about 11 nm inland to the northwest, and Girona (LEGE) some 55 nm to the northeast. Clear Mediterranean light gives the best read on the old-city rooftops.
