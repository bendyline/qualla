# Plaça de Sant Felip Neri

A quiet Gothic Quarter square whose shrapnel-scarred church walls record a 1938 bombing that killed 42 people, most of them children, and the lie later told to hide it.

**Location:** 41.3834, 2.1749

**Categories:** history, barcelona, spanish civil war, memorials, landmarks, social history

## Introduction

The walls of the church on Plaça de Sant Felip Neri are pitted and gouged, the stone chewed as if by some enormous hail. For decades visitors were told the scars came from bullets, from an execution carried out during Spain's convulsions. They were told wrong. The marks are shrapnel, and they record one of the cruelest mornings of the Spanish Civil War, when bombs fell on a hidden square where children had been sent to be safe. Few small plazas anywhere hold their grief so openly, or were so deliberately lied about afterward.

## A Hidden Square

Tucked into the Gothic Quarter of Ciutat Vella, a short walk from the Barcelona Cathedral, Plaça de Sant Felip Neri is the kind of place travellers stumble on by accident and remember for years. It is small and still, shaded and quiet, presided over by the baroque Church of Saint Philip Neri that gives it its name. An octagonal fountain plays at its centre, dedicated as a symbol of life. To one side stands the school of Sant Felip Neri, which still uses the square as its playground; to the other, a house of the Oratory. Renaissance and Baroque facades enclose the space, and the effect is of a room open to the sky.

## Stones That Travelled

Part of the square's harmony is a careful illusion. It was once home to the Palace of Neri, built in 1752, but much of what surrounds it arrived later and from elsewhere. In the mid-twentieth century, rather than demolish Renaissance buildings standing in the way of urban development in other parts of the city, planners dismantled them and moved them here stone by stone, reassembling them around the plaza. When the Hotel Neri was added, it too was faced in matching Gothic stone to preserve the mood. The square is, in a sense, a gathering of architectural refugees, rescued fragments given a second life in one sheltered corner.

## The 30th of January

In 1938, as Franco's forces laid siege to Barcelona, the war reached this quiet place. The church had been turned into a makeshift orphanage, and on 30 January a Nationalist bombing killed some thirty people, most of them children from the parish school, among them refugee children who had been evacuated from Madrid in the belief they would be safer here. As neighbours rushed in to pull survivors from the rubble, a second bomb struck the crowded square, killing twelve more. Forty-two people died in all, and it stands as the second deadliest bombing Barcelona suffered during the war. They were, overwhelmingly, the young, sent to a hiding place that could not hide them.

## Scars and a Lie

The evidence of that morning never left. The shrapnel scars remain visible across the church's stone, an unhealed record in plain sight. And here the story turns doubly cruel. Francoist authorities, unwilling to admit to bombing an orphanage, put about a myth that the pockmarks came from bullets, the traces of an anarchist execution of priests carried out against the wall. The invention served to smear their enemies and to bury the truth of what their own bombs had done. Only later was the record set straight, the walls reclaimed as testimony to the children who died rather than propaganda against the living.

## A Quiet Place Now

Time has softened the square without erasing it. The Museu del Calçat, a small footwear museum that once occupied a corner, closed in 2015 and its building sits unused. But the fountain still runs, the old stones still glow honey-coloured in the afternoon, and the school still turns the plaza into a playground, so that children's voices once more fill the space where children were killed. That overlap is the square's quiet power. It asks nothing of visitors but a pause, and it offers, in return, both the weight of what happened and the ordinary, stubborn continuation of life around it. Locals will tell you it is at once the most beautiful square in Barcelona and the saddest, and standing among the scarred stones, you understand how both can be true.

## Flight Context

Plaça de Sant Felip Neri lies at 41.383°N, 2.175°E in the Barri Gòtic, one block southwest of the Barcelona Cathedral. It is an intimate old-town square, invisible from altitude and best understood on the ground; from the air the useful reference is the cathedral's twin spires and octagonal lantern rising from the dense medieval grid between La Rambla and Via Laietana. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) is about 7 nm southwest along the coast; Sabadell (LELL) sits inland to the northwest; Girona (LEGE) roughly 50 nm northeast. The cathedral and Port Vell are the clearest visual anchors in clear coastal conditions.
