# Ictíneo II

Launched in Barcelona harbour in 1864, Narcís Monturiol's Ictíneo II was the first submarine that could make its own air and burn fuel underwater, decades before the rest of the world caught up.

**Location:** 41.3796, 2.1834

**Categories:** History, Science and technology, Submarines, Maritime history, Spanish inventions

## Introduction

Decades before anyone else managed it, a wooden submarine slipped beneath Barcelona harbour and did something no machine had ever done: it made its own air. Inside the hull of the Ictíneo II burned a chemical furnace that produced heat and breathable oxygen at the same time, letting the craft stay submerged without a snorkel, a battery, or a gasp. Its builder, Narcís Monturiol, was not a naval officer or a tycoon. He was a self-taught engineer, a printer, and an idealist who launched his vessel on 2 October 1864 with a conviction that would eventually ruin him. He wanted, of all things, to make the deep sea safe.

## A Utopian and His Subscription

The Ictíneo II grew out of an earlier, hand-cranked machine, the Ictíneo I, and Monturiol built the second in the hope of fixing the first. The Spanish Navy promised support and then quietly declined to supply any, so he turned to the public. He wrote a letter to the nation, appealing directly to ordinary citizens, and the popular subscription he launched raised 300,000 pesetas. With that money he founded a company, La Navegación Submarina, to carry the work forward. His ambition was oddly gentle for a submarine: he dreamed of harvesting coral from reefs, of a craft that served science and commerce rather than war. The idealism that funded the vessel would also, in the end, help sink it.

## Olive Wood and a Copper Skin

The craft was small and strange and beautiful. It measured 14 metres long, 2 wide, and 3 high, displaced 46 tonnes, and enclosed just 29 cubic metres of space. Monturiol built it from olive wood reinforced with oak, then wrapped the whole thing in a two-millimetre layer of copper. A narrow deck ran along the top, pierced by a hatch and three thick glazed portholes, and the helmsman steered from a conning tower using an endless screw gear. Four ballast tanks, symmetrical on either side, flooded to dive and were blown clear with pumped air to surface. To pitch the boat nose-down or nose-up, the crew slid a weight along a rail. An emergency mechanism could dump the ballast to shoot the vessel up in a hurry.

## The Breath in the Belly

The real breakthrough sat below deck. Monturiol devised a chemical reaction of zinc, manganese dioxide, and potassium chlorate that heated a boiler while releasing oxygen the crew could breathe. He bought a six-cylinder steam engine and split it in two: one half ran off a coal boiler on the surface, the other off his chemical furnace when submerged. It was air-independent propulsion, a phrase that would not become standard for generations. On 20 May 1865 the Ictíneo II made its maiden voyage under human power and dove to 27.5 metres. Reading of the American Civil War and its crude submarines, including the doomed CSS Hunley, the increasingly desperate Monturiol even wrote to the US Secretary of the Navy. By the time a reply came, the war was over.

## Too Early to Win

History is unkind to inventors who arrive before their moment, and Monturiol arrived very early indeed. The Ictíneo II was the first combustion-powered submarine that did not need to surface for air, and the first machine to solve the fundamental problems of powered underwater travel. Yet air-independent propulsion would not become routine in submarines for roughly a century, when navies finally caught up with the idea a Catalan idealist had built by hand. His contemporaries admired the craft and then let it slip away, unable or unwilling to fund a vision so far ahead of the shipyards around it. The tragedy of the Ictíneo II is not that it failed. It is that it worked, and the world was not ready to keep it.

## Sold for Scrap

Genius did not pay the bills. On 23 December, La Navegación Submarina went bankrupt, its funds entirely spent. Monturiol had poured in 100,000 duros, enough to buy several frigates or 160 kilograms of gold, and no new investors would come. His chief creditor called in the debt, and Monturiol was forced to surrender the submarine, his only remaining asset. It passed to a businessman who, handed a tax bill for owning a ship, chose to dismantle the vessel and sell the pieces. The surface engine went to a textile factory. The portholes, ground to see the depths of the Mediterranean, ended their days as bathroom windows. Monturiol died largely forgotten, but Barcelona remembered later. A full-scale replica now rests at the Port of Barcelona, and the dream of a Catalan submarine surfaced again in the crewed submersible Ictineu 3.

## Flight Context

The Ictíneo II replica sits beside the water at 41.380 N, 2.183 E, at the old harbour of Barcelona near the Rambla de Mar and the Maremagnum complex, with the Columbus Monument and the wide sweep of Port Vell as obvious markers from above. A coastal pass at 1,500 to 2,500 feet lines up the harbour, the marina, and the cargo terminals stretching south. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) is about 6 nm southwest down the shoreline, Sabadell (LELL) roughly 12 nm inland to the northwest, and Girona (LEGE) about 47 nm to the northeast. Sea breezes build haze by afternoon; the crispest harbour views come early on clear days.
