# Fabra Observatory

From a domed Modernista observatory on the slopes of Tibidabo, Josep Comas i Solà found the first evidence that a moon could have an atmosphere.

**Location:** 41.4184, 2.1240

**Categories:** Astronomy, Astronomical observatories in Catalonia, Science, Architecture of Barcelona, Sarrià-Sant Gervasi, Tourist attractions in Barcelona

## Introduction

In 1908, an astronomer peering through a great brass telescope on a hillside above Barcelona noticed something no one had ever documented: the edges of Titan, a distant moon of Saturn, were dimmer than its center, as if wrapped in haze. Josep Comas i Solà had found the first evidence that a moon could possess an atmosphere of its own. He made the observation from the Fabra Observatory, a domed temple to science perched on the wooded slopes of Tibidabo, and more than a century later the same instrument still turns toward the same sky.

## The Dome on the Mountain

The observatory stands at 415 meters on the flank of the Collserola range, high enough to lift its telescopes above much of the city's haze and glow. Its home is no plain scientific shed. Designed by the architect Josep Domènech i Estapà and completed in 1904, the building wears the ornamented, Modernista style then sweeping Barcelona, its octagonal dome crowning a facade of classical flourishes. From the plain below, the white dome reads as a landmark against the dark green of the mountain, a reminder that in this city even a place built for cold measurement was made to be beautiful.

## A Marquis, a King, and a Gift

The observatory owes its existence to a benefactor, Camil Fabra i Fontanills, the Marquis of Alella, whose generosity funded its construction and gave it his name. When it opened on April 7, 1904, the ceremony drew King Alfonso XIII himself to the mountaintop, a mark of how much the young institution mattered to the science and pride of Catalonia. It was placed under the Royal Academy of Science and Arts of Barcelona, which still oversees it. What began as one wealthy man's gift became a permanent public instrument, and today the Fabra ranks among the oldest observatories anywhere in the world that remain in continuous operation.

## The Great Glass Eye

At the heart of the observatory sits a double refractor built in 1904 by the Paris workshop of Mailhat, two long tubes mounted as one. The lower, visual tube gathers light through a 38-centimeter lens down a six-meter length, patient and precise for an astronomer's eye at the eyepiece. Its twin, tuned for photography, shares the same aperture but a shorter four-meter focus, catching starlight on a glass plate. More than a century of use has not retired it. The instrument that first revealed Titan's veil of air still functions, one of the reasons the Fabra is treasured as a working monument to the dawn of modern astronomy.

## The Man Who Watched the Planets

Josep Comas i Solà, the observatory's first director, was the sort of patient watcher the science demanded. His observation of the haze around Titan was the first time anyone had reported evidence of an atmosphere on a moon anywhere in the solar system, a claim that later spacecraft would spectacularly confirm. Beyond that, he discovered a comet that now bears his name, 32P/Comas Solà, and tracked down a string of minor planets drifting through the solar system, eleven of them over the course of his career. He belonged to a generation that mapped the sky by eye and photographic plate, night after night, comparing images to tease faint moving points out of a field of fixed stars. His work gave the Fabra an international reputation and made a Barcelona hillside, for a time, one of the places where the boundaries of the known solar system were being pushed outward.

## Three Sciences, Still Watching

The Fabra was never only about the stars. From its earliest days it has pursued three sciences under one roof: astronomy, meteorology, and seismology. Its weather records form one of the longest continuous series in Europe, a day-by-day account of the Mediterranean climate stretching back generations, and its seismographs remain part of the region's earthquake-monitoring network. There is something fitting in that breadth. The same building that reached out to a moon of Saturn also keeps its instruments trained on the ground beneath Barcelona and the air above it, still measuring the world in every direction after more than a hundred years.

## Flight Context

The Fabra Observatory sits at 41.4184°N, 2.1240°E on the slopes of Tibidabo in the Collserola hills, at 415 meters above sea level on the northwest edge of Barcelona. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet its white dome and Modernista roofline stand out against the forested mountainside above the city; the nearby Tibidabo summit with its church and amusement park and the Torre de Collserola communications tower are unmistakable landmarks. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies about 8 nautical miles to the southwest, Sabadell (LELL) roughly 7 nm to the northwest just beyond the ridge, and Girona (LEGE) some 53 nm to the northeast. Clear, dry nights are what make this site valuable, and clear days give the sharpest view of the dome.
