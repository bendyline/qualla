# Eixample

The clipped-corner grid that defines Barcelona from the air was a 19th-century social utopia by Ildefons Cerdà, half-built into the city's most elegant district.

**Location:** 41.3920, 2.1633

**Categories:** Urban planning, Districts of Barcelona, Architecture, Modernisme architecture in Barcelona, Eixample, History

## Introduction

From the air, Barcelona looks as if someone laid down graph paper and then pressed a thumbnail into every intersection. Those clipped corners, thousands of them marching in perfect rank, are the signature of the Eixample, the district whose Catalan name simply means "the expansion." It was born in the 19th century when Barcelona finally tore down the medieval walls that had strangled it for centuries and spilled outward across the plain toward the once-separate towns of Gràcia, Sants, and Sant Andreu. What filled that space was no accident of growth. It was a plan, and one of the most ambitious a European city ever attempted.

## The Corners That Give It Away

The Eixample runs on long straight streets and a strict grid crossed by wide avenues, its blocks squared off but for one crucial detail: every corner is chamfered, sliced at forty-five degrees, so that four blocks meeting at a crossroads open into a small octagonal plaza. Catalans call the blocks illes, islands; in Spanish they are manzanas. The cut corners were not decoration. Their designer wanted better sightlines, freer air, and room for horse-drawn wagons to swing around a turn. Today those same widened junctions catch the sunlight, hold a few parked cars, and let cafe tables spill into the open. Multiply the effect across the whole district and you get the texture that makes Barcelona instantly recognizable from a plane window.

## The Man Who Dreamed the Grid

The mind behind it was Ildefons Cerdà, an engineer who treated city-building as a question of public health. He wanted sunlight and ventilation reaching every home, and he studied traffic and transport with a rigor that was decades ahead of his time. His plan reserved space for markets, schools, and hospitals at regular intervals, so that daily life would never be far from what it needed. His blocks were meant to be built up on only two or three sides, wrapped around generous interior gardens where residents could breathe. It was less a real-estate scheme than a vision of a fairer, healthier city, one where rich and poor might live side by side across an even grid.

## A Utopia, Compromised

Reality had other plans. Developers filled in all four sides of the blocks and built over the green courtyards Cerdà had reserved. The streets came out narrower than he intended, only one of his two grand diagonal avenues was ever cut through, and the mixed community he imagined never arrived; the Eixample became a district of the well-off, its central spines around Passeig de Gràcia and Rambla de Catalunya among the most desirable addresses in the city. And yet the bones of the dream survived. Most of the neighborhood markets still trade on the very corners Cerdà assigned them more than a century ago, quiet proof that a plan can outlast the compromises made to it.

## The Architects' Playground

As the grid filled in, it became a stage for the Modernisme movement, Catalan Art Nouveau at full flower. Antoni Gaudí left two of his masterpieces here on the Passeig de Gràcia, the rippling Casa Milà and the bone-and-scale Casa Batlló, along with the still-rising Sagrada Família. One block, the Illa de la Discòrdia, sets three rival mansions shoulder to shoulder, Gaudí's Casa Batlló beside Domènech i Montaner's Casa Lleó Morera and Puig i Cadafalch's Casa Amatller. Its name plays on a pun: manzana means both "city block" and "apple," turning the block into the mythical Apple of Discord. Nearby, Puig i Cadafalch's spiky Casa de les Punxes rises like a medieval castle dropped onto a modern crossroads.

## The Grid Reinvents Itself

The Eixample has never stopped arguing with its own design. Cerdà wanted air and openness; a century of cars filled his junctions with traffic and fumes. In recent years the city has answered with superilles, or superblocks, knitting groups of blocks together and pushing traffic to their edges so the inner streets can return to people, trees, and play. It is, in a sense, an attempt to build the courtyards Cerdà was denied, at the scale of whole neighborhoods. The most planned district in Barcelona remains a work in progress, its clipped corners still catching the light exactly as their inventor hoped.

## Flight Context

The Eixample fills the plain between Barcelona's old town and the hills, centered near 41.3920°N, 2.1633°E. It is the most identifiable feature of the city from the air: from 2,000 to 4,000 feet the relentless chamfered grid reads unmistakably, sliced by the diagonal gash of Avinguda Diagonal and threaded by the broad Passeig de Gràcia. The Sagrada Família's spires stand near the grid's northeast quarter as the surest landmark. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies about 6 to 7 nautical miles southwest along the coast, Sabadell (LELL) roughly 9 nm inland to the northwest, and Girona (LEGE) some 55 nm northeast. Clear coastal daylight best reveals the geometry.
