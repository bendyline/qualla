# Illa de la Discòrdia

On one block of Passeig de Gràcia, Barcelona's greatest Modernista architects built houses that clash on purpose, giving the city its famous Block of Discord.

**Location:** 41.3914, 2.1650

**Categories:** Modernisme architecture in Barcelona, Antoni Gaudí buildings, Architecture, Eixample, History

## Introduction

Barcelona made a joke, and the joke stuck. Around the turn of the twentieth century, when the local press was busy mocking the wild new Modernista buildings springing up across the city, someone noticed that several of the loudest examples had landed on a single block of Passeig de Gràcia, jostling one another for attention. They called it the Illa de la Discòrdia, the Block of Discord. The name is a double pun. Illa means block, but the older word mansana also means apple, which summons the golden Apple of Discord from the Judgment of Paris, the fruit tossed among three goddesses each insisting she was the fairest. On this block the rivals were not goddesses but architects, and none of them intended to lose.

## A Boulevard for the New Rich

The stage was the Eixample, the gridded expansion that Ildefons Cerdà laid out for Barcelona under his 1860 building regulations. By the late nineteenth century the district had become deeply fashionable, and Passeig de Gràcia, its tree-lined main artery, filled with the homes of the wealthy. A vogue took hold: rather than build from scratch, affluent families bought existing houses and hired the star architects of the surging Modernista movement to reinvent them, the more conspicuously the better. A remodelled facade on Passeig de Gràcia was a declaration of status. The block between Carrer del Consell de Cent and Carrer d'Aragó would become the most competitive address of all.

## The First Provocation

It began, relatively quietly, with Casa Amatller at number 41. The house dated from 1875 and was plain enough to blend into the terrace around it. In 1898 the Amatller family commissioned Josep Puig i Cadafalch to transform it, and he gave them a frontage in Flemish Gothic dress: a tall stepped gable, glazed ceramic tiles, and sculpted animal figures climbing the facade. In old photographs you can see Casa Amatller rearing up above its uniform, well-behaved neighbours like a guest who arrived in costume. It was the opening move. Once one owner had broken the rhythm of the street so gleefully, the others could hardly resist answering.

## Gaudí's Dragon

The most audacious reply came next door at number 43. The house there had stood since 1877 until the textile industrialist Josep Batlló bought it in 1900, determined to own something unlike anything his neighbours had. He hired Antoni Gaudí, and Gaudí obliged with a building that seems to have grown rather than been built. Completed in 1906, Casa Batlló drew on Modernisme and Art Nouveau to produce a facade of flowing, bone-like stonework and a scaled, iridescent roof that arches like the back of a sleeping dragon. Locals read the roofline as the beast of Saint George, Catalonia's patron, pierced by his lance. It remains one of the most photographed houses on earth.

## Five Voices, One Block

The chorus filled out fast. In 1905 Lluís Domènech i Montaner finished Casa Lleó Morera at the block's southern corner, number 35, a florid, eclectic composition thick with ceramic ornament. Enric Sagnier answered in 1906 with Casa Mulleras at number 37, comparatively restrained, offering a sober Neoclassical front softened by a Rococo loggia. Between the headliners sat Casa Bonet at number 39, by Marcel·lí Coquillat i Llofriu. The whole point is that none of these buildings agrees with any other. Their styles argue across the pavement, and that quarrel, once a target of newspaper satire, is now precisely why crowds gather. The Block of Discord became one of Barcelona's most beloved sights by refusing to harmonize.

## From Insult to Icon

The name was never meant as a compliment. It surfaced around the turn of the twentieth century, when much of the local press was busy ridiculing the strange new Modernista styles as garish, undisciplined, and faintly absurd. Calling the block a scene of discord was a jab, a way of saying these architects had let their egos run wild in full public view. Time flipped the joke entirely. The very variety the critics mocked now reads as a compressed museum of Catalan Modernisme, four or five of its greatest practitioners set side by side for direct comparison. Visitors stand on the pavement and do exactly what the newspapers once did, look from one facade to the next, except now they are marveling rather than sneering.

## Flight Context

The Block of Discord lies at 41.391 N, 2.165 E, midway along Passeig de Gràcia in the heart of the Eixample, whose diamond-chamfered grid is unmistakable from above and helps pin the exact block between two long diagonals of the district. A slow pass at 1,500 to 2,500 feet lines up the boulevard running toward Plaça de Catalunya, with Gaudí's spired Sagrada Família rising a few blocks northeast as a fix. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) sits about 7 nm southwest, Sabadell (LELL) roughly 10 nm north-northwest, and Girona (LEGE) about 45 nm northeast. Morning light rakes the ornate east-facing facades; afternoon haze off the sea tends to flatten the detail.
