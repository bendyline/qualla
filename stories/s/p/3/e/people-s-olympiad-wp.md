# People's Olympiad

Barcelona's 1936 anti-fascist answer to the Nazi Olympics, an alternative games that never opened because the Spanish Civil War arrived on the morning of its start.

**Location:** 41.3649, 2.1549

**Categories:** history, sports, barcelona, spanish civil war, olympics, social history

## Introduction

The starting gun never fired. On the morning of 19 July 1936, the day the People's Olympiad was to open in Barcelona, thousands of athletes who had travelled from across the world woke not to a fanfare but to rifle fire echoing through the streets. A military uprising had begun two days earlier, and the fighting reached the city before dawn. The games meant to stand against fascism were swept away by a war against it, and the Estadi Olímpic on Montjuïc, built to host them, stood empty as its would-be competitors picked up guns instead.

## Two Cities, One Vote

The story carries a bitter irony at its root. In 1931 the International Olympic Committee met in Barcelona and, at that very session, awarded the 1936 Summer Olympics to Berlin, then still the capital of the Weimar Republic, defeating Barcelona's own bid by 43 votes to 16. The decision was made in the city that would later try to answer it. That same year, Spain was transformed: King Alfonso XIII went into exile, a republican constitution was adopted, and Catalonia was recognized as an autonomous region after a brief proclamation of a Catalan Republic. Within two years, the Berlin Games would fall under the control of the Nazi Party, and a contest over sport became a contest over ideology.

## An Answer to Berlin

As Berlin prepared to stage a spectacle for Hitler, opposition gathered elsewhere. Following the 1936 general election in Spain, the Pro-People's Sports Committee of Catalonia, backed by the Government of Catalonia and the newly elected Popular Front government, advocated a boycott of the Nazi Olympics and the staging of an alternative games in Barcelona. It was, in effect, the first global attempt to boycott a modern Olympics, a deliberate anti-fascist counter-event. The plan was ambitious and hurried, assembled in barely three months, with the hotels built for the 1929 International Exposition pressed into service as an improvised Olympic village.

## A Different Kind of Games

The People's Olympiad was designed to look nothing like Berlin's. Scheduled from 19 to 26 July, it would have closed six days before the Nazi Games even began. Alongside the usual sports it planned chess, folk dancing, music, and theatre, a festival of culture as much as competition. Its athletes came not from national committees but from trade unions, workers' clubs, and left-wing parties. Contingents arrived from the United States, Britain, the Netherlands, Belgium, the Nordic countries, and beyond, joined by teams of German and Italian political exiles, and by squads representing Jewish exiles, Alsace, Galicia, the Basque Country, and host Catalonia. Even the Soviet Union, which ran its own Spartakiad, agreed to take part. President Lluís Companys of Catalonia was to open the games.

## The Morning the War Came

By mid-July the city was full. An estimated 6,000 athletes from some two dozen countries, about half from outside Spain, had gathered in Barcelona along with tens of thousands of spectators. Then, on 17 July 1936, army officers rose against the Republic, and the Spanish Civil War began. The revolt reached Barcelona in the small hours of the 19th, the very day of the opening ceremony, and the athletes awoke to gunfire in the streets around them. The Olympiad was abandoned before a single event could be held. The festival of solidarity had run headlong into the catastrophe it had been convened to warn against.

## From Track to Trenches

What happened next gave the failed games their lasting meaning. Rather than flee, many of the assembled athletes chose to stay and defend the Republic, and more than two hundred are said to have joined workers' militias raised to protect it. Among them was the swimmer Clara Thalmann, who fought in Spain and later helped hide Jewish refugees in Nazi-occupied Paris. Their competition had been cancelled, but the cause behind it drew them into a far graver contest. Redemption, of a kind, came half a century later. Fifty-six years after the People's Olympiad was silenced, Barcelona hosted the 1992 Summer Olympics in that same stadium on Montjuïc, by then renamed for Lluís Companys, and the city finally held the games it had been denied.

## Flight Context

The intended main venue, the Estadi Olímpic Lluís Companys, sits at 41.365°N, 2.155°E on Montjuïc, southwest of central Barcelona. View from 2,500-4,000 ft: the open oval of the Olympic stadium is unmistakable on the wooded hill, alongside the white Palau Sant Jordi dome and Calatrava's slender communications tower. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) lies about 7 nm southwest along the coast; Sabadell (LELL) is inland to the north; Girona (LEGE) roughly 55 nm northeast. Clear Mediterranean light off the sea gives the sharpest view of the Montjuïc complex.
