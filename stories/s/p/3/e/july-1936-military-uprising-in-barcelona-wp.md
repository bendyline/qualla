# July 1936 military uprising in Barcelona

Over two days in July 1936, a city with almost no army of its own defeated a full military coup, as barricades, deceived soldiers, and anarchist militias turned Barcelona against the rebels.

**Location:** 41.4019, 2.1714

**Categories:** History, Spanish Civil War, Military history, Anarchism, History of Barcelona

## Introduction

The soldiers marching into Barcelona on the morning of 19 July 1936 had been told they were putting down a rebellion. Their officers handed out alcohol and orders and assured them they were defending the government against an anarchist mob. It was a lie. The soldiers were themselves the rebellion, the local edge of a military coup against Spain's elected Republic, and many would learn the truth only when workers rushed their gun positions with empty hands raised, pleading with them not to fire on their own people. Over the next two days, a city with almost no army of its own would defeat one of the best-armed garrisons in Spain.

## A Coup Against the Republic

The uprising was the Catalan front of a nationwide coup, launched in reaction to the electoral victory of the left-wing Popular Front. In Catalonia the rebel officers badly outnumbered the forces loyal to the Republic, and the Catalan government could muster fewer than two thousand poorly trained Assault Guards. Most of the resistance came from elsewhere, from the anarchist movement organized through the trade unions of the CNT and the affinity groups of the FAI. In the tense days beforehand, the anarchists begged the Catalan government for weapons and were refused again and again. When the shooting started, they went into it with rifles hidden since an earlier revolt and grenades made by hand.

## Barricades of 1909

The workers had done this before, and they remembered how. Barricades built from torn-up paving stones, a technique many had learned during the Tragic Week of 1909, proved sturdy enough to blunt artillery fire. Their plan was patient and precise: let the army advance deep into the city, then block its line of retreat, cut each detachment off from the others, and force the soldiers to burn through their ammunition with no way to resupply. Neighbourhood defense committees knew their own streets intimately and did not need to move far to fight. From balconies, women hurled debris down onto the troops. It was urban guerrilla warfare, improvised by civilians against a professional army.

## The Trick and the Turn

The rebels tried deception of their own. Columns entering the squares shouted Viva la Republica to sow confusion, and here and there it worked, drawing wavering Assault Guards briefly to their side. But the trick cut both ways. Again and again, small groups of workers walked toward the artillery crews with their rifles held over their heads, telling the gunners the plain truth: that their officers had deceived them, that they were being made to fire on their compatriots. At the port, convinced by exactly such an approach, a detachment turned its cannons around and opened fire on the remaining rebels. When the Civil Guard came down on the side of the Republic, the balance tipped for good.

## Goded Surrenders

The Nationalist commander in Catalonia, General Manuel Goded, arrived around midday on 19 July, flown in by seaplane from Mallorca to take charge of a battle already slipping away. He telephoned the loyal Civil Guard commander José Aranguren and ordered him to submit; Aranguren replied that he took orders only from the Republic. Cornered and abandoned, Goded surrendered that same afternoon. Fighting dragged into the following day as loyalist planes dropped leaflets over the holdout barracks urging the soldiers to give up. On 20 July the anarchist militias finally stormed the Drassanes barracks in the old dockyards, and with its fall the uprising in Barcelona was over.

## The Revolution That Followed

Victory left an extraordinary vacuum. Having beaten the army in the streets, the anarchist militias now held the real power in Barcelona, and the Catalan government had effectively lost its monopoly on force. In the weeks that followed came the Spanish Revolution of 1936, as workers brought much of Catalonia's farmland and industry under collective ownership. Yet the anarchists made a fateful choice: rather than seize the government outright, they chose to uphold a united antifascist front. They helped create the Central Committee of Antifascist Militias of Catalonia and raised the militia columns that soon marched west toward Aragon. The two days that saved Barcelona from the coup had opened straight into the long agony of the Spanish Civil War.

## Flight Context

The uprising raged across central Barcelona, anchored at Plaça de Catalunya around 41.402 N, 2.171 E, the great square at the head of the Rambla where rebel and loyalist forces clashed, and it ended a mile south at the Drassanes barracks beside the harbour. From the air the broad open square, the tree-lined Rambla running down to Port Vell, and the surrounding old-city and Eixample grids together trace the ground where the battle was fought. A pass at 1,500 to 2,500 feet frames the scene against Montjuïc rising to the south. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL), whose airbase stayed loyal to the Republic in 1936, lies about 7 nm southwest; Sabadell (LELL) is roughly 10 nm northwest and Girona (LEGE) about 46 nm northeast. Clear coastal mornings give the sharpest view of the city center.
