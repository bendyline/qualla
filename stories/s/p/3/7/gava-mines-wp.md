# Gavà Mines

Six thousand years ago, Neolithic people tunneled deep beneath what is now Gavà to reach variscite, a green gem worn as jewellery, leaving behind the oldest known mines of their kind in Europe and, in their exhausted shafts, their dead.

**Location:** 41.3100, 2.0000

**Categories:** Archaeology, Neolithic, Prehistoric mining, Catalonia, Baix Llobregat, Museums

## Introduction

Roughly six thousand years ago, people crawled into the dark beneath what is now the town of Gavà, and they did it not for metal, not for salt, not for water, but for the colour green. Their prize was variscite, a soft, apple-green mineral they ground and drilled into beads and pendants. To reach it, Neolithic miners sank shafts and burrowed galleries into the rock at the foot of the Garraf massif, west of the Llobregat river, creating what are today recognized as among the oldest and largest mines anywhere in Europe, and the only Neolithic variscite mines known to exist.

## The Only Mines of Their Kind

The scale is hard to square with the Stone Age. The site spreads across some two hundred hectares and includes more than a hundred known mines, with many more likely still hidden underground. What the miners were after was ornament, pure and simple: variscite that would be worked into jewellery for the living and the dead. The green stone of Gavà did not stay put, either. Chemical analysis has traced beads back to it from burials far inland, in the province of Huesca, hundreds of kilometres away. Long before writing, before cities, before bronze, a small corner of the Catalan coast was supplying a luxury good across a wide swath of the western Mediterranean world. The rock kept its usefulness long after the variscite ran thin. In Iberian and Roman times, and again through the Middle Ages, later peoples returned to these same slopes to dig, this time for iron ore, layering millennia of mining one atop another.

## Into the Dark

Mining here meant hand-to-rock labour of a kind almost unimaginable now. The workers cut through slates and limestones laid down more than four hundred million years earlier, using tools of stone, bone and antler and the dim light they could carry with them. A typical mine began as a narrow vertical well barely a metre across, dropping into the earth before branching into low galleries a person could only crawl or stoop through, some running ten metres or more and stepping down in levels as the miners chased a seam. There were no maps and no ventilation to speak of, only the knowledge, passed hand to hand, of where the green stone ran. These were not miners alone. The soil inside the galleries has yielded arrowheads, grinding stones and sickle teeth, the debris of farmers who kept cattle, sheep and pigs, grew barley and wheat on the plain, hunted deer and wild boar in the hills, and fished the sea below. The mine was one corner of a full and settled Neolithic world.

## A Green Stone Worth Dying Beside

When a mine was worked out, it was not always abandoned. Some shafts were given a second life as tombs. In one, known to archaeologists as Mine 83, a burial chamber was cut off the elbow of a gallery, sealed with limestone slabs, and blocked at the surface with an egg-shaped boulder more than a metre tall. The offerings buried with these Neolithic dead speak of real reach and real reverence: necklaces of variscite and red coral, a blade of obsidian carried all the way from Sardinia. The site's most famous finds deepen the sense of a people with an inner life, the Venus of Gavà, a small clay female figure thought to represent a mother goddess, and a skull bearing the marks of trepanation, the ancient practice of cutting into a living cranium.

## Rediscovered by Accident

The mines waited a very long time to be understood. Local people had always known of odd holes in the ground, but their true nature only surfaced in 1975, when building work in the Can Tintorer neighbourhood broke into the rock. Archaeological excavations began in 1978 under a local research group, and the Gavà Museum was founded that same year to explore, protect and explain the finds. Part of the site opened to the public in 1993 as the Gavà Mines Archaeological Park, where visitors can now descend into reconstructed galleries and stand, briefly, in the world of the people who dug here for beauty long before history began.

## Flight Context

The mines lie at 41.31 N, 2.00 E, in the town of Gavà on the Baix Llobregat plain, where the flat, farmed delta land meets the abrupt limestone slopes of the Garraf massif southwest of Barcelona. From 2,500-4,000 ft AGL the transition from delta fields to hillside is the key visual cue, with the Mediterranean shore just to the south and the Llobregat river to the east. Barcelona-El Prat (LEBL) sits only about 10 km northeast across the delta, so expect controlled airspace; Sabadell (LELL) is inland to the north, and Girona (LEGE) far up the coast. Hazy summer air over the coastal plain can reduce visibility.
