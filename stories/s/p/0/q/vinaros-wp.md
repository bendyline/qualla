# Vinaròs

A fishing city on the border of Valencia and Catalonia, famous for its prized prawns, a fortress-like Renaissance church, and a long past built on shipyards, wine, and the sea.

**Location:** 40.4704, 0.4754

**Categories:** Coastal Towns, Fishing, Medieval History, Spain, Cuisine, Baix Maestrat

## Introduction

The prized catch of Vinaròs is a prawn, and the town will not let you forget it. The langostino de Vinaròs, hauled from the waters off this stretch of the Mediterranean, is one of the most sought-after prawns in Spain, a delicacy that has done as much for the city's name as any monument. Vinaròs is a working fishing port first and a tourist town second, planted on the coast exactly where the Valencian Community meets Catalonia, and its story has always come off the boats.

## The Prawn Coast

When the trawlers come in, the town's rhythm follows them. Vinaròs is the capital of the Baix Maestrat, a border city where two regions and two dialects overlap, and the sea is its common language. The fishing fleet and its fresh-off-the-boat auction anchor the local economy alongside the summer tourists who fill the beaches, and the celebrated local prawns crown the menus of restaurants that take their seafood seriously. It is an unpretentious kind of fame. There are grander towns on this coast, but few can claim that people drive for hours simply to eat what the Vinaròs boats bring home that morning. The town also lends its name to the Taula del Sénia, a cross-border association of municipalities gathered along the Sénia river, a fitting role for a place where the line between Valencia and Catalonia is a matter of dialect and pride far more than any real divide.

## From Ibn Arus to Aragon

The name itself carries the town's oldest chapter. Vinaròs first enters the record in 1233 as a Moorish hamlet called Binarlaros, from Ibn Arus, in the eastern reaches of Al-Andalus, when it was taken by King James I of Aragon during his sweeping conquest of these lands. Afterward it passed through the hands of the era's great military orders, held by the Knights Templar between 1294 and 1311 and then by the Order of Montesa through the fourteenth century. Each ruler left the little coastal settlement a bit more fortified and a bit more firmly Christian, a frontier town slowly finding its footing on a contested shore.

## Ships and Vines

Vinaròs grew into real prosperity in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when fortifications went up and shipyards took root along the water. In the two centuries that followed, the town thrived on building ships and trading Valencian wine, its fortunes rising with the barrels leaving its harbor. Then came collapse. In the early twentieth century, phylloxera, the vine-killing insect that devastated vineyards across Europe, tore through the region's wine country and gutted the trade that had sustained the town. Vinaròs had to remake itself, turning back toward the two things the sea had always offered: fish to catch and a coast that visitors would pay to enjoy.

## The Fortress Church and Its Sons

At the heart of the old town stands the Església Arxiprestal de l'Assumpció, a church built like a stronghold. Raised in the prevailing Renaissance style between 1583 and 1596, its heavy, fortress-like walls speak of a coast that still feared attack from the sea, though a graceful Baroque portal was added at the turn of the eighteenth century to soften the severity. Vinaròs has sent its share of notable figures into the wider world, among them the avant-garde pianist and composer Carles Santos, and it was here that the French military commander Louis Joseph, Duke of Vendôme, died in 1712. And every winter the whole town erupts into its famous Carnival, thirty-three troupes strong, the loudest expression of a place that has never taken the quiet season lying down. The town has sent others out into the world too, among them the actress María Conesa and the pianist Leopoldo Querol, and it fills through the long, dry Mediterranean summer before winter hands it back to the fishermen and their nets.

## Flight Context

Vinaròs lies at 40.47°N, 0.48°E on the Mediterranean coast at the northern tip of Castellón province, on the Valencia–Catalonia border. The city, its fishing harbor, and the beaches to either side are best viewed at 2,000–3,500 ft. Reus (LERS) sits to the north, Castellón–Costa Azahar (LECN) to the south, and Valencia (LEVC) further down the coast. Look for the working port and breakwater and the compact old town set just behind it, with the bulk of the fortress-like archpriest's church as an anchor; the coastline and the nearby river mouth help fix position in clear conditions.
