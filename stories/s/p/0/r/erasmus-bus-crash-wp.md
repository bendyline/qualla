# Erasmus bus crash

On a March morning in 2016, a bus carrying international Erasmus students home from a Valencian festival crashed on the AP-7 near Freginals, killing thirteen young women from across the world.

**Location:** 40.6600, 0.5111

**Categories:** History, Memorials, Spain, Catalonia, Modern History, Transport

## Introduction

They had spent the weekend watching a city set fire to its own artwork. The Fallas of Valencia fill the March streets with towering satirical sculptures, then burn them all in a single blazing night, and for a group of exchange students it must have felt like the perfect adventure, far from home and gloriously alive. In the small hours of 20 March 2016, their bus turned north on the AP-7 motorway toward Barcelona. Near the quiet town of Freginals, it collided with a car and left the road. Thirteen young women did not survive.

## The Road North Before Dawn

The bus, a Tata Hispano coach, was carrying Erasmus students back from the festival when the crash happened on the busy AP-7 near Freginals, in the flat farmland just inland from the Ebro Delta. It was still dark. What began as a weekend of shared discovery ended on a stretch of motorway that thousands pass every day without a second thought. Thirteen of the passengers were killed, all of them young women, students in the middle of their studies abroad. Many others were injured. In an instant, an ordinary journey home became one of the worst road tragedies to strike Spain's international student community in memory.

## Thirteen Young Women, So Many Homes

The people on that bus had gathered from across the world. Emergency services confirmed the passengers came from twenty countries and territories, among them France, the Netherlands, Finland, Hungary, Germany, Sweden, Norway, Switzerland, the Czech Republic, New Zealand, Great Britain, Italy, Peru, Bulgaria, Macedonia, Poland, Ireland, the Palestinian territories, Japan, and Ukraine. They had been strangers a few months earlier and had become friends in the way that people do when they are all far from home at once, leaning on one another in an unfamiliar country. The grief, when it came, did not belong to one nation. It fell on a dozen and more, in a dozen languages, in homes scattered right across the map. Some had crossed oceans to spend that year in Spain, others had come from only a few countries over, but all of them had made the same choice, to spend a piece of their youth somewhere unfamiliar, among people they had not yet met.

## A Program Built to Cross Borders

There is a particular cruelty in where this happened. The Erasmus program exists to send young Europeans across the continent's borders, to study in one another's cities and come home changed by it, and for millions it has been among the most formative experiences of their lives. Named for the Renaissance humanist who himself wandered from one European university to the next, it is, at its core, a promise about the freedom to move and to belong anywhere. That these students died on a road while living out exactly that promise made the loss resonate far beyond the crash site. It touched something a whole generation recognized as its own. For anyone who had ever studied abroad, the news carried a chill of recognition. Any of them might have been on a bus like that, on a road like that, at the tired, happy end of an ordinary weekend away.

## Grief Without a Verdict

In the years that followed, the families were left to carry their loss without the resolution that a courtroom sometimes offers. The legal process never produced a conviction, and in April 2023 the bus driver died of a heart attack, closing the case without the accounting some had hoped for. What endures is not a verdict but a memory: of thirteen young women at the very beginning of their adult lives, of the friends and families who loved them, and of a weekend that should have been nothing more than a bright story to tell later. The road near Freginals looks like any other. It is not, for the people who lost someone there.
