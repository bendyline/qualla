# The Daughters' Inheritance

In Alcossebre, daughters once inherited the less valuable seafront while sons took the fertile fields inland; since the 1980s the fishing village has grown into a resort that kept its old centre, framed by pirate watchtowers, a hilltop hermitage and a Templar castle.

**Location:** 40.2447, 0.2772

**Categories:** Seaside Villages, Beaches, Costa del Azahar, Province of Castellón, Valencian Community

## Introduction

Traditionally, land here passed down in a particular way. Sons inherited the fertile fields around Alcalà de Xivert, the inland town that governs this stretch of coast, while daughters were left the less valuable property down by the sea. Then, beginning in the 1980s, the tranquil fishing village of Alcossebre began to grow into a seaside resort, and the daughters turned out to be the winners. The village still belongs to the municipality of Alcalà de Xivert, but its fortunes face the water: about ten kilometres of coastline on the Costa del Azahar of Castellón province, south of Peñíscola, at the southern end of the Serra d'Irta.

## Ten Kilometres of Shore

Most visitors come for the beaches. Platja del Carregador, near the village centre, is the largest at 720 metres, its promenade lined with cafés and tapas bars. Protected sand dunes separate it from Platja Romana, the second largest, and beyond a pebble beach and a quiet bay lies the much smaller Platja del Moro, ringed by red sandstone cliffs. Up by the port, at Les Fonts, fresh water rises through the sand from the karst system of the Serra d'Irta, which keeps the sea there ice-cold. Several of Alcossebre's beaches have carried the Blue Flag. South of the village the sand gives way to natural pebble shores with water clear enough to see fish, including the kilometre-long naturist beach of Serradal near the dry mouth of the Riu de les Coves. Past Capicorb, a footpath leads on to the beach of the neighbouring village of Torrenostra, where the surfing is good and the people are few.

## Keeping Watch from the Hills

This coast once had reason to watch the horizon. Torre Ebrí, a 16th-century watchtower north of the village, was built as part of a defensive network of coastal forts and towers against Barbary pirates. In the hills above, at 315 metres, the small whitewashed hermitage of Sant Benet i Santa Llúcia dates from the 17th century and has been restored. Around it stand an old pilgrims' hostel, a stable, two wells and the remains of another 16th-century watchtower. The view runs south along the Costa del Azahar as far as Oropesa del Mar, and on clear days the volcanic Columbretes Islands, some 50 to 60 kilometres offshore, can be seen without binoculars. Down at Capicorb a different kind of devotion is remembered: a small, simple chapel to Saint Anthony of Padua, built in 1773 on the spot where an image of the saint was found on the beach in 1690. The newest watcher is the Irta lighthouse, planned under Spain's 1985-89 maritime signals plan and lit in 1993.

## The Templars' Hill

Inland, on a summit in the foothills of the Serra d'Irta, stand the ruins of the Castell de Xivert. It began as a Muslim fortress of the 10th and 11th centuries, raised over the remains of a Bronze Age settlement and surrounded by a small fortified town. By 1234 the Knights Templar held the castle, and that year they granted a settlement charter to Xivert. Accounts differ on how it fell: one describes a conquest after a long siege, another a negotiated surrender under which the Muslim inhabitants kept their customs and religion, though they had to move their homes to the castle's western slope. The Templars remodelled the fortress, adding two great round towers, a church and a cistern. The village's story ended in exile. By 1609 Xivert was a village of Moriscos, converts to Christianity and their descendants, with 52 households, yet Philip III ordered them expelled from Spain along with the rest of the Moriscos. With them gone, sixteen Christian families resettled the place in 1616 but left in 1632, and castle and village fell into abandonment and ruin. Today the castle is reached by footpath or dirt track, and its views over the surrounding country are excellent.

## A Resort That Kept Its Village

Alcossebre avoided the worst of the over-development and mass tourism seen elsewhere on the Spanish coast, and unlike most coastal towns it is not fully urbanized. It has kept its historic centre of narrow alleys, small shops and cafés, and it has few large buildings. Most shops close for the midday siesta. In summer the population swells, by as much as tenfold according to the town council, mostly with Spanish and French visitors, along with a few Germans and Dutch. Protected land surrounds it on three sides. To the north rises the Serra d'Irta natural park, a combined mountain park and marine reserve with trails for hiking and mountain biking; the Columbretes Islands natural park lies far out to sea to the east; and the Prat de Cabanes natural park lies to the south.

## Flight Context

Alcossebre lies at about 40.24°N, 0.28°E on the Castellón coast, at the southern end of the Serra d'Irta, whose high ground runs north along the shore toward Peñíscola. From 2,000-4,000 feet, look for the village's string of beaches running south from its port, with the Irta lighthouse just to the northeast and a pebble shoreline continuing south past Capicorb toward Torrenostra. In clear weather the Columbretes Islands are visible far offshore, about 28 nautical miles to the southeast. Castellón-Costa Azahar Airport (LECH) lies about 10 nautical miles west-southwest; Valencia Airport (LEVC) is about 57 nautical miles southwest. Coastal haze in summer can hide the islands; the hills behind the village make a clear landmark.
