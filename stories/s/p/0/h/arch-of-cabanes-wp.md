# Arch of Cabanes

A lone Roman arch standing in an open Castellón plain for some eighteen centuries, likely built to mark a grave rather than a victory.

**Location:** 40.1656, 0.0147

**Categories:** Spain, Valencian Community, Ancient Rome, Archaeology, Historic monuments, History

## Introduction

It stands alone in the middle of an open plain, a single stone arch with nothing around it but fields and the ghost of a Roman road. The Arch of Cabanes has held that spot for roughly eighteen centuries, built in the second century AD along the via Augusta, the great highway that carried travellers and legions up the eastern Spanish coast. Most arches like it were raised in cities, framing a forum or a triumphal route. This one was raised in the countryside, and archaeologists think it was never about triumph at all.

## An Arch for the Dead

Despite the grand name, the Arch of Cabanes was most likely a funerary monument, probably tied to a rural villa somewhere nearby. Coins and pottery found at its base date it to the second century, and its placement beside the road fits a Roman habit of honouring the dead where the living would pass. Roads were where Romans staged memory; a tomb by the highway kept a family's name in front of every traveller heading north or south. If that reading is right, the arch is less a monument to a battle than a message from one household to the centuries, a way of saying that someone once lived, and mattered, on this plain.

## What the Centuries Took

Time has stripped the monument down to its bones. Gone are the entablature, the spandrels and all the carved decoration, the mouldings and ornament that would once have made it read as a proud, finished thing. What remains are the two square pylons and the arch that springs between them, standing about 5.8 metres high and 6.92 metres wide. Look closely at the curve overhead and you can count fourteen wedge-shaped stones set radially, the voussoirs that hold each other in place through nothing but their own pressure. It was built of limestone on a base of granite blocks, laid without mortar, and it has outlasted the empire that raised it on balance alone.

## The Road That Moved

The arch appears in the written record surprisingly early, named in a charter of 1243, not long after the Christian reconquest of this coast. For centuries a working street still passed beneath its span, until in 1873 the provincial monuments commission took the unusual step of rerouting the road around it to keep carts and traffic from wearing it away. That decision, to bend a living road for the sake of an old stone, marks the moment the arch stopped being infrastructure and became heritage. Spain formally recognised it among the nation's artistic treasures in 1931, and in 2004 the town of Cabanes adopted a special plan to protect both the arch and the surviving trace of the Roman road.

## Naming a Plain

The arch gave its name to the land around it, standing about two kilometres from the town of Cabanes in the province of Castellón. There is something fitting in that. The people who built it are unknown, their villa vanished, their family name lost, yet the plain still carries the memory of their monument. Travellers on the modern roads nearby pass it much as Roman travellers once did, a solitary frame of stone against the sky, marking a spot whose original meaning has faded but whose presence refuses to.

## Reading a Roman Road

To understand the arch you have to picture the road. The via Augusta was the great Roman artery of eastern and southern Hispania, running from the Pyrenees down the Mediterranean flank of the peninsula and on toward the far south, the spine along which armies marched, goods moved and messages travelled. A monument set beside it was not stranded in the countryside at all; it stood on one of the busiest routes in the Roman west, seen by everyone who passed. That is the likely reason a family would spend real money to raise a tomb here rather than tuck it away on their own land. The arch was an advertisement for the dead, addressed to a stream of strangers. The traffic is long gone and the paving mostly vanished, yet the arch still marks the line the road once took, a single surviving punctuation mark on a sentence written across the landscape two thousand years ago.

## Flight Context

The Arch of Cabanes stands in open country at 40.166°N, 0.015°E, about 2 km from the town of Cabanes and set back from the coast on the inland plain of Castellón. From 2,000-4,000 feet it is a tiny target, best located by finding the town of Cabanes and the line of the old road rather than the arch itself; the surrounding farmland offers little contrast. The Desert de les Palmes range rises to the south and the Mediterranean lies to the east. Castellón-Costa Azahar (LECN) is the nearest field, just southeast toward the coast; Valencia (LEVC) lies down the coast to the southwest and Reus (LERS) to the northeast. Clear, still days give the best chance of picking out the monument's shadow on the plain.
