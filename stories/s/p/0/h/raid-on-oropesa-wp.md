# Raid on Oropesa

An 1801 Royal Navy raid on the Valencian coast, led in part by Thomas Cochrane, the daring captain who inspired a century of fictional naval heroes.

**Location:** 40.0828, 0.1456

**Categories:** Spain, Valencian Community, Naval history, Military history, Napoleonic Wars, Coastal towers

## Introduction

One of the two small British warships that came for the Spanish convoy on 9 June 1801 was commanded by a man who would later inspire half the naval fiction of the following century. Thomas Cochrane, captain of the tiny brig Speedy, was already famous: only weeks earlier he had taken his fourteen-gun vessel alongside a Spanish frigate several times its size and captured it. Off the little port of Oropesa del Mar, he and the brig Kangaroo found a merchant convoy huddled beneath the guns of a coastal tower, and set about a raid that was audacious even by Cochrane's standards.

## The Chase Up the Coast

The two brigs were hunting. A British privateer out of Menorca had brought word of a Spanish convoy, a dozen vessels shepherded by five escorts, moving somewhere along the coast, and Speedy and Kangaroo set out to run it down. On 8 June they came upon a tower at Almenara sheltering an armed Spanish privateer. The tower mounted only two four-pounder guns, yet it refused to surrender. Cochrane's landing party stormed it, took it and pulled it down, with only Cochrane and two of his men lightly hurt. Then they pressed on north, still chasing the convoy that had slipped ahead of them.

## Under the Torre del Rey

They found their quarry sheltering at Oropesa, gathered under the guns of a large square tower, the Torre del Rey, armed with around a dozen cannon. Guarding the convoy were a twenty-gun xebec and three gunboats, later joined by a twelve-gun felucca and two more. Cochrane, ever the opportunist, wanted to wait for darkness and send in a cutting-out party. But Kangaroo had arrived, and her commander, George Charles Pulling, was the senior officer; he chose to wait for morning instead. When it came, the two brigs anchored close in, some 250 yards off the shore and 500 from the town, and opened fire.

## A Long Day's Gunnery

The exchange ground on through the day. By mid-afternoon the xebec and two of the gunboats had taken too much punishment and sank. Around half past six the Spanish fire began to slacken, and Kangaroo closed on the tower, silencing it by seven, while Speedy drove off the felucca and the surviving gunboats. Still the British took small-arms fire from the shore until midnight, when boats from the two brigs rowed in and brought out three Spanish brigs loaded with wine, rice and bread. Cochrane went back for a second try, only to find that everything else in the harbour had already been sunk or run aground. The British left with the prizes they had.

## The Making of a Legend

The raid was a minor action in a long war, but the man at the centre of it was not. Thomas Cochrane, later the tenth Earl of Dundonald, was among the most daring frigate captains of the age, and the little Speedy was the vessel that made his name. Only weeks before Oropesa, off Barcelona, he had fought and captured the Spanish frigate El Gamo, a warship carrying several times his own guns and crew, in one of the most celebrated single-ship actions in Royal Navy history. His later career was turbulent, marked by dazzling exploits, political scandal and service to the navies of Chile, Brazil and Greece in their wars of independence. Novelists mined his adventures for generations; the fictional captains Jack Aubrey and Horatio Hornblower both owe a clear debt to the real Cochrane. The small brig that harried a convoy off Oropesa belonged, in other words, to a genuine legend of the sea.

## The Cost and the Sequel

The butcher's bill fell unevenly. Kangaroo lost a midshipman killed and several seamen wounded, some of them severely, while Speedy came through without a single casualty, though she had fired away some 1,400 rounds and had barely a broadside of shot left. Spanish losses went unrecorded, as the losses of the defending side so often did. The commander-in-chief, Lord Keith, sent his congratulations. The sequel was harder on the victors than the battle: within weeks Speedy herself was run down and captured by a powerful French squadron under Rear-Admiral Charles Linois, an encounter that helped set the stage for the Battle of Algeciras Bay, while Kangaroo sailed home to England and was quietly sold off the following year.

## Flight Context

The action took place just off Oropesa del Mar at roughly 40.083°N, 0.146°E, on the Costa del Azahar between Benicàssim and Peníscola. The enduring landmark is the Torre del Rey, the square coastal tower that sheltered the convoy and still stands on the shore, a useful visual fix from low altitude. The town's marina and beaches now spread around the old fortification. Castellón-Costa Azahar (LECN) lies just to the south-southwest; Valencia (LEVC) is farther down the coast to the southwest and Reus (LERS) up the coast to the northeast. The offshore Columbretes islands lie to the east. Coastal visibility is usually good; the Torre del Rey and the harbour make the historic anchorage easy to place from about 2,000-3,000 feet.
