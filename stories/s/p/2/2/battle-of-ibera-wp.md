# Battle of Ibera

On a plain near modern Tortosa in 215 BC, a Carthaginian army dissolved from the inside when its reluctant conscripts refused to fight—and the shockwave reached all the way to Rome.

**Location:** 40.8000, 0.5167

**Categories:** History, Ancient Rome, Military History, Second Punic War, Catalonia

## Introduction

In the spring of 215 BC, on a plain near the mouth of the Ebro, two armies of roughly 25,000 men formed up to decide the fate of a war being fought hundreds of miles away in Italy. Then, almost before the fighting began, the entire centre of one army simply walked away. The Iberian tribesmen holding the middle of the Carthaginian line had no wish to win—because winning meant being shipped off to fight and die for Hannibal in a country they had never seen. Their commander, Hasdrubal Barca, watched his own line dissolve from within. The battle that followed would ripple all the way to the gates of Rome.

## Why Rome Had Come to the Ebro

By 215 BC, Hannibal was loose in Italy, having crossed the Alps and shattered one Roman army after another. But an invasion needs feeding: fresh men, silver, supplies. Hannibal's lifeline ran back to Carthaginian Iberia—modern Spain—where his brother Hasdrubal held the family's rich domains of silver mines and fighting men. Rome understood this perfectly. Late in 218 BC it had landed an army in northeastern Iberia under two brothers, Gnaeus and Publius Scipio, who won an early foothold at the Battle of Cissa and dug in along the coast between the Ebro River and the Pyrenees. That lodgement was the whole point. So long as the Scipios held it, no Carthaginian army could march overland from Iberia to reinforce Hannibal. Hasdrubal had already tried to dislodge them once, in 217 BC, and seen his fleet mauled at the Battle of the Ebro River.

## A Question of Loyalty

In early 215 BC, reinforced and under orders to break through to Italy at last, Hasdrubal marched north. The Scipios had crossed the Ebro and were besieging Ibera, a town on the river's south bank—later the Roman Dertosa, and today the city of Tortosa—that had sided with Carthage. Hasdrubal answered by besieging a town allied to Rome, and the Scipios lifted their own siege to come and fight him. For several days the two armies camped a few miles apart, sending out skirmishers, taking each other's measure. When they finally deployed, Hasdrubal made a fateful choice: he set his locally recruited Iberian infantry in the very centre of his line, flanked by tough African and Carthaginian heavy infantry, with cavalry on the wings and perhaps twenty war elephants ranged in front. The centre was the hinge of everything. And the men holding it did not want to be there.

## The Gap in the Line

The Roman legions advanced against the Iberian centre. Javelins flew, the lines closed—and the Iberians broke almost at once and ran. According to the historian Livy, they understood that a Carthaginian victory would mean marching to Italy with Hasdrubal, and their unwillingness to leave home drained the fight out of them. Their own countrymen in Hannibal's army, fresh from three years of victories, had held firm at the centre of the line at Cannae the year before; these men, serving a general who had known mostly defeat, simply left. The Romans poured through the sudden gap. Rather than chase the fugitives, the disciplined legions wheeled left and right and struck the exposed rear of the Carthaginian heavy infantry, catching them between two fires. The African and Libyan troops fought hard, inflicting and taking heavy losses before they were overwhelmed. Hasdrubal's cavalry and elephants fled the field. The Romans stormed the Carthaginian camp and carried off its provisions, equipment, and treasury; Hasdrubal escaped with most of his horsemen and a scatter of infantry.

## The Battle That History Half-Forgot

For all its consequence, Ibera is a battle we see only through a fog. Polybius, the most reliable ancient historian of the Punic Wars, wrote an account of it that has not survived; what remains comes largely from Livy, whom modern scholars consider unreliable, especially on battles, so the casualty figures are genuinely unknown. Yet the strategic result is not in doubt. Hasdrubal had lost his chance to reinforce Hannibal at the height of his success, and an army being readied to sail for Italy under the brothers' youngest sibling, Mago, was diverted to Iberia instead. The Scipios stayed and fought on, raiding Carthaginian holdings and turning the Iberian tribes, until both brothers were killed at the Battle of the Upper Baetis in 212 BC. The historian Klaus Zimmermann has gone so far as to call the Scipios' victory here "the decisive battle of the war." Rome never had to face Hannibal and a full second Carthaginian army at the same time—and a plain outside Tortosa is part of the reason why.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lay on the south bank of the Ebro near ancient Ibera, close to modern Tortosa at about 40.80°N, 0.52°E, where the river's broad lower valley opens between hills before reaching the Mediterranean delta. From 4,500–6,500 ft AGL the silver thread of the Ebro is the dominant feature, with the massif of Els Ports rising to the southwest and Tortosa's hilltop Suda castle marking the old town. Reus (LERS) lies about 65 km to the northeast and Lleida-Alguaire (LEDA) inland to the north, with Barcelona (LEBL) the coastal gateway. The valley is often hazy on summer afternoons; clear winter air gives the sharpest view of the river's course.
