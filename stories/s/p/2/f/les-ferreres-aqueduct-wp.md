# Les Ferreres Aqueduct

A two-tiered Roman aqueduct bridge north of Tarragona, so imposing that locals credited its construction to the devil, still standing two thousand years after it carried water to the city of Tarraco.

**Location:** 41.1456, 1.2439

**Categories:** Roman archaeology, World Heritage Sites, Aqueducts, Ancient Rome, Catalonia, Tarragona

## Introduction

The people who lived here later could not quite believe that human hands had raised it, so they gave the credit to the devil. For centuries the great double tier of arches north of Tarragona was known simply as the Pont del Diable, the Devil's Bridge, as if only an infernal bargain could explain 27 meters of dry-fitted stone marching clean across an empty valley. The truth is more impressive than any legend. Roman engineers built it around two thousand years ago, and they built it so well that it still stands, arch upon arch, doing nothing now but holding up the sky where water once ran. It is, in every sense, a monument to a difficult problem elegantly solved.

## An Aqueduct for Tarraco

Tarragona was once Tarraco, one of the most important cities of Roman Hispania, and a city of that stature needed water: reliable, abundant, and delivered by gravity alone. The solution began 15 kilometers to the north, at the Francoli river. From there a channel threaded across the landscape, following the land's gentle downhill grade so precisely that the water flowed the entire distance without a single pump. Where a valley interrupted the route, the Romans simply bridged it. Les Ferreres is that bridge, the surviving showpiece of the system that kept Tarraco's fountains, baths, and households supplied. It most likely dates from the reign of Augustus, Rome's first emperor, when the city was being remade in monumental stone and its water supply was being engineered to match its ambition.

## Two Rows of Arches

Up close, the aqueduct is a lesson in Roman discipline. It rises in two levels: eleven arches along the lower tier, twenty-five along the upper. Every arch spans the same width, 20 Roman feet, just under six meters, with a variation across the whole structure of no more than 15 centimeters, a tolerance that would satisfy a modern builder. The pillars march at regular intervals, their centers set 26 Roman feet apart. At its tallest the bridge reaches 27 meters, and it runs 249 meters from end to end. There is no mortar-slathered bulk here, no wasted stone. What you see instead is the confident repetition of a single idea, scaled up until it strides across the gap as though the valley were barely an inconvenience.

## The Channel on Top

All of that engineering existed to serve one narrow feature: the specus, the water channel that runs along the very top of the structure, carried on a low wall at either end. The arches, the pillars, the careful geometry, every stone was in service of holding that channel at exactly the right height and the gentlest possible slope, so the water would keep moving but never rush. It is easy to admire the arches and forget the point of them. Why go to such trouble for a trough of flowing water? Because the alternative was a city that ran dry. The Romans did not build Les Ferreres to be beautiful; they built it to lift water a few meters higher than the ground would allow. The beauty is simply what precision looks like at scale.

## What Survives

Two thousand years later, the aqueduct still bridges its valley, dry now but intact, framed by the pines of a small park just beyond the edge of the modern city. In 2000 it was inscribed, together with the rest of the Archaeological Ensemble of Tarraco, on the UNESCO World Heritage list, recognized as one of the finest Roman aqueduct bridges left standing anywhere outside Italy. Visitors can follow the wooded path to its base, and the bold can even cross along the very top, where the water once flowed. Seen from the path below, the arches glow a pale gold in the afternoon sun, and their scale registers fully only when a person walks beneath them and shrinks to almost nothing. Stand there and the old nickname begins to make sense. It really does look like something that should not be possible. For a Roman engineer, there could be no higher compliment.

## Flight Context

Les Ferreres Aqueduct stands at 41.15 degrees N, 1.24 degrees E, about 4 km north of central Tarragona and the Mediterranean coast. From the air it reads as a pale double row of arches spanning a green, pine-wooded gully just inland of the AP-7 motorway and the Francoli river valley, with the sea and Tarragona's port to the south. Best viewed at low altitude, roughly 1,500 to 3,000 ft AGL, in the clear, bright light typical of this coast. Reus (LERS) lies only about 7 km to the west and is the closest field; Lleida-Alguaire (LEDA) is inland to the northwest, and Barcelona (LEBL) sits roughly 90 km up the coast to the northeast.
