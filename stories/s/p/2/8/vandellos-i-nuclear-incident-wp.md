# Vandellòs I nuclear incident

On an October night in 1989, a turbine fire at a Catalan reactor built partly to make bomb-grade plutonium came within reach of catastrophe, and the people inside held it back by hand.

**Location:** 40.9514, 0.8667

**Categories:** History, Nuclear power, Disasters and accidents, Industrial heritage, Catalonia, 20th century

## Introduction

The fire started where nobody wanted a fire in a nuclear plant: in the turbine hall. On the night of 19 October 1989, a mechanical failure in the steam turbine of the Vandellòs I Nuclear Power Plant ignited a blaze on the Catalan coast, and the flames found cabling that was never made to resist them. As the fire spread, it began knocking out the very systems meant to keep the reactor safe, including the control computer and part of the cooling system. What happened over the following hours would be remembered as one of the most serious nuclear accidents in Western Europe, and as a near miss that only human nerve prevented from becoming far worse.

## A Reactor Built for Two Purposes

Vandellòs I was a product of its era, and that era was the tail end of Francoist Spain. France sold Spain a UNGG reactor, a gas-and-graphite design that was already obsolete by the time it was installed. Its appeal was not efficiency. As a by-product, this kind of reactor yielded plutonium, the material from which atomic bombs are made, and that dual potential was part of the point. The plant was inaugurated in 1972, when Spain had only two other operating nuclear stations, Garoña and Zorita. For seventeen years it ran on rudimentary technology, the kind of aging system in which small failures can cascade in ways the original designers never fully anticipated.

## The Night the Turbine Failed

Seventeen years after the plant opened, that cascade arrived. The failure in the steam turbine was mechanical and ordinary in isolation, the sort of breakdown that happens in power stations of every kind. Here it lit a fire, and the fire exposed how thin the margins really were. The cabling was not fireproof. As it burned, the plant lost instruments and controls, and damage crept toward the cooling system that stood between an ordinary bad night and a reactor core running out of control. In those hours, the plant was closer to disaster than the public would understand for some time, and the outcome hung on what the people inside could do with their hands and their judgment.

## The Hours That Held

A core meltdown was avoided, and it was avoided by people. The Corps of Firefighters of Catalonia fought the blaze alongside the plant's own technicians, and together they managed to put the fire out and shut the reactor down manually, overriding the automated systems that the flames had crippled. It is worth pausing on what that meant. Firefighters walked into a burning nuclear plant whose safety instruments were failing, and operators worked to bring a reactor to a controlled stop without the computers built to help them do it. There were no headlines about heroism at the time, only a job done under conditions no one should ever face. Their success is the reason Vandellòs is remembered as an incident rather than a catastrophe, and the reason a stretch of the Catalan coast was not evacuated.

## A Place in a Grim Catalogue

Vandellòs I earned a permanent entry in the short, sobering catalogue of Western Europe's most serious civil nuclear accidents. It is studied alongside the great cautionary tales of the atomic age, from the Windscale fire in Britain to Three Mile Island in the United States, as an example of how a nuclear plant can be pushed toward disaster by causes that have nothing to do with the reactor core itself. Here the trigger was almost mundane, a turbine breaking and a fire spreading through cabling that should never have burned so freely. That ordinariness is precisely what makes the case unsettling. Catastrophe did not require sabotage or an earthquake, only an aging machine, a spark, and materials that were never meant to be tested by flame.

## What Vandellòs Left Behind

The reactor never ran again. The damage to its systems was severe enough that the plant was decommissioned, its working life ended not by age alone but by the night the turbine burned. Classified as a serious incident on the International Nuclear Event Scale, Vandellòs I forced a hard reckoning: the old safety protocols had proven insufficient, and new ones were introduced in their place. The lesson traveled well beyond one Catalan headland. A fire that was never supposed to threaten the core had come close to doing exactly that, and the near miss became a case study in how obsolete technology, flammable cabling, and a single mechanical failure can align into something genuinely dangerous.

## Flight Context

The Vandellos site stands on the Costa Daurada at 40.951N, 0.867E, just inland of the Mediterranean shore south of Tarragona. From 3,000 to 6,000 feet the plant's coastal industrial footprint and the adjacent shoreline are easy to pick out against the water. Reus (LERS) lies roughly 20nm to the northeast, Barcelona (LEBL) about 55nm northeast, and Lleida-Alguaire (LEDA) around 45nm to the northwest. Sea-breeze haze can soften the coast on summer afternoons, but the site and its harbor works usually read clearly from the air.
