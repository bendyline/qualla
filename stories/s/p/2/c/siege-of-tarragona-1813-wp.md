# Siege of Tarragona (1813)

In June 1813, a British general landed 16,000 men to take Tarragona from a garrison a tenth their size, then lost his nerve, spiked his guns, and sailed away, an episode that ended his command.

**Location:** 41.1156, 1.2494

**Categories:** History, Military history, Napoleonic Wars, Peninsular War, Catalonia, 19th century

## Introduction

The eighteen heavy siege guns had already breached the walls of Ciudad Rodrigo and Badajoz. Now they lay spiked and abandoned on a beach near Tarragona, left behind by an army that outnumbered its enemy ten to one and never fought the battle it came for. The siege of June 1813 is remembered not for its bloodshed, which was slight, but for the spectacle of a commander unraveling in real time. Lieutenant General John Murray had every advantage a soldier could ask for. Over nine days he squandered all of them, and the guns rusting in the sand became the monument to his failure.

## Wellington's Errand

The plan was sound, and it came from a sound source. In April 1813, Murray's Anglo-Sicilian-Spanish army, based at Alicante, had checked Marshal Suchet's corps at the Battle of Castalla. Wellington, preparing his great summer offensive against King Joseph Bonaparte, wanted Suchet pinned down and unable to send help north. So he ordered Murray to strike at Tarragona, the Spanish port about 65 miles southwest of Barcelona. Take the town, threaten the coast, and Suchet would have to stay put. It was a supporting move in a larger campaign, the kind of task a competent general executes without drama. Murray was not that general.

## An Overwhelming Force

On 2 June, Rear-Admiral Benjamin Hallowell Carew's squadron put 16,000 men ashore at Salou Bay, six miles south of the town. They were joined by General Francisco Copons and his division of 7,000 Spanish soldiers, and on 3 June the siege began. Against this host stood General Bertoletti with roughly 1,600 men, a battalion of French line infantry, a battalion of Italians, a couple of artillery companies, and some French sailors, defending walls that had never been repaired since Suchet stormed the place in 1811. Knowing he could not hold the long outer defenses, Bertoletti made a cold, correct choice: he abandoned the walls, pulled his men into the old town, and left token garrisons in two outworks.

## The Nerve That Broke

A decisive commander would have rushed the two weak outworks at once. Murray instead insisted on the slow ritual of breaching batteries. By 7 June, Fort Royal lay helpless under his guns, yet he set the assault for 11 June and waited. That pause was fatal to his own resolve. Word came that Suchet was marching north from Valencia with 8,000 men, while General Mathieu pushed south from Barcelona with 6,000, the two columns aiming to meet at Reus, just ten miles inland. Murray's anxiety curdled into panic. He issued secret withdrawal orders, then countermanded himself, then abandoned the assault entirely and ordered his stores and heavy guns back aboard ship, though his chief gunner warned the guns could not be moved in under thirty hours.

## Guns Left Spiked

It was all unnecessary. Suchet, hearing of a threat to Valencia, turned back; Mathieu brushed against Copons' outposts, saw he faced a combined army of 23,000, and withdrew northward. None of it reached Murray, who kept issuing contradictory orders that infuriated Admiral Hallowell and sowed chaos on the beach. By the night of 12 June the whole force had re-embarked, leaving the eighteen siege guns spiked and stores strewn behind. Copons, the loyal Spanish general, was simply advised to flee into the mountains. An astonished Bertoletti sent word to Mathieu that the coast was clear. Murray was not quite finished: he landed again at Balaguer, talked Copons into supporting him once more, then re-embarked the instant French troops appeared, abandoning his ally a second time.

## The Reckoning

On 18 June the Mediterranean Fleet came over the horizon, and Lord William Bentinck relieved Murray of command. The thwarted expedition sailed back to Alicante. The human cost had been almost trivial for such a large operation, fifteen Anglo-Allied dead against thirteen French, a casualty list that only underscored how little fighting there had been. The fiasco did not derail Wellington, whose campaign climaxed three days later in a crushing victory over King Joseph at the Battle of Vitoria. Murray faced a court-martial in 1814. He was acquitted on every charge but one, abandoning his guns without just cause, for which the court formally admonished him, a mild rebuke for a spectacular collapse of command.
