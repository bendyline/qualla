# Roman Theatre (Tarraco)

Built into a hillside under Augustus to seat a Roman provincial capital, Tarragona's Roman theatre is now watched only from a railing, five surviving rows of a stage that once held a whole city.

**Location:** 41.1128, 1.2494

**Categories:** Ancient Rome, Archaeology, World Heritage Sites, History, Architecture, Catalonia

## Introduction

It was designed to be watched, and now it is barely seen. Two thousand years after crowds filed up its stone steps to catch a comedy above the harbor, the Roman theatre of Tarraco sits behind a modern railing on Sant Magí street, glimpsed from a lookout by passersby who rarely stop. Much of it was destroyed in the twentieth century, and what endures stands without ceremony, a World Heritage monument left largely to weeds and time. Yet the bones are still legible: the curved seating, the flat orchestra, the raised stage, all carved into a slope by builders working at the height of Augustan Rome.

## Theater on a Hillside

The theatre rose at the end of the 1st century BC, in the age of Augustus, as Tarraco reshaped itself into a proper imperial capital. Its architects chose the site cleverly, setting it near the local forum and the port, in the busy lower city where people gathered anyway. Rather than raise the whole structure from level ground, they read the land and let it work for them, using the steep natural slope of the hill to carry much of the seating, exactly as the builders of the city's amphitheatre did nearby. Where the hillside ran out, they turned to engineering, supporting the remaining tiers on a system of ring-shaped vaulted passages called cryptoporticus.

## Where the City Watched

A Roman theatre was a machine for showing rank as much as drama. The design broke into three essential parts: the cavea, the sweep of seating; the orchestra, the semicircular space before the stage; and the scaena, the stage itself, raised on a podium and dressed with exedras. The audience did not sit at random. Seating followed a strict social and census-based hierarchy, so that where you sat announced who you were, the powerful settled close to the action and the humble banished to the upper tiers. Approaching the scaena, spectators passed through a landscaped entrance planted with gardens and centered on a pond, its statues standing on pedestals. The stage's facade, the proscaenium, was enclosed and richly worked, and behind the actors rose the frons scaenae, the ornately decorated wall that framed every performance in carved stone. A visit to the theatre was theatre in itself, a public display of who Tarraco's people were.

## Stone Robbed for Stone

The theatre's working life ran until the end of the 2nd century, when it stopped serving as a theatre and was put to other uses. Then, in the 3rd century, a fire swept through the compound, and the Romans did what practical people have always done with ruins: they mined them. New buildings went up in the monumental area beside the theatre, raised from materials salvaged out of the theatre itself. That slow cannibalization was only the first act of loss. The twentieth century proved far harsher, destroying much of what centuries had spared, so that the structure visitors see today is a fragment of the building that once dominated this corner of the lower city.

## Five Rows That Survived

What remains is precise and poignant. Only the first five rows of seating around the orchestra are still intact, along with two of the three radial staircases that once divided the cavea into wedges. The foundations of the pulpitum and the scaenae frons survive, and you can still find the sockets where the stage curtain's supports were once fixed. Excavation in and around the theatre has recovered capitals, friezes, columns, and sculptures, the scattered ornament of a vanished whole. There is an irony in its status. Since 2000 the theatre has been part of the Archaeological Ensemble of Tárraco, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, yet for years it stood largely neglected, without proper interpretation for the public. The site has waited long for the care its billing implies. Work is now underway to restore both the theatre and the constructions around it, and the viewpoint on Sant Magí street lets visitors read the ruin's shape at last.

## Flight Context

The Roman theatre lies in Tarragona's lower old city at 41.113N, 1.249E, close to the harbor front. From 2,500 to 5,000 feet over the coast, orient on the port of Tarragona and the amphitheatre set dramatically above the beach; the theatre is inland of it among the dense streets of the Part Baixa. Reus (LERS) is only about 6nm to the west-northwest, Barcelona (LEBL) roughly 50nm northeast, and Lleida-Alguaire (LEDA) about 50nm northwest. Clear Costa Daurada light makes the Roman city's coastal monuments stand out sharply from the sea.
