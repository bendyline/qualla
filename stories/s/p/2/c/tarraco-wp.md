# Tarraco

The oldest Roman city on the Iberian Peninsula grew so important that a whole Roman province was named after it, and its emperors, walls, and aqueduct still shape modern Tarragona.

**Location:** 41.1165, 1.2552

**Categories:** Ancient Rome, Archaeology, World Heritage Sites, History, Catalonia, Cities and towns

## Introduction

Most cities are named after their region. Tarraco did it the other way around. When Augustus redrew the map of Roman Spain in 27 BC, he called the vast new province Hispania Tarraconensis, after the port that governed it, and so a single hilltop town lent its name to a territory stretching across central and northern Hispania. That was the measure of the place. Tarraco was the oldest Roman settlement on the Iberian Peninsula, a war camp that became a capital, a harbor that convalescing emperors chose over Rome, and the seed from which the modern city of Tarragona grew.

## The Oldest Rome in Spain

Before Rome, this coast belonged to the Iberians, who had traded here with Greeks and Phoenicians since the 5th century BC. Rome arrived with the Second Punic War. In 218 BC, Gnaeus Cornelius Scipio Calvus landed up the coast at Empúries, and the Roman conquest of Hispania began at Tarraco's doorstep. The Romans seized a Punic supply depot near a place called Cissis, possibly Tarraco itself. Reinforcements came the next year under Publius Scipio, and he and his brother strengthened the town's fortifications and built a military port, raising Roman walls atop older Iberian stonework. After both Scipio brothers died in Spain, the young Scipio Africanus wintered here, and the loyal fishermen of Tarraco even sailed their boats to help besiege Carthago Nova.

## From War Camp to Capital

For two centuries Tarraco served as a supply base and winter quarters in Rome's grinding wars against the Celtiberians, its garrison likely perched in the high quarter still called the Part Alta. When Hispania was split in 197 BC, Tarraco lay in Hispania Citerior, and though Carthago Nova was the nominal capital, the geographer Strabo notes that governors resided here too. Its loyalties paid off. When Julius Caesar fought Pompey's supporters at Ilerda in 49 BC, Tarraco fed his army, and after his victory at Munda around 45 BC the city was likely raised to the rank of colonia, dressed in a grand official title it would keep for the life of the Empire: Colonia Iulia Urbs Triumphalis Tarraco.

## Augustus Slept Here

The city reached its height under Augustus, and not only in name. In 27 BC the emperor came to Spain to oversee the campaigns in Cantabria, but poor health kept him in Tarraco, effectively running the Roman world from a provincial port. A story preserved by Quintilian captures the man. When the townspeople proudly told Augustus that a palm tree had miraculously sprouted on an altar built in his honor, he replied drily that this only proved how seldom they lit fires on it. Under his rule the old via Herculea became the Via Augusta, the great road running the length of the peninsula, and the writer Pomponius Mela could call Tarraco the richest port on this coast.

## A City of Emperors

Tarraco kept making history. In 68 AD Galba, who had lived in the city for eight years, was proclaimed emperor, and under Vespasian the granting of Latin citizenship across Hispania spurred a building boom. The great monuments rose in this era, the amphitheatre by the shore, the temple precinct, and the vast Provincial forum crowning the upper city, most of their statues set in place between 70 and 180 AD. The city gave Rome one of its most powerful senators in Lucius Licinius Sura, right hand to the Emperor Trajan, and around 122 AD the Emperor Hadrian himself visited, holding a provincial assembly and rebuilding the temple of Augustus.

## Stones That Outlived the Empire

The ancient city faded slowly. Diocletian's reforms left Tarraco the capital of a shrunken province, raids by Franks and Alemanni bruised the harbor around 260, and in 476 the Visigoths under King Euric took the city, apparently without a fight. Its ancient life closed with the arrival of the Muslims in 713 or 714. Yet Tarraco never truly vanished. Today its archaeological ensemble, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2000, is among the largest of Roman Hispania: seaside amphitheatre, cyclopean walls with inscriptions in Latin and even Phoenician, the great double-tiered aqueduct known as the Devil's Bridge striding across a valley outside town, and the lonely funerary Tower of the Scipios standing guard to the northwest.

## Flight Context

Ancient Tarraco underlies modern Tarragona at 41.116N, 1.255E, on the Costa Daurada. From 3,000 to 6,000 feet the Roman city reveals itself through its monuments: the amphitheatre above the beach, the walled upper town, the harbor, and the Devil's Bridge aqueduct set inland to the north. Reus (LERS) lies about 6nm west-northwest, Barcelona (LEBL) roughly 50nm northeast, and Lleida-Alguaire (LEDA) some 50nm northwest, along the line of the old Via Augusta. Clear Mediterranean light typically gives long visibility over the coastal plain and its scattered Roman remains.
