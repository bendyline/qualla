# Siege of Lérida

In 1810, the French general Suchet broke Lleida not with cannon alone but by driving its own civilians under the castle walls, then bombarding them, in one of the Peninsular War's cruelest sieges.

**Location:** 41.6139, 0.6256

**Categories:** Sieges of the Peninsular War, Catalonia in the Peninsular War, Conflicts in 1810, Lleida, Napoleonic Wars

## Introduction

Louis Gabriel Suchet did not break Lerida with his cannons alone. He broke it by turning the city's own people into a weapon against the men sworn to protect them. In the spring of 1810, with the Peninsular War grinding across Spain, the French general laid siege to the fortress city on the Segre and, when the walls gave way, herded the trapped civilian population toward the citadel and shelled it while they crowded beneath. The Spanish commander, Major General Jaime Garcia Conde, surrendered rather than watch the slaughter continue. It was a victory won by design, and it opened a run of conquests that would make Suchet a Marshal of France.

## The Unstoppable General

Suchet had wanted Lerida for months. Early in 1810 he had planned to move against it, only to be ordered first toward Valencia, an expedition that fizzled for want of siege guns. By mid-April he was finally before the city with around 13,000 men, eighteen battalions and thirty artillery pieces, facing a fortress mounting 105 cannons. Inside stood roughly 8,000 Spanish defenders under Garcia Conde, no stranger to hard sieges; the year before, he had fought a 4,000-man convoy through the Italian lines to resupply besieged Gerona and slipped out again. The two commanders were about to test each other on ground that had absorbed armies for a thousand years.

## Ambush at Margalef

A city under siege lives or dies on the hope of relief, and Lerida's hope came marching under Major General Henry O'Donnell. Suchet learned of the approaching column and rode out to find it. On 23 April, near the hamlet of Margalef southeast of the city, O'Donnell's lead division blundered into the French. When the heavy horsemen of the 13th Cuirassiers charged into their flank, the Spanish formation disintegrated, the cavalry riding down the fleeing infantry in a butchery that a second Spanish division, arriving late, could not reverse. O'Donnell lost hundreds killed and wounded and thousands taken prisoner; the French lost a hundred, all cuirassiers. Lerida's best chance of rescue was gone before the formal siege had even begun.

## Behind the Breach

The city sat on the west bank of the Segre, with a bridgehead across the water and its defences climbing the heights. A hill to the north was crowned by the citadel, the old Castle of La Suda, while Fort Gardeny and two redoubts held the hill to the south. The wall on the north side, between citadel and river, was the weakest link, and Suchet knew it. The formal siege opened on 29 April; by 7 May his heavy batteries were in action, and within six days they had battered a breach. On the night of 12 to 13 May the French overran the southern redoubts, and late on the 13th their assault columns stormed the breach and carried it. Garcia Conde pulled his surviving soldiers back into the citadel and prepared for the worst.

## The Weapon of the Innocent

What followed was deliberate cruelty. Suchet ordered his soldiers to drive the civilian population of Lerida up under the castle's walls, killing anyone who resisted on the spot. These were families, the ordinary people of a besieged city, herded toward the guns of the citadel to force its commander's hand. When Garcia Conde opened the gates and let the non-combatants inside rather than leave them exposed, the French answered with high-angle fire, howitzers and mortars lobbing shells into the packed fortress. Most of the roughly 500 civilians who died in the siege were killed this way. Horrified as the shells struck down soldiers and townspeople together, Garcia Conde asked for terms at noon on 14 May. Seven thousand Spanish soldiers marched into captivity; the garrison had already lost 1,700 killed and wounded. The arithmetic of the surrender was written in the bodies of people who had never carried a weapon.

## The Sieges That Followed

Lerida was only the beginning. Within days Suchet's corps opened the siege of Mequinenza, which fell in early June, and from there the campaign rolled on with grim momentum: Tortosa capitulated in January 1811, then Tarragona fell after a savage assault that summer, the victory for which Napoleon made Suchet a Marshal of France. Saguntum surrendered that autumn, and in January 1812 the great prize of Valencia gave up more than 16,000 men. For a stretch of two years, Suchet's army seemed genuinely unstoppable along Spain's Mediterranean flank. Lerida's fall had shown the method that made it so, and the city's people had paid the first, terrible installment of the price.

## Flight Context

The siege unfolded around Lleida at 41.61 N, 0.63 E, on both banks of the Segre. The defining feature from the air is the pair of hills that framed the fight: the northern citadel hill crowned by the Seu Vella cathedral and the Castle of La Suda, and the southern hill of Gardeny, with the river threading between and the old town on the west bank. Lleida-Alguaire (LEDA) is the nearest field, about 15 km northwest; Reus (LERS) lies roughly 90 km southeast and Barcelona (LEBL) about 150 km east. Margalef, where the relief force was destroyed, lies a short way southeast. Best viewed from 3,000 to 4,000 feet in clear air; winter fog can blanket the plain.
