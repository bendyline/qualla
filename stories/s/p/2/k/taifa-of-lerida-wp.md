# Taifa of Lérida

A small Muslim kingdom on the Ebro frontier that survived by paying protection money to Christian counts, until the money ran out and the Aragonese advance closed in.

**Location:** 41.6167, 0.6167

**Categories:** Taifa kingdoms, Lleida, History of Aragon, 11th century in al-Andalus, Former monarchies of Europe

## Introduction

Every year, gold left Lerida heading north, and not as plunder. It went as payment. The Taifa of Lerida, a small Muslim kingdom on the northeastern edge of al-Andalus, spent much of its brief life buying its own survival, handing tribute to the Christian counts of Barcelona and Urgell in exchange for being left alone a little longer. Known in Arabic as Larida and to the Catalans as Lleida, the taifa flickered in and out of independence between 1039 and the early twelfth century, sometimes a kingdom in its own right, more often a province of mightier Zaragoza, always living in the lengthening shadow of the advancing north.

## A Kingdom That Bought Its Peace

The taifa's defining fact was its weakness, and the system that fact produced. When the great caliphate of Cordoba shattered in the early eleventh century, dozens of small successor states, the taifas, carved up Muslim Spain, and the strongest neighbours learned to extract tribute, the parias, from the weaker. From 1045, Count Ramon Berenguer I of Barcelona forced Lerida and its parent state to pay for the privilege of not being attacked. It was protection in the plainest sense. For the Christian counts of the north these payments were a steady, welcome income; for Lerida, they were simply the price of drawing breath. The taifa was rarely a fully independent state at all; for long stretches it was simply the eastern wing of the taifa of Zaragoza, administered by a governor while the real power sat further west.

## The Sons of Hud

For much of its life the taifa belonged to one restless dynasty. Under the late Cordoban caliphate, Lerida had been governed by the Banu Tujib; then in 1039 it was seized, along with Zaragoza, by al-Mustain of the Banu Hud. Before his death in 1046 he gave Lerida to a younger son, Yusuf al-Muzaffar, while the elder son al-Muqtadir took Zaragoza, and the brothers soon fell to quarrelling, blaming each other for the temporary loss of Barbastro to a Christian army in 1064. Al-Muqtadir eventually swallowed his brother's lands in a civil war, only for his own realm to be split again among his sons when he died in 1081. The son named al-Mundhir inherited Lerida, along with the perennial problem of holding it.

## The Frontier Closes In

As the northeasternmost of the Hudid states, Lerida stood directly in the path of a hardening Christian advance. King Sancho Ramirez of Aragon and his son Peter pushed steadily down the valley of the Cinca, taking Graus in 1083 and then Estada, Monzon, and Almenar over the following decade, while the count of Urgell struck toward Balaguer in 1089. When al-Mundhir died in 1090, his heir Sulayman was a child, and the regents promptly stripped away Denia and Tortosa for themselves, leaving him a rump Lerida. Peter, now king of Aragon, seized Barbastro and Sarinena in 1100 and Albalate de Cinca in 1101, bringing his soldiers to the doorstep of Lerida itself. The buffer was running out of room.

## Two Lords and the Almoravids

In its final years, Lerida paid tribute to two Christian counts at once, Ramon Berenguer III of Barcelona and Ermengol V of Urgell, a measure of how little it could defend itself. Paying two masters at the same time was a confession of helplessness, a small state buying time from every direction while its options narrowed year by year. Then a new force arrived to complicate everything: the Almoravids, the austere North African dynasty sweeping up the fragmented taifas in the name of a stricter Islam. In September 1102, Count Ermengol V was killed at the battle of Mollerussa fighting these Almoravids as they moved to subdue Lerida, and the city appears to have fallen to them that same year, though some accounts stretch its life to 1110. The Almoravids ruled it through appointed governors until the Catalans finally took Lerida for good in 1149, closing the long, precarious story of a kingdom that had outlived its own strength by paying, year after year, for one more season of peace.

## Flight Context

The Taifa of Lerida was centred on the city at 41.62 N, 0.62 E, commanding the Segre frontier of the Ebro basin. From the air, the fortified hill above modern Lleida, crowned by the Seu Vella cathedral and the ruined Castle of La Suda, marks the old Andalusi citadel that anchored the taifa. The surrounding plain fans out toward the Cinca and Ebro to the west and south, the very corridor the Aragonese advanced along. Lleida-Alguaire (LEDA) is the nearest field, about 15 km northwest; Reus (LERS) lies roughly 90 km southeast and Barcelona (LEBL) about 150 km east. Best viewed from 3,000 to 5,000 feet in the clear, dry air typical of the region outside the winter fog season.
