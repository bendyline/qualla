# Siege of Lleida (1123)

When Alfonso the Battler tried to strangle Muslim Lleida in 1123, the army that rode to save the city was Christian, led by the Count of Barcelona and the first troubadour of Europe.

**Location:** 41.6167, 0.6333

**Categories:** Sieges of the Reconquista, Sieges involving Aragon, Sieges involving the Almoravid Empire, 12th century in Aragon, Medieval Catalonia

## Introduction

The lines on this frontier did not run neatly between Christian and Muslim. When Alfonso of Aragon marched on Muslim Lleida in 1123, the army that rode to rescue the city was Christian, led by the Count of Barcelona and, remarkably, by William IX of Aquitaine, the earliest troubadour whose poems survive. The siege that unfolded that spring was less a clash of faiths than a contest of ambitions, in which alliances crossed every expected boundary and a king built a castle for the sole purpose of choking a city to death. It ended not in conquest but in a strange, half-remembered confrontation on the plain below the walls.

## A Frontier of Strange Alliances

By 1123, Alfonso the Battler was riding high. He had taken Zaragoza from the Muslims in 1118, and Lleida looked like a natural next prize. But the politics of the frontier had a logic all their own. For reasons now lost, Ramon Berenguer III, Count of Barcelona, had made himself the protector of Lleida's Muslim governor, Abu Hilal, who ceded castles to the count and paid him tribute in exchange for defence against exactly the kind of attack Alfonso was planning. Tribute of this kind, the parias, was the common currency of the frontier, and a Muslim ruler paying a Christian lord for protection was less a scandal than a standard arrangement of the age. On this border, a Christian count could be sworn to shield a Muslim city, and money and castles flowed across the religious divide as readily as armies did.

## The King Comes South

In February 1123, having first secured his western borders against the Leonese kingdom, Alfonso marched south to Lleida. The stakes were more than one city. Taking Lleida could mend and renew his alliance with the neighbouring County of Urgell, tightening the Christian grip on the whole Segre frontier. To press the siege, Alfonso raised a castle on a hill above the city and named it Gardeny, a fortified base from which he could dominate the ground below. For Alfonso, the campaign was the logical next move after Zaragoza, an attempt to push the reach of Aragon down the Segre toward the sea. The fort was a weapon aimed squarely at the town, a permanent shadow thrown over its walls and its people.

## Strangling a City

Alfonso's plan was strangulation rather than storm. From Gardeny his forces blockaded Lleida, cutting off the supplies that came up the Segre and trapping the inhabitants inside their own defences. It was a patient, pitiless kind of warfare, meant to let hunger do what assault might not. But the Almoravid garrison holding the city proved stubborn, refusing to break under the pressure, and every week the siege dragged on gave the city's protectors time to gather a response. Inside the walls, the trapped inhabitants could only wait and ration what they had, hoping the river blockade would break before their supplies did. A blockade only works if no one comes to lift it, and someone was coming.

## Battle or Tournament?

The relief force that arrived was a striking company. It was led by Ramon Berenguer III of Barcelona, the city's Christian patron, and by William IX, Duke of Aquitaine, a great lord of southern France and, in an age that prized the sword, the first named troubadour of Europe, a poet-warrior whose eleven surviving songs helped shape a literary tradition. What happened when they met Alfonso is oddly uncertain: the sources describe it as either a battle or a tournament, a real fight or a formalized passage of arms between proud lords. The ambiguity is telling: on this frontier, war and chivalric display could shade into one another, honour and interest settled in the same afternoon. Whatever it was, it settled the matter. Alfonso raised the siege in May 1123 and withdrew. His own fort at Gardeny did not last long after him — it was later abandoned and no trace of it remains — but the hill he had chosen kept its strategic value: it passed to the Count of Urgell within a generation, and in time to the Knights Templar, who built the castle that would stand on the site for centuries after the 1123 siege was forgotten.

## Flight Context

The 1123 siege centred on Lleida at 41.62 N, 0.63 E, on the Segre. Two hills tell the story from the air: the cathedral hill topped by the Seu Vella and the Castle of La Suda, and the lower hill of Gardeny to the southwest, where Alfonso built his siege castle. The river runs between them across the flat plain. Lleida-Alguaire (LEDA) is the nearest field, about 15 km northwest; Reus (LERS) lies roughly 90 km southeast and Barcelona (LEBL) about 150 km east. Best viewed from 3,000 to 4,000 feet in clear weather; winter mornings can fill the plain with fog while the hilltops stay clear.
