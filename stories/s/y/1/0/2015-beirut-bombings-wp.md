# 2015 Beirut Bombings

Two suicide bombers sent by ISIL attacked a busy commercial street in the Bourj el-Barajneh neighborhood of southern Beirut on November 12, 2015, killing 43 people and wounding more than 200 in the deadliest bombing in Beirut since the end of the civil war.

## Introduction

Adel Termos was walking with his daughter through the crowded streets of Bourj el-Barajneh when the first explosion tore through the neighborhood. It was just before 6:00 p.m. on November 12, 2015, a Thursday evening in a densely packed southern suburb of Beirut where shops were open and families were out. The first bomb detonated outside a Shia mosque. When Termos saw the second bomber approaching moments later, he tackled him. Both men died in the blast, but Termos's split-second decision almost certainly saved dozens of lives in the crowd that had rushed toward the first explosion to help the wounded.

## The War Next Door

By November 2015, the Syrian civil war had been raging for more than four years, and its violence had been bleeding across the border into Lebanon with increasing frequency. Lebanese factions had aligned themselves along the same sectarian divisions that fractured Syria. Hezbollah was fighting openly on the side of the Assad regime, while Sunni militant groups -- including the Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant -- saw Lebanon's Shia communities as legitimate targets. Bourj el-Barajneh, a working-class neighborhood in Beirut's southern suburbs, was home to a predominantly Shia Muslim population and had long been associated with Hezbollah's political base. Lebanon itself had been without a president since April 2014, paralyzed by the same sectarian gridlock that made governance nearly impossible. Into this vacuum, ISIL projected its violence.

## Five Minutes Apart

The attack was designed for maximum casualties. The first bomb went off outside a mosque, drawing crowds of people rushing to help. The second detonated about twenty meters away, five to seven minutes later, targeting those first responders and bystanders. A third attacker was killed before he could detonate his vest -- his body was found with legs torn off but the explosive belt still intact, apparently caught in the second blast's radius. The original plan, investigators later learned, had been to send five suicide bombers to a hospital in the neighborhood, but heavy security forced a change of target to the commercial street. The Health Ministry reported 43 dead and 239 wounded. Hospitals in the area issued emergency calls for blood donations. Emergency workers pleaded with bystanders to clear the streets so ambulances could reach the injured.

## The Man Who Tackled a Bomber

In the chaos between the two explosions, Adel Termos made a choice that defined the tragedy. A father and resident of Beirut, Termos saw the second bomber moving toward the gathering crowd and threw himself at the man. The bomb detonated on impact, killing both of them instantly. By intercepting the bomber before he reached the densest part of the crowd, Termos absorbed the force of the blast that would otherwise have struck dozens more people. On social media, he was immediately hailed as a hero. His story resonated far beyond Lebanon, becoming one of the defining human moments of a year saturated with terrorist violence. Among the other victims were two staff members of the American University of Beirut and a senior figure in Hezbollah's security apparatus. The dead were shopkeepers, students, worshippers, and people who had simply been walking home.

## Overshadowed by Paris

ISIL claimed responsibility, stating that two Palestinians and a Syrian had carried out the attacks. Within days, Lebanese and Syrian security forces arrested eleven suspects, most of them Syrian nationals picked up in a Palestinian refugee camp in Bourj el-Barajneh and a flat in the eastern Achrafieh district. Connections were drawn to a bombing in Arsal that had killed four people on November 5. But the global attention that the Beirut bombings might have commanded was eclipsed almost immediately. The very next day, November 13, ISIL launched coordinated attacks across Paris that killed 130 people. The disparity in international coverage became its own story -- a painful reminder that violence in some parts of the world generates more Western attention than violence in others. Lebanese Prime Minister Tammam Salam declared November 13 a day of national mourning, but the mourning was overshadowed by the horror unfolding in France.

## The Persistence of a Neighborhood

Bourj el-Barajneh is not a place that appears on tourist itineraries. It is a dense, working-class neighborhood built around a Palestinian refugee camp, its narrow streets lined with shops and apartments stacked haphazardly on top of one another. From the air, it is part of the sprawling southern suburb of Beirut that stretches inland from the coast -- a landscape of flat-roofed concrete buildings packed tightly together, intersected by roads that seem too narrow for the traffic they carry. Hezbollah's leader Hassan Nasrallah declared that the bombings would only increase his movement's determination to fight in Syria. The neighborhood itself simply continued. Shops reopened, the mosque was repaired, and the daily rhythms of a community accustomed to violence reasserted themselves with the quiet resilience that has characterized southern Beirut through decades of conflict.
