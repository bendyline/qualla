# 2025 Homs Mosque Bombing

A bombing during Friday prayers at an Alawite mosque in Homs exposed the fragile fault lines of post-Assad Syria, killing eight worshippers and igniting protests across the country.

**Location:** 34.7025, 36.7275

**Categories:** terrorism, syrian-civil-war, sectarian-violence, modern-history, middle-east

## Introduction

Friday prayers had just been called at the Imam Ali ibn Abi Talib Mosque in Homs's Wadi al-Dahab neighborhood when the explosion tore through the western wall. It was 26 December 2025, barely a year after the fall of the Assad regime, and the blast killed eight people and wounded eighteen more in what became one of the deadliest sectarian attacks of Syria's turbulent transition. The mosque served the Alawite community -- a religious minority that had dominated Syrian politics for decades under the Assads and now found itself vulnerable in ways it had not been for generations.

## Between the Call and the Prayer

An eyewitness told Al Jazeera Arabic that the explosion struck between the adhan, the call to prayer, and the iqama, the second call that signals the start of communal worship. That narrow window -- perhaps five minutes, when worshippers are settling into rows, greeting neighbors, quieting children -- was chosen deliberately. The attacker had left a bomb-laden bag inside the mosque. Saraya Ansar al-Sunnah, an extremist group formed shortly after the Assad regime collapsed in December 2024, claimed responsibility on Telegram, boasting of detonating several explosive devices and warning that operations would "continue to escalate." The group had already carried out a suicide bombing inside a church in Damascus in June 2025. Syria's Ministry of Interior suggested the Islamic State may also have been involved.

## A City on Edge

Homs had been fracturing along sectarian lines for months. After decades of Assad rule -- during which Alawites held disproportionate power in the military and intelligence services -- the community found itself a target of reprisals. The Syrian Observatory for Human Rights documented multiple episodes of communal violence in and around Homs during the post-Assad period, including retaliatory killings of Alawite civilians. The mosque bombing did not emerge from a vacuum. Tensions had been escalating through the autumn of 2025, with a killing of a Bedouin couple in November sparking further sectarian clashes. For the Alawite residents of Wadi al-Dahab, the bombing confirmed their worst fears about what the end of the Assad era would mean for their safety.

## Condemnation and Fury

The reaction was swift and layered. Syrian government ministries -- Foreign Affairs, Endowments, Interior, Justice, Information -- issued coordinated statements condemning what they called a cowardly terrorist act. The Supreme Alawite Islamic Council described the bombing as part of a broader campaign of violence against Alawites. Internationally, the condemnation was near-universal: Bahrain, France, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Palestine, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and the United Arab Emirates all denounced the attack. The Arab League, the Gulf Cooperation Council, and the United Nations added their voices. But official statements did little to calm the anger building in Alawite communities along the Syrian coast.

## The Streets Respond

Two days after the bombing, on 28 December, thousands of Alawites took to the streets in Tartus and Latakia. Sheikh Ghazal Ghazal of the Supreme Alawite Islamic Council had called for the demonstrations, and the turnout was massive -- a display of grief and defiance that quickly outpaced its organizers. In Latakia, protesters set fire to vehicles belonging to security forces, and clashes between demonstrators and police left two people dead. In Tartus, someone threw a hand grenade at a police station, injuring two security personnel. What had begun as mourning transformed into something rawer: a community demanding protection from a new government it did not fully trust, in a country still learning whether its post-Assad future could accommodate the very people most associated with the old regime.

## Wounds Within Wounds

Syria's transition was always going to be perilous. The Assad family had governed for over five decades, and the sectarian architecture of their rule -- Alawite officers in the military, Alawite agents in the intelligence services -- meant that the fall of the regime did not simply change who held power. It rearranged the entire social order. For the Alawite community, the mosque bombing crystallized a particular dread: that they would be held collectively responsible for the sins of a regime most of them had not chosen. In January 2026, Syrian authorities announced the arrest of two members of the Islamic State in connection with the attack. Whether justice, when it came, could do anything to heal the deeper fractures remained an open question -- one that Homs, a city that had already endured years of siege during the civil war, was wearily familiar with asking.
