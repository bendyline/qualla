# Rhodes (City)

Layer upon layer, Rhodes' old city preserves Hospitaller fortifications, Ottoman minarets, and Italian-era reconstructions inside the most complete medieval town wall in Europe.

**Location:** 36.4333, 28.2167

**Categories:** Greek cities, Medieval cities, UNESCO World Heritage, Knights Hospitaller, Ottoman architecture, Dodecanese

## Introduction

Three cities, founded separately in the second millennium BC, decided in 408 BC to combine into one. Lindos, Ialyssos, and Kamiros surrendered their independence and laid out a new capital on the northern point of the island, with a grid plan that would shape the place for the next two and a half millennia. The Romans came and adopted Rhodian maritime law for their own shipping. The Knights Hospitaller arrived in 1309 and built the walls that still ring the old city. The Ottomans took the place in 1522, and Italian colonial architects rebuilt parts of it in the 1920s and 1930s. Walk through the medieval gate today and every century you cross was put there on purpose.

## The Knights Build Their Citadel

When the Hospitallers arrived in 1309, fresh from the Pope's confirmation of their ownership of the island, they began constructing one of the strongest fortified towns in the eastern Mediterranean. The Street of the Knights still runs in a straight line through the upper town, lined with the inns of the order's seven national "tongues": France, Provence, Auvergne, Italy, Aragon, England, Germany. The Grand Master's Palace dominates the high ground, rebuilt by Italian engineers in the 1930s after a catastrophic 1856 explosion. By 1521, when Grand Master Pierre d'Aubusson died, Rhodes possessed defenses unmatched by any Christian outpost in the world, including underground passages running to the moat that could be detonated to seal off advancing besiegers.

## Twice Besieged, Once Lost

In 1444, a Mamluk fleet under Aynal Gecut laid siege to the city. The Knights, helped by the Burgundian commander Geoffroy de Thoisy, beat off the attack within weeks. In 1480, after the Fall of Constantinople had brought the Ottomans to a new pitch of expansion, Sultan Mehmed II sent Mesih Pasha with an invasion force. The defenders held both landward and seaward sides, the Ottomans withdrew defeated, and a planned simultaneous Ottoman invasion of Italy collapsed in the wake. Then came 1522, and Suleiman the Magnificent in person, with an army the Knights had no realistic hope of matching. They held out for months and inflicted heavy casualties before negotiating terms. On January 1, 1523, the surviving Knights sailed out of Rhodes carrying their wealth, headed eventually for Malta, leaving the city to the sultan.

## Ottoman Layer, Italian Layer

Under the Ottomans, the Greek population had to leave the walled city and resettle outside. Most churches became mosques. The Knights' palaces became Ottoman mansions, and the medieval facades acquired enclosed wooden balconies over the narrow streets along with hammams tucked behind. Most of the Hospitaller stonework was preserved through this conversion rather than destroyed. Then in 1856, lightning struck a gunpowder magazine stored under the old Church of Saint John, possibly since 1522. The explosion killed many people and destroyed most of the Grand Master's Palace. After Italy seized the Dodecanese in 1912, the architect Florestano Di Fausto laid out a new master plan in 1923, restoring the medieval town and creating a green belt where the Jewish and Ottoman cemeteries had been.

## 1944 and What Was Lost

In July 1944, with the war turning against them, the Nazi authorities ordered the deportation of the Jewish community of Rhodes. Over 1,700 men, women, and children were forced onto a journey that became one of the longest deportations to Auschwitz in the entire Holocaust. About 1,200 of them were murdered there. The community had lived in Rhodes for two thousand years, becoming an important Sephardi center after the Ottoman conquest, with rabbis whose work was read across the Mediterranean diaspora. The Turkish Consul Selahattin Ülkümen managed to save 42 Jewish families with Turkish citizenship, about 200 people, at considerable risk to himself. La Juderia, the Jewish quarter, still stands inside the walls. The Kahal Shalom Synagogue, built in 1577, is the oldest synagogue in Greece and remains in use.

## The Modern City Around the Old

Today the city is the capital of the South Aegean region and home to roughly 56,000 permanent residents, swelling to over 100,000 in summer. The medieval town was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1988 and is one of the best-preserved walled cities in Europe. Cruise ships anchor at the Central port; ferries to the Cyclades, mainland Greece, Crete, and Marmaris on the Turkish coast leave from Akandia and Kolona. Diagoras International Airport, named after the Olympic boxer of ancient Rhodes, sits 14 km southwest of the city. From the moat, you can still see the gate of St. Anastasius, where Suleiman entered in 1522. The bronze deer statues at Mandraki harbor mark the spot where, by tradition, the Colossus of Rhodes once straddled the entrance, though the actual position of that lost wonder remains a guess.

## Flight Context

Rhodes city sits at 36.43°N, 28.22°E on the northern tip of Rhodes island. The medieval walled town with its distinctive star-shaped fortifications and the Grand Master's Palace dominate the approach. Nearest airport is Diagoras International (ICAO: LGRP) about 14 km southwest. Best viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 ft. The three Mandraki harbor windmills and Fort Saint Nicolas form distinctive landmarks at the northern point.
