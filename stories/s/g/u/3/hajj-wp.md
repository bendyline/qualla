# Hajj

A pilgrimage whose rituals trace back to Abraham, formalized by Muhammad in 632 CE: the fifth pillar of Islam, performed once in a lifetime by every Muslim who is able.

**Location:** 21.4225, 39.8262

**Categories:** Hajj, Islamic pilgrimages, Five Pillars of Islam, Culture in Mecca

## Introduction

Scholars date the present sequence of Hajj to a single pilgrimage made by Muhammad in 632 CE - his first and his last. He performed it on 9 Dhu al-Hijjah 10 AH, a Friday that corresponded, in the Gregorian calendar, to 9 March 632. He instructed the pilgrims around him in the rites, delivered what Muslims know as the Farewell Sermon at Arafat, and died a few months later. From that moment the Hajj became a pillar of Islam - a duty for every capable adult Muslim, once in a lifetime. But the ritual's mythic core reaches back far earlier, to a family story about Abraham and his son Ishmael and a desperate mother named Hagar.

## Hagar's Seven Runs

The Quran tells the story in fragments, completed by tradition. Abraham was commanded by God to leave Hagar and the infant Ishmael in the desert of what would become Mecca, with little food and water. When the water ran out, Hagar ran seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwah, searching for anything - a caravan, a shepherd, a spring. She found nothing. Returning in despair to Ishmael, she saw him scratching the ground with his foot; a spring of water broke forth beneath him. That spring is Zamzam, still flowing inside the Grand Mosque today, still drunk by pilgrims. When Ishmael grew up, Abraham returned and, with his son, built the Kaaba - the cube-shaped sanctuary at the center of the Grand Mosque. The pilgrim's Sa'i, walking seven times back and forth between Safa and Marwah, is a literal reenactment of Hagar's desperate search. It remembers a woman.

## The Rites in Sequence

The Hajj unfolds over five to six days, from the 8th to the 12th or 13th of Dhu al-Hijjah. Pilgrims enter ihram - unstitched white cloth for men, modest white covering for women - and make their intention. They perform Tawaf, walking counter-clockwise seven times around the Kaaba. They kiss or point to the Black Stone mounted in the Kaaba's eastern corner, which tradition says the archangel Gabriel brought from Heaven for Abraham. They perform Sa'i between Safa and Marwah. They travel to Mina, to Arafat for the day-long vigil that Muhammad described as the essence of Hajj, to Muzdalifah to gather pebbles, to Mina again for the Stoning of the Devil - throwing seven pebbles at each of three pillars that represent Satan's attempts to tempt Abraham away from sacrificing his son. They offer an animal sacrifice, commemorating Abraham's willingness and God's substitution. They shave or trim their hair. They return for a final Tawaf. Across all of it, they are sharing the rites with millions of others, identically dressed, speaking dozens of languages.

## The Lunar Wheel

The Islamic calendar is lunar. Its year is about eleven days shorter than the Gregorian solar year, which means Hajj drifts earlier each Gregorian year - through every season over a thirty-three-year cycle. In 2024 (1445 AH), Dhu al-Hijjah fell from 7 June to 6 July. In 2025 (1446 AH) it ran 28 May to 25 June. In 2026 (1447 AH), it will span 18 May to 15 June. Hajj can thus occur in cool winters or in Arabia's killing summer heat, when temperatures in Mecca routinely exceed 45 degrees Celsius. Because the calendar shifts, the Hajj season can in rare years fall twice within a single Gregorian year - the last time in 2006, the next not for another generation.

## Caravans and the Road

For most of Hajj's history, getting to Mecca was the hard part. Pilgrims assembled in the great cities - Damascus, Cairo, Baghdad, Istanbul - and set off in caravans of tens of thousands, with a military escort commanded by an amir al-hajj and physicians to tend the sick. They followed ancient roads, some of them predating Islam by millennia - the King's Highway in the Transjordan, the Darb Zubaydah across the Najd. A caravan from Cairo might take forty days to reach Mecca, under the Mamluk and then the Ottoman sultans who understood that protecting the hajj was one of the principal legitimacies of their rule. Medieval pilgrim-scholars like Ibn Jubayr of al-Andalus and Ibn Battuta of Tangier left travelogues that preserve what those journeys felt like - the fear of Bedouin raids, the waterless stages, the wonder of arriving.

## Two Billion People, One Place

Islam today counts roughly two billion adherents. Only a small fraction can afford the journey, secure a visa, or survive the crowds in any given year - but for those who make it, the Hajj is a moment that concentrates an entire faith. The sight, repeated at every Tawaf, of people from every nation dressed identically, praying identically, moving together around a small stone cube - that is what pilgrims come home trying to describe. Saudi Arabia has invested billions in expanding the sanctuaries, building metro lines, rebuilding the Jamarat into a multi-level bridge to reduce stampedes. The expansions have not been without controversy - critics note that Ottoman-era buildings have been replaced with steel towers, that commerce has crept close to sanctity. But for those who have stood at Arafat on the afternoon of the 9th of Dhu al-Hijjah and heard the collective cry of labbayk allahumma labbayk - "Here I am, God, here I am" - the architecture seems, for a moment, beside the point.

## Flight Context

Mecca at 21.42°N, 39.83°E in Saudi Arabia's Hejaz region. Mecca airspace is closed to all aircraft. Pilgrim traffic is staged through King Abdulaziz International (OEJN) in Jeddah 70 km west. During Hajj season hundreds of wide-body flights land at the dedicated Hajj Terminals. Medina's Prince Mohammad Bin Abdulaziz International (OEMA) is the secondary pilgrim gateway.
