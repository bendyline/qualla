# South Sudan

The world's newest widely-recognized country is a land of Nilotic peoples, the enormous Sudd swamp, and a population that voted 98.83 percent for independence in 2011 - only to plunge into civil war two years later, and to keep rebuilding through floods, famine, and fragile peace.

**Location:** 7.0000, 30.0000

**Categories:** South Sudan, country, Nilotic peoples, Nile, conflict

## Introduction

Ninety-eight point eight three percent. That is the share of voters who chose independence in the January 2011 referendum that created South Sudan - one of the most decisive political verdicts in the history of the modern world. On 9 July 2011, the country raised its flag in Juba and became the 54th independent nation in Africa, the 193rd member of the United Nations, and the newest sovereign state with widespread international recognition. Two and a half years later, it descended into a civil war that would kill roughly 400,000 people and displace more than four million. This is the story of how a place with the lowest Human Development Index ranking on Earth keeps refusing, against every reasonable prediction, to give up on itself.

## Nilotic Homelands and a Giant Swamp

South Sudan's population of about 12.7 million in 2024 is composed mostly of Nilotic peoples: the Dinka (roughly 40 percent), the Nuer (roughly 20 percent), the Azande, the Shilluk, the Bari, the Murle, the Anuak, the Mundari, the Toposa, the Lotuka, and dozens more. These communities first entered the region sometime before the tenth century, coinciding with the fall of medieval Nubia; later migrations from the 15th to 19th centuries settled the Dinka and Nuer across the Bahr el Ghazal and Upper Nile floodplains, and brought the Azande kingdom into Equatoria. The landscape that shaped them all is defined by the Nile. Running south to north through the middle of the country is the Sudd - one of the largest wetlands on Earth, a vast grass swamp of 57,000 square kilometers that hosts over 400 bird species and some of the continent's last great antelope migrations. South of the Sudd lies the Boma-Jonglei Landscape, home to the white-eared kob and tiang whose migrations rival those of the Serengeti.

## Two Civil Wars Before There Was a Country

South Sudan's modern history is a history of civil wars that predate its existence as a state. The first Sudanese civil war ran from 1955 to 1972, fought between the government in Khartoum and the Anyanya rebel army - Anya-Nya being the Madi word for snake venom. The second lasted from 1983 to 2005, pitting the Sudan People's Liberation Army/Movement under John Garang against Khartoum, and killing more than 2.5 million people while displacing millions more. British colonial policy had developed the Arabized north and mostly ignored the non-Arab south, leaving it without schools, hospitals, or roads. After Sudanese independence in 1956, Khartoum's continued neglect of the south turned long-simmering grievance into the longest civil war on the African continent. The 2005 Comprehensive Peace Agreement opened a path to the referendum, but the cost of getting there - for the Dinka, Nuer, Shilluk, Anuak, Murle, Bari, Mundari, and all the others caught in it - was almost unspeakable.

## The Civil War That Should Not Have Happened

On 15 December 2013, a political power struggle inside the ruling Sudan People's Liberation Movement erupted into armed conflict, with President Salva Kiir accusing his former Vice-President Riek Machar of attempting a coup. The rivalry was political, but it quickly became communal - government soldiers targeting Nuer civilians, rebels targeting Dinka. The 2014 Bentiu massacre, in which hundreds of civilians sheltering in a mosque, a church, and a hospital were killed, was only the most visible of many atrocities documented by the UN Commission on Human Rights in South Sudan. An estimated 400,000 people were killed; more than four million displaced - 1.8 million internally, another 2.5 million as refugees in Uganda, Sudan, Ethiopia, and beyond. A coalition government was finally sworn in on 22 February 2020, with Machar returning as First Vice-President. The fighting has not fully stopped; in 2025 violence resurged, with Machar again arrested and the UN Secretary-General warning that "the peace agreement is in shambles." And still the country persists.

## Famine, Flood, and the Weight of Everything

On 20 February 2017, the United Nations declared famine in parts of Unity and Northern Bahr el Ghazal states - a formal declaration that by definition meant people were already dying of hunger. The World Food Programme reported that 67 percent of the South Sudanese population - 4.9 million people - needed urgent food assistance. More than a million children were acutely malnourished. The famine was driven by war, not by failed rains: displacement had prevented planting; markets had collapsed; currency hyperinflation peaked at 380 percent. In the years since, floods have overlapped with drought, cholera outbreaks have followed disruption, and climate change has pushed weather patterns further from predictability. The country has the third-highest military spending as a share of GDP in the world. Its maternal mortality is the highest. Its female literacy is the lowest. Its oil reserves are among the region's largest. Its future is being negotiated daily, between parties who have failed before, in a country whose people have every right to demand that this time be different.

## What South Sudan Is Anyway

Strip away the geopolitics and the war statistics and you are left with the country itself: a green, flooded, cattle-defined land where more than sixty indigenous languages have constitutional status, where Juba Arabic is the street lingua franca, where English became the official language only in 1972 and still remains a minority first language. Seventy percent of the population is under thirty. The Dinka, Nuer, Shilluk, Bari, Anuak, Zande, Murle, and dozens of other peoples trace their histories back centuries in this land, through cattle camps and migration routes and lineages whose names their elders still recite from memory. Juba, the capital on the White Nile, has grown rapidly since independence into a chaotic, vital city of markets, NGO compounds, universities, and neighborhoods that show the scars of war alongside the architecture of hope. South Sudan has been called many things - the world's youngest country, its least developed, its most troubled - but what it is, most fundamentally, is the homeland of people who have survived enormous things and are still here, still teaching their children the old names for the rivers.

## Flight Context

Country spans roughly 3.5°N to 12°N and 24°E to 36°E. Capital Juba at 4.85°N, 31.60°E; Juba International Airport (HSSJ) is the primary gateway. Major regional airports at Malakal (HSSM), Wau (HSWW), and Rumbek (HSMK). Terrain ranges from Equatoria's foothills and forested mountains in the south to Bahr el Ghazal's floodplains and the vast Sudd wetland in the center, to the oil country of Upper Nile in the north. Recommended cruising altitudes FL350+ for continental overview; FL200-FL300 for regional detail. The Sudd is visually striking from altitude especially during wet season (May-November).
