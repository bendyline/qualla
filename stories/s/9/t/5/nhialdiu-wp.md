# Nhialdiu

A Leek Nuer village at a strategic crossroads in Unity State, Nhialdiu has absorbed wave after wave of displaced neighbors while its own farmlands went quiet under the shadow of oil-road warfare.

**Location:** 9.0231, 29.6792

**Categories:** South Sudan, Unity State, Nuer, conflict

## Introduction

The roads matter here. Nhialdiu sits at a junction of dirt tracks in Rubkona County, about 40 kilometers southwest of Bentiu, and almost everything that has happened to this Leek Nuer village since the late 1990s can be read through what moved along those roads - soldiers, cattle raiders, oil company convoys, terrified families dragging bundles behind them. Between 1997 and 1998, roughly 70 percent of its residents fled to Bentiu and Mankien when fighting closed in. They came back. They fled again. And still the village endures.

## Leek Nuer Country

Nhialdiu lies in the heart of Leek Nuer country, in the Greater Upper Nile region of South Sudan, a landscape where cattle, kinship, and the rhythm of the rains define daily life more than any border drawn by outsiders. The Leek are one of the western Nuer groups, long settled around the cattle camps and seasonal grazing lands that stretch toward the White Nile. Before the war reshaped everything, the economy here moved with the cows: dry-season pastures along the toich, wet-season retreats to higher ground, births and marriages paced by herd wealth. That older rhythm has not disappeared. It has simply been forced to share the ground with younger, more violent rhythms imposed by outside forces who wanted something else from this land.

## When the Oil Roads Came

Satellite data tells a quiet version of a loud story. Between 1999 and 2002, Landsat imagery showed farming stopping in bands extending ten kilometers on either side of the newly built oil roads through Block 5A. Traditional fields were abandoned. Planting drifted south and west, away from the pipelines and the pickup trucks that moved along them. By 2002, no farming activity at all could be seen around Nhialdiu. Villages had been attacked for three years running. The health center was destroyed. Between July and August 2000, a long stretch of country between Nimne and Nhialdiu was depopulated and burned. The crops did not fail. The farmers simply were not there anymore, because staying had become more dangerous than leaving.

## The Peace That Wasn't Quite Peace

The civil war formally ended in January 2005, but Nhialdiu kept receiving people who had nowhere else to go. On 23 March 2005, Leek Nuer raiders struck cattle camps around the village, killing twenty people and driving off 24,000 head of cattle - a number that reads less like livestock accounting and more like a measure of displaced lives, because cattle here are dowry, savings, identity, and food all at once. Six thousand to ten thousand civilians took refuge in Nhialdiu itself. When the Bul launched a counterattack on the Leek, more civilians fled in. In August 2011, a public bus running between Bentiu and Nhialdiu hit landmines, killing two and wounding six, the government blaming a militia linked to Gai Yoach. By 2010, the Primary Health Care Center was staffed by qualified nurses, but short on vaccines and medicine - a sentence that captures an entire decade of the post-war condition.

## What Endures

There is no monument at Nhialdiu's crossroads, no museum of what its people have been through. The village simply keeps functioning as a gathering point, a place where relatives take in relatives and strangers are fed because that is what Nuer custom requires. The resilience is unromantic. It looks like a nurse at a half-stocked clinic, a household that absorbs cousins from a burned village, a cattle camp rebuilt on the ashes of the last one. From the air, Nhialdiu is a small cluster of tukuls and iron-roofed buildings in a vast green-brown plain. From the ground, it is the place that people keep coming back to, even when everything around it has pushed them to leave.
