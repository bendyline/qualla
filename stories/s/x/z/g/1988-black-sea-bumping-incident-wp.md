# 1988 Black Sea Bumping Incident

On a February morning off the Crimean coast, two Soviet warships deliberately steered into two American ones, turning a dispute over a sentence of maritime law into hull-on-hull contact.

**Location:** 44.2500, 33.5900

**Categories:** cold-war, maritime-history, naval, black-sea

## Introduction

At 10:02 on the morning of 12 February 1988, a Soviet frigate did something navies almost never do on purpose: it steered its steel hull directly into another warship. The target was the American destroyer USS Caron, cruising a little more than ten nautical miles off the southern coast of Crimea. Minutes later a second Soviet frigate shouldered into the cruiser USS Yorktown. Overhead, Soviet Tu-16 bombers watched the whole thing. No shots were fired, no one was killed, yet for a few tense minutes the Cold War was being fought not with missiles but with paint, plating, and the sheer momentum of ships pressed against ships.

## A Quarrel Over One Sentence

The confrontation grew from a genuine legal dispute. The United States held that a coastal nation had no basis to confine passing warships to narrow sea lanes, and sent Yorktown and Caron to assert the right of innocent passage through Soviet territorial waters. The Soviets disagreed. What made the argument almost surreal was where it eventually landed: the U.S. State Department later found that the Russian and English texts of the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea did not match. The Russian version of Article 22 let a coastal state regulate innocent passage whenever necessary; the English version did not. Two superpowers had brought warships into contact over a discrepancy between translations of a single paragraph.

## Steel Against Steel

The tactic had been discussed at the highest levels. After an earlier 1986 encounter involving the same two American ships, Soviet Navy chief Vladimir Chernavin had proposed to Mikhail Gorbachev and other senior officials that intruding warships could be driven off by several means, bumping among them. The Bezzavetny that carried out the order against Yorktown was, by its own commander Captain Vladimir Bogdashin's account, half the American cruiser's length and only a third its displacement, armed with two cruise missiles rather than four. It was, in effect, a smaller ship deliberately leaning on a larger one. Yorktown reported minor damage to its hull, with two Harpoon missile canisters struck, but no holing and no risk of flooding. Caron, shouldered by the frigate SKR-6, came away undamaged.

## The Sea After the Bump

For all its danger, the incident ended not in escalation but in agreement. Soon afterward the two governments issued a joint statement on the uniform acceptance of the rules governing innocent passage, each acknowledging the other's right to transit territorial waters. A collision that could have spiraled instead helped settle the very question that provoked it. The waters where it happened, off Crimea's southern tip near Cape Sarych, would see their share of later tension, but on that February morning the two navies stepped back from the edge. The lasting image is a small frigate and a large cruiser grinding together on a gray sea, restraint measured in dented plating rather than gunfire.

## Flight Context

The incident occurred at roughly 44.25 degrees north, 33.59 degrees east, in open water off Crimea's southern coast near Cape Sarych, about ten nautical miles offshore. From 5,000 to 8,000 feet the dark headlands of the southern Crimean shore and the lighthouse at Sarych make the best reference points against the open Black Sea. The nearest airfields are at Sevastopol (Belbek, UKFB) and Simferopol (UKFF) to the north.
