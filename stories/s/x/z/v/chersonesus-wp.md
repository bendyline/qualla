# Chersonesus

For two and a half thousand years this Greek colony on the Black Sea passed from democracy to empire to empire - and left behind a stone oath, sworn by its citizens, that still survives to be read.

**Location:** 44.6113, 33.4925

**Categories:** Archaeology, Ancient History, UNESCO World Heritage, Sevastopol

## Introduction

Around 2,500 years ago, colonists from the Greek city of Heraclea Pontica sailed across the Black Sea and founded a town on a rocky spur of the southwestern Crimean coast. They named it plainly: Chersonesos, the Greek word for peninsula. What they built would outlast their own civilization by two millennia, passing in turn from Greek democracy to Roman garrison, from Byzantine outpost to medieval principality, its harbor and its walls fought over by Scythians, Huns, Goturks, and Genoese. Today its ruins lie on the outskirts of Sevastopol, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2013, where toppled columns and the foundations of an ancient basilica still trace the outline of one of the longest-lived cities the classical world ever produced.

## A Democracy at the Edge of the World

For much of its early life, Chersonesos governed itself. Through the Classical period it operated as a democracy, ruled by elected officials called archons and a council, on the very frontier of the Greek world - a small city clinging to good deep-water harbors at the edge of the territory of the indigenous Taurians. Its citizens took their self-rule seriously enough to carve it into stone. The Oath of the Citizens of Chersonesos, dating to the third century BC, still survives, its inscribed lines a promise to defend the democracy and the city's freedom. In time the government drifted toward oligarchy, power concentrating in fewer hands, and the city expanded up the western coast, absorbing the colony of Kerkinitida and ringing itself with fortifications against the pressures of the steppe.

## Passed From Empire to Empire

Chersonesos survived by knowing when to bend. Threatened by the Bosporan Kingdom and the surrounding Scythians and Tauri, it called around 110 BC for the help of the powerful king Mithradates VI, and fell under Bosporan sway. Then came Rome, which garrisoned the city from the first century BC until the Huns took it in the 370s AD. It rose again as a Byzantine possession, withstanding a siege by the Goturks in 581, ruled lightly by a small imperial garrison that left the town much to its own devices. Century after century the flags changed above its walls - Byzantine, then the medieval Principality of Theodoro, then Genoese merchants - yet the city endured, a survivor perched between the sea and the vast grasslands to the north.

## The Baptism That Made a Nation

The city's most consequential moment may have come in 988. By the account in the oldest Rus chronicle, Chersonesos - which the Rus called Korsun - fell to Vladimir the Great of Kievan Rus, who agreed to give it up only if the Byzantine emperor's sister were given to him in marriage. As a condition of that marriage, Vladimir was baptized here, opening the way to the Christianization of Kievan Rus. Historians debate the details, since Greek sources are silent, but the tradition held firm. Nineteen centuries after the Greeks founded the town, the Russian Empire raised the great St. Vladimir Cathedral on a hill above the ruins to mark the spot, ensuring that this one moment in the city's long life would never be forgotten.

## Reading the Stones

What remains is remarkable. Excavated by Russian archaeologists starting in 1827 and studied by international teams ever since, the site preserves a defensive wall once some 3.5 kilometers long and up to ten meters high, enclosing about thirty hectares of streets and buildings that blend Greek, Roman, and Byzantine hands. Its most famous ruin, the so-called 1935 basilica - named simply for the year it was uncovered - is so iconic that its image appears on a Ukrainian banknote. Recent digs continue to surprise: fragments of a Greek altar carved with gods, and a study of ancient burials that upended old assumptions about who was buried how. Threatened by coastal erosion and the creep of the modern city, Chersonesos endures as it always has - a stubborn survivor, still offering its stones to anyone willing to read them.

## Flight Context

The ruins lie at approximately 44.61 degrees north, 33.49 degrees east, on the coast of Sevastopol's western Heracles Peninsula beside the Black Sea. From lower altitudes in clear weather the large silver dome of St. Vladimir Cathedral marks the site, with the grid of ancient walls and the sea beyond. The nearest airport is Sevastopol's Belbek (ICAO UKFB); Simferopol International (UKFF) lies inland to the north.
