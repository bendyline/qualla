# The Thin Red Line

A single regiment of Scottish Highlanders, two ranks deep instead of the usual four, stood between charging Russian cavalry and the port of Balaklava, and a war correspondent turned their stand into a phrase that outlived the war.

**Location:** 44.5230, 33.5986

**Categories:** military history, Crimean War, Crimea, battles

## Introduction

"There is no retreat from here, men. You must die where you stand." Sir Colin Campbell said it to his Highlanders on the morning of 25 October 1854, and he meant it literally. Russian cavalry was pouring toward the port of Balaklava, and the only thing in their path was Campbell's 93rd Sutherland Highlanders, perhaps five hundred men strung across a low rise on the Crimean plain. By the drill books of the day, infantry met a cavalry charge in a dense square four ranks deep. Campbell did not have the men for it. So he stretched his soldiers into a line just two ranks deep, a red thread of wool and steel drawn thin across the hillside, and waited for the horses to come.

## Two Ranks Against a Charge

The situation was as dire as it looked. A Russian cavalry force numbering some 2,500, under General Ryzhov, was on the move, and a body of them wheeled toward the British base at Balaklava with almost nothing to stop them. Campbell reinforced his Highlanders as best he could: about a hundred walking wounded pulled from the hospital tents, forty detached Guardsmen, and a supporting body of Turkish infantry on the flanks. It was thin, and it grew thinner. As the cavalry bore down, the Turks on the wings broke and ran, leaving the 93rd more exposed than before. The Highlanders held their ground and loosed disciplined volleys into the oncoming riders. Then something in the Russians hesitated, and the charge sheered away before it ever reached the line, the horsemen turning back rather than closing the final distance.

## Discipline Over Glory

What makes the moment memorable is not just that the line held, but how it held. When the Highlanders, blood up, started forward to chase the retreating cavalry, Campbell checked them at once, bellowing, "93rd, damn you Highlanders for all that eagerness!" He knew a scattered pursuit could undo everything the steady line had won. It was restraint, not recklessness, that carried the day. Some later observers, including the Canadian military historian George Denison, argued the Russian squadrons had never truly intended to charge home at all, and were only probing the allied position. Whether or not that is so, the men of the 93rd could not have known it as the horses thundered closer, and they stood anyway, which is the whole of the thing.

## A Phrase That Outlived the War

The stand might have been a footnote had a journalist not been watching. William Howard Russell of The Times, reporting from the heights, wrote that between the Russians and Balaklava he could see nothing but a "thin red streak tipped with a line of steel." In later tellings he sharpened it to the phrase the world remembers: the thin red line. It became an emblem of steady courage against long odds, celebrated in a war that back home was increasingly seen as badly managed and grimly costly. The painter Robert Gibb captured the scene in his 1881 canvas, now in the Scottish National War Museum at Edinburgh Castle. From there the phrase itself slipped free of its origins, coming to mean any small force holding firm when everything is against it, and later spawning echoes far removed from Crimea, from the thin blue line of the police to countless retellings in song and film. It is a rare thing for a single moment on a hillside to lodge so permanently in a language, but the image of that two-deep row of Highlanders, refusing to break, proved impossible to forget. The same day at Balaklava produced the doomed Charge of the Light Brigade, and between them the two episodes fixed this obscure Crimean valley forever in the imagination of the English-speaking world.

## Flight Context

The action took place on the Balaklava plain around 44.52°N, 33.60°E, inland from the hidden bay of Balaklava on Crimea's southwestern coast. From 5,000 to 7,000 feet the twisting Balaklava inlet to the south and the broad valley floor where the cavalry maneuvered are the key features, with Sevastopol's harbor a short distance to the northwest. Nearest airports are Sevastopol/Belbek (UKFB) and Simferopol International (UKFF), about 40 miles to the northeast.
