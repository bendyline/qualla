# Mamelon

A modest hillock outside Sevastopol so fiercely contested in the Crimean War that its French nickname entered a Gilbert and Sullivan patter song as shorthand for military expertise.

**Location:** 44.6005, 33.5561

**Categories:** military-history, Crimean-War, battlefield, Crimea

## Introduction

The hill got its name from its shape. French soldiers besieging Sevastopol in the 1850s looked at a low, rounded rise between the lines and called it the Mamelon, their word for a breast-shaped hillock. The British, sharing the trenches, adopted the French name but also called it Gordon's Hill. It was not much of a hill. Yet for the better part of a year, this unremarkable swell of earth soaked up assault after assault, and thousands of men would fight and die to hold or take a summit a person could stroll across in a minute.

## A Ring of Earth and Fire

The struggle for the Mamelon began badly for the French. In October 1854 they tried to seize its summit in a sudden rush, a coup de main, and were thrown back with roughly six hundred officers and men killed or wounded, an appalling toll for so small a place. Over the winter of 1854 and 1855, the Russians made the hill even harder to take. They built the Kamchatka Redoubt on its crest, one link in a formidable defensive ring drawn around Sevastopol, a system of double and in places triple lines of continuous fieldworks. The Mamelon had become a fortress in miniature, and every attacker who approached it now faced layered walls of earth and massed guns.

## The Storming

Two more French attempts failed before the hill finally fell. In the campaign season of 1855, after a heavy bombardment and the capture of the surrounding defenses, French troops stormed the Kamchatka Redoubt in early June and took it at last. The cost was heavy on every side. In the final assault the British seized some of the outlying works and lost thirty officers and three hundred and fifty other ranks; the French, who threw far more men into the main attack, suffered roughly three times the British casualties. The Mamelon changed hands, but the arithmetic of that change was written in men. A hillock scarcely worth a name on a map became a byword for the brutal cost of siege warfare.

## From the Trenches to the Stage

The Mamelon had one more surprising life ahead of it, far from the mud of Crimea. A generation later, in 1879, the word turned up in a comic opera. In Gilbert and Sullivan's The Pirates of Penzance, the bustling Major-General Stanley rattles off everything he knows in his famous patter song, boasting among his many accomplishments that he understands what is meant by mamelon and ravelin. To a Victorian audience, still fresh from the newspapers and dispatches of the Crimean War, the reference landed instantly. The Mamelon was recent, vivid, and just faintly absurd as a thing for a modern general to parade as knowledge, precisely the sort of half-relevant expertise the song mocks. A place that had cost thousands of lives had become, in a single comic line delivered at a gallop, a small joke about the difference between knowing the vocabulary of war and knowing how to fight one.

## Why a Hillock Mattered

It is worth pausing on the strangeness of it all. The Mamelon was small enough to earn a nickname about its shape, yet it drew assault after assault and consumed men by the hundreds. The reason lay in the geometry of siege warfare. Around Sevastopol the Russians had drawn a comprehensive defensive ring, double lines and in places triple lines of continuous works, and the Mamelon with its Kamchatka Redoubt was a strongpoint in that web, a piece that had to be taken before the pieces behind it could be reached. A hill that means nothing on its own can mean everything in a system, and this one did. That is the quiet lesson buried in the name. Long after the redoubts were leveled and the trenches filled in, the Mamelon endures as shorthand for a hard truth of war, that the ground which decides a battle is rarely the ground that looks important, and that the smallest rise can be the most expensive.

## Flight Context

The Mamelon rises at 44.600 N, 33.556 E, on the high ground southeast of central Sevastopol among the ridges where the 1854 to 1855 siege lines ran. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet it reads as a low rounded hill among the folds east of the city, with Sevastopol Bay to the northwest and Malakhov Kurgan nearby. The nearest airport is Belbek (UKFB) to the north; Simferopol (UKFF) lies northeast.
