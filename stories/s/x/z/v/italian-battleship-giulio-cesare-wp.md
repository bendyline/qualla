# Italian battleship Giulio Cesare

An Italian dreadnought named for Julius Caesar that outlived two navies and three flags, only to capsize one October night in Sevastopol harbor with the loss of 617 men.

**Location:** 44.6186, 33.5356

**Categories:** military-history, maritime, shipwreck, World War II

## Introduction

She was named for a man who conquered, but her longest war was against obsolescence. Laid down in Genoa in 1910 and named Giulio Cesare, for Julius Caesar, she belonged to the first generation of Italian dreadnoughts, and she nearly outlived her own kind. Across four decades she flew the flag of the Kingdom of Italy and then the flag of the Soviet Union, dodged British shells in the Mediterranean, and steamed into the Black Sea under a new name. The end came not in battle but at anchor, on a calm night in a home port, in the deadliest disaster the Soviet Navy would ever suffer in peacetime.

## A Quiet First War, a Second Youth

Her opening act was almost silent. Completed in May 1914, Giulio Cesare served as a flagship in the Adriatic during the First World War and saw no combat at all; the threat of mines and submarines kept Italy's capital ships largely in harbor. She supported operations off Corfu in 1923 and then drifted into reserve and training duties. But an aging dreadnought could be reborn. Between 1933 and 1937, at a Genoa shipyard, she was cut open and rebuilt: a new bow grafted on, her length stretched, her old guns bored out to fire heavier shells, her engines replaced until she could make about 27 knots, far faster than the ship she had been. She emerged looking almost modern, ready for a war that was coming.

## Duels in the Mediterranean

That war found her in July 1940 off Calabria, in one of the first great fleet actions of the conflict. Italian and British battle lines sighted each other in the afternoon haze and opened fire at nearly 29,000 yards, an immense range. Giulio Cesare's shells began to straddle the British battleship Warspite, but the exchange turned against her. One Warspite shell, fired from some 26,000 yards away, reached across the water, pierced her rear funnel, and detonated inside it. Smoke poured down into the boiler rooms until the men there could not breathe, four boilers fell offline, and her speed dropped. The Italian admiral broke off. She survived the British torpedo raid on Taranto that November, covered convoys to North Africa, and fought at Cape Spartivento and the First Battle of Sirte before being retired to training duties in 1942. After Italy's surrender in 1943, she steamed to Malta to be interned, ending her Italian service.

## A New Flag, a New Name

The peace treaties handed her to the Soviet Union as war reparations, and in 1949 the old Italian dreadnought became Novorossiysk, flying a third flag over the same hull. The Soviets used her, as the Italians had, largely for training. Then came the night of 28 October 1955. At anchor in Sevastopol, an explosion tore a great hole in her bow, forward of the first gun turret. The flooding spread faster than it could be fought, pouring through the wound into the forward compartments, and in the hours before dawn she rolled over and capsized where she lay. Six hundred seventeen men died, among them sixty-one sailors sent over from other ships to help save her, who went down with the vessel they had rushed to rescue. The most probable cause was a German bottom mine, one of dozens still lurking on the harbor floor from the war, triggered as her anchor chain dragged across the seabed while she moored for the last time.

## The Riddle on the Harbor Floor

The true cause has never been settled beyond doubt, and that uncertainty has given the disaster a long afterlife. The official finding, the one still regarded as most likely, pointed to those German mines, and the evidence was hard to dismiss: later searches of the harbor floor turned up thirty-two of them, some resting within fifty meters of the blast, and the force of the explosion matched a large charge of the kind such mines carried. It is possible more than one detonated at once. But other stories have circulated for decades, and the most colorful holds that Italian frogmen, veterans of a wartime special operations unit, sank her more than ten years after the fighting ended, whether to avenge the handing of an Italian ship to the Soviets or on some outside behest. There is no proof of it. What is certain is the arithmetic of loss. Novorossiysk was struck from the register in 1956, raised from the seabed in 1957, and broken up for scrap, ending a life that had spanned four decades, three flags, and two world wars, only to close on a calm night in a home port.

## Flight Context

The disaster site lies in Sevastopol Bay near 44.62 N, 33.54 E. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet the long protected inlet and the naval harbor spread out clearly, with the city on the southern shore and the fortress batteries guarding the bay mouth to the west. The nearest airport is Belbek (UKFB) to the north; Simferopol (UKFF) lies northeast.
