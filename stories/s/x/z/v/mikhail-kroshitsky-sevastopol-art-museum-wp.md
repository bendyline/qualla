# Mikhail Kroshitsky Sevastopol Art Museum

A Sevastopol art museum in a merchant's ornate mansion, named for the director who smuggled its finest works three thousand miles into Siberia to save them from a burning city.

**Location:** 44.6136, 33.5224

**Categories:** museum, art, cultural-heritage, Crimea

## Introduction

When the siege lifted, eleven buildings still stood. Sevastopol had been bombarded into rubble in the Second World War, and of the whole city only eleven structures survived undamaged. The grand mansion that held the art museum was not among the lucky eleven. But by then its treasures were already gone, spirited nearly three thousand miles east to a Siberian city, saved by one man's refusal to let them burn. That man was Mikhail Kroshitsky, and today the museum carries his name.

## A Merchant's Mansion, an Imperial Collection

The museum lives inside a piece of nineteenth-century pride. Its home is a four-story mansion with a lavishly decorated facade, built in the late 1800s by a Sevastopol merchant, Semyon Gavalov, who held the honorary title of citizen of the city. After the Crimean Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic was formed in 1921, the museum opened here in November 1927, declared to be for the broadest masses of the people. Its first works had a glittering origin. Much of the founding collection was gathered from the summer residence of the Russian tsars at the Livadia Palace and from the aristocratic estates strung along Crimea's southern shore, the paintings and porcelain of an imperial world newly swept away, hung now on the walls of a public gallery.

## The Three Thousand Mile Rescue

The war reached Crimea with terrible force. Museum collections across the peninsula were destroyed, including the one in the capital, Simferopol, where almost everything went up in flames. As German forces closed on Sevastopol and the shells fell, the museum's director, Mikhail Kroshitsky, made a decision that verged on the impossible. He packed more than a thousand of the collection's most important works and carried them out of the doomed city, across a country at war, all the way to Tomsk in Siberia, a journey of roughly three thousand miles. The mansion he left behind did not survive unscathed. The art did. It was, in the fullest sense, an act of wartime heroism, and it is why Kroshitsky's name replaced the museum's old title in 1991.

## Ten Thousand Works and Counting

The rescued core has grown into something vast. Today the museum holds more than ten thousand works, spanning paintings, drawings, sculpture, and printed and graphic art, a collection built up over nearly a century from that imperiled beginning. Its Western European rooms trace back to those first princely estates, gathered from the holdings of families such as the Golitsyns, with Dutch and Flemish masters of the seventeenth century, works of the Italian Renaissance, French painting of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Meissen porcelain, and European bronzes. The whole ensemble carries the perfume of a vanished aristocratic world, the art that once hung in seaside palaces now open to anyone who walks in. Fully two-thirds of the holdings, though, belong to the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. As of 2022 the Gavalov mansion was under renovation and interior modernization, and the collection was on temporary display elsewhere in the city, still gathering, still growing.

## A Walk Through the Galleries

To move through the museum is to move through Russian art itself. The imperial galleries capture the mood of a court that once wintered on the Crimean coast, and they are hung with landscapes that read like a roll call of the country's great painters: the luminous seascapes of Ivan Aivazovsky, the quiet fields of Isaac Levitan, the forests of Ivan Shishkin, alongside portraits by masters such as Ilya Repin and Ivan Kramskoi and genre scenes by Vladimir Makovsky and Vasily Vereshchagin. Beyond them lie the twentieth-century rooms, filled with Soviet and Ukrainian painters and with the crafts, graphics, and sculpture of a more recent age. Yet the collection saves its deepest pride for its own place. It is most notable of all for its Crimean artists, and especially the local painters of Sevastopol, the men and women who have looked out at this same bay and this same light and set it down on canvas, so that a museum saved from a burning city keeps, at its heart, the picture of home.

## Flight Context

The museum's home, the Gavalov mansion, stands at 44.614 N, 33.522 E, in the center of Sevastopol near the seafront. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet the dense historic core sits on the southern shore of Sevastopol Bay, with the harbor and the Monument to the Sunken Ships close by. The nearest airport is Belbek (UKFB) to the north; Simferopol (UKFF) lies northeast.
