# Valley of the Shadow of Death

Roger Fenton's 1855 photograph of an empty, cannonball-strewn ravine in Crimea became one of the most famous war images ever made, and one of the first ever accused of being staged.

**Location:** 44.5830, 33.5400

**Categories:** photography, art, Crimean War, Crimea

## Introduction

There are no soldiers in the picture, no smoke, no bodies. Only a bleak ravine in the Crimean hills, a rutted track winding away between bare slopes, and scattered across the road like dark hail, dozens of spent cannonballs. Roger Fenton made the exposure on 23 April 1855, and its very emptiness is what unsettles. The British troops who fought and died in this gully had a name for it, born of the shells that fell there without pause: the Valley of Death. Fenton's camera found the place after the guns had gone quiet, and the silence it captured has haunted viewers for more than a century and a half.

## A Camera Goes to War

Fenton did not stumble into Crimea. He was sent, commissioned by the print dealer Thomas Agnew of Agnew & Sons to document a distant and increasingly unpopular war in which Britain, France, the Kingdom of Sardinia, and the Ottoman Empire fought the Russian Empire. Photography was young and cumbersome; every image demanded bulky gear, toxic chemicals, and long exposures made in a horse-drawn darkroom under a hostile sky. Fenton produced some 360 photographs of the war, but this is the one that endured. When Agnew exhibited it in London in September 1855, as part of a series called Panorama of the Plateau of Sebastopol in Eleven Parts, he reached past the soldiers' blunt nickname and past Tennyson's famous poem to Psalm 23, retitling the image Valley of the Shadow of Death and lending a barren hillside the weight of scripture.

## Two Photographs, One Question

There is a twist buried in the frame. Fenton actually made two exposures of this spot: one with cannonballs strewn across the road, and one with the road nearly clear, the balls resting only in the ditch alongside. For generations, no one could say which came first, or why they differed. In 2007 the filmmaker Errol Morris traveled to Sevastopol to stand on the exact ground and settle it, and after close study of the light and the terrain he concluded that the version without the cannonballs on the road was taken first. That ordering carries a charge. If the empty road came first, then someone moved the cannonballs onto it before the second shot, raising the possibility that Fenton arranged them to heighten the drama.

## The Ethics in the Ditch

The mystery has never been fully closed. Morris himself remained unsure of the motive; perhaps Fenton composed the scene for effect, or perhaps something more mundane occurred. The Musée d'Orsay has argued that deliberate staging is unlikely, reasoning that the fighting nearby would hardly have left a photographer free to rearrange a battlefield. Another explanation is simpler still: soldiers salvaging cannonballs for reuse may have tossed them down from higher ground onto the road and ditch, to be gathered up later. Whatever the truth, the debate turned this quiet image into a founding case study in whether a photograph tells the truth. Long before the digital age made every image suspect, Fenton's ravine was already forcing viewers to ask how much a photographer shapes what the lens records. The question matters all the more because there are no bodies in the frame. Fenton, working with slow equipment that could not freeze motion, photographed war as absence and aftermath rather than action, and perhaps that restraint is its own kind of honesty. Prints hang today in the Royal Collection, the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Musée d'Orsay, the Museum of Modern Art, the Getty, and beyond, each one inviting the same unanswerable question.

## Flight Context

The ravine Fenton photographed lies in the hills near Sevastopol, around 44.58°N, 33.54°E in Crimea. From 5,000 to 7,000 feet the folded, gully-cut terrain south and east of Sevastopol's harbor gives the area its character, with the harbor inlet itself to the northwest. Nearest airports are Sevastopol/Belbek (UKFB) and Simferopol International (UKFF), about 40 miles to the northeast.
