# Sevastopol

A white-stone city of thirty bays and two thousand monuments, where an ancient Greek colony, a Christian conversion, and centuries of naval history all crowd the same shoreline.

**Location:** 44.6050, 33.5225

**Categories:** cities, Crimea, Black Sea, history

## Introduction

Count the bays and you begin to understand the city. More than thirty of them notch the coastline here, deep-water inlets folding into the land like fingers, and from the earliest sailors to the modern fleet, everyone who came to this corner of Crimea came for that water. Ships still glide past the tented beer stalls on the waterfront each summer evening while locals ride ferries across the harbor to reach the white-sand beaches on the far shore. Sevastopol is a working port wearing the costume of a resort, a place where two thousand years of history press right up against the promenade.

## Where a Prince Was Baptized

Long before the modern city, the Greeks built Chersonesus Taurica here, and its bleached columns still stand near the sea, a basilica open to the fog and the gulls. This ground holds one of the pivotal moments in Eastern European history: in A.D. 988, Vladimir the Great, prince of the Kievan Rus, was baptized into the Eastern Orthodox Church on this very coast, a conversion that would set the religious course of an entire civilization. The settlement that grew from those ancient stones was sacked repeatedly by the Mongol Horde in the 13th and 14th centuries, then abandoned altogether. For centuries the ruins lay quiet by the water, until Russia refounded the site in 1783 as the base of its new Black Sea Navy, giving the empty harbor a second life.

## The Besieged City

Geography made Sevastopol a fortress the way it made Gibraltar and Halifax fortresses. Deep, sheltered, easily defended, its harbor became one of the most strategically prized anchorages in the world, and armies have bled for it more than once. During the Crimean War the city endured a long and famous siege, and its defenders passed into legend. Admiral Nakhimov, who had already destroyed a Turkish fleet at sea, took charge of the land defense and died in its trenches; his statue now presides over a square at the head of the old city. A century later, in the Second World War, Sevastopol earned its title as one of the Soviet Union's Hero Cities. War is stitched into the streets here, remembered in more than two thousand monuments scattered among the parks and boulevards.

## A City on a Hill

The living city climbs a ridge above its bays. Two main streets, Lenina and Bolshaya Morskaya, run parallel below a spine of high ground crowned by Sovietskaya Street, and countless flights of steps stitch the levels together. Minibuses called marshrutki thread up one street and down the other, faster and cheaper than walking the circuitous, hilly routes the city grew into as it wrapped itself around the shoreline. Sevastopol spent the Soviet decades as a closed city, off-limits to outsiders, and something of that reserve lingers. Visitors who arrive with a few words of Russian and a little humility, though, find the locals will often go out of their way to help, warming quickly to anyone who takes their home and its long, hard-won history seriously.

## Life Along the Water

For all its martial memory, Sevastopol knows how to enjoy its coast. Ferries crisscross the harbor all day, carrying commuters as much as sightseers, and riding them from bay to bay is the finest way to see the city rise from the water, monument by monument. The best beaches lie a short trip away on the northern shore and in nearby villages, crowded in high summer with families who treat this stretch of coast as their own. In the warm months, tented bars and cafes spread along the waterfront, and there is no better place to sit at dusk than with a cold drink, watching the grey shapes of ships slide in past the headlands. The city is a working port and a holiday town at once, its history never far, its sea always in view.

## Flight Context

Sevastopol sits at 44.60°N, 33.52°E on the southwestern tip of Crimea. From 6,000 to 8,000 feet the deeply indented, many-fingered harbor is unmistakable, with the ancient columns of Chersonesus visible near the shore on the city's western edge. The nearest airport is Sevastopol/Belbek (UKFB) just north of town; the better-connected Simferopol International (UKFF) lies about 40 miles inland to the northeast.
