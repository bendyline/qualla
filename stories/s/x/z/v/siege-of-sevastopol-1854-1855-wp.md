# Siege of Sevastopol (1854-1855)

One of history's last classic sieges dragged on for nearly a year in the mud above the harbor, rehearsing the trench warfare that would define the century to come.

**Location:** 44.6170, 33.5170

**Categories:** military history, Crimean War, Crimea, sieges

## Introduction

They came ashore expecting a triumphal march. In September 1854, an allied army of British, French, Ottoman, and Sardinian troops landed at Eupatoria on the Crimean coast and set out for Sevastopol, the tsar's great naval fortress, certain the city would fall in weeks. It held for almost a year. What began as a confident campaign settled into eleven grinding months of bombardment and counter-attack, of frozen trenches and mounting graves, with the dead, wounded, and disease-stricken on both sides eventually numbering well into the hundreds of thousands. The siege of Sevastopol would be remembered as one of the last classic sieges in history, and one of the first modern ones.

## The City Sinks Its Own Fleet

When the field army under Prince Menshikov withdrew rather than be trapped, the defense of Sevastopol fell to its sailors. Vice Admirals Vladimir Kornilov and Pavel Nakhimov, aided by the brilliant engineer Eduard Totleben, took command of a garrison of just over 35,000, most of them naval seamen suddenly turned soldiers. In a desperate, decisive stroke, the Russians scuttled their own warships across the harbor mouth, sinking the very fleet the allies had come to destroy so that no enemy vessel could enter. The guns were hauled ashore to arm the fortifications, and the crews took up positions in the earthworks. Kornilov did not survive the first bombardment. Nakhimov held on until the following summer, when a sniper's bullet found him on the ramparts he had refused to leave.

## A Rehearsal for a Later War

Totleben transformed the ground into a lesson the world would relearn sixty years later. As British engineers pushed siege works toward the Malakoff redoubt, the key that unlocked the whole position, he answered spade with spade, extending bastions and digging rifle pits from which his men could pick off the attackers. The struggle became a war of trenches, of nightly raids and slow suffocating pressure, a grim foretaste of the Western Front of the First World War. Then winter came and made everything worse. A savage storm in November wrecked the allied camps and cut the supply lines, and through the long cold months men and horses starved and sickened in the freezing mud, dying in numbers the fighting alone never matched. It was the suffering of this war, and the reports that carried it home, that helped drive reforms in military nursing and medicine, as the world began to reckon with how badly armies cared for their own wounded.

## The Railway and the Fall

The allies broke the deadlock with industry. Contractors Thomas Brassey and Samuel Morton Peto built the Grand Crimean Central Railway, a 14-mile line finished in March 1855 that carried more than five hundred guns and a flood of ammunition from Balaklava up to the siege lines. The final bombardment, opening on 5 September 1855, was the most terrible of all: 307 cannon hurled roughly 150,000 rounds into the city, inflicting two to three thousand casualties a day. On 8 September the French stormed and seized the Malakoff while the British assault on the Great Redan was thrown back with heavy loss. The Malakoff was enough. That night the Russians set fire to what remained and slipped across the harbor, abandoning the southern side by morning. Among the young officers who lived through it was Leo Tolstoy, whose Sebastopol Sketches carried the ordinary soldier's suffering out of the trenches and into literature.

## Flight Context

The siege lines ran across the Chersonese uplands south of the harbor around 44.62°N, 33.52°E, in present-day Sevastopol, Crimea. From 6,000 to 8,000 feet the great harbor inlet and the high ground to its south, where the bastions once stood, define the terrain, with Balaklava's hidden bay to the southeast marking the old allied supply base. Nearest airports are Sevastopol/Belbek (UKFB) and Simferopol International (UKFF), about 40 miles northeast.
