# Siege of Sevastopol Panorama

A painting so vast you stand inside it, wrapping a single day of the Crimean War around the viewer in a circle of canvas 115 meters long.

**Location:** 44.5955, 33.5233

**Categories:** art, museums, Crimean War, Crimea

## Introduction

Step into the round building on Sevastopol's Historical Boulevard and the walls disappear. In their place, stretching in an unbroken ring the entire way around you, is a single June morning in 1855: smoke, cannon, the shattered earth of the Malakhov bastion, defenders and attackers frozen at the moment of assault. The canvas runs 115 meters long and rises 14 meters high, and where it meets the floor, real debris and terrain blur into the paint so seamlessly that the eye cannot find the seam. This is Franz Roubaud's panorama of the siege, an entire day of war wrapped around a single standing figure: you.

## One Day, Frozen in a Circle

The panorama captures the allied assault on the Malakhov Battery on 18 June 1855, one of the fiercest days of the eleven-month siege, when a great British and French attack broke against the Russian defenses. Roubaud did not paint from imagination. Arriving in Sevastopol in 1901, he walked the actual ground, pored over historical documents, and sought out the men who had been there, listening to survivors and eyewitnesses before he drew a single line. He sketched the composition in Saint Petersburg, then brought in painters and twenty students from the Bavarian Academy of Fine Arts to execute the enormous canvas. The finished work reached Sevastopol in the summer of 1904 and was unveiled on 14 May 1905, exactly fifty years after the defense it commemorates. Among the very first to file in and see themselves rendered on the walls were aging Russian veterans of the siege.

## The Faces in the Smoke

Roubaud filled his battlefield with real people, not just anonymous soldiers. There stands Admiral Pavel Nakhimov, the beloved commander who died defending the city. There too is Dasha, a young woman remembered as one of the first military nurses, who tended the wounded amid the fighting when no organized nursing corps yet existed. And there is Nikolay Pirogov, the pioneering surgeon whose work under fire helped transform the treatment of battlefield casualties. By placing them in the panorama, Roubaud turned a scene of carnage into something closer to a memorial, insisting that the viewer see the individuals, healers as well as fighters, whose courage and suffering the smoke would otherwise swallow.

## Rescued on the Last Ship Out

The painting nearly did not survive the next war. In 1942, during the Second World War siege of Sevastopol, the domed building took heavy damage; its cupola was blown apart and the panorama itself partly burned. As the city fell, the surviving fragments of the canvas were loaded aboard the destroyer leader Tashkent, the last ship to leave the harbor. Running a gauntlet of enemy aircraft, the Tashkent's crew shot down two attackers and carried the wounded, the refugees, and the remnants of Roubaud's masterpiece safely to Novorossiysk, an escape so daring the whole crew was decorated for it.

## Standing Inside History

Restored in the 1950s, the panorama hung once more in its circular hall on Historical Boulevard, the high ground where the bastions once stood, until a June 2026 fire during a strike on the city badly damaged that mid-century canvas; officials said fragments of Roubaud's original 1905 painting, kept separately for a planned exhibit, survived intact. At its best, though, the experience makes plain why this art form once captivated the world before film existed. A panorama was immersion before the word meant a screen: you climb to a central viewing platform, and the painted world curves away on every side, its far edge dissolving into real terrain and objects so that the seam between paint and place disappears. For a few minutes the modern city vanishes and you are surrounded by the smoke and struggle of 18 June 1855. Few paintings ask so much of their viewers, or give back so much. The Siege of Sevastopol does not hang on a wall to be glanced at. It closes around you, and for as long as you stand within it, you are there.

## Flight Context

The Panorama Museum stands on Historical Boulevard at 44.60°N, 33.52°E in central Sevastopol, Crimea. From 5,000 to 7,000 feet the distinctive round, domed rotunda sits on high ground just west of the main harbor inlet. Nearest airports are Sevastopol/Belbek (UKFB) to the north and Simferopol International (UKFF) about 40 miles to the northeast.
