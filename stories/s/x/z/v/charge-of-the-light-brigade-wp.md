# Charge of the Light Brigade

Six hundred horsemen rode into a valley raked by fire from three sides, sent there by a single misunderstood order - and turned a catastrophic blunder into one of history's most enduring images of doomed courage.

**Location:** 44.5378, 33.6242

**Categories:** Military History, Crimean War, Battlefields, Balaclava

## Introduction

A wide sweep of the arm, and six hundred men rode toward their deaths. On the morning of 25 October 1854, in a shallow valley outside Balaclava, Captain Louis Nolan gestured down the length of the wrong ground - toward the massed Russian guns at the far end rather than the redoubts to the side - and the British Light Brigade set off at the trot into an enfilade of artillery that would tear it apart. Nolan was killed by a shell within the first minute, taking the truth of his intentions with him. The rest rode on. What they did in that valley became legend not because it succeeded, but because it should never have been asked of anyone at all.

## The Order That Doomed Them

The Light Brigade was built for speed, not slaughter. Its roughly 670 troopers rode light, fast horses and carried lances and sabres, ideal for reconnaissance and for cutting down a retreating enemy - and useless against dug-in cannon. Lord Raglan, watching from the heights, wanted them to stop the Russians from carrying off captured guns from the Causeway Heights, a task their speed suited perfectly. But the order that reached the valley was vague, and the lie of the land hid its true objective from Lord Lucan below. When Lucan asked which guns he meant, Nolan flung out his arm toward the mass of Russian artillery a mile away: There, my lord, is your enemy; there are your guns. It was almost certainly the wrong battery. His reasons died with him moments later.

## Into the Valley

By the war correspondent William Howard Russell's count, sickness and other losses had left 607 sabres to make the charge that day. They set off down the valley with Lord Cardigan riding out in front on his horse Ronald, never once looking back. Almost at once Nolan rushed across the front of the advancing line - perhaps realizing the terrible mistake, perhaps trying to turn them - before a shell struck him down. The brigade came on under fire from three sides: batteries to the right, batteries to the left, riflemen along the slopes, and the guns dead ahead. Cardigan himself later described advancing three-quarters of a mile with the batteries vomiting forth shells and shot, until at fifty yards from the muzzles the whole force was encircled by a blaze of fire. And still, incredibly, they reached the guns.

## The Reckoning

They broke through. The leading regiments cut down Russian gunners at the very mouths of the cannon, scattered a body of cavalry several times their number, and then - because there was nothing else to be done - turned and rode back up the same gauntlet they had just survived. The surviving Russian artillerymen returned to their guns and fired grapeshot and canister into the tangle of friend and foe alike. French cavalry, the Chasseurs d'Afrique, charged the Fedyukhin Heights to clear the flank and cover the withdrawal, and their intervention saved many who would otherwise have died. When it was over, the brigade had not been annihilated, but it had been gutted: 113 men killed, 134 wounded, some 60 taken prisoner. After the survivors regrouped, only 195 were still with horses. These were not statistics to the men who rode; they were friends and horses that did not come back up the valley.

## Magnificent, But Not War

Watching from the heights, the French Marshal Pierre Bosquet spoke the words that would outlive everyone present: It is magnificent, but it is not war. Then, less often quoted, he added: It is madness. The Russian commanders reportedly assumed the British must have been drunk to attempt such a thing. Blame flew afterward between Raglan, Lucan, and the dead Nolan, and none of it could undo the valley. But the story would not be forgotten. Within six weeks Alfred, Lord Tennyson had written The Charge of the Light Brigade, praising the wild charge they made while mourning that someone had blundered, and a nation took the doomed six hundred to its heart. To this day military students study the valley as a lesson in what happens when orders are unclear and intelligence is lacking - and the courage of the men who rode anyway remains, more than a century and a half on, impossible to look away from.

## Flight Context

The charge took place in the valley north of Balaclava, at approximately 44.54 degrees north, 33.62 degrees east, southeast of Sevastopol. From altitude in clear weather the valley runs between the Fedyukhin Heights and the Causeway Heights, with the small harbor of Balaklava tucked into the coast to the south. The nearest airport is Sevastopol's Belbek (ICAO UKFB); Simferopol International (UKFF) lies to the north.
