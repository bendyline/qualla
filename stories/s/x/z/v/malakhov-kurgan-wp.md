# Malakhov Kurgan

A low hill named for an honest captain that twice decided the fate of Sevastopol, took the lives of two Russian admirals, and gave a young Leo Tolstoy the scene that made him a writer.

**Location:** 44.6036, 33.5493

**Categories:** military-history, memorial, Crimean-War, Crimea

## Introduction

The hill takes its name from a man who never fought a battle on it. In 1827 a captain named Mikhail Malakhov moved to Sevastopol from Kherson, and in a short time he became known among the poorest sailors and townsfolk as an honest and just man. People brought their disputes and their troubles to his house near the rise east of the harbor, and little by little the whole hill came to be called by his surname. It first appeared on Russian maps in 1851. Within four years, this hill of a fair-minded captain would become the most fought-over ground in Europe.

## The Height That Held the City

Geography made Malakhov Kurgan matter. The low hill rises about four kilometers from the center of Sevastopol and looks down over Sevastopol Bay, the anchorage of the Russian fleet, so whoever held the height held the key to the city. The name carries an older memory too: kurgan means burial mound, and the word hints at an ancient grave beneath the modern battlefield. When the Crimean War brought the armies of France and Britain to besiege Sevastopol in 1854, Russian engineers threw up a defensive tower here that autumn and ringed it with field fortifications. Those works became the Kornilov bastion, and around them the longest and bloodiest struggle of the siege would turn.

## Two Admirals and a Young Writer

The hill exacted a terrible price from the men who commanded its defense. Vice-Admiral Vladimir Kornilov, for whom the bastion was named, was mortally wounded here in late 1854. Admiral Pavel Nakhimov, another leader of the defense, was later struck down on the same slopes. Among the officers who served in the defense of the city was a young lieutenant named Leo Tolstoy, who had volunteered to fight and arrived in time to witness the hill's capture. What he saw here he could not put down. He turned it into fiction, and his Sevastopol sketches, written from inside the siege, launched the career of one of the world's greatest novelists. When the French finally stormed the height in the Battle of Malakoff, the Russians evacuated Sevastopol that same day, 8 September 1855. The hill had decided the war.

## Mutiny, and a Second Siege

The hill kept gathering history long after the guns of the Crimean War fell silent. During the Russian Revolution of 1905, revolutionary workers, soldiers, and sailors met on its slopes, and in a nearby gully that June, sailors of the Black Sea Fleet agreed among themselves to mutiny, so that the height of an old war became a stage for a new kind of upheaval. Then came another war and another siege. In late 1941, with German forces closing on Sevastopol, a battery of two 130-millimeter guns was set up on Malakhov Kurgan, the barrels themselves salvaged from the harbor bed after being blown off a wrecked destroyer, weapons given a second life on the old bastion. As the city fell in July 1942, Captain-Lieutenant Aleksei Matyukhin kept his guns firing until the ammunition ran out and the position was overrun around him; he was taken prisoner. Once again, the hill had been held to the very end.

## The Tower and Its Memory

After the Second World War, the story of Malakhov Kurgan reached even into the cinema. Following the city's liberation by Soviet forces in 1944, a film was released telling the story of a widow caught up in the fighting in the final days before Sevastopol fell to the Germans, drawing this ground into the nation's memory of the war. The hill itself became contested in a gentler way. The Russian government proposed gathering all of Sevastopol's Crimean War monuments onto the site and turning it into a single open-air museum, but the local authorities and the Black Sea Fleet successfully opposed the plan, and the memorials stayed where they had always stood. The defensive tower that anchored the Kornilov bastion still crowns the height, and it now holds a small museum. It is a quiet marker over ground that twice held the fate of a city, and twice extracted a fearful price for it.

## Flight Context

Malakhov Kurgan rises at 44.604 N, 33.549 E, in the southeastern part of Sevastopol, about four kilometers from the center. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet the tower-topped hill overlooks Sevastopol Bay to the north, with the city spread between the height and the water. The nearest airport is Belbek (UKFB) to the north; Simferopol (UKFF) lies northeast.
