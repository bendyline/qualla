# Inkerman

A small Crimean town whose name means 'cave fortress,' quarried for the white stone that built a peninsula and remembered for a fog-bound battle that made it briefly famous across the world.

**Location:** 44.6186, 33.6058

**Categories:** city, history, military-history, Crimea

## Introduction

The stone came out white, soft enough to saw and hard enough to last. For centuries, workers cut it from the cliffs above the Chernaya River and hauled it away to raise the houses, walls, and churches of half of Crimea, and for a stretch of the Soviet era the town was renamed to say exactly that: Belokamensk, the white stone city, a name that meant precisely 'white stone.' But the older name kept returning, because the older name told the deeper truth. Inkerman means 'cave fortress' in Turkish, and the fortress was here long before the quarries. This is a place defined by rock, both the rock people carried away and the rock they carved into and fought over.

## The Fortress Called Kalamita

Long before Inkerman, there was Kalamita. Somewhere in the eighth or ninth century, builders raised a fortress on a strategic cliff overlooking the river's estuary, guarding the approach to a natural harbor, and in the fourteenth century they expanded it into something stronger. Kalamita belonged to the Principality of Theodoro, a small Christian state clinging to the mountains of southwestern Crimea, a last outpost of a Byzantine world in retreat. That world ended in 1475, when the Ottomans swept the principality away and renamed the stronghold Inkerman. The fortress had a long twilight after that. When the Polish diplomat Martin Bronevski visited in 1578, he found Greek inscriptions still legible on the old stones, ghosts of the vanished principality. After the Treaty of Kucuk Kaynarca in 1774 pried Crimea from Ottoman control, the fortress was abandoned and slid into ruin, though a small settlement lingered on at the base of the cliff, too stubborn to disappear.

## A Morning of Fog and Fame

In 1854 this obscure town became, for a season, the center of the world's attention. The Crimean War had drawn the armies of France, Britain, and Russia to the hills around Sevastopol, and Inkerman gave its name to the battle fought here that November, as well as to the later Battle of Chernaya River nearby. Both ended in defeat for the Russian side. The fighting at Inkerman unfolded in thick autumn fog, where officers lost sight of their own men and the struggle broke apart into a hundred confused, close-quarters actions among the ridges and gullies, a battle so chaotic that it was sometimes called a soldiers' battle, decided by small groups fighting on their own. Thousands of soldiers on every side never left these slopes. The name traveled home with the survivors and lodged deep in British military memory, where a company of the Grenadier Guards still carries it to this day, and even a cavalry horse of the Household Cavalry has borne it since.

## The Warehouse Under the Cliffs

The cliffs that once held a fortress later held something far more dangerous. During the Soviet era, a vast ammunition depot for the Black Sea Fleet was tunneled beneath the rock, the old caverns turned into a magazine for a modern navy. An explosion damaged the facility in the 1970s and it was abandoned, its stockpiles left where they lay in the dark. No one secured the site for two decades. Then, in the 1990s, local residents began slipping in to salvage the explosives for scrap and profit, a grim and desperate trade that cost several lives when the old munitions did what old munitions do. Ukrainian army engineers finally began the slow, dangerous work of extracting and decommissioning the material in 2000, under a special government program, clearing away a hazard that had been buried and forgotten beneath the town.

## Back to the Quiet

For all its dramatic history, Inkerman has spent most of its life as a modest place, and to that modesty it has returned. Once the wars and the explosions were behind it, the town settled back into the near-obscurity it had known before, serving now as a quiet suburb of Sevastopol, linked to the downtown core by commuter ferries that cross the inlet. There is wine here: one of Crimea's well-known wineries carries the Inkerman label, aging its bottles in cool galleries not unlike the caves that gave the town its name. And there is the pull of the mountains. A popular hiking trail leading up into the Crimean Mountains sets out just east of the streets, drawing walkers past the white cliffs and the ruined fortress. The cave fortress endures, no longer guarding a frontier, watching instead over ferries, vineyards, and the footsteps of hikers heading for the hills.

## Flight Context

Inkerman lies at 44.619 N, 33.606 E, roughly five kilometers east of central Sevastopol at the head of the bay where the Chernaya River empties in. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet, look for the white quarry cliffs, the cave-riddled monastery rock, and the ruined Kalamita fortress on the plateau, with the long inlet running west toward the sea. Belbek (UKFB) is just to the north; Simferopol (UKFF) lies northeast.
