# Monument to the Sunken Ships

A slender column rising from the waters of Sevastopol Bay, crowned by a bronze eagle, that remembers the day a fleet was deliberately sunk to save the harbor it guarded.

**Location:** 44.6184, 33.5243

**Categories:** monument, memorial, maritime, Crimea

## Introduction

It rises straight out of the sea. A few meters offshore in Sevastopol Bay, a slim classical column stands on a rough stone base with the waves lapping at its foot, and at its summit a bronze double-headed eagle spreads its wings and turns its gaze toward the entrance of the harbor. The column commemorates a strange and painful act of war: the day sailors sank their own ships. To understand why a city would build its proudest landmark to a fleet that was deliberately destroyed, you have to go back to the first siege of Sevastopol.

## A Fleet Sunk on Purpose

During the Crimean War, as British and French forces closed on Sevastopol in 1854 and 1855, the defenders faced an agonizing choice about their own navy. Rather than let enemy warships sail straight into the bay, the Imperial Russian Navy scuttled many of its own vessels, most of them ships of the Black Sea Fleet, sinking them across the harbor mouth to bar the way. It was a sacrifice turned into a shield: proud warships sent to the bottom by their own crews so that the city behind them might hold. Fifty years later, in 1905, the monument was raised on the anniversary of that siege to honor the ships lost in those waters, a memorial standing over the very seabed where they went down.

## The Eagle and Its Maker

The monument owes its form to an artist from far to the north. It was designed by Amandus Adamson, an Estonian sculptor trained in Saint Petersburg, and completed in 1905, built by Valentin Feldmann. Adamson gave the memorial a quiet, upward drama. The Corinthian column lifts from a base of rugged natural stone, so the whole structure seems to grow out of the sea itself, and the crowning bronze eagle, wings outstretched, looks steadily out toward the harbor entrance where the ships were sunk. There is no bombast to it, only a slender vertical line against the water and the sky. It has become, more than any building or square, the image that means Sevastopol.

## The Symbol of a City

Over the following century the monument grew into the city's emblem in the most literal way, moving from the harbor onto the official face of Sevastopol itself. On 12 February 1969 it was placed on the coat of arms of the city, and on 12 April 2000 it entered the city flag, so that the image of the column and its eagle became the sign under which Sevastopol represents itself. It has traveled the mails under successive states, appearing on postage stamps from Soviet issues to a Ukrainian series to Russian ones, a constant emblem beneath changing names. And it reaches into people's pockets. Since 2017 the column has been printed on the face of the 200-ruble banknote, after featuring on a commemorative note two years before. Few memorials so completely become the place they stand in. A slender column over drowned ships, a bronze eagle facing the sea, is now the first picture that rises to mind at the name of Sevastopol.

## The Sea Keeps Its Watch

What lingers, in the end, is the quiet of the thing. For all that it has been stamped on flags, coins, and coats of arms, the monument itself asks for stillness. It stands a little apart from the promenade, out in the water, so that to look at it you must also look at the sea, and the two become a single image, the column and the bay it commemorates. Waves break against the rough stone base in every weather, and the double-headed eagle at the top holds its gaze fixed on the harbor entrance, watching the same threshold the sunken ships were sent to guard. Generations have gathered along the seafront to see it, in celebration and in mourning alike, and it has outlasted the wars and the regimes that swirled around it. A memorial to a sacrifice, raised from the water where that sacrifice was made, it keeps its long watch over Sevastopol, patient and unmoving, facing the open sea.

## Flight Context

The monument stands just offshore at 44.618 N, 33.524 E, in Sevastopol Bay along the central seafront promenade. From 1,000 to 2,000 feet it is a tiny vertical mark in the water at the city's edge, best picked out against the harbor with the historic center immediately behind it. The nearest airport is Belbek (UKFB) to the north; Simferopol (UKFF) lies northeast.
