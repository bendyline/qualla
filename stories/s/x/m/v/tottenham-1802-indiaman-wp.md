# Tottenham (1802 Indiaman)

An East Indiaman launched in 1802 that spent nearly two decades sailing between Britain, India, and eventually Australia — carrying trade goods, a letter of marque, and two hundred transported convicts before being sold for breaking up in 1820.

**Location:** 41.7000, 30.7167

**Categories:** British East India Company, Age of Sail, merchant ships, convict transport, maritime history

## Introduction

Six voyages for the British East India Company, one for the convict transport system, then the breaker's yard. The working life of a merchant ship in the early nineteenth century was precisely this utilitarian: built, employed, worn out, dismantled. The Tottenham was launched in 1802 — the same year Napoleon Bonaparte made himself Consul for Life — and by 1820 she was gone, her timbers sold. In between, she crossed the Indian Ocean repeatedly, rounded the Cape of Good Hope more times than most sailors of her era, and once carried 200 men convicted of crimes in Britain to the penal colony of New South Wales. Her story is the story of British imperial commerce at its most matter-of-fact.

## The East India Company's Working Fleet

The British East India Company did not own its ships; it chartered them from private owners who built vessels specifically to Company specifications. The Tottenham was built at Stockton-on-Tees for Sir Robert Wigram, one of the most prominent merchant shipowners of the age, and launched on 19 April 1802. Her first master was Captain Thomas Jones, who sailed her for Bengal on 7 July 1802, leaving the Downs — the sheltered anchorage off the Kent coast where East Indiamen traditionally gathered — and reaching the Cape of Good Hope on 4 October. She arrived at Calcutta on 6 February 1803, a journey of roughly seven months from launch to destination. The route was one of the most traversed in the world by then, a track through the Atlantic south to the Cape and then northeast across the Indian Ocean that generations of Company captains had refined into near-routine.

## A Letter of Marque

By the time of her third voyage, Britain and France were again at war — the Peace of Amiens had collapsed in 1803, and Napoleonic Europe made every merchant voyage a potential encounter with enemy privateers. Captain Jones acquired a letter of marque on 21 March 1806, authorizing the Tottenham to arm herself and, if opportunity arose, to take enemy prizes. He sailed from Portsmouth on 10 June 1806, bound for Madras and Bengal. The ship touched Madeira in late June and the Cape in October, reaching Madras on 21 December and Saugor (near Calcutta) on 4 February 1807. The homeward journey took her back through Madras and the Cape and St Helena before she made the Downs on 4 April 1808. Nearly two years at sea. The letter of marque was a precaution that many Company captains took in this period; whether the Tottenham ever needed to use it, the record does not say.

## Two Hundred Men to Botany Bay

After her six voyages for the East India Company, Tottenham's owners sold her for use as a transport — a vessel carrying government cargo, troops, or convicts rather than Company trade goods. In 1818, under the command of Captain Dugald McDougall, she was assigned to the convict transport service. She left England on 17 April 1818 with 200 male convicts embarked. They were men convicted of crimes under British law — the specific offences not recorded in the ship's surviving documentation — and sentenced to transportation, the system by which Britain relieved its prisons by sending offenders to the Australian colonies. Ten of the 200 men died during the voyage. The Tottenham arrived at Port Jackson on 14 October 1818, a passage of nearly six months. The 190 survivors were disembarked into a colony that had been founded barely three decades earlier. Tottenham then returned via Bengal and Madras, arriving back in England on 30 March 1820.

## The Lives Behind the Numbers

Transportation was understood by those who endured it as a severe punishment — exile from family, community, and country, with no certainty of return. For many of the men aboard the Tottenham in 1818, New South Wales was a destination from which they would never come back, even after serving their sentences. The ten men who died at sea during the voyage were buried in the ocean without ceremony, their deaths logged as facts of the passage. The 190 who arrived faced years of assigned labor. The Tottenham's captain and crew would have regarded the voyage as a routine charter; for the convicts below decks, it was the hinge point of their lives. Ships like the Tottenham carried the machinery of British penal policy across twelve thousand miles of ocean, their cargo lists written in human beings.

## End of Service

After returning to England in March 1820, the Tottenham was sold for breaking up. She had been in service for eighteen years — a respectable working life for a wooden sailing vessel of her era. Her hull would have been inspected, found no longer economical to maintain, and her timbers, copper fittings, rigging, and ironwork sold off separately. Nothing of the physical ship survives. What remains is a partial record in Lloyd's Register, the British Library archives, and the logs of the East India Company — columns of dates, ports, cargoes, and captains' names that reconstruct the outline of a working life. The Tottenham carried the commerce of empire for nearly two decades: spices, cotton, and Company correspondence eastward; silk, indigo, and saltpetre homeward. And once, two hundred men sent far from everything they knew.
