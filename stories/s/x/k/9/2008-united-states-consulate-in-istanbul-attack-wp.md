# 2008 Attack on the U.S. Consulate in Istanbul

On July 9, 2008, three armed attackers assaulted the U.S. Consulate General in Istanbul; three Turkish police officers died defending the compound, and all three attackers were killed in the confrontation.

**Location:** 41.1173, 29.0469

**Categories:** terrorist attack, Istanbul, Turkey, 2008, memorial, diplomatic mission

## Introduction

On the morning of July 9, 2008, three men got out of a car near the entrance to the Consulate General of the United States in Istanbul. One opened fire immediately. What followed was a sustained gun battle between the attackers and the Turkish police officers stationed to protect the compound. When it ended, three officers were dead, and so were all three attackers. No consulate staff or visitors were harmed. The Turkish police, tasked under international convention with the security of the diplomatic facility, paid the full cost of that morning with their lives.

## The Officers Who Died

Three Turkish National Police officers were killed defending the consulate. Two have been identified in the public record: Erdal Oztas, 28 years old, from the village of Karahacik in Çorum Province, and Nedim Calik. The name of the third officer has not been consistently documented in available records. Erdal Oztas was 28 — a young man from a village, doing his job at a guard post outside a diplomatic building in Istanbul. These three officers did not choose the moment of the attack, and they did not have the opportunity to retreat. Their duty was to stand between the approaching threat and the people inside the compound, and they fulfilled it at the cost of their lives. U.S. Ambassador to Turkey Ross Wilson said of them: "The Turkish police responded quickly and effectively. We are deeply grateful for the work that they do to protect our official U.S. government establishments here."

## The Attack

The three attackers were Turkish nationals, later identified as Erkan Kargın, 26, and Raif Topçıl, 20, both from the southeastern city of Bitlis, and Bülent Çınar, 23, from the eastern city of Iğdır. Armed with a shotgun and Glock automatic pistols, they arrived in a car being used as an unlicensed taxicab. Security camera footage showed them emerging from the vehicle, opening fire near the main entrance, and running toward a guard post as officers returned fire. The driver of the vehicle, who had no prior connection to the attackers, later turned himself in to authorities. All three assailants died in the confrontation. No one inside the consulate compound was injured.

## Vienna Convention and the Duty of Protection

Under Article 22 of the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations — the international framework that governs the treatment of diplomatic missions — the host state bears responsibility for the physical security of foreign diplomatic premises. Turkey fulfilled that obligation on July 9, 2008, at the cost of three officers' lives. This legal framework, often invisible in ordinary times, was made visible in the most concrete way that morning: Turkish police officers, under national obligation to protect an American facility, stood in the line of fire. The consulate was, as the source record notes, at full function when the attack occurred — a normal working morning for the American diplomats inside, who owed their safety to the officers outside.

## Investigation and Aftermath

Turkish officials initially attributed the attack to al-Qaeda. Subsequent investigation found that while possible training connections existed, the attackers had no confirmed direct link to a transnational terrorist organization. In the days after the attack, twelve individuals were detained for questioning. One was arrested on July 13 and charged with membership in a terrorist organization; two others were charged and released pending trial. By December 2008, two suspected al-Qaeda members had been formally charged in connection with the consulate attack. The United States temporarily increased security at all of its Turkish diplomatic missions in response. The prosecution also examined the role of a man named Kadir Özmen, who had allegedly arranged the financing for the weapons and had originally planned to participate in the attack before withdrawing.

## What Was Defended

The U.S. Consulate General in Istanbul, located in the Istinye neighborhood on the European shore of the Bosphorus, has served for generations as a point of official contact between the United States and Turkey — processing visas, supporting American citizens, and managing the day-to-day work of diplomacy in one of the world's most strategically positioned cities. On July 9, 2008, that ordinary function was violently targeted. The three officers who died were not policy makers, not politicians, not symbols of any government's decisions. They were people at their posts, doing a job that most days asks nothing more than watchfulness. That morning, it asked everything. Their names should be remembered: Erdal Oztas, Nedim Calik, and a third officer whose name the record has not fully preserved.
