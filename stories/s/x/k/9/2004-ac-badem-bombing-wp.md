# 2004 Acıbadem Bombing

On May 20, 2004, a car bomb detonated in the shopping center car park of Acıbadem, on Istanbul's Asian shore — a rare instance in which timely police action prevented any casualties.

**Location:** 41.0033, 29.0438

**Categories:** Maoism in Turkey, Terrorism in Turkey, Terrorism in 2004

## Introduction

The bomb was already in the car park of the Acıbadem shopping center when the police received the call. The warning came fifteen minutes before detonation — enough time to evacuate the building and clear the surrounding area. On May 20, 2004, the device detonated as planted, damaging numerous parked vehicles. No one was killed. No one was injured. The near-miss was the direct result of a security response that worked, in a city that had learned the hard way what a bombing that didn't receive advance warning could cost.

## The Attack and Its Context

Acıbadem is a residential and commercial neighborhood on the Asian shore of Istanbul, in the Kadıköy district — a busy, ordinary part of the city far from the tourist center but densely inhabited. The car park where the bomb was placed served a shopping center frequented by local residents.

The attack came less than six months after the devastating November 2003 bombings, in which four truck bombs killed 59 people across two days and struck two synagogues, the British Consulate, and the HSBC headquarters. Istanbul in 2004 was a city still absorbing that trauma. The Acıbadem bombing, coming in this environment, was understood immediately not merely as property damage but as another act of political violence against civilians — even if the police warning had, this time, prevented deaths.

## Investigation and Arrests

In the week following the attack, Turkish authorities arrested eight people with suspected connections to the bombing. The judicial process that followed identified them as members of TİKKO — the Turkish acronym for the armed wing of the Communist Party of Turkey/Marxist-Leninist (TKP/ML). TİKKO had operated as an armed militant organization since the 1970s, conducting attacks against state and military targets as part of its Maoist political program.

The investigation also determined that the individuals arrested in connection with the Acıbadem bombing were linked to a separate incident in Tokat province, where two soldiers had been killed. The same network, in other words, had been responsible for both acts of violence. The arrests were presented by authorities as a significant disruption of TİKKO's operational capacity in Istanbul.

## A City on Alert

The Acıbadem bombing arrived in a period when Istanbul was examining its security posture with unusual urgency. The city had faced multiple attacks from different political directions: PKK-linked violence in 1999, al-Qaeda-orchestrated attacks in November 2003, and now a leftist militant cell in May 2004. Each attack had a different ideological origin and a different tactical approach, but they shared a geography — the dense, populated streets of a city of many millions — and a human cost measured in fear even when, as in Acıbadem, the physical toll was held to zero.

The fact that no one died on May 20, 2004, was not coincidence. It was the result of someone making a call and police moving quickly. That outcome stood in stark contrast to what had happened the previous November, and to what would happen again in the years ahead. Istanbul's residents in 2004 lived with both the relief of narrowly avoided disaster and the knowledge that the warning would not always come in time.

## Flight Context

Acıbadem lies at approximately 41.003°N, 29.044°E on the Asian shore of Istanbul, in the Kadıköy district. From the air, the neighborhood is part of the continuous urban fabric that stretches across the Asian side of the Bosphorus. The nearest major airport is Sabiha Gökçen (LTFJ), approximately 20 kilometers to the east. Istanbul Airport (LTFM) is on the European shore, approximately 45 kilometers to the northwest. The Bosphorus strait, which divides Europe and Asia, is clearly visible from altitude, with the Bosphorus Bridge (now the 15 July Martyrs Bridge) and the Fatih Sultan Mehmet Bridge connecting the two shores.
