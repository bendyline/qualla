# Killing of Pro-Biafra Protesters (2015-2016)

Between 2015 and 2016, Nigerian security forces killed scores of unarmed protesters across southeastern Nigeria who were demanding the restoration of the Republic of Biafra, drawing international condemnation from Amnesty International and the European Union.

**Location:** 5.1216, 7.3733

**Categories:** history, human-rights, conflict

## Introduction

On the morning of May 30, 2016, security forces stormed a Catholic church compound in Nkpor Agu, near Onitsha, where pro-Biafra demonstrators had gathered to mark the anniversary of a declaration made nearly half a century earlier. Gunfire erupted before dawn. By the time the sun rose over southeastern Nigeria, the cycle of protest and state violence that had been building for months had reached its bloodiest chapter yet. The people who died that day and in the months before and after were not combatants. They were citizens carrying flags and chanting slogans, killed for demanding something their government refused to discuss.

## The Wound That Never Closed

The roots of these protests stretch back to 1967, when the Igbo-dominated Eastern Region declared independence as the Republic of Biafra. The civil war that followed killed an estimated one to three million people, many of them civilians who starved during a federal blockade. Nigeria won. Biafra ceased to exist as a political entity. But among the Igbo people of southeastern Nigeria, the memory of that war -- and the grievances that sparked it -- never faded. By 2015, a new generation had taken up the cause. The Indigenous People of Biafra, led by Nnamdi Kanu, organized rallies across cities in the southeast, demanding a referendum on self-determination. Their grievance was rooted in what they described as decades of political marginalization: infrastructure neglect, economic exclusion, and a sense that the Nigerian federation had never truly reintegrated the southeast after the war.

## Bullets for Banners

The Nigerian government's response was lethal. Between August 2015 and August 2016, Amnesty International documented the killing of at least 150 peaceful pro-Biafra advocates by security forces. The pattern was consistent: protesters gathered, often unarmed, and soldiers or police dispersed them with live ammunition, frequently with little or no warning. In Onitsha on December 2, 2015, about eleven people died, including two police officers and nine activists. In Aba on February 9, 2016, six IPOB members were shot dead, twenty others injured, and dozens arrested. IPOB alleged that in a separate incident in Aba, Nigerian soldiers killed activists and burned their corpses to conceal the evidence. The federal government and military denied all allegations, calling them attempts to tarnish the reputation of security forces.

## A Bridge Turned Checkpoint

The crackdowns extended beyond the protests themselves. On December 23, 2015, soldiers set up stop-and-search operations on the Onitsha Niger River Bridge -- the main crossing for Igbo travelers returning home from western Nigeria for Christmas. Cars were halted and occupants interrogated for hours. The resulting gridlock lasted up to twelve hours, with families and commuters forced to spend the night trapped on both ends of the bridge. It was a collective punishment directed at an entire ethnic group: the Igbo, who were at the forefront of secessionist agitation, found themselves targeted not just at rallies but in the mundane act of traveling home for the holidays. For many, the bridge checkpoint crystallized the sense that they were being treated as suspects in their own country.

## Biafra Day, 2016

The violence peaked on May 30, 2016 -- Biafra Day, the annual commemoration of the 1967 independence declaration. Across southeastern Nigeria, protests turned deadly as security forces clashed with pro-Biafra groups. Local media reported approximately forty people killed and over fifty arrested in Onitsha alone. The pre-dawn raid on the church in Nkpor Agu, where pro-Biafrans had camped overnight, left five dead and ten wounded. The Nigerian Army justified its actions by citing "widespread panic, tension and apprehension" generated by IPOB and MASSOB members. Amnesty International independently documented the killing of at least seventeen unarmed separatists in Onitsha in the days leading up to the march.

## Voices Near and Far

The killings drew responses from across Nigeria and beyond. Ike Ekweremadu, Deputy President of the Nigerian Senate, stated plainly: "In a democracy people should be entitled to speak their minds and to assemble under responsible circumstances." Former Vice President Atiku Abubakar called for restructuring the Nigerian federation to address the underlying grievances. Internationally, British parliamentarian Harriet Harman petitioned for the release of IPOB leader Nnamdi Kanu, who had been detained. Amnesty International called for investigations into what it described as consistent excessive force. The European Union's High Representative, Federica Mogherini, expressed support for a peaceful referendum process. Yet for the families of those killed in Onitsha, Aba, and elsewhere across the southeast, the international statements offered little comfort. Their sons and daughters had marched for a cause and been met with bullets. The question of Biafra remained unresolved, but its human cost continued to grow.
