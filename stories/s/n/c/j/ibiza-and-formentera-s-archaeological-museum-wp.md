# Ibiza and Formentera's Archaeological Museum

A two-campus museum in Ibiza's old town that holds three thousand years of island history and sits atop the western Mediterranean's greatest Phoenician-Punic necropolis.

**Location:** 38.9069, 1.4365

**Categories:** museums, archaeology, history, Phoenician, Ibiza, UNESCO

## Introduction

Three thousand years fit inside this museum, and one of its two buildings stands directly on top of the dead. The Museo Arqueologico de Ibiza y Formentera preserves the deep past of two islands whose story runs from the first settlers to the Catalan conquest of 1235. It occupies two campuses within Ibiza's old town: one inside the fortified upper city of Dalt Vila, the other on the hill of Puig des Molins, planted amid the graves it interprets. To walk through it is to move from the living town down into the oldest layers of the island's memory.

## Two Houses for Three Thousand Years

The museum splits itself between two settings, and the division is deliberate. The archaeological museum proper occupies Dalt Vila, the fortified hilltop old town that UNESCO recognizes as part of Ibiza's World Heritage site. Its collection spans the entire arc of island prehistory and antiquity, from the earliest people who settled these shores through Phoenician traders, Punic Carthage, and Rome, ending at 1235 when Catalan forces took the island. The second campus, the Monographic museum, sits farther down the slope on Puig des Molins, so close to its subject that the exhibits and the excavation share the same ground.

## The Society That Started It

The museum grew out of local passion before it became a state institution. In 1903 a handful of Ibizans founded the Archaeological Society of Ibiza and began digging into their own island's past. Four years later, in 1907, they donated their collection to the state, and those finds became the first permanent exhibition in the Dalt Vila museum. The holdings swelled in 1913 when a local family gave up material recovered from Ses Torres and Portmany and from the sanctuaries of Illa Plana and Es Culleram. What began as amateur curiosity had, within a decade, assembled the core of a serious archive.

## A City of the Dead

Puig des Molins was chosen for the dead in the 7th century BC by the Phoenicians who founded the city of Ibiza. Through antiquity it served as the urban necropolis, and generations of Phoenician, Punic, and Roman burials layered the hillside with graves and grave goods. Excavations since 1903 have pulled an enormous volume of material from the soil. In 1931 the Spanish state declared the necropolis a Historical and Artistic Monument, formal recognition of what archaeologists already knew: this is the largest and best-preserved Phoenician-Punic necropolis in the entire western Mediterranean.

## Artifacts in Their Ground

The Monographic museum's power comes from where it stands. Because it is built inside the archaeological site itself, its most important objects are explained in the context of the very necropolis that produced them, rather than stripped of place and set behind glass in some distant capital. Visitors read the history of the site and then step out among the tombs that made that history. Above them rises Dalt Vila's ring of Renaissance walls, and beyond that the modern town. Few museums let you stand so literally between the living island and the three thousand years buried beneath it.

## Flight Context

The museum sits at 38.907N, 1.437E in Ibiza's old town, on and just below the fortified hill of Dalt Vila, roughly 3-4 nm northeast of Ibiza Airport (LEIB). From 2,000-3,500 feet the unmistakable landmark is Dalt Vila itself: a walled hilltop crowned by the cathedral and castle, rising directly above the harbor and the modern city. Puig des Molins lies just inland of the walls. Approach from over the harbor for the clearest view; visibility in summer is typically excellent, though the low sun of late afternoon throws the fortress walls into strong relief.
