# Puig des Molins

Windmills' Hill in Ibiza Town holds a Punic necropolis founded in the 7th century BC, a museum thirty years in the making, and a bust of the goddess Tanit at its heart.

**Location:** 38.9072, 1.4295

**Categories:** archaeology, museums, history, Phoenician, Ibiza, necropolis

## Introduction

A hill named for its windmills turns out to be built on the dead. Puig des Molins, literally Windmills' Hill, rises on the edge of Ibiza Town, and beneath its slopes lies a burial ground that the Phoenicians founded in the 7th century BC. The site once served as a medieval Islamic rural estate; today it holds an archaeological museum wrapped around the Punic Necropolis it interprets. At the center of that collection sits a bust of the goddess Tanit, gazing out across three millennia of graves as if she still presided over them.

## Windmills' Hill

The name records a later life of the place, when windmills turned on the high ground above the town. But the hill's identity runs in layers. Long after the Phoenicians and Romans buried their dead here, it became a medieval Islamic rural property, farmed and settled by people who may never have grasped what lay under their fields. Only in the twentieth century did the hill's oldest purpose reclaim it, as archaeologists and curators built a museum directly over the necropolis. The result is a place where farming, milling, and mourning have all left their marks on the same modest rise.

## The Goddess at the Center

The heart of the museum's collection is a bust of Tanit, the great Phoenician and Punic goddess associated with fertility, protection, and the afterlife. Her presence is fitting on a hill devoted to the dead, for Tanit watched over both birth and burial in the Carthaginian world. Around her, the museum arranges the objects that ancient islanders placed with their dead, the amulets, figurines, and vessels meant to accompany souls beyond life. To stand before her is to meet the face that the people of Punic Ibiza carried in their imaginations when they thought about what came after.

## A King, a War, a Thirty-Year Wait

The museum took a generation to arrive. The idea was first floated on 8 October 1929, when King Alfonso XIII visited the island, and in 1931 the necropolis was formally protected as a Historic and Artistic Monument. A proposal in 1935 called for a new building to gather all of Ibiza's finds and replace the older museum at Dalt Vila, which had opened in 1907. Then the Spanish Civil War broke out and stalled everything. Construction was not completed until 1965, and the museum finally opened its doors in 1966, more than three and a half decades after a king's visit first set the plan in motion.

## Five Rooms of the Dead

The necropolis the museum guards is vast. The original burial ground covered around 10,000 square meters, and the protected site today spans five hectares, layered with Phoenician graves and, later, Roman ones. Inside, the collection unfolds across five rooms and roughly 1,514 square meters, each room tracing a chapter of mortality: death in the Phoenician period, Punic funerary rituals, Punic burials, death in Roman times and Late Antiquity, and the Sainz de la Cuesta collection. Walking the sequence, you move through a thousand years of how different peoples on this one island prepared their dead for whatever they believed came next.

## Flight Context

Puig des Molins sits at 38.907N, 1.430E, on a low hill just west of the fortified old town of Dalt Vila in Ibiza Town, about 3-4 nm northeast of Ibiza Airport (LEIB). From 2,000-3,000 feet the anchoring landmark is Dalt Vila, the walled hilltop crowned by cathedral and castle above the harbor; Puig des Molins is the adjacent rise immediately inland of the walls. Approach over the harbor for the clearest orientation. Summer visibility along this coast is usually excellent, with strong afternoon light sharpening the old-town ramparts.
