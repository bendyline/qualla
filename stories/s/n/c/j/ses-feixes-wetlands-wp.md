# Ses Feixes Wetlands

A thousand-year-old Arab irrigation garden of narrow farmed strips beside Ibiza Town, now abandoned to marsh and to more than 140 species of migrating birds.

**Location:** 38.9178, 1.4514

**Categories:** wetlands, nature, conservation, history, Ibiza, birdwatching

## Introduction

The name means, plainly, the strips. Ses Feixes is a crescent of low, wet ground curving around Ibiza Bay from the edge of Ibiza Town to the beach at Talamanca, and for centuries it was carved into hundreds of narrow farming plots, each a slender strip of soil reclaimed from the marsh. Muslim engineers a thousand years ago turned this soggy ground into one of the island's richest gardens. Today the plots lie mostly abandoned, and the water they were built to control has crept back, drawing flocks of migrating birds to a place people have largely forgotten.

## The Strips

Ses Feixes takes its name from the feixes themselves, the narrow bands of cultivated land laid out in parallel lines so that each farmer worked his own strip. The plots were subdivided into scores of small holdings, and every one was entered through a distinctive little arch, a portal de feixa, a stone gateway that still marks the old boundaries where it survives. The whole system spread across the wet lowlands between Ibiza Town and Talamanca, a geometry of channels and gardens pressed right up against the growing city. It is one of the more unusual agricultural landscapes in the Mediterranean, a marsh turned into a grid by hand.

## Water Engineered by Hand

The genius of Ses Feixes was its plumbing. During the island's period of Muslim rule, farmers introduced sophisticated hydraulic techniques, threading the ground with channels called sequies, along with sluices and waterwheels, to move and distribute water precisely across the plots. A road cut through the wetland in the 1970s, fragmenting the marsh into two sectors, the Prat de Vila and the Prat de Ses Monges. For generations these gardens were among Ibiza's main sources of vegetables, grains, legumes, and fruit. The irrigation network is considered so remarkable a legacy of the island's Islamic agriculture that it has been proposed for recognition alongside Ibiza's other UNESCO-listed heritage.

## A Haven the World Forgot

As farming retreated, nature returned. The wet lowlands of Ses Feixes are now an important wintering ground and resting place for migratory birds, a key haven for wildlife on an island otherwise crowded with people. The area supports a wide variety of flora and fauna, including rare bats and reptiles and more than 140 species of birds. Its value has only grown as wetlands like it vanish across the Mediterranean, drained and built over from one coast to the next. What looks, from the roadside, like neglected scrub is in fact one of the region's increasingly scarce refuges for wild things.

## Between Neglect and Rescue

The future of Ses Feixes has hung in the balance for years. The plots suffered decades of abandonment as their farmers moved on and their arches crumbled, and the site sits under constant pressure from the expanding city at its edge. Authorities have designated the area a green belt and floated stronger protections, but the measures have not always matched the site's importance, and its long-term condition remains uncertain. Ses Feixes is caught between two identities: a historic engineering marvel worth preserving, and an unglamorous marsh easily paved. Which one wins will decide whether a thousand years of strips survives the next century.

## Flight Context

Ses Feixes lies at 38.918N, 1.451E, on the northern edge of Ibiza Town, forming a green, low-lying crescent that curves around the head of Ibiza Bay toward the Talamanca beach; it sits about 4 nm northeast of Ibiza Airport (LEIB). From 1,500-2,500 feet it reads as a patch of flat, marshy green wedged between the dense city and the water, distinct from the built-up blocks around it. Use Ibiza Town's harbor and the old town of Dalt Vila as your orientation anchor, then look just north. Summer visibility is typically excellent; the wetland's texture shows best in low, angled light.
