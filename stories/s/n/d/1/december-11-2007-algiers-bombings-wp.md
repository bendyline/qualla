# December 11, 2007, Algiers Bombings

Two car bombs targeting the United Nations and Algeria's Supreme Constitutional Court killed dozens in Algiers, marking al-Qaeda's deadliest strike in North Africa and devastating the international humanitarian community.

**Location:** 36.7761, 3.0600

**Categories:** modern-conflict, terrorism

## Introduction

At 9:30 on a Tuesday morning, the first car bomb detonated in the Ben Aknoun district of Algiers, near the Supreme Constitutional Court. Ten minutes later, a second blast struck the road separating the United Nations offices from the UNHCR headquarters in the Hydra neighborhood. The UNHCR building was leveled. The UN building partially collapsed, burying staff beneath the rubble. It was December 11, 2007, and al-Qaeda in the Islamic Maghreb had just carried out its most devastating attack, striking at the heart of both the Algerian state and the international community.

## Two Blasts, Ten Minutes Apart

Each car bomb contained approximately 800 kilograms -- nearly 1,700 pounds -- of explosives. The first targeted Algeria's constitutional court, a symbol of state authority. The second was aimed with precision at the cluster of international organizations in the Hydra neighborhood, where the UN Development Programme, UNHCR, and other agencies maintained their offices. The explosion in Hydra was catastrophic. The UNHCR offices were flattened entirely. The section of the UN building that collapsed housed primarily UNDP staff. Among the dead was a Chinese construction worker from a nearby site. The official death toll, provided by Interior Minister Yazid Zerhouni, was significantly lower than the figures reported by hospital and rescue officials, who put the number at roughly three times the government's count.

## An Attack on Humanitarians

The targeting of the United Nations compound transformed what might have been seen as a domestic Algerian security matter into an international crisis. Eleven UN workers were confirmed dead, a number later raised to seventeen as search teams worked through the rubble. UN spokeswoman Maria Okabe reported that many people remained unaccounted for, potentially trapped beneath collapsed concrete. Jean Fabre, head of the UNDP's Geneva office, described the ongoing search for survivors. Al-Qaeda in the Islamic Maghreb claimed responsibility, framing the attacks as a religious triumph by "the Knights of the Faith" in defense of Islam. The bombings underscored a grim evolution in the Algerian insurgency: from the civil war's internal massacres of the 1990s to attacks calibrated for maximum international attention and symbolic impact.

## The Long Shadow of Civil War

The 2007 bombings were not an isolated eruption. They represented the latest chapter in a cycle of violence that had begun with the military's cancellation of the 1992 elections and the ensuing civil war, which claimed an estimated 200,000 lives. By 2007, the worst years of mass killings had passed, but armed Islamist groups had regrouped and rebranded. The Salafist Group for Preaching and Combat had formally merged with al-Qaeda the previous year, adopting the name al-Qaeda in the Islamic Maghreb. The December bombings were both a declaration of continued capability and a bid for relevance within the global jihadist movement. For Algiers, a city that had endured waves of violence across decades -- from the Battle of Algiers in 1957 to the suburban massacres of the 1990s -- the bombings were another wound in a landscape already scarred beyond easy healing. Chadli Hamza, a UN staff member killed in the blast, was later memorialized by his colleague Karima Bennoune, who described him simply as a neighbor and a friend.
