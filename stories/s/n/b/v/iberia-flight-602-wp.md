# Iberia Flight 602

On 7 January 1972 a routine Valencia-to-Ibiza flight, carrying workers home after the holidays, flew into a mountainside minutes from landing, killing all 104 aboard.

**Location:** 38.9036, 1.2511

**Categories:** Aviation History, Air Disasters, Ibiza, Balearic Islands, Aviation Safety, 1972

## Introduction

'Get me a beer ready, we are here.' Those were among the last words from the cockpit of Iberia Flight 602, radioed to Ibiza Airport a little after noon on 7 January 1972. The captain sounded relaxed, a man minutes from home after a short hop across the water. He was, in fact, minutes from a mountainside. Moments later the aircraft flew into high ground on Ibiza and exploded on impact, killing all 98 passengers and 6 crew aboard. There were no survivors.

## A Short Hop Home

Flight 602 was meant to be the easiest kind of journey. It was a domestic service from Valencia across to Ibiza, barely a hundred kilometres of open water, the sort of flight regular travellers stop noticing. Most of the 98 passengers were Valencia natives heading back to Ibiza for work, returning to the island after the Christmas and New Year holidays. Early January, the festivities over, ordinary lives resuming. They were workers and families, the everyday human traffic between two cities joined by a short stretch of sea. For many it was simply the last leg of the holidays, the routine crossing that reconnected the island to the mainland. Some were surely dozing, some watching the Mediterranean slide past below, all of them expecting to be on the ground within the hour. None of them knew the flight would not arrive.

## The Maestro Victoria

The aircraft carrying them had a name and a history. It was a Sud Aviation SE 210 Caravelle, a sleek French twin-jet that first flew on 25 June 1963 and was delivered to Iberia two weeks later, on 9 July. Powered by a pair of Rolls-Royce Avon turbojets, it was christened Tomás Luis de Victoria, after the great Spanish Renaissance composer, a name later shortened to the simpler Maestro Victoria. Caravelles like it were a familiar sight in Spanish skies, workhorses of Iberia's domestic network, and this one had flown safely for more than eight years. By the winter of 1972 it was a mature, well-proven machine, nothing about it a cause for concern.

## The Crew

The men flying it were seasoned. In command was Captain José Luis Ballester Sepúlveda, thirty-seven years old, with seven thousand hours in the air behind him. Beside him sat first officer Jesús Montesinos Sánchez, and behind them flight engineer Vicente Rodríguez Mesa worked the systems. It was an experienced cockpit on a familiar route, the kind of crew and flight that inspired no special caution. That, in the end, was part of the tragedy: nothing about the day marked it as different until it already was.

## Into the Mountain

At about 12:15 pm the captain radioed Ibiza Airport and asked to descend to 5,500 feet. It was then, according to the airport, that he added the line that has followed the disaster ever since: 'Get me a beer ready, we are here.' It was a small, human remark — the sign-off of a man who believed the hard part was over. But the aircraft was descending toward rising ground it could not see, and it flew straight into the side of a mountain on Ibiza. The Caravelle exploded on impact. All 98 passengers and all 6 crew were killed instantly.

## The Weight of an Ordinary Flight

There was nothing wrong with the aeroplane. That is the hardest fact of Flight 602. This was a controlled flight into terrain — a sound aircraft, flown by a capable crew, driven into the earth by a fatal gap between where they believed they were and where they actually were. In the decades that followed, accidents of exactly this kind pushed the aviation world toward the ground-warning systems and disciplined procedures that now guard against them. But that is cold comfort for 1972. Somewhere in Valencia and Ibiza, families set places that were never filled. What was lost that January noon was not a statistic. It was a plane full of people going back to work, a few minutes from home.

## Flight Context

Flight 602 came down at roughly 38.90°N, 1.25°E, in the hills of southwestern Ibiza while descending toward Ibiza Airport (LEIB), about 10 km to the southeast. The island's terrain rises quickly here — Sa Talaiassa, Ibiza's highest point at around 475 m, stands nearby — a sobering reminder for pilots of the very hazard that destroyed this flight: high ground on the approach path. Overfly the area with terrain awareness at 3,000–5,000 ft. Treat the site as a place of memory for the 104 people who died here in 1972.
