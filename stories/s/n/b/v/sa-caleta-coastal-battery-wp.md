# Sa Caleta Coastal Battery

Three concrete gun rings on a low Ibizan headland, built in the Spanish Civil War to guard the airfield that is now the island's holiday gateway.

**Location:** 38.8672, 1.3342

**Categories:** History, Military History, Spanish Civil War, Fortifications, Ibiza, Ruins

## Introduction

Three rings of concrete crouch on a low headland above the sea, their rims cracked, their guns long gone, the only movement the wind off the water. Look up and you may catch a jetliner sliding down the sky toward Ibiza, wheels already dropping, a few hundred holidaymakers aboard. They are following, almost exactly, the line these guns were built to defend. In 1936, as the Spanish Civil War tore the country apart, someone stood on this rock west of the cove called Sa Caleta and decided it commanded the approaches far too well to leave undefended.

## Chosen for the View

The battery sits on a small rocky headland on Ibiza's southwest coast, just west of the sheltered cove of Sa Caleta. Its builders picked the spot for one reason above all: from here the guns looked clear across the bay to the approaches of a new airfield. During the war that airfield was a temporary military strip known as Es Codolar - the same stretch of flat coastal ground where Ibiza's international airport handles millions of passengers today. Nationalist forces raised the emplacements between 1936 and 1937, early in a conflict that would grind on until 1939 and cost Spain hundreds of thousands of lives, neighbour set against neighbour. On this island the front line proved less a battle than a garrison, but the concrete went up all the same, aimed at the sky and the sea.

## Three Rings of Concrete

The battery is built around three open gun emplacements, each a circular pit five metres across with concrete walls a full metre thick. Two of them are linked by a tunnel; the third stands slightly apart, reached by its own stairway and paired with a small stone building of its own. A fourth concrete structure nearby once held something more delicate - a rangefinder, perhaps, or searchlights to sweep the water after dark. The guns themselves vanished long ago, but they left their signatures behind. In two of the emplacements, a ring of twenty-eight base-plate bolts, each about thirty millimetres across, still studs the floor where the barrels once bolted down. Run a hand over them and you are touching the exact geometry of a weapon that no longer exists.

## The Barracks Below

This was not only a firing position but a place where men lived. Downhill from the guns, away from the cliff edge, stand the remains of stone bunkhouses and barracks, most of them burnt out inside and slumping toward ruin. The largest is a U-shaped block roofed with arched ceilings, and on a small rise nearby sits what looks like a rainwater tank - a reminder that this dry headland offered no fresh water of its own. From the back of the big barracks a trench runs seaward, deepening into a tunnel that opens above the beach, its mouth still some six metres off the ground. Machine-gun positions guard both flanks of the site. One of them is scorched and broken, damaged by fire at some point no plaque bothers to explain.

## Concrete Outlasts the Cause

What strikes you most is how thoroughly the sea and the sun have taken the place back. Salt has pitted the walls, fire has hollowed the barracks, and scrub has crept over the trenches. The war that raised these guns has passed into history books and family silences, but the emplacements remain, too heavy and too solid to bother demolishing. Walkers on the coastal path pick their way among the rings, peer into the tunnel, and photograph the bolt patterns without always knowing what they meant. Children clamber over emplacements that once held live artillery; couples watch the sunset from a firing step. Overhead the descending jets keep coming, season after season, tracing the very approach these guns once stood ready to protect. It is a strange kind of memorial - unlabelled, weather-worn, and impossible to ignore once you know what you are looking at.

## Flight Context

At 38.8672 N, 1.3342 E, the Sa Caleta battery sits on Ibiza's southwest coast just west of the island's airport (Ibiza, LEIB), roughly 3 km from the runway at Es Codolar. From 1,000-2,500 ft it appears as a small dark headland above a rocky cove, the concrete emplacements visible as pale circles against the scrub. It lies almost directly under approach and departure paths to LEIB, so expect controlled airspace and traffic - VFR sightseeing here means close attention to arrivals. Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies about 150 km northeast across the sea on the Spanish mainland. Clearest views come with the low, raking light of early morning or late afternoon, when the circular pits throw sharp shadows.
