# Cala Comte

A west-facing pair of beaches on Ibiza's quiet southwest shore, famous for glass-clear turquoise water and a sunset staged behind a chain of offshore islets.

**Location:** 38.9625, 1.2203

**Categories:** Beaches, Ibiza, Balearic Islands, Nature, Coastal

## Introduction

At Cala Comte, the sun does not simply set — it stages a performance, sinking behind a chain of low islets scattered across the western horizon like stepping stones into the sea. S'Espartar, Illa des Bosc, Sa Conillera: their silhouettes turn the water gold and then rose, and each evening a crowd gathers on the headland to watch. This is the southwest corner of Ibiza, where a small sandy beach and a scatter of rocky coves hold some of the clearest water in the Balearic Islands.

## The Count's Beaches

The name reaches back to the 13th century. Cala Comte — Cala Conta in Spanish, Platges de Comte in Catalan — takes its title from Nuño Sánchez, Count of Sardinia and Roussillon, who played a part in the Christian conquest of Ibiza in 1234. Comte simply means 'count,' and the plural, Platges de Comte, 'the Count's Beaches,' is the more honest description of what you find. There are really two beaches here, set side by side on a low rocky headland: Platja de Tramuntana, which faces north, and Platja de Ponent, which opens to the west. Between them a spine of pale rock reaches toward the sea, and around its edges a handful of smaller coves hide in the folds of the shore.

## Water Like Glass

What draws people, above all, is the water. Shallow and astonishingly clear, it shades from pale turquoise near the sand to deep blue offshore, kept transparent by the sea currents that sweep constantly through the coves. The western beach, gentle and shallow, fills with families and children; the eastern side, quieter and more sheltered, has long served as an unofficial nudist spot. The clarity is not merely luck — in 2010 Cala Comte was one of fifteen beaches on Ibiza to earn a Blue Flag, the international mark of clean water and careful management. On a still day the seabed stays visible many metres out, every ripple of sand and dark patch of weed distinct beneath the surface.

## Islands on the Horizon

Cala Comte's great gift is its outlook. Because the main beach faces west, the horizon here is not empty water but a broken line of islets — S'Espartar, Illa des Bosc and Sa Conillera — rising low and wild just offshore. They give the view depth and drama, and they turn the daily sunset into something people plan their evening around. As the light drops, the islets darken to flat silhouettes and the sea between them seems to catch fire, and the headland fills with onlookers who have come, quietly, for nothing more than this. Few beaches on the island frame the end of the day so completely.

## Reaching the Cove

For all its beauty, Cala Comte is easy to reach — which in high summer is both blessing and curse. On the crown of the headland sit a restaurant, a shop and toilets, with lifeguards on duty through the warm months and a cliff-top car park that overflows by midday in July and August. The beach lies in the municipality of Sant Josep de sa Talaia, but the nearest large resort is Sant Antoni. From there it is a signposted drive on the EI-700 road, or a summer bus, or — best of all — one of the ferries that run across the bay of Sant Antoni and set you down almost on the sand.

## The Other Ibiza

It is worth remembering where this is. Ibiza's global fame rests largely on its nightclubs, but Cala Comte belongs to the island's other self — the quieter southwestern shore of low cliffs, pine and open sea. Here the draw is not the beat of a club but the slow turn of the light, the cold clear water and the islands offshore. People come, swim, and stay to watch the sun go down. In a place built on spectacle, it is a spectacle that costs nothing at all.

## Flight Context

Cala Comte lies at 38.96°N, 1.22°E on the southwest coast of Ibiza. From 2,000–3,000 ft the low twin-beach headland is easy to identify by the vivid turquoise shallows around it and by the string of offshore islets — S'Espartar, Illa des Bosc and Sa Conillera — running out to the west; the broad bay of Sant Antoni opens just to the north. Best viewed in late-afternoon light, when the west-facing water glows. Ibiza Airport (LEIB) is about 15 km to the southeast on the island's southern plain, the natural approach and departure field for the area.
