# Xàbia

A Costa Blanca town where a teardown as easily reveals a Bronze Age farm as a foundation, layering thirty thousand years of history beneath its streets and the shadow of Montgó.

**Location:** 38.7892, 0.1631

**Categories:** Coastal Towns, Costa Blanca, Marina Alta, Archaeology, Beaches, Spanish History

## Introduction

Demolish an old house in Xàbia and you are as likely to uncover history as foundations. One teardown near the church revealed a 14th-century dwelling with its cistern and well still intact. Another 18th-century home turned out to have been built directly on top of a 3,000-year-old Bronze Age farm, its ancient cabins and grain silos preserved beneath the newer floors. The old Valencians who wrote the local guides put it plainly: walk where you like here — town, valley or hillside — and you are walking on history.

## Three Towns in One

Xàbia — Jávea, in Spanish — is really three places wearing one name. The old town climbs inland in a maze of narrow, winding streets, its houses hung with wrought-iron balconies and pierced by Gothic windows, gathered around ancient churches like the fortress-like Sant Bertomeu. Down at the water, the working port lines its quays with restaurants and bars where the day's catch still comes ashore. A few kilometres along the shore, the Arenal spreads its sand and its beach clubs — the town's commercial and holiday heart. Over all of it rises Montgó, the elephant-shaped massif whose slopes hold much of Xàbia's deepest past.

## Under Every Street, a Century

Those slopes have been inhabited for at least 30,000 years, and deep inside the Cova del Barranc del Migdia, Neolithic artists left schematic rock paintings some 4,500 years ago. Residents digging in their own gardens have turned up Stone Age handaxes, Roman potsherds and Muslim ceramics without trying. The hills around Xàbia hold some of the earliest known Neolithic settlements in the Western Mediterranean, where people farmed and herded from around 3000 BC into the Valencian Bronze Age, between 1900 and 900 BC. The local museum displays the harvest of all this: Iberian beads, decorated pottery, stone axes, pestles and mortars — some, remarkably, first exposed by a fox digging its burrow into a hill crowned by the Santa Llúcia hermitage.

## The Prince and the Monk

One story the town keeps is older and stranger than most. In the 6th century, Christian Visigoth monks are said to have founded the monastery of San Martín here. Local tradition holds that Hermenegild, son of the Visigoth king Leovigild of Toledo, fled to that monastery after enraging his father by marrying a Christian girl. When the king's soldiers came to arrest him, every monk but one aged brother escaped to Portichol — and the prince and the old monk were killed. Whatever the truth of the tale, its residue is real enough: even today you meet people in Xàbia carrying Visigoth names.

## Eight Centuries, Then Gone

The Moors held this coast for centuries, arriving around 714 AD. After the Christian reconquest, new settlers repopulated the town, and archaeologists have found their 14th-century graves — some reopened again and again to add later burials — clustered near the vanished foundations of a fortified tower. Most of the Muslim inhabitants who remained were farmers, the people who first terraced these hillsides and coaxed orchards from thin soil. The last of them, by then the Moriscos, were expelled from Jávea and Dénia in 1609. Of their long presence little survives above ground now but a scatter of inscribed gravestones and broken ceramics.

## The Sea's Larder

Xàbia has always fed itself from the water. Its kitchens are famous for seafood — paella marinera bright with shellfish, and arròs a banda, rice cooked in fish stock and served with humbler fish and potatoes. The hills lend sweetness in turn: the town is known for almond pastries and for its raisins, the dried Muscat grapes that were once a mainstay of the local economy. Beyond the plate lies the coast itself. The cove of Granadella, ringed by pine and pale cliff, is among the loveliest on the Costa Blanca, and the clear water off Xàbia draws divers, kayakers and sailors to a shore that has been rewarding visitors for thirty thousand years.

## Flight Context

Xàbia sits at 38.79°N, 0.16°E on the Marina Alta, wrapped around a bay between two capes. From 3,000–4,000 ft the town's three parts read clearly — hilltop old town, port, and the Arenal beach strip — beneath the bulk of Montgó (753 m) rising immediately to the northwest. The headlands of Cap de Sant Antoni and Cap de la Nau, and the pine-ringed cove of Granadella to the south, make strong visual waypoints. Nearest airports are Alicante-Elche (LEAL), about 90 km southwest, and Valencia (LEVC) to the north, with Ibiza (LEIB) out across the sea to the east. Watch for terrain-induced turbulence and cloud building on Montgó's summit.
