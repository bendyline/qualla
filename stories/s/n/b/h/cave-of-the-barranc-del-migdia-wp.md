# Cave of the Barranc del Migdia

A cave high on the sunlit face of the Montgo where, nearly five thousand years ago, Copper Age people carried the bundled bones of their dead for a second, sacred burial.

**Location:** 38.7889, 0.1661

**Categories:** Caves of Spain, Landforms of the Valencian Community, Archaeological sites in Spain, Prehistoric sites in Spain, Marina Alta

## Introduction

Nearly five thousand years ago, people climbed the sunlit flank of the Montgo carrying the bones of their dead. They were not burying the freshly deceased. The remains they carried had already been laid to rest once, somewhere else, and stripped by time to the skeleton; now they were gathered into bundles and brought up to a cave near the head of a ravine, 375 metres above the sea, to be placed in the earth a second time. The Cave of the Barranc del Migdia keeps the record of that careful, deliberate act.

## A Place Called Midday

The cave takes its name from the ravine at whose head it sits, the Barranc del Migdia, the Ravine of Midday, so called for the southern, sun-facing side of the Montgo on which it lies. It is a fitting name for a burial place: a slope that holds the light, high above the valley where the living worked their fields. To reach it meant a real ascent, up through the scrub of the mountain to a threshold between the everyday world below and whatever the people who climbed here believed lay beyond. The effort itself was surely part of the meaning.

## Carried Up the Mountain

When archaeologists opened the cave's central hall, beginning excavations in 2009, they found it had served as a funeral chamber. Across three seasons of work they recovered the remains of eight people, women, men, and children, deposited not as full bodies but as ordered packets of bones. That detail tells a story on its own. These were secondary burials: the dead had been interred elsewhere first, then exhumed once reduced to skeletons and reburied in this high, sunlit place. Someone chose this cave, and made the climb, and arranged the bones with intention. Whatever these people believed about death, it asked real effort of the living.

## Gifts for the Journey

The dead did not come empty-handed. Buried alongside them were objects meant to help them on the passage to another life: finely worked flint arrowheads, handmade ceramic vessels, an adze of polished stone, and, most telling of all, a copper awl. That copper marks the moment in prehistory the cave belongs to, the Chalcolithic or Copper Age, when metal was new and rare and precious enough to send into the ground with the dead. The grave goods speak of a people who imagined death as a journey and equipped their loved ones for it accordingly.

## 4,683 Years

Radiocarbon dating gives the burials a startlingly precise age: around 4,683 years, placing them in the late Neolithic. The people who made them lived in the valley below in small hamlets, growing crops and keeping livestock, still hunting the slopes of the Montgo for what the land offered. On the walls of the cave they left another trace of themselves, schematic rock paintings, the spare, symbolic art of Iberian prehistory, whose meaning we can guess at but never quite read. The cave was clearly a place set apart, for the dead and for something sacred. These were not wandering hunters but rooted people, the first farmers of the valley, and the care they took with their dead reflects settled lives with room for ritual.

## The Cave Gives Up Its Secrets

The site was first discovered in 1989, but only recently has modern science begun to question it in earnest. Under the direction of the Cirne Foundation, researchers have studied the physical anthropology of the bones, the prehistoric diet, the vegetation of the ancient landscape, and the DNA of the buried, the last analysed at the University of Mainz in Germany. Among the questions they hope to answer is the most human one of all: were these eight people a single family, laid together across generations, or were they individuals singled out for the honour of burial here? The Montgo has not finished speaking. Whatever the DNA finally reveals, the cave has already given back something rare: eight individuals, named by science if not by history, whose burial makes the prehistoric people of this coast suddenly, movingly specific.

## Flight Context

The cave lies at about 375 metres on the southern, sun-facing slope of the Montgo massif at 38.79 degrees north, 0.17 degrees east, above Xabia. It is not visible from the air as such, but the great bulk of the Montgo, a prominent limestone ridge between Denia and Xabia, is one of the region's dominant landmarks and marks the site's location. A pass at 3,000 to 5,000 feet gives the whole massif and the coast beyond. Alicante-Elche (LEAL) lies about 80 km southwest; Ibiza (LEIB) sits east across the sea.
