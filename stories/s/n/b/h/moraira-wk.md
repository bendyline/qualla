# Moraira

A Costa Blanca fishing village that refused to become Benidorm, keeping its pines, its low skyline and an 18th-century gun fort right on the sand.

**Location:** 38.6880, 0.1345

**Categories:** Coastal Towns, Costa Blanca, Marina Alta, Beaches, Spanish Cuisine, History

## Introduction

A squat stone fort sits directly on the beach at Moraira, its curved seaward wall facing the water it was built to watch. Locals describe its footprint as a pezuña de buey — an ox's hoof — for the odd rounded shape it presses into the sand. For most of its history this was not a resort at all but a fishing village bracing against the horizon. And for centuries, the horizon was exactly where the danger came from.

## The Fort on the Sand

The threat was the Barbary corsairs, raiders from North Africa who swept the Mediterranean coast seizing captives for the slave markets. Moraira and its neighbours fortified themselves against them for generations, and by the early 1740s the Spanish crown decided the old defences were no longer enough. Under Philip V, the military engineer Nicolás Bodín directed construction of a modern gun fort, built from cut stone quarried on the coast itself. The royal Bourbon coat of arms above the single north-facing gate is dated 1742. The semicircular façade turns south toward the sea, so that any ship approaching the anchorage came under the castle's guns before it could put a boat ashore. Restored and open to visitors, the little fortress is now the most photographed building in town.

## The Town That Refused to Grow Tall

Drive south along the Costa Blanca and the skyline eventually erupts into the wall of towers that is Benidorm. Moraira chose the opposite path. The town capped building heights and took deliberate steps to protect the native pine groves that shade the valley down to the shore, and the result is a low, green, whitewashed resort rather than a canyon of concrete. That restraint made it magnetic to a certain kind of visitor. Foreign holidaymakers and retirees — many of them British, German and Dutch — flock here each summer until the population swells, then drain away in winter, leaving the pines and the fort to keep their long watch over the bay.

## From Nets to Villas

Moraira never entirely shed its fishing-village roots. It shares an identity with the inland town of Teulada, the pair often spoken of in a single breath as Teulada-Moraira — one guarding the hills, the other the harbour. Above the resort, the 16th-century watchtower on the Cap d'Or headland still surveys the coast, a companion sentinel to the beach fort below, and from both you look down on the little port and its coves. From the town's terraces the eye is drawn north to the Penyal d'Ifac, the great limestone monolith rearing straight out of the sea at neighbouring Calp — a landmark visible for miles up and down this stretch of shore.

## The Port and Its Coves

For all the villas on the hills, Moraira's heart is still its little port. Fishing boats share the water now with pleasure craft, and the shoreline breaks into a string of small beaches and coves — the sandy Platja de l'Ampolla near the castle, the sheltered Cala de l'Andragó a short walk away. Above them, the cliffs of the Cap d'Or hold the Cova de les Cendres, a cave whose deposits record thousands of years of human use on this coast, from the Stone Age onward. Inland, the modern Auditori de Teulada-Moraira gives the twin town a stage for concerts and performance — a deliberate sign that this is a living community, not merely a summer address.

## A Table by the Sea

The kitchen here still faces the water. Moraira's cooks lean on what the boats bring in, turning it into arròs a banda — rice simmered in fish stock and served apart from the fish it was cooked with — and paella marinera thick with shellfish. The surrounding hills answer with wine. This corner of the Alicante Denominación de Origen is known for Moscatel Alicante, a pale white pressed from Muscat grapes, and for mistela, a sweet fortified dessert wine made from the same fruit. Order a glass at dusk, with the old fort blackening against the last light on the water, and the whole long history of the place seems to condense into a single sip.

## Flight Context

Moraira sits at 38.69°N, 0.13°E on the Marina Alta coast, roughly midway between Calp and Xàbia. Track the shoreline at 2,000–3,000 ft: the 18th-century Castell de Moraira on the beach and the watchtower on the Cap d'Or headland are the close-in markers, while the towering Penyal d'Ifac at Calp, just up the coast, is unmistakable from far off and makes an excellent visual waypoint. Nearest airports are Alicante-Elche (LEAL), about 80 km southwest, and Valencia (LEVC) to the north, with Ibiza (LEIB) across the sea to the east. Summer afternoons bring a reliable onshore sea breeze along this coast.
