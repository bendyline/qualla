# Lake Turkana

A vast turquoise sea in the heart of one of Earth's hottest deserts, Lake Turkana is the largest permanent desert lake in the world - and the cradle of humankind.

**Location:** 4.0500, 36.0167

**Categories:** Lake Turkana, Archaeological sites in Kenya, Archaeological sites in Ethiopia, African Great Lakes, Endorheic lakes of Africa, Lakes of Ethiopia, Lakes of Kenya, Ethiopia–Kenya border, International lakes of Africa

## Introduction

From the air it looks impossible: a sheet of jade water, nearly 290 kilometers long, shimmering in the middle of a desert so hot and dry that nothing around it suggests a lake should exist. Sailors of the air and the few who reach its shores call it the Jade Sea, for the turquoise glow that algae lend its surface in calm weather. This is Lake Turkana, the largest permanent desert lake on Earth and the world's largest alkaline lake - a body of water with no outlet, fed by a river born in distant highlands, and ringed by some of the most important fossil grounds in human history.

## A Lake That Should Not Be

Three rivers pour into Turkana - the Omo from Ethiopia and the Turkwel and Kerio from Kenya - but none flow out. The lake loses water only to the fierce desert sky, evaporating roughly two meters a year, and its level rises and falls with the moods of rain hundreds of kilometers away. The water is brackish and so heavy with fluoride that it makes poor drinking, pushing communities toward underground springs. Winds scream across the surface because the water heats and cools more slowly than the surrounding land, and violent storms blow up without warning. In the middle stands Central Island, an active volcano that still breathes vapor into the air. Everything here is extreme: the heat, the wind, the chemistry, the light.

## Black Lake, Jade Sea, Rudolf

The lake has worn many names. The Samburu called it Basso Narok - "Black Lake" - long before any European saw it. In 1888 the Hungarian explorer Count Samuel Teleki and his companion Ludwig von Hoehnel reached its shores and named it Lake Rudolf, after Crown Prince Rudolf of Austria-Hungary, their expedition's royal patron. The prince died the very next year, but his name clung to the maps through the entire colonial era. Only in 1975 did Kenya's first president, Jomo Kenyatta, rename it Lake Turkana, after the people who have long lived along its shores. The nickname Jade Sea, born from that distant turquoise sheen, is itself likely a European coinage - one poetic label among the many this water has answered to.

## The Cradle of Humankind

No lake on Earth has told us more about who we are. The Turkana Basin's volcanic ash and shifting shorelines proved ideal for burying and preserving the dead, and generations of researchers - the Leakey family foremost among them - have read that record bone by bone. West of the lake, the Lomekwi 3 site yielded stone tools 3.3 million years old, the oldest ever found. Australopithecus anamensis fossils here reach back some four million years. The two-million-year-old Skull 1470 emerged from the eastern shore in 1972, and in 1984 Kamoya Kimeu found the Turkana Boy, a nearly complete Homo erectus skeleton, near the dry Nariokotome riverbed. Meave Leakey added the 3.5-million-year-old Kenyanthropus platyops, "the flat-faced man of Kenya." UNESCO inscribed the lake's national parks as a World Heritage Site in 1997 for exactly this reason - and listed them as endangered in 2018.

## Life on the Edge

For all its harshness, Turkana teems. About fifty fish species swim its waters, a dozen found nowhere else, descendants of a time when the lake brimmed high enough to spill into the Nile and let crocodiles and Nile perch migrate in. In a 1968 survey the lake held an estimated 14,000 Nile crocodiles, once Africa's largest population, and its shallows still draw greater flamingos and clouds of migrating birds. On the parched grasslands beyond, Grevy's zebra, beisa oryx, and reticulated giraffe move between the predators. People, too, have always adapted: more than a dozen languages from Nilotic and Cushitic roots are spoken around the lake, evidence of countless migrations, and communities still shift between fishing, herding, and farming according to what each year allows. Violence is part of the record as well - at Nataruk, west of the lake, archaeologists found the roughly 10,000-year-old remains of people who died in what may be the earliest scientifically dated evidence of warfare among hunter-gatherers.

## A Sea Under Threat

Turkana's future hinges on a river it cannot control. The Omo supplies as much as ninety percent of the lake's water, and Ethiopia's Gibe III dam, with the irrigation schemes it enables, threatens to throttle that flow. Estimates of the damage vary sharply - from a drop of a couple of meters during the reservoir's filling, as dam proponents argue, to ten meters and a sharp rise in salinity, as Kenyan groups like Friends of Lake Turkana warn. A ten-meter fall could turn the brackish water undrinkable and gut the fishery that hundreds of thousands of people depend on, with hardship rippling across the borders of Kenya, Ethiopia, and South Sudan. The lake has its defenders, and even a wind farm of hundreds of turbines now harvests its famous gales. But the Jade Sea, ancient cradle and desert miracle, has rarely faced a test like this.

## Flight Context

Lake Turkana stretches from about 4.05 degrees N, 36.02 degrees E northward across the Kenya-Ethiopia border, roughly 290 km long and unmistakable as a long, narrow jade ribbon in otherwise barren desert. Central Island's volcanic cone near the middle is a striking visual reference. Recommended viewing altitude is 6,000-10,000 feet above ground for the full sweep of the lake; visibility is generally excellent in the dry air, but the lake is notorious for sudden, violent winds and storms, so expect turbulence near the surface. Lodwar (HKLO) lies near the southwest shore and Eldoret (HKEL) farther south are the principal serviced airfields; an airstrip at Sibiloi serves the eastern parks. Heat haze is severe at midday.
