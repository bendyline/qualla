# Tripoli, Greece

Three times razed and three times rebuilt, the city the Ottomans called Tripolitsa has always risen from its plateau — carrying inside it the violent pivot point of Greek independence.

**Location:** 37.5083, 22.3750

**Categories:** Tripoli, Greece, Municipalities of Peloponnese (region), Populated places in Arcadia, Peloponnese, Greek prefectural capitals, Greek regional capitals

## Introduction

The folk song called it simple and direct: "Forty lads from Livadeia." It was a song about the siege of Tripolitsa, and it circulated in Greek villages for generations before the city had been rebuilt, before the walls had gone up again, before the modern name — Tripoli — had quite settled. The city on the Arcadian plateau had been destroyed and begun again so many times that its name itself was uncertain: Italian mapmakers of 1687 called it Goriza e Mandi et Dorbogliza. In 1463 it appeared in documents as Droboliza, and in ruins. The Ottomans settled on Tripoliça and built it into an administrative capital. Each name was a new layer on the same limestone plateau.

## Tripolitsa Under the Ottomans

By the early nineteenth century, the city the Ottomans called Tripoliçe was the administrative center of the Morea Eyalet — the Ottoman province of the Peloponnese, sometimes called the pashalik of Tripolitsa. It was a multiconfessional city with large Muslim populations, both Turkish and Albanian, as well as a Jewish community. The walled city commanded the Arcadian plateau from its position at roughly 650 meters elevation, its fortress visible across the surrounding basin.

The first major rupture came in 1770, during the uprising known as the Orlov Revolt, when Greek insurgents backed by Russian encouragement rose against Ottoman rule. The uprising collapsed, and in retaliation Albanian mercenaries in Ottoman service entered Tripolitsa and killed approximately 3,000 of the city's Greek inhabitants before the intervention of Osman Bey, their commander, halted the massacre. The city recovered its Ottoman character, but the memory of 1770 did not leave the Arcadian countryside.

## The Siege and the Fall of 1821

When the Greek War of Independence broke out in 1821, Tripolitsa became the central objective of the rebellion in the Peloponnese. The Greek insurgents under Theodoros Kolokotronis, who was born near the city and knew the terrain intimately, began a systematic siege. Their headquarters was the village of Valtetsi, 12 kilometers to the west, where the terrain favored defense. The Maniots under Petros Mavromichalis joined the encirclement from the south.

The city fell on 23 September 1821, following months of siege and starvation inside the walls. The capture was violent: the Muslim and Jewish populations of Tripolitsa were killed or dispersed. This was a catastrophic event, and its violence was acknowledged even among Greek writers of the period. Kolokotronis himself later described the destruction in terms that combined triumph and horror. What had been the Ottoman capital of the Morea became, in a day, a ruin.

Ibrahim Pasha of Egypt, commanding Ottoman forces on behalf of the Sultan, retook the city on June 22, 1825, after the Greek garrison had withdrawn. His army occupied the plateau for nearly three years. In early 1828, as the Egyptian expeditionary force prepared to evacuate the Peloponnese under European diplomatic pressure, Ibrahim destroyed the city and tore down its walls. The folk song about forty lads from Livadeia was already old by then. The city it commemorated had been leveled twice in seven years.

## A Capital Rebuilt

The independent Greek state was formally established in 1830, and the question of what to do with Tripolitsa's ruins was an administrative one as much as an emotional one. The site was central to the Peloponnese, its geographic position unchanged by the destruction. The old Ottoman buildings — the walls, the mosques, the administrative structures — were cleared away. In their place, in the neoclassical style that the new Kingdom of Greece adopted as its official architectural language, a European-looking city rose on the Arcadian plateau.

Architect Ernst Ziller, a German-born designer who built public buildings across Greece in the second half of the nineteenth century, contributed the courthouse and the archaeological museum to the new Tripoli. The city was renamed — dropping the Turkish suffix, recovering a Greek-inflected identity. It served as the capital of the Arcadia district and emerged over the following decades as the administrative, commercial, and transportation center of the central and southern Peloponnese.

The municipality had 44,165 inhabitants at the 2021 census. The University of the Peloponnese established its flagship campus here in 2000. Among the city's notable sons: Theodoros Kolokotronis himself (born 3 April 1770), poet Kostas Karyotakis (1896–1928), ultramarathon runner Yiannis Kouros (born 1956), and three men who served as Prime Minister of Greece — Epameinontas Deligeorgis, Konstantinos Georgakopoulos, and Alexandros Papanastasiou.

## The Weight of the Names

Modern Tripoli carries the sediment of its names carefully. The castle walls built during the Ottoman period still define the footprint of the city's older core, even though the walls themselves were demolished after 1830. The main squares are named Kennedy and Georgiou B' (George II) — Cold War-era gestures that have outlasted the politics that prompted them. The southern district runs along a street called Washington.

What the city chose to keep, deliberately, is the memory of Kolokotronis. His statue stands in the main square. The football stadium bears his name. The War Museum on the square of Agios Vassilios holds his bust, his weapons, and the material record of 1821. The siege of Tripolitsa — the event that destroyed the old city and made the new one possible — is the axis on which modern Tripoli turns its own history. It is both the city's founding wound and its founding myth, held in plain sight at the center of the plateau.

## Flight Context

Tripoli sits at 37.508°N, 22.375°E at approximately 650 meters elevation on the central Arcadian plateau. From altitude, the city is the dominant urban form in the broad Tripoli Basin, a flat agricultural polje flanked by the Mainalo mountains to the northwest and the Parnon range to the southeast. The Moreas Motorway (A7) bisects the basin and leads the eye directly into the city grid. Nearest major airport is LGKL (Kalamata International Airport), approximately 65 km to the southwest. The flight path from Kalamata to Tripoli crosses the Taygetos foothills before opening onto the plateau — a dramatic transition from steep mountain terrain to flat basin.
