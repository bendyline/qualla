# Axum

The sacred city of Ethiopia - capital of an ancient empire, keeper of granite obelisks, and home to a chapel that Ethiopians say holds the Ark of the Covenant.

**Location:** 14.1218, 38.7315

**Categories:** Axum (city), Capitals of former nations, Holy cities, Populated places in the Tigray Region, World Heritage Sites in Ethiopia, Populated places established in the 1st millennium BC, Ancient Greek geography of East Africa, Cities and towns in Ethiopia

## Introduction

The granite obelisks still stand. The tallest survivor, the Obelisk of Axum, rises 24 meters above a dusty field in the Tigray Highlands, carved with mock windows and a false door to suggest a thirteen-story tower. It is roughly 1,700 years old. When Italian troops seized Axum in October 1936, they broke the obelisk into five pieces and shipped it to Rome on Mussolini's orders. It took Ethiopia until April 2005 to get it back. UNESCO supervised the reinstallation in 2008. Standing under it now, you realize these stones marked graves - one under your feet held a king - and that the people who carved them were running an empire that minted its own coins, translated the Bible into its own Ge'ez script, and kept libraries and palaces and churches across a highland capital that Cosmas Indicopleustes, visiting in the sixth century, described as adorned with bronze statues of unicorns.

## The Aksumite Empire

The settlement above which Axum rose has roots in the 7th-4th centuries BC. By the first century AD, the anonymous Greek author of the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea called it a metropolis, reachable by camel caravan from the Red Sea port of Adulis. At its height, the Aksumite Empire controlled trade routes from the Sudanese interior to the Arabian Peninsula. Its kings - Ousanas, Ezana, Kaleb, Wazeba - inscribed granite thrones with their victories in three languages: Ge'ez, Sabaean, and Greek. The Ezana Stone, engraved between AD 330 and 356, records the king's conquests and his conversion to Christianity. Ethiopia became the second kingdom in history to adopt Christianity as a state religion. Saint Frumentius, a Syrian Christian captured and raised at the Aksumite court, is credited with bringing the new faith to Ezana. Coptic monks working in Axum later produced the only surviving complete copy of the Book of Enoch - a text that otherwise disappeared from world literature.

## The Stelae

The granite stelae of Axum are unlike anything else in Africa. In the Northern Stelae Park, the Great Stele - intended to be the tallest, at 33 meters - broke during construction and now lies in pieces on the ground. These obelisks marked royal tombs. Metal discs were originally affixed to their flat front faces, and their sides were carved with false doors, false windows, and the horizontal beam-ends that Aksumite architects called monkey-heads. The style repeats in Aksumite palaces and churches across the Tigray region - at Adulis on the coast, at Matara, at the 4th-century Ta'akha Maryam and 6th-century Dungur palace ruins in Axum itself. The Gudit Stelae, west of town, mark 4th-century graves of a different kind. King Bazen's Tomb, a megalithic chamber, may be the oldest structure in the city. Nothing about this is fake. Every monument is exactly as old as the plaque says.

## The Ark of the Covenant

Axum is the spiritual capital of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and at its center stands a compound that contains two churches of Our Lady Mary of Zion, a small chapel between them, and - according to every Ethiopian Christian who has ever told the story - the actual Ark of the Covenant. The old church was built in 1665 by Emperor Fasilides. The new cathedral was commissioned by Emperor Haile Selassie in 1955 to fulfill his vow to Our Lady of Zion for Ethiopia's liberation from Italian occupation. Between them is the Chapel of the Tablet, built at the same time as the new cathedral, funded personally by Empress Menen Asfaw. Only one person is permitted inside: the guardian monk, who enters upon his appointment and does not leave. No pilgrim, no patriarch, no emperor may enter. Haile Selassie interrupted a state visit by Queen Elizabeth II in 1965 to attend the new cathedral's dedication. The Ark - whatever is in the chapel - is what the entire ritual orbits around.

## Coronation and Collapse

In 1270 the Solomonic Dynasty was founded, and medieval Ethiopian emperors began returning to Axum for coronations. Zara Yaqob was crowned there in 1436 and stayed three years. The ceremony involved cutting a cord held by the daughters of Aksum, marking the emperor's ascent to King of Zion. Then in 1535, during the war between Ethiopia and the Adal Sultanate, the troops of Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi sacked Axum and burned the church. Before the sack, Emperor Lebna Dengel had distributed 1,705 golden objects from the cathedral to provincial governors for safekeeping. A large stone object - unnamed in the records - was carried off to a place called Tabr. The Portuguese Jesuit Manuel de Almeida, visiting after the war, counted only 100 inhabitants in a landscape of ruins. In 1872, Emperor Yohannes IV was crowned in Axum - the last emperor crowned there - trying to restore something of what had been lost.

## 2020

Before the Tigray War began in November 2020, Axum was one of Ethiopia's leading tourist destinations. Between 28 November and 15 December 2020, Eritrean soldiers operating alongside federal Ethiopian forces killed somewhere between 100 and 800 civilians in the town and around it. The Axum massacre targeted men and boys at the old cathedral and across the surrounding neighborhoods. Survivors buried their dead in shallow graves while the fighting continued. The war ended on paper in November 2022 with the Pretoria Agreement, but the Tigray region has been slow to recover. Tourism has not returned. The priests at the old cathedral keep up the liturgy. The guardian monk stands watch over the Chapel of the Tablet. The obelisks still tilt and lean where they have stood for seventeen centuries, outlasting, so far, whatever has tried to take Axum apart.

## Flight Context

Axum sits at 14.122 N, 38.731 E in Ethiopia's Tigray Region, at 2,131 m elevation near the base of the Adwa mountains. Axum Emperor Yohannes IV Airport (HAAX) serves the town. Mekelle (HAMK) is 250 km southeast; Gondar (HAGN) 200 km west. The Ethiopian Highlands require cruise altitudes of 14,000+ feet - mountain wave turbulence is common, particularly in the afternoon. Subtropical highland climate brings clear dry seasons from October to May, ideal for visual flight; summer rains reduce visibility. Ethiopian airspace remains sensitive near the Eritrean border.
