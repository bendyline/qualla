# Gondar

Ethiopia's royal capital from 1636 until the late 18th century, city of 44 churches, emperors, empresses, and two hundred years of Ethiopian political power.

**Location:** 12.6000, 37.4667

**Categories:** city, ethiopia, history, capital, amhara

## Introduction

In 1636, Emperor Fasilides made a choice that would shape Ethiopia for two centuries. He picked a hilltop 35 kilometers north of Lake Tana and declared it his katama - his royal camp - and then made it permanent. Before Fasilides, Ethiopian emperors had always moved with the court, setting up tents wherever the season and the politics demanded. Afterward, there was Gondar: the city, the capital, the home of emperors through what Ethiopians would remember as the Gondarine period. The ruins of their castles still rise above the modern city. Over 443,000 people live among them now.

## How Fasilides Changed the Game

Gondar's location was not arbitrary. Situated in the midst of Kemant country, it sat where caravans from Sudan and the Red Sea coast naturally converged. Taxation of trade was easier when the emperor stayed put. Fasilides built his castle - the imposing Fasil Ghebbi - and five other churches including Madhane Alem and Fit Abbo. A pleasure pool with a stone pavilion beside the Qaha River became known as the Bath of Fasilides. A Yemeni ambassador who visited in 1648, Hassan ibn Ahmad al-Haymi, marveled at beds from Constantinople, mattresses from India glittering with gold, and swords from Sennar with hilts 'set with marvelous stones and precious gems.' By the time Fasilides died in 1667, the city was so established that his successor Yohannes I saw no reason to move.

## Quarters of a Capital

In 1669-70, Emperor Yohannes I ordered the separation of the city into Christian, Muslim, and Jewish sectors. Muslims settled in the lower town near the Qaha River, in a quarter called Eslam Bet. The Beta Israel - Ethiopian Jews - were assigned four quarters including Abarra and Kayla Meda, also near the Qaha. Other key areas included the royal enclosure itself, known as Makkababiya or Fasil Gemb; Addebabay, the public square and marketplace; Abun Bet, home of the Abuna who headed the Ethiopian Church; Eccage Bet, residence of the chief monk; Qagn Bet, the noble quarters; and Dengay, inhabited by Christian merchants. During Iyasu I's reign (1682-1706), the city's population was estimated to exceed 60,000. The emperor is said to have kept the doors of his palace open at all hours, even at night, to show his subjects that he trusted them. His castle was described by contemporaries as 'more beautiful than the house of Solomon.'

## Decline and the Mahdist Sack

After Iyasu I's death in 1706, the emperors became more interested in palace luxuries than in governing. Power drifted to regional warlords. The traveler James Bruce observed that the true center of gravity was moving east, to Tigray, whose governors controlled access to the Red Sea. Ras Mikael Sehul, a Tigrayan warlord, effectively took control of the throne in 1767 after killing the young emperor Iyoas. The Zemene Mesafint - the Era of the Princes - followed, with Gondar sacked, burned, looted, and sacked again by various factions. Emperor Tewodros II, who took control of the town in 1854, eventually decided Gondar was a 'town of priests who do not love me' and ordered it violently sacked in late 1864. Then came the worst blow: in January 1888, Mahdist Sudanese forces entered the city and burned all its churches. 'Those who were brave were slaughtered on the spot,' the Ethiopian historian Heruy Wald Selassie recorded, 'while the cowardly fled. The remainder, women and children, were made prisoners and taken into slavery.' A year later the Mahdists came back. Gondar's population, once 60,000, had fallen to about 8,000 by the 1870s. The 'Camelot of Africa' was a ruin.

## The Italian Piazza

On 1 April 1936, during the Second Italo-Ethiopian War, General Achille Starace's 3,000 troops on more than 400 vehicles occupied Gondar without a fight after a difficult 380-kilometer march from Omhajer. Within two years, 2,000 Europeans had moved in. Italian planners segregated local inhabitants into districts west and south of the castle enclosure and built a new 'piazza' retail district adjacent to the Royal Enclosure in 1930s modernist style. Cinemas, shops, and government buildings went up in simplified Italian Moderne. The Italians built a mosque with a minaret beside the Saturday market - the first minaret in Gondar - to curry favor with local Muslims. A paved road connected the city to Asmara in Italian Eritrea. On 13-17 November 1941, British and Italian forces fought in Gondar during the East African Campaign of World War II; Italian resistance in the city marked the last organized stand of Italian East Africa.

## The 20th and 21st Centuries

After liberation, Gondar slowly rebuilt. The University of Gondar - today home to Ethiopia's main faculty of medicine - became a major regional institution. In May 1991 the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front captured Gondar during Operation Tewodros, part of the offensive that ended the Derg regime. The 21st century has been difficult again. The War in Amhara, which began in 2023, brought violence back to a city whose stones have absorbed too much of it already. In September 2023, Fano militia launched a large-scale offensive against Gondar; heavy fighting left at least nine civilians dead and dozens injured. The Royal Enclosure still stands. The Debre Berhan Selassie Church still has its ceiling of 80 angelic faces. Timkat still fills the streets each January with white-robed worshippers. Notable sons and daughters of the city include the singer Aster Aweke, the filmmaker Haile Gerima, and Yityish Titi Aynaw, crowned Miss Israel in 2013 - reminders that a city famous for emperors goes on producing everything else.

## Flight Context

Gondar is at 12.60°N, 37.47°E, elevation 2,133 meters, in the Amhara Region of northwestern Ethiopia. Gondar Airport - Atse Tewodros (ICAO: HAGN, IATA: GDQ) - is 18 km south of the city center in Azezo. From cruising altitude, the city appears as a dense cluster of red-roofed buildings on a plateau overlooking the 3,600 sq km expanse of Lake Tana 35 km to the south. The Simien Mountains rise to the northeast with peaks above 4,500 meters. The Blue Nile drains from Lake Tana southeast toward the Sudanese border. Best visibility October through May; June through September brings intense afternoon thunderstorms.
