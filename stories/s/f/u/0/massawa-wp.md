# Massawa

An Eritrean Red Sea port that has served Ottoman, Khedivate, Italian, Ethiopian, and Eritrean flags without ever really belonging to any of them.

**Location:** 15.6000, 39.4333

**Categories:** Red Sea ports, Eritrea, Italian colonial history, Ottoman Empire, Eritrean War of Independence

## Introduction

The Italian architects who rebuilt Massawa after the 1921 earthquake did something unusual for a colonial power. They preserved the street plan. The irregular pattern of narrow alleys between coral-block houses - the pattern the Ottomans had known, the pattern that predated even them - stayed intact. They faced the new reinforced-concrete buildings with coral and cement plaster to keep the look. Then fascism arrived and they introduced apartheid. It is Massawa in miniature: a port that has lived under seven flags in roughly a thousand years, where each occupier has built on top of what the last one left, and where the thing that endures is not any imperial project but the city itself, sitting in one of the hottest marine climates on Earth.

## A Port That Replaced a Port

Massawa sits on two islands - Basé, with the historical center, and Taulud - connected to the mainland and to each other by causeways. The city emerged as a port sometime between the eighth and tenth centuries, rising as the older port of Adulis declined just 50 kilometers to the south. Arab geographers knew it early. The ninth-century chronicler Ya'qubi called it Badi in his Kitab al-Buldan, borrowing from the local Tigre name Batsee. The tenth-century writer al-Masudi knew it as Nase. The Dahlak Archipelago offshore was dominated by Arab traders while the coast itself remained under the Beja. When the Sultanate of Dahlak rose in the eleventh century, Massawa became the critical link between the Muslim-dominated Red Sea coast and the Christian highlands of Ethiopia. Ethiopian emperors like Yeshaq I and Zara Yaqob projected power here, sometimes taking tribute, sometimes raiding; in 1464 or 1465 Abyssinian forces sacked both Massawa and Dahlak.

## Portuguese, Ottomans, and the Qadi's Court

Diogo Lopes de Albergaria secured a Portuguese foothold in 1513. King Manuel I ordered a fortress that was never built. Portuguese ships used the cisterns and wells, and in 1541 D. João de Castro charted the harbor on his way to attack El Tor and Suez. Then the Ottomans arrived under Özdemir Pasha and pushed the Portuguese out. The Ottoman governor tried to rule through a naib drawn from the Balaw people, answerable to the Ottoman governor at Suakin across the Red Sea in Sudan. Underneath this layered imperial authority, the local qadi - the Islamic judge - held real power, with jurisdiction stretching from the Habab in the north to the Afar coast in the south. In 1809 Ottoman interest revived and a Turkish governor was posted. In 1813 to 1823 an Egyptian governor followed. After that, Ottoman-appointed governors became regular, and the old naibs lost much of their role.

## A British Policy, an Egyptian Project

In 1855, Emperor Tewodros II told the British consul Walter Plowden he intended to take Massawa, partly because the Ottomans there were using the port as a depot for kidnapped Christian children being sold into slavery. Plowden was sympathetic. The Foreign Office, viewing the Ottomans as a useful ally, refused to back the annexation. In 1865, Ottoman consent handed Massawa to the Khedivate of Egypt. The Egyptians initially considered abandoning the port - buildings were derelict, troops slept in tents, cholera was endemic - and favored nearby Zula instead. Then Werner Munzinger took over as governor. In March 1872 construction began on a new government building and customs house. By June there was a school and a hospital. Causeways were extended. After the Ethiopian victory at the Battle of Gura, Emperor Yohannes IV demanded the port; the Egyptians refused, and Ras Alula marched 30,000 men on Massawa before withdrawing. The Egyptian grip held through the 1870s and into the 1880s.

## Italian Colony, Italian Apartheid

The British, thinking the Egyptians could not hold on and unwilling to take the port themselves or see it fall to France, let the Italians seize it in February 1885. Massawa became the capital of Italian Eritrea until Governor Ferdinando Martini moved the administration to Asmara in 1897. The disastrous Italian defeat at Adwa in 1896 ended their highland ambitions. Massawa was rebuilt after the 1921 earthquake in reinforced concrete faced with coral. The Eritrean Railway and the Asmara-Massawa Cableway - at 75 kilometers the longest ropeway conveyor in the world - connected port to capital. By 1928 Massawa had 15,000 inhabitants, 2,500 of them Italian. Then came fascism. Racial laws imposed segregation in residential areas and forbade Eritreans from bars and restaurants reserved for whites. Relationships between Italian men and Eritrean women continued regardless, producing a generation of meticci children whom the Italian state formally refused to acknowledge.

## The Liberation That Came by Speedboat

World War II brought heavy Italian military use, then British dismantlement of the port facilities. Sylvia Pankhurst, the British suffragette-turned-Ethiopianist, protested the British destruction in her book Eritrea on the Eve. Under the Ethiopian-Eritrean Federation of 1952 to 1962, Massawa developed as a naval base with American assistance. Then the long war began. The Eritrean Liberation Front besieged the port in 1977 and 1978 without success. In February 1990, the Eritrean People's Liberation Front launched Operation Fenkil, a surprise attack from land and sea using infiltrated commandos and speedboats. The Ethiopian 606th Corps was destroyed. The supply line to the Ethiopian Second Army in Asmara was cut. Ethiopia's leader Mengistu Haile Mariam responded by ordering the air force to bomb Massawa, causing heavy damage to a city of civilians. When Eritrean independence was complete in 1991, Ethiopia became landlocked. Massawa's Memory Square now holds a memorial of three tanks, monuments to what it cost to liberate the port.

## Flight Context

Massawa is at 15.60°N, 39.43°E. Massawa International Airport (HHMS / MSW) serves the city. Surface-level heat is extreme with mean annual temperature near 30°C. Summer humidity combined with desert heat produces apparent temperatures that are among the world's highest. Winter rainfall totals around 185 mm. From altitude the dual-island geography of Basé and Taulud is distinctive, with causeways visible connecting to the mainland. The Dahlak Archipelago spreads to the east. Nearby Gulf of Zula to the south. Watch for dust haze during windy periods. The Eritrean Railway alignment is visible running west toward Asmara.
