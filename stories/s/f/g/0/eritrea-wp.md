# Eritrea

A Horn of Africa republic of nine recognized peoples, a 1,200-kilometer Red Sea coastline, and one of the longest independence struggles in modern history.

**Location:** 15.3333, 38.9333

**Categories:** country, horn-of-africa, red-sea, eritrea, independence

## Introduction

Nine languages are recognized as indigenous here. Tigrinya speakers make up about half the country; Tigre speakers another third; and then come the Saho, Kunama, Nara, Afar, Beja, Bilen. Arabic rounds out the list. Nowhere else in the Horn of Africa does so much linguistic diversity fit into so small a country - Eritrea is about the size of Pennsylvania. The name comes from the Greek Erythraean, meaning red, the ancient term for the sea that shapes everything here. Italy adopted the name for its colony in 1890. The Eritreans kept it after they won, which took thirty years.

## From Aksum to the Bahr Negus

People have been farming these highlands for thousands of years. South Arabian migrants settled the plateau in the first millennium BC and founded the Kingdom of D'mt. From the third to seventh centuries, this region was the northern heart of the Kingdom of Aksum - a trading power that minted its own coins, built obelisks, and converted to Christianity around 340 AD, making Eritrea one of the first Christian regions on earth outside the Roman world. Later, the central highlands were called Mereb Melash - 'Beyond the Mereb' - and ruled by a governor whose title was Bahr Negus, King of the Sea. Medieval Ethiopian emperors claimed authority here. Ottoman sultans seized the coast in the 16th century. Egypt's Khedive briefly held parts of the lowlands in the 1860s. And then Italy arrived.

## Italy's First-Born Colony

Italian troops took Massawa in 1885, pushed into the highlands, and in 1889 the Ethiopian emperor Menelik II recognized the Italian presence at the Treaty of Wuchale - a document whose Italian and Amharic versions famously did not say the same thing. Italy declared the Colony of Eritrea in 1890. For the next fifty years, Italian planners built what would become one of Africa's most architecturally distinctive cities: Asmara, 'Piccola Roma,' filled with modernist boulevards, art-deco cinemas, and the streamlined Fiat Tagliero Building with concrete wings that seem to defy gravity. Eritreans themselves were conscripted into the Ascari, Italy's colonial military force. The colony was the staging ground for Italy's invasion of Ethiopia in 1935-36. In 1941, British and Commonwealth forces drove the Italians out.

## The Thirty-Year War

In 1952, the UN General Assembly federated Eritrea with Ethiopia. The arrangement was supposed to preserve Eritrean autonomy - its own parliament, its own constitution, its own languages of government. In 1962 Ethiopia dissolved the federation and annexed Eritrea outright. The Eritrean Liberation Front formed in 1961; the Eritrean People's Liberation Front split off in 1970. What followed was one of Africa's longest and deadliest liberation wars. Eritrean fighters dug trenches through the Sahel mountains, built underground schools and hospitals in caves near Nakfa, and outlasted multiple Ethiopian regimes - including the Soviet-backed Derg that took power after 1974. In May 1991 Eritrean forces entered Asmara. A 1993 referendum confirmed independence with 98.5 percent approval. Africa had a new country; Ethiopia had lost its coastline.

## A State That Refuses Elections

Tell this part honestly. Since independence, Eritrea has been governed by one party - the People's Front for Democracy and Justice - and one man, President Isaias Afwerki, the former EPLF commander. No national elections have ever been held. The constitution drafted in 1997 has never been implemented. Obligatory military and civil service, introduced after the 1998-2000 war with Ethiopia, stretches for years with no fixed end. Journalists who dissent disappear into prisons. Hundreds of thousands of young Eritreans have fled across borders rather than serve. Eritrea consistently ranks among the world's least-free countries. In 2018, a peace treaty with Ethiopia's Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed officially ended the frozen war that had justified much of the repression. Some restrictions eased. Most did not. In December 2025, Eritrea withdrew from the Intergovernmental Authority on Development, accusing the regional body of 'becoming a tool against' countries like itself.

## The Landscapes Between the Extremes

Eritrea contains some of the most varied topography in a small country anywhere. The highlands around Asmara lie between 1,500 and 3,500 meters, with a Mediterranean climate and valleys carved into plateaus that turn emerald after the summer rains. The western lowlands drop to tropical savanna where elephants still roam near Tessenei. The Sahel mountains chain north toward Sudan through terrain so difficult it sheltered a liberation army for decades. The eastern escarpment holds Eritrea's only subtropical rainforest. And the southern coast includes the Danakil Depression, where temperatures can reach 55 degrees Celsius - some of the hottest recorded temperatures on Earth - and volcanic vents bubble with sulfuric mud. The Dahlak Archipelago offshore hosts coral reefs and dugongs. The country fits almost every major African biome into one border.

## Flight Context

Eritrea spans from 12.36°N to 18.05°N along the western Red Sea, with Asmara at 15.32°N, 38.93°E (elevation 2,325 m). Primary airports: Asmara International (ICAO: HHAS, IATA: ASM) and Massawa International (HHMS). From cruising altitude, the spine of the country is the dramatic escarpment where the highlands drop more than 2,000 meters to the coastal desert - one of Africa's sharpest elevation transitions. The Dahlak Archipelago scatters across turquoise shallows offshore. The Danakil Depression lies to the southeast, visibly ash-dark from volcanic flows. Airspace is tightly controlled; consult NOTAMs before overflight. Best visibility November through March; summer brings the southwest monsoon over the highlands and intense heat haze over the coastal plain.
