# Djibouti City

Founded in 1888 on an uninhabited stretch of coast, this Red Sea port now hosts bases from six nations and calls itself, with characteristic bluntness, the French Hong Kong in the Red Sea.

**Location:** 11.5950, 43.1481

**Categories:** geography, djibouti, capitals, ports, horn-of-africa

## Introduction

In 1888 the French picked an uninhabited point on the southern coast of the Gulf of Tadjoura and declared it a town. They called it Djibouti, after the peninsula, and chose it over the older settlement of Obok because the harbour was deeper and Harar was closer by caravan. By 1895 Djibouti had 5,000 inhabitants. By the start of the twentieth century, 10,000. Today, including its suburbs, it has 780,000 - more than two-thirds of Djibouti's entire national population, concentrated in a single coastal city that serves as the country's capital, its biggest port, its financial centre, and the gateway through which 70 percent of Ethiopia's maritime trade passes.

## Why the French Came

The diplomatic notes exchanged between France and Britain on 2 and 9 February 1888 fixed the colonial boundary that gave France the southern coast of the Gulf of Tadjoura - including the peninsula called Ras Djibouti. French strategists wanted a coaling station and fuelling point for ships bound to Madagascar and Indochina. What they got, once the Franco-Ethiopian Railway started running inland in the 1910s, was an entrepot that quickly eclipsed the old caravan trade with Zeila in British Somaliland. Harari coffee and khat from southern Ethiopia began flowing through Djibouti. Issa and Afar nomads left their herds and came to work as dockers - the first urban proletariat of what would eventually become an independent country. Yemeni, Egyptian, Greek, Armenian, and Italian merchants followed. Leonce Lagarde became the first governor of the French Somali Coast in 1896, and a town built for 5,000 soon needed infrastructure for ten times that.

## The Carmii

The Second World War broke Djibouti harder than most colonial cities. On 21 June 1940, Italian airstrikes from nearby Italian East Africa hit the town. Two Italian planes were shot down, but fires and explosions tore through the port. After France fell, Djibouti's colonial government came under the pro-Axis Vichy regime. The Royal Air Force bombed the town on 25 September in response, and the Vichy governor Pierre Nouailhetas instituted a brutal reign of terror. Famine set in. Malnutrition killed many; 70 percent of the dead were women and children. The locals called it the carmii, after a coarse sorghum normally fed to cattle. The head doctor at the main hospital killed himself in despair. Arab dhows smuggled in what they could through the Allied blockade. After Vichy's leaders - no humanitarians themselves - recalled Nouailhetas in October 1942 for cruelty, he escaped to Portugal; returning later, he was acquitted by a French military tribunal in 1953, a verdict that sparked outrage in Djibouti. The colony was liberated by Free French forces on 28 December 1942.

## The Port That Became a City

In 1948, Djibouti received the status of French overseas territory. Port infrastructure expanded to handle 2,000 ships a year. A new Côte Française des Somalis currency was introduced in 1948, pegged to the gold standard. Djibouti became a free port. In August 1966 an official visit by French President Charles de Gaulle was met with demonstrations and rioting by people demanding independence. De Gaulle ordered a referendum. Eleven years later, on 27 June 1977, Djibouti declared independence. The capital then held over 110,000 people. Since then - under the presidency of Hassan Gouled Aptidon and then his successor Ismail Omar Guelleh, who has ruled since 1999 - Djibouti City has grown into the most densely populated and geopolitically significant point on Africa's eastern coast.

## Who Lives Here

Djibouti City is multi-ethnic. The largest group is Somali - about 303,000 speakers. The second largest is Afar, about 101,000. Both languages belong to the Cushitic branch of the Afroasiatic family. Arabic and French are the two official languages. Colloquial Arabic includes about 40,000 speakers of Ta'izzi-Adeni (Southern Yemeni) Arabic, reflecting the Yemeni refugee waves that arrived during the 2015 civil war. French, inherited from the colonial period, remains the primary language of instruction and is spoken as a first language by about 14,200 people. Omani Arabic and Amharic are also spoken in immigrant communities. The population rose sharply through the twentieth century through successive refugee waves from Ethiopia, Somalia, and Yemen. In 2001 many undocumented immigrants were expelled. In 2023 the government launched a major campaign against illegal immigration.

## The Base Landlord of Africa

Djibouti today earns a substantial portion of its national income by leasing land to foreign militaries. The United States maintains Camp Lemonnier - the largest U.S. base in Africa - next to Djibouti-Ambouli International Airport. France, Italy, and Japan have their own bases. In 2017 China opened the Chinese People's Liberation Army Support Base, its first ever overseas military installation. The port handles about 2,500 ships a day, and the new Djibouti International Free Trade Zone (DIFTZ) west of the city is among the largest in Africa. The Doraleh container terminal handles most of Ethiopia's trade. The 2017-opened standard-gauge railway connects Djibouti to Addis Ababa, replacing the century-old Franco-Ethiopian line. The presidential palace overlooks the Gulf of Tadjoura. The Place du 27 Juin in the city centre is marked by Moorish-inspired arches. The city was nicknamed the French Hong Kong in the Red Sea for its cosmopolitan mix. It is a model of political stability in a notoriously unstable corner of the world. What that stability costs - in political freedom, in press restrictions, in the loyalty expected of its citizens - is a question the people of Djibouti answer every time they talk about their country, and every time they do not.

## Flight Context

Djibouti City is at 11.60N, 43.15E, on the coast of the Gulf of Tadjoura, essentially at sea level. Djibouti-Ambouli International Airport (HDAM/JIB) is 6 km south of the city centre. Summer temperatures regularly exceed 41C with high humidity near the coast - significant density altitude and performance considerations. The airspace is shared by the U.S., French, Japanese, Italian, and Chinese military - extensive coordination required. Recommended viewing altitude 4,000-6,000 ft AGL for the coastal urban area; higher for regional context. The approaches over the Gulf of Tadjoura provide excellent visibility of the Red Sea corridor.
