# Djibouti

A small nation at the mouth of the Red Sea where five armies rent basing rights and 1,066,809 people speak Somali, Afar, Arabic, and French.

**Location:** 11.8000, 42.4333

**Categories:** country, horn-of-africa, red-sea, strategic-geography, port

## Introduction

Six foreign militaries pay rent here. On a patch of volcanic desert smaller than Massachusetts, American drones share airspace with French Mirages, Chinese patrol boats, and Japanese logistics planes. Djibouti charges all of them, pockets the hard currency, and uses the strategic real estate - positioned at the throat of the Red Sea where 30 percent of global shipping squeezes through the Bab-el-Mandeb - as its primary export. No other country has turned geography into revenue quite this efficiently.

## The Bab-el-Mandeb Chokehold

Look at a map of Djibouti and the country's logic becomes clear in a glance. It sits at the Gate of Tears - the Bab-el-Mandeb - where Africa and the Arabian Peninsula nearly touch. Ships heading to or from the Suez Canal must pass within sight of Djiboutian radar. The Gulf of Tadjoura cuts a deep fjord into the interior, providing natural deep-water anchorage. The Danakil Depression, one of the hottest places on Earth, burns to the south. The 1,066,809 people counted in the May 2024 census live clustered around a capital city that has grown vertical with glass towers paid for by Chinese port concessions. Roughly 76 percent of Djiboutians are urban dwellers now. The pastoralists who once herded camels across the Grand Bara desert still do, but fewer every year.

## Somali, Afar, and a French Legacy

The country's two largest ethnic groups - the Somali Issa and the Afar - shape everything about daily life. Somali poets still compose gabay, epic verse that can exceed 100 lines and is judged the highest form of literary attainment. Afar elders preserve oral traditions going back thousands of years, to a time when the ancestors of today's Djiboutians traded hides and frankincense with Pharaonic Egypt. The country has two official languages, Arabic and French, inherited from different conquests. Arabic connects Djibouti to the wider Muslim world; 98 percent of the population practices Islam. French, imposed during the colonial period that ended only in 1977, remains the language of instruction and government. Around 17,000 Djiboutians speak French as a first language, and roughly 59,000 speak the local Ta'izzi-Adeni Arabic dialect.

## A Republic of Port Fees

Djibouti the country is essentially Djibouti the port writ large. Ethiopia - landlocked, populous, hungry for imports - sends more than 95 percent of its trade through Djiboutian docks. The revenue from that traffic, plus the rent from foreign military bases, funds a government budget that allocates 20.5 percent to education. New classrooms go up. The University of Djibouti, opened in 2006, graduates engineers and teachers who a generation ago would have had to study abroad. Life expectancy has risen to 64.7 years. Maternal mortality has fallen steeply, from 606.5 per 100,000 births in 1990 to 300 in 2010. Progress is uneven - 93.1 percent of women and girls have undergone female genital cutting, legally banned in 1994 but deeply embedded in custom - and the gap between the city and the pastoral interior remains wide.

## Fah-Fah, Frankincense, and Oud

Djiboutian cuisine tastes like the trading crossroads the country has always been. Fah-fah, also called Soupe Djiboutienne, is spicy boiled beef in a broth thickened with chilies; street vendors in Djibouti City ladle it out at dawn for dockworkers heading to shift. Yemeni fishermen grill their catch in tandoori-style ovens, a culinary fingerprint of centuries of Red Sea trade. Halva - called halwo locally - arrives at Eid celebrations in slabs of sugar, cardamom, nutmeg, and ghee, often studded with peanuts. After meals, hostesses light frankincense in a clay burner called a dabqaad, filling the house with the same resin that Phoenician merchants once carried north. Somali women perform balwo, love songs typically built on a pentatonic scale. Afar men sing praise poetry. The oud, Arabic in origin, plays in both traditions, threading them together.

## Arta, Archery, and the Long Game

In 2007, the Djibouti national football team beat Somalia 1-0 in a World Cup qualifier. It was the country's first World Cup-related win of any kind, and the capital celebrated for days. More recently the World Archery Federation has helped Djibouti establish an international archery training center at Arta, a hill town 40 kilometers southwest of the capital where the Gulf of Tadjoura shimmers below. The project aims to make Arta the archery hub for East Africa and the Red Sea region - a modest ambition that fits the country's approach to its outsized geography. Djibouti cannot be large, so it positions itself as indispensable: a launchpad, a training ground, a trans-shipment point. The Bab-el-Mandeb will not move. As long as ships thread that gate, Djibouti has leverage.

## Flight Context

Djibouti lies at 11.80°N, 42.43°E at the southwestern gate of the Red Sea. Djibouti-Ambouli International Airport (ICAO: HDAM, IATA: JIB) shares runways with Camp Lemonnier, the U.S. military's only permanent base in Africa. From cruising altitude, look for the distinctive triangular coastline where the Gulf of Tadjoura stabs inland, the salt flats of Lake Assal glittering 155 meters below sea level - the lowest point in Africa - and the volcanic cone of Ardoukoba rising from the Afar Depression to the west. Clear visibility is common; the country averages under 150 mm of rainfall annually.
