# Lalibela

Ethiopian rock-hewn churches carved downward into living stone, the work of hands that emptied the earth instead of building on top of it.

**Location:** 12.0356, 39.0462

**Categories:** Ethiopian Orthodox, World Heritage Sites, Rock-hewn architecture, Ethiopia, Medieval Christianity

## Introduction

The eleven churches of Lalibela were not built. They were excavated. At 2,500 meters in the Ethiopian highlands, medieval masons carved downward into volcanic tuff, cutting away stone until the churches emerged - walls, pillars, altars, and vaults all of a piece, each structure a single block of rock with the negative space of a sanctuary inside. A sixteenth-century Portuguese visitor, Miguel de Castanhoso, saw them and wrote that the work appeared superhuman. He was not wrong to be astonished. To Ethiopian Orthodox Christians, Lalibela is one of the holiest cities in the world, a medieval attempt to build a second Jerusalem in stone that the faithful could still reach even as the first Jerusalem fell to Saladin.

## The Zagwe King and His Living Rock

Tradition ascribes the churches to the Zagwe king Gebre Meskel Lalibela, who reigned roughly from 1181 to 1221 and gave the town its name - it was previously known as Roha. The layout of Lalibela's eleven churches was designed as a symbolic representation of Jerusalem, which is why some scholars connect the construction to the years after 1187, when the Muslim leader Saladin retook the actual Jerusalem from the Crusaders. Carving a church out of living rock is not fast work. David Buxton, who established the accepted chronology, believed the labor must have stretched into the fourteenth century. David Phillipson of Cambridge proposed something different: that the churches of Merkorios, Gabriel-Rufael, and Danagel were originally carved between 600 and 800 CE as fortifications or palaces during the Kingdom of Aksum, and were only later repurposed and associated with the name of King Lalibela. Archaeological finds of pottery and animal remains from 900 to 1100 CE suggest the site was a secular settlement before it became a religious one.

## Aksumite Echoes in Stone

Look closely at the doors and windows. They are framed with a repeated motif that mimics the shape of the great stelae at Aksum, the older Ethiopian capital far to the north. The church of Biete Amanuel imitates, in carved stone, the interlocking wooden beams used in Aksumite architecture centuries earlier. Biete Maryam's pitched roof and linear moldings show Syrian Christian influence, and subtle Coptic details appear in decorative elements throughout. But the core style is Ethiopian. David Buxton was adamant on this: the rock-churches follow the tradition of local built-up structures, which themselves preserved Aksumite forms. Foreign travelers from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries - Manuel de Almeida, Hiob Ludolf, Francisco Álvares - often credited Egyptian builders, and an Ethiopian manuscript given to the French explorer Achille Raffray in 1882 even named an overseer, Sidi-Meskal. Later scholarly review of that manuscript showed Raffray's translation was mostly wrong. There is no mention of foreign builders in the original Coptic, Arabic, and Ge'ez texts.

## Fire, Siege, and Survival

The churches have been tested by fire and sword. According to the Futuh al-Habasha of the chronicler Arab Faqih, Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi burned a church at Lalibela during his sixteenth-century invasion of Ethiopia. He described a church carved from the mountain with pillars cut from the same rock, and said a fire was lit inside while a nun threw herself into the flames before being pulled out. The historian Richard Pankhurst expressed skepticism, noting that the Royal Chronicles describing Ahmad's depredations between July and September 1531 do not mention damage to the churches. If Ahmad did burn one, Pankhurst suggested it was probably Biete Medhane Alem, or possibly Gannata Maryam 16 kilometers east of town. Castanhoso, visiting in the 1540s, reported that the Moors had tried to destroy the churches with crowbars and gunpowder and done no damage at all. The stone held.

## A Pilgrim's Town in Modern Wars

Lalibela's modern history has been marked by the wars Ethiopia could not avoid. During the Second Italo-Ethiopian War, Emperor Haile Selassie made a pilgrimage to the churches at considerable personal risk before retreating to his capital in April 1936; Italian forces captured the town shortly after. In 1968, the Shah of Iran visited alongside Haile Selassie. During the Ethiopian Civil War, the town was frequently attacked by the Ethiopian People's Democratic Movement. During the Tigray War that began in 2020, Lalibela changed hands repeatedly between Tigrayan and Ethiopian forces in 2021, and in November 2023 fierce fighting broke out between the Ethiopian National Defense Force and Fano fighters. Through all of this, the churches have remained in use by the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. Pilgrims still come. Liturgies still happen. The town is currently under Ethiopian government control.

## Living Stones

Lalibela is a small town - the 2007 census counted 17,367 residents - built around its airport, its market, its two schools, and its hospital. The earthen architecture of the town itself tells a parallel story to the churches. The two-story stone tukuls and the single-story chika buildings of earth and wattle carry their own centuries of vernacular tradition. Lalibela became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1978, and three-dimensional digital scans of the churches are now accessible online, letting the world see from a distance what the artisans of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries carved by hand. The largest of the churches, Biete Medhane Alem, is considered the biggest monolithic church in the world. Priests still process around its columns with silver crosses. The candles still lean against the rock walls, dripping wax into grooves worn smooth by 800 years of pilgrim footsteps.

## Flight Context

Lalibela is located at 12.04°N, 39.05°E, at approximately 2,500 meters elevation in the North Wollo Zone of Ethiopia's Amhara Region. Lalibela Airport (HALL / LLI) serves the town. The eleven rock-hewn churches are arranged in two principal groups on either side of the Jordan River seasonal stream - from the air, the excavated trenches around Bete Medhane Alem and Bete Giyorgis (church of St. George, cross-shaped) are distinctive. The surrounding highlands are rugged; maintain safe altitude. Weather is generally clear outside the June-September rainy season.
