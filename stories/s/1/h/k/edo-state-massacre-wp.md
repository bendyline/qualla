# Edo State Massacre

In March 2025, sixteen Hausa hunters traveling home for a holiday were killed by a mob in Edo State after false accusations of kidnapping -- a tragedy rooted in Nigeria's cycle of suspicion and vigilante violence.

**Location:** 6.5000, 6.0000

**Categories:** tragedy, civil-rights, modern-history

## Introduction

They were going home for the holidays. On March 28, 2025, sixteen Hausa hunters were traveling from Port Harcourt in Rivers State northward to Kano State, where their families waited for the Sallah festivities. They had been hunting -- it was their livelihood -- and they carried Dane guns, the traditional single-shot rifles used across rural Nigeria for generations. They never arrived. In the town of Uromi, in Edo State's Esan South East Local Government Area, the hunters were stopped, accused, and killed by a mob that acted on suspicion rather than evidence. The weapons in their vehicle were enough to condemn them.

## A Routine Stop, a Fatal Accusation

At approximately 1:30 in the afternoon, members of the Edo State Security Corps and local vigilantes stopped the hunters' vehicle at Udune Efandion, a community within Uromi. During a search, the security personnel found the Dane guns. These are legally common in Nigeria -- crude, muzzle-loading firearms used primarily for hunting game -- but their presence triggered immediate suspicion. In a country where kidnapping and banditry have made travelers from unfamiliar regions objects of fear, the hunters were accused of being criminals. The accusations were false, but they spread faster than any verification could follow. Rather than turning the travelers over to police for proper identification and questioning, the vigilantes raised an alarm. A crowd gathered, and what happened next was captured on video: the sixteen men were beaten, attacked, and set ablaze.

## The Weight of a Pattern

What happened in Uromi was not an isolated event. A 2024 Amnesty International report documented at least 391 mob-related killings across Nigeria between 2019 and 2022, noting a sharp increase over the preceding decade. The pattern is grimly consistent: an accusation -- of kidnapping, theft, witchcraft, or blasphemy -- triggers a crowd, and the crowd becomes executioner before any authority can intervene. Nigeria's security challenges are real. Kidnapping for ransom has plagued travelers on highways across the country, and communities have formed vigilante groups partly because they feel the police cannot protect them. But the gap between vigilance and violence has claimed hundreds of innocent lives, people whose only crime was being unfamiliar in the wrong place at the wrong time.

## Condemnation from Every Corner

The response was swift and came from across Nigeria's political spectrum. President Bola Tinubu called the killings "dastardly" and ordered security agencies to identify and arrest the perpetrators, reaffirming that every Nigerian has the right to move freely within the country. Edo State Governor Monday Okpebholo described the violence as "barbaric and condemnable" and directed a full investigation. The Arewa Youth Consultative Forum demanded justice for the victims and called on the government to guarantee the safety of all citizens regardless of their ethnic origin. Politicians Peter Obi and Atiku Abubakar expressed grief, framing the massacre as part of a broader crisis of violence in Nigerian society. The unanimity of the condemnation underscored how deeply the incident disturbed the nation -- and how familiar the pattern had become.

## Aftermath and Accountability

In the days following the massacre, the Edo State Police Command moved to make arrests. More than thirty suspects were taken into custody in connection with the attack. The state government pledged a comprehensive investigation and vowed that those responsible would face legal consequences. Whether those promises will be fully kept remains an open question -- accountability in mob violence cases has historically been difficult in Nigeria, where the sheer number of participants and the chaos of the events make prosecution challenging. But the scale of public outrage suggested that this case, at least, would not simply fade from memory. The sixteen hunters who died on that road in Uromi were ordinary people with families and a trade, killed by fear and rumor on what should have been an unremarkable journey home.
