# Nimbo Massacre

In April 2016, over 500 armed Fulani herdsmen attacked seven farming villages near Nimbo in Enugu State, Nigeria, killing at least 40 people despite advance warnings to security agencies.

**Location:** 6.7500, 7.2000

**Categories:** tragedy, civil-rights, modern-history

## Introduction

The attack was announced in advance. On April 23, 2016, word reached the farming communities near Nimbo, a border town in the Uzo-Uwani area of Enugu State, southeastern Nigeria, that armed Fulani herdsmen were planning an assault. The intelligence was reported to security agencies immediately. Meetings were held. Preparations were discussed. Two days later, at 5:15 in the morning on April 25, the herdsmen came anyway -- more than five hundred of them -- and killed at least forty people across seven villages. The security response that should have prevented the massacre never materialized.

## Dawn in Seven Villages

The communities of Ekwuru, Nimbo-Ngwoko, Ugwuijoro, Ebor, Enugu-Nimbo, Umuome, and Ugwuachara were farming settlements in a fertile part of Enugu State, close to the border with Kogi State. The people who lived there grew crops on land that had sustained their families for generations. The conflict that reached them was not new -- it was part of a broader and escalating confrontation between settled farming communities and nomadic Fulani herders over land use across Nigeria's middle belt. In the neighboring community of Abbi, just weeks earlier in February 2016, thirty masked Fulani herdsmen had killed two siblings, Fidelis and Mercy Okeja, and left nineteen people missing. Seven houses and motorcycles were destroyed. For the people of Nimbo, the February attack was a warning of what was coming.

## A Warning That Went Unheeded

What made the Nimbo massacre particularly devastating was the fact that authorities knew it was coming. Enugu State Governor Ifeanyi Ugwuanyi later confirmed that intelligence reports had identified herdsmen massing at Odolu, across the border in Kogi State, in preparation for the assault. Those reports were passed to security agencies. But when the attack came before dawn on April 25, the response was absent. The Igbo cultural organization Ohanaeze Ndigbo expressed what many felt -- deep disappointment at the security failures that allowed a forewarned attack to succeed. For the survivors, the betrayal was double: first the violence itself, then the realization that the institutions meant to protect them had failed despite having every opportunity to act.

## Displacement and Fear

In the aftermath, the surviving residents of Nimbo's seven villages fled. Many went to the neighboring communities of Nkpologu and Uvuru-Agada, but the fear spread outward: people in those communities also abandoned their homes, moving further to Nsukka in anticipation of follow-up attacks. The displacement rippled across the region, emptying farming communities and leaving crops untended. The human cost extended far beyond the forty dead. Families were scattered, livelihoods were destroyed, and a pervasive sense of vulnerability settled over communities that had considered themselves safe. The violence exposed how thin the line was between ordinary life and catastrophe in areas where land disputes intersected with ethnic tensions and inadequate security.

## A National Reckoning

The Nimbo massacre intensified a national debate that had been building for years. At its center was the question of land: whether the federal government should create grazing reserves and routes for herders, or whether cattle ranching should be modernized into private operations that did not encroach on farming communities. Nigeria's Minister of Agriculture, Audu Ogbe, argued against grazing routes, noting that ranched cattle produce better yields than grazed ones. Oyo State Governor Abiola Ajimobi warned that seizing land for federal grazing reserves would violate the Land Use Act. The Deputy Senate President, Ike Ekweremadu, reassured the public that the National Assembly would not support the creation of grazing reserves. For the people of Nimbo, the policy debate was not abstract. Forty of their neighbors had died in a dispute over who had the right to use the land they had farmed for generations.
