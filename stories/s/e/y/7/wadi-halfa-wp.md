# Wadi Halfa

Once Nubia's great trading gateway between Egypt and Sudan, Wadi Halfa has been a crossroads for pilgrims, steamers, soldiers, and railways - and finally, for a reservoir that swallowed its older self.

**Location:** 21.7928, 31.3775

**Categories:** Nubian history, Sudan, Lake Nasser, Mahdist War, Communities depopulated for reservoir creation

## Introduction

Medieval pilgrims bound for Mecca passed through this stretch of Nile, and so did telegraph lines, and so did British gunboats, and so did the entire population of a town. Wadi Halfa has been a threshold for as long as anyone has kept records of it. The name in Arabic means something like Valley of Esparto grass, a humble agricultural description for a place that spent most of its history being anything but agricultural - a port, a rail terminus, a caravan node, a garrison, a pilgrim station on the long walk east. Then in 1971 the reservoir rose and turned the whole landscape into something else again.

## Ancient Gateway

Settlements have existed here since before written history. Just across the river, during Egypt's Middle Kingdom period, stood the massive fortress of Buhen - a colonial outpost of the pharaohs, garrisoned to control the Second Cataract trade. Rome reached south this far but never quite occupied the future site of Wadi Halfa itself; the town lived just beyond the edge of imperial jurisdiction. When Christianity came to Nubia, Wadi Halfa fell within the Kingdom of Makuria, and medieval travelers crossing from Egypt toward the Red Sea ports for the hajj to Mecca passed through. The modern Nubian photograph from 1910 of a ruined medieval church near the town suggests how layered this landscape has always been - Egyptian fort, Christian kingdom, Muslim pilgrimage road, all in the same dusty miles of riverbank.

## Steamers and Rails

The modern town was founded in the 19th century as a river port where steamers from Aswan - like the S.S. Nubia - met the desert trade. During the Turko-Egyptian conquest of 1820, Egyptian troops heading south to subdue Sudan used Wadi Halfa as a staging point. A telegraph line to Egypt was completed in 1866. An ill-fated attempt to push a railway farther upstream to Kerma began in 1873 and collapsed. But when the Sudanese rail head was eventually established at Wadi Halfa - connected by steamer to the Egyptian network via Asyut - the town eclipsed the old caravan center of Korosko and became the pivot point of north-south travel in the Nile Valley. Goods transferred from rail to ferry here, then from ferry to steamer, then moved on.

## The Mahdist War

In 1885, Wadi Halfa fell inside the territory claimed by the Mahdist state - the theocratic Islamic movement that had seized most of Sudan and killed General Gordon at Khartoum the same year. Border skirmishes became routine. In 1889 Abd al-Rahman al-Mahdi's army marched through on its way to the Battle of Toski. A decade later, when the British-led Anglo-Egyptian forces moved south to reconquer Sudan, Wadi Halfa became Kitchener's advance base. A photograph survives of the 21st Lancers boarding a steamer here bound for Asyut during the campaign; another, harsher image shows chained Sudanese prisoners hauling British baggage through the town in 1898. These are the faces of empire moving up and down the river, and the town that watched them pass.

## The Resettlement

By 1956 Wadi Halfa had grown to a population of eleven thousand, served by a famous railroad hotel, and wartime British garrisons had kept it busy during the Second World War as a communications post for Allied forces in Africa. Then came the Aswan High Dam. When Sudan's military government announced the forced relocation of the Nubian population to make way for Lake Nasser, the Nubians of Wadi Halfa resisted. On October 23 and 24, 1960, demonstrations broke out in the town against the resettlement. Protests spread to Khartoum on October 26, where police dispersed demonstrators with tear gas. The protests did not change the outcome. Around fifty thousand people were forcibly relocated from the broader flooded region, many of them to New Halfa in the Butana, where disease killed many of the new arrivals.

## The Town Now

The old Wadi Halfa is underwater. The modern town, rebuilt on the shore of the reservoir that drowned its predecessor, has a population of about fifteen thousand and an economy that still centers on the weekly ferry to Aswan. Agriculture remains locally important. The climate is punishing - a mean annual rainfall of half a millimeter, summer temperatures that routinely break 50°C, sunshine ninety-seven or ninety-eight percent of possible hours. A 2005 proposal for a museum and interactive Nubian village to preserve what was lost never quite materialized. Meanwhile, plans for new dams upstream threaten more of what remains of the ancient Nubian landscape - the pyramids of Meroe and El-Kurru, older than those of Egypt, still stand farther south, waiting to see whether they, too, become memories beneath new reservoirs.
