# Abu Simbel

The colossal temples of Ramses II, sawed into numbered blocks and moved sixty meters uphill to save them from a rising reservoir.

**Location:** 22.3369, 31.6256

**Categories:** ancient-egypt, temple, unesco, archaeology, ramses

## Introduction

On September 22, 1968, Rene Maheu, the Director-General of UNESCO, stood in front of a temple that had been cut into pieces, lifted sixty meters uphill, and reassembled in the desert, and gave a speech marking the completion of one of the strangest engineering projects in human history. Behind him, the Great Temple of Ramses II, carved into a sandstone cliff around 1265 BCE for the greatest pharaoh of the New Kingdom, had just been resurrected. The four twenty-meter colossi of Ramses gazed eastward again. The interior chambers aligned, still, on the rising sun. The Nile, however, was no longer below them. In its place stretched the growing blue expanse of Lake Nasser, which would have drowned the temples within a few years if UNESCO had not intervened.

## Ramses Builds Himself a Mountain

The Great Temple took approximately twenty years to build and was completed around the 24th year of Ramses II's reign. He was declaring, in the most expensive propaganda available to a Bronze Age empire, that Egypt's power extended deep into Nubia. The site sat at Egypt's southern frontier, facing the territory of Kush. Any fleet of river traffic heading north from inner Africa, and any army considering an assault from the south, would first have to pass the gaze of four seated Ramseses, each twenty meters tall, each wearing the double crown. The temple was dedicated to the gods Amun, Ra-Horakhty, and Ptah, and to Ramses himself, newly deified. Twice a year the rising sun penetrates the entire length of the temple to illuminate three of those four gods in the inner sanctum. Engineers rebuilding the temple in 1968 preserved the alignment almost exactly.

## The Rescue

The Aswan High Dam, begun in 1960 under Gamal Abdel Nasser, would create a reservoir that would flood Lower Nubia. Abu Simbel would be underwater within a decade. In 1960 UNESCO launched the International Campaign to Save the Monuments of Nubia, asking member states to fund what seemed like an impossible solution: cut the temples into transportable blocks and rebuild them on higher ground. The Swedish, Egyptian, and international engineering teams began in 1964. Over four years they sawed both the Great Temple and the smaller Temple of Nefertari into roughly a thousand numbered sandstone blocks, some weighing over twenty tons. Workers lifted the blocks clear of the rising water, carried them to a site sixty meters higher and two hundred meters back, and reassembled them inside a specially constructed concrete dome disguised as a mountain. In September 1968, the work finished. Nefertari's temple had moved with the Great Temple. The colossi watched a different horizon now.

## The Airstrip and the Movies

In 1964, before the relocation was complete, a committee from Egypt's Ministry of Civil Aviation scouted Abu Simbel for an airstrip. They needed a way to fly tourists in once the salvaged temples reopened. Abu Simbel Airport was built in 1969 with a single 2,000-meter runway and an apron large enough for two small aircraft. It remains a domestic airport, with ambitions to expand. The temples themselves became cinematic shorthand for Egyptian grandeur. They appear in the 1978 film adaptation of Agatha Christie's Death on the Nile, and in the 2001 film The Mummy Returns as part of the road to Ahm-Shere. Tourists continue to arrive by plane from Aswan and Cairo, climb the ridge in the predawn dark, and watch the sun rise behind the colossi the way it has for three thousand years.

## A Record of Visitors

The temples collected graffiti for two and a half millennia before archaeologists arrived. In 1813, the Swiss explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt found the heads of the colossi half-buried in drifting sand, the rest of the temple swallowed by the desert. Four years later, the Italian Giovanni Belzoni returned, tunneled in, and entered the chambers. On the legs of the statues, visitors over the centuries have left Greek, Phoenician, Aramaic, and Coptic inscriptions. The most famous is a five-line Ionic Greek inscription on the left shin of the northernmost colossus, left by mercenaries of Psamtik II during his campaign against Kush in 593 BCE. These graffiti came with the blocks when the temples moved in the 1960s. The names scratched into sandstone traveled sixty meters uphill along with everything else.

## Visible from Above

From the cockpit, Abu Simbel is a geometry problem solved in the desert. The relocated Great Temple and the smaller Nefertari Temple sit at the edge of a broad concrete-and-sandstone dome, designed to blend with the surrounding cliffs when seen at ground level. From the air the dome gives the complex a slightly too-neat profile, a hill too regular to be natural. Lake Nasser stretches to the east, deep blue against the tan of the Libyan Desert. The airstrip runs parallel to the shore. Below the ridge lies a small village also built for tourism. The pharaoh who wanted to be seen from the Nile is now seen from the sky, and the mountain he claimed for himself is now, partly, a modern creation that exists to protect what he actually made.

## Flight Context

Located at 22.34 N, 31.63 E on Lake Nasser's west bank in southern Egypt. Nearest airport: Abu Simbel Airport (ICAO HEBL), 2 km from the temple complex, 2,000-meter runway. Best viewed from the east at low to medium altitude.
