# Gashaka Gumti National Park

Nigeria's largest national park straddles two worlds -- flat savanna woodlands in the north and rugged montane rainforest in the south -- harboring 103 mammal species and the ruins of a German colonial fort atop Gashaka Hill.

**Location:** 7.3500, 11.5200

**Categories:** national-parks, wildlife, nigeria, conservation, colonial-history

## Introduction

Somewhere on the summit of Gashaka Hill, the crumbling walls of a German fort still stand among the grass. Built before 1918 as a watchtower over the surrounding valleys, the garrison was seized by the British Frontier Force during the scramble for the Mambilla Pass -- a narrow corridor into Cameroon that both empires considered worth fighting over. Nearby, the headstones of fallen German soldiers dot the hillside, half-swallowed by vegetation. Below, stretching across 6,731 square kilometres of northeastern Nigeria, Gashaka Gumti National Park sprawls from the flat Gumti woodlands in the north to the cloud-wrapped mountains of the Gashaka sector in the south. Established in 1991, it is the largest national park in Nigeria, and one of the least visited.

## A Park of Two Halves

The park's name comes from two old settlements that anchor its geography. Gumti, in Toungo local government area of Adamawa State, lends its name to the northern sector: relatively flat terrain draped in savanna woodlands and grasslands where elephants, lions, and hartebeest move through the dry season. The Gashaka sector to the south is a different world entirely. Here the land buckles upward into mountains cloaked in rainforest, with montane grasslands crowning the highest ridges. Gangirwal Mountain, the highest point in Nigeria, rises along the Cameroonian border at the park's southern edge. The contrast between sectors is so stark that temperatures can vary from a sweltering 32.5 degrees Celsius in the lowlands to a cool 21 degrees on the highland plateaus. Between April and October, the wet season turns streams into torrents, but even in the markedly dry months from December to March, river flow remains abundant -- the park is a critical water catchment for the Benue River.

## Where Chimpanzees Still Climb

With 103 documented mammal species, Gashaka Gumti is one of West Africa's richest wildlife reserves. The roster reads like a catalog of the continent's most charismatic animals: elephants, leopards, golden cats, gorillas, hyenas, waterbucks, and multiple species of antelope. But the park's signature inhabitants are its primates. In 1999, researchers from University College London launched the Gashaka Primate Project, focused on the chimpanzees and monkeys inhabiting the Kwano forest -- a lush rainforest just a three-hour trek from Gashaka village. The project combined biological research with practical conservation: boundary demarcation, anti-poaching patrols, community outreach, and ecotourism development. Kwano remains one of the best places in Nigeria to observe wild chimpanzees in their forest canopy, alongside colobus monkeys, baboons, and other primates that move through the dense understory.

## Colonial Ghosts and Fulani Herders

The park carries layers of human history beyond the German fort. Enclaves for local Fulani pastoralists exist within its boundaries, a pragmatic arrangement that allows farming and grazing to continue in a designated protected area. These communities have grazed cattle across these grasslands for generations, and their continued presence is woven into the park's management rather than excluded from it. The British left their own mark too -- an army post near Gashaka village, built by the Frontier Force after they seized the Mambilla Pass from the Germans. Together with the German fort and its scattered tombs, these relics make Gashaka Gumti something unusual: a national park where the story of European colonial rivalry in Africa can be read directly in the landscape. The park is contiguous with Faro and Tchabal Mbado National Parks across the border in Cameroon, forming part of a larger transboundary conservation corridor.

## The Long Road In

Getting to Gashaka Gumti requires commitment. The nearest commercial airport is in Yola, and from there a taxi ride to the park takes roughly seven hours along roads that test the suspension of any vehicle. An airstrip for light aircraft exists at Serti, near the main entrance to the Gashaka sector, but scheduled service is unreliable. The Gumti sector entrance lies along the route from Yola through Mayo Belwa, Ganye, and Sugu to Toungo. For those willing to invest the travel time, the rewards are singular. At the Hippopotamus Viewing Pool near Mayo-Kam, hippos bathe in plain sight during the dry season, reachable by a short walk from a wooden bridge. The bat forest near Gashaka village thrums with fruit bats and wild pigs. And for the truly committed, Daneji -- on the Cameroonian border -- offers encounters with elephants, buffalo, and rhinoceros, though reaching it requires a six- to eight-day trek on foot from Toungo.

## Flight Context

Located at 7.35N, 11.52E in northeastern Nigeria, straddling Taraba and Adamawa states along the Cameroon border. From cruising altitude, the contrast between the flat northern Gumti sector and the mountainous southern Gashaka sector is clearly visible. Gangirwal Mountain, Nigeria's highest point, marks the park's southern edge near the Cameroonian border. Nearest airport is Yola (DNYO), approximately 7 hours by road. A light-aircraft airstrip exists at Serti near the Gashaka entrance. The park is contiguous with Cameroon's Faro and Tchabal Mbado National Parks, forming a large transboundary green corridor visible from altitude.
