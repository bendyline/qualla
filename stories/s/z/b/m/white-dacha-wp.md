# White Dacha

The white, asymmetrical house Anton Chekhov built above Yalta in the last years of his life, where he planted a garden, kept tame cranes, and wrote some of his greatest work.

**Location:** 44.4911, 34.1416

**Categories:** literary, museum, history, Crimea

## Introduction

Anton Chekhov did not come to Yalta to be a country gentleman. He came because his lungs were failing. Tuberculosis had turned the mild Crimean coast into a prescription, and so in 1898, flush with the success of a play that had nearly broken him, he built a white house on a hillside above the sea and set about the strange work of dying slowly while writing brilliantly. The house he raised there, all pale walls and unexpected angles, is now a museum, but it began as one man's attempt to make a home out of borrowed time.

## A House Built on a Second Chance

The White Dacha rose in 1898, funded in part by Chekhov's success with The Seagull, the play whose disastrous premiere he had survived before its triumphant revival restored his faith in the theater. He moved in after his father's death, seeking both a fresh start and the gentler climate his illness demanded. The architect L.N. Shapovalov gave him something genuinely original. The writer Aleksandr Kuprin called it perhaps the most original building in Yalta: all white, pure and light, beautifully asymmetrical, with a tower and unexpected ledges, a glass veranda below and an open terrace above, and windows scattered broad and narrow across its face. It was a house that refused symmetry the way its owner refused sentimentality, and it suited him.

## The Garden of a Dying Man

Chekhov threw himself into the garden with the energy of someone who knew his time was short. He planted trees of every kind he could get to grow: mulberry, cherry, almond, peach, cypress, citrus, acacia, birch. He put in roses with names like poetry, Gloire de Dijon and Souvenir de la Malmaison and La France, and a Rambler climbing crimson through the others. He kept dogs. He kept tame cranes, which stalked the grounds like odd, elegant guests. For a man famous for the melancholy in his stories, the garden was a stubborn act of hope, each sapling a small bet that there would be seasons enough to watch it grow. It was here, in this house and among these trees, that he wrote some of his finest work, including the story The Lady with the Dog, set in the very town spread out below him.

## A Table Where the Century Sat Down

Chekhov was a generous host, and the White Dacha drew the giants of Russian culture up its hill. Leo Tolstoy came. So did the great bass Feodor Chaliapin, and the composer Sergei Rachmaninoff, and the writer Maxim Gorky, who was becoming a friend and, in time, a champion. To sit on that terrace in those years was to sit at one of the crossroads of Russian art. Chekhov died in 1904, only a few years after the house was finished, but the dacha kept its shape and its spirit, and eventually became a house museum devoted to him. The rooms, the veranda, the terrace where the century's great artists once gathered, all survive to be walked through. Visitors still come, including heads of state; in 2003 the presidents of Ukraine and Russia toured it together, and campaigns have worked to keep the fragile white house from slipping into disrepair. Yet the deepest reason to climb this hill is simpler: to stand in the rooms of a man who built beauty against the clock, and mostly won.

## Flight Context

The White Dacha sits at 44.49°N, 34.14°E on the western edge of Yalta, on a hillside above the Black Sea in the Autka district. From a lower approach of 2,000 to 4,000 feet, use Yalta's crescent harbor and waterfront as your reference, with the house among the greenery on the slopes just west of the center. Nearest major airport is Simferopol International (UKFF), about 45 minutes inland.
