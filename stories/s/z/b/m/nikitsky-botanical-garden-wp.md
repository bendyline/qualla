# Nikitsky Botanical Garden

One of Europe's oldest botanical gardens, planted in 1812 on a Black Sea slope, where a Swiss-descended botanist gathered the world's plants and generations kept them alive.

**Location:** 44.5094, 34.2328

**Categories:** botanical-garden, nature, science, Crimea

## Introduction

A young botanist arrived on this Crimean hillside in 1812 with an ambitious and slightly improbable plan: to grow the entire temperate and subtropical world on a single sunlit slope above the Black Sea. His name was Christian von Steven, Russian by profession and Swiss by descent, and the garden he began that year still climbs the terraces below the settlement of Nikita, near Yalta. More than two centuries later, it holds tens of thousands of species, some of them descended from the first cuttings he coaxed into this warm, salt-scented air.

## A Collector's Terraces

Von Steven founded the garden and served as its first director, but its great expansion came under his successor, Nicolai Anders von Hartwiss, a Livonian who directed it from 1827 to 1860. Under his hand the collections swelled with imported trees, ornamentals, and fruit varieties gathered from across the world and tested against the Crimean climate. The setting rewards the effort. Sheltered by mountains behind and warmed by the sea below, this narrow coast enjoys a mild, humid subtropical climate where oleanders, cypresses, palms, and roses flourish side by side. Walking the terraces today, you move through microclimates the way you might move through rooms: a grove of cacti and succulents in one turn, banks of white roses and lilies in another, oleander shrubs spilling over stone walls that have held their line for generations.

## A Laboratory Disguised as a Park

For all its beauty, Nikitsky was never merely ornamental. Spread across roughly eleven square kilometers, it functions as a working scientific institution: a research center, a producer of saplings and seeds, and a guardian of one of the region's great living gene banks. Its collection counts more than 50,000 species, cultivated forms, and hybrids, assembled through the patient labor of studying wild flora and introducing new agricultural plants suited to the south. Its scientific work reaches beyond ornament into the practical business of feeding a region, studying wild flora, gathering seeds into a living gene bank, and breeding new agricultural plants suited to the warm south of Ukraine, Russia, and beyond. For much of its modern life the garden belonged to the Ukrainian Academy of Agrarian Sciences, with subsidiaries elsewhere in Crimea and in Kherson Oblast. After the 2014 annexation of Crimea, Russian authorities subordinated the garden first to Crimea's Ministry of Education and then, from 2015, to the Russian Academy of Sciences, a jurisdictional shift Ukraine does not recognize. Every rose bed and fruit trial here doubles as an experiment, quietly answering a question the founders first asked: what will grow, and thrive, on this particular slope of earth?

## Two Centuries and Still Reaching

A garden this old accumulates its own history alongside its plants. A monument to Christian von Steven stands among the greenery, a reminder of the man who staked out these terraces when Napoleon was still marching across Europe. The institution has weathered empires and upheavals, changing hands and administrations while its trees simply kept growing. It remains an active center of research and outreach, and in 2022 it even opened a representative office abroad, in Damascus, extending its expertise to another corner of the subtropical world. That reach outward is fitting for a place built, from the very beginning, by gathering the world in one spot and coaxing it to grow. For a visitor, though, the appeal is simpler and older: to walk beneath trees a founder planted, and to see two hundred springs of accumulated bloom held in a single hillside.

## Flight Context

The garden lies at 44.51°N, 34.23°E on the coastal slope just east of Yalta, above the Black Sea. From 3,000 to 5,000 feet, look for the green terraced hillside set against the darker Crimean Mountains, with Yalta's harbor a short way west along the coast. Nearest major airport is Simferopol International (UKFF), roughly 45 minutes inland; coastal visibility is usually good in the warm months.
