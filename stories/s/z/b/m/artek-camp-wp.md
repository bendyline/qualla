# Artek

A seaside children's camp beneath Bear Mountain that grew from eighty children in tents into the most coveted summer of a Soviet childhood, its long history shadowed by the politics that swirled around it.

**Location:** 44.5497, 34.2997

**Categories:** History, Culture, Crimea, Black Sea coast

## Introduction

In the summer of 1925, eighty children pitched tents on a stretch of Black Sea shore beneath the great humped silhouette of Ayu-Dag, Bear Mountain. That modest camp near the town of Gurzuf on the Crimean coast would become Artek, the most famous children's center in the Soviet world and, for millions of Soviet children, the dream of a lifetime. To be sent here was a badge of honor, a reward that a child might spend years hoping to earn.

## The Dream by the Sea

For decades, a summer at Artek was one of the most coveted prizes a Soviet childhood could hold. The camp founded in 1925 as the V. I. Lenin All-Union Pioneer Camp grew with astonishing speed. At its height it welcomed 27,000 children a year, and between 1925 and 1969 it hosted some 300,000, including more than 13,000 from seventy foreign countries. By 1969 it sprawled across 3.2 square kilometers, a small city of 150 buildings with three medical clinics, a school, three swimming pools, its own film studio, and a stadium seating seven thousand. Unlike most Pioneer camps, the mild coastal climate kept Artek open all year. It was divided into ten smaller camps, each with its own name, and the seaside Pribrezhny complex, built in the early 1960s by architects led by Anatoly Polyansky, was admired enough to win the USSR State Prize for architecture in 1967.

## A Stone Facing the Wrong Way

Even a children's paradise sat on ground that held older, harder stories. In 1952 the Turkish poet Nâzım Hikmet visited the camp and was horrified to find himself standing on a broken gravestone, a verse of the Quran still legible where it faced up toward the sky. Years earlier, after the forced deportation of the Crimean Tatars from their homeland, Muslim cemeteries across the peninsula had been dismantled and their headstones reused as ordinary building material. Only after Hikmet's protest was that particular stone turned over. It is a quiet, devastating detail: a place built for the joy of children resting, in part, on the erasure of an entire people, and the many visitors who walked past the stone without noticing it. The Crimean Tatars who had lived and prayed on this coast deserve to be remembered here.

## Camp on the Screen

Artek was photogenic, and Soviet cinema could not resist it. In 1965 the camp opened its own film studio, Artekfilm, and over the years dozens of films used its sunlit shore. Some told stories set in the camp itself, from a 1928 children's film written by the poet Vladimir Mayakovsky to the 1935 stop-motion fantasy The New Gulliver, in which an exemplary young Pioneer dreams his way into Jonathan Swift's world. Other productions simply borrowed its scenery, standing in for tropical islands and adventure lands, including the 1987 Soviet version of Agatha Christie's And Then There Were None. Beyond the movies, the camp became a stage for gestures of goodwill; among its most famous foreign guests was the young American Samantha Smith, whose visit in the 1980s made her a symbol of hope for peace between two wary superpowers.

## Children in the Crossfire

In recent years the camp's story has grown painful, and honesty requires facing it. After 2014 the operation split: the staff relocated to Bukovel in the Carpathian Mountains to run an "Artek-Carpathians" center, while the original Crimean site stayed open under new administration and vast reconstruction budgets. Its annual numbers climbed again into the tens of thousands. But during the war that followed the Russian invasion of Ukraine, Artek became the subject of grave international concern. Ukrainian children from occupied regions were reportedly held here, some kept from parents who could not safely cross the front line to retrieve them, and taught a version of the war at odds with their own families' reality. The United States sanctioned the camp and its director over the forced transfer of children. Behind every statistic is a child far from home, and their well-being is what matters most in the telling.

## Flight Context

Artek stretches along the coast at roughly 44.550°N, 34.300°E, between Gurzuf and the towns to the east, tucked at the western foot of Ayu-Dag. From the air the massive rounded headland of Bear Mountain is the unmistakable landmark, jutting into the Black Sea with the camp's buildings and beaches spread on its western flank. Fly the shore at 2,000 to 3,000 feet for the best view. The nearest major airport is Simferopol International (UKFF), about 55 km to the northwest across the Crimean Mountains.
