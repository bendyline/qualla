# Massandra Winery

A cliffside cellar built for a czar, where century-old bottles have outlasted revolutions, occupations, and every leader who tried to give them away.

**Location:** 44.5169, 34.1867

**Categories:** winery, history, Crimea, food-and-drink

## Introduction

In 1987, a diplomatic courier carried a case of wine into the Kremlin, hand-delivered, intended as a gift from Mikhail Gorbachev to Ronald Reagan. The bottles came from a single cellar on a Black Sea hillside, chosen because they were bottled in the year of Reagan's birth. Reagan never received them; the bottles were carried by hand to the Kremlin, then quietly withdrawn. The wine went back into the dark, joining the endless racks of Massandra, a cliffside winery above the Black Sea where bottles routinely wait longer than empires last.

## The Prince and the Czar

The winery was founded in 1894 by Prince Lev Golitsyn, working under the patronage of Czar Nicholas II. Golitsyn was not a modest man about wine. He built tunnels into the coastal slope above Yalta, seven of them radiating from a central gallery, cool and constant year-round, and he set out to prove that this narrow strip of Crimean coast could produce fortified wines to rival Portugal and Spain. The Russian aristocracy, summering nearby near the czar's own retreats, made Massandra fashionable almost overnight. The setting helped: mountains rising sharply behind, the sea glittering below, and a warm subtropical air that lets grapes ripen to syrup. What Golitsyn built was less a factory than a vault, engineered to hold sweetness in suspension for a century or more.

## Bottles That Outlived the Century

History kept trying to empty these cellars, and mostly failed. In 1922, in the wake of the Russian Revolution, the winery was nationalized. A 1936 law granted state protection to the collection itself, an unusual gesture toward something so perishable. During the Second World War, Nazi occupiers looted the winery and made off with its guestbook, though the deepest reserves survived. When Gorbachev's anti-alcohol campaign of the 1980s ordered vineyards uprooted across the Soviet Union, Massandra was spared, its historic vines judged too valuable to sacrifice. The cellars became a kind of liquid archive, holding vintages reaching back to Golitsyn's own lifetime, protected not because anyone would drink them soon but because they had become irreplaceable.

## Writers, Wanderers, and a Poem on the Wall

The guestbook the Nazis stole would have read like a strange century's roll call. Anton Chekhov came, living just down the coast in Yalta. Maxim Gorky came too, and admired the place enough to write a tribute that was cast into a metal plaque and fixed to the winery wall. Later visitors arrived from further afield: Ho Chi Minh and Josip Broz Tito among them, drawn to a cellar that had become part working estate, part pilgrimage. The winery has twice been the source of wine meant as a gift for a president of the United States, and twice the gesture went astray. Reagan's case never reached him in 1987. Seven years later, in 1994, a bottle from the year of Bill Clinton's birth was entrusted to an American businessman to deliver, and nothing more was ever heard of it. In 2015, Vladimir Putin toured the tunnels alongside former Italian prime minister Silvio Berlusconi, a visit that made news well beyond the wine world. Ownership of the winery was transferred to Russia following the 2014 annexation of Crimea, a move Ukraine regards as illegal, and today most of Massandra's output flows to Russian markets. The bottles, indifferent to all of it, keep aging in the dark.

## Flight Context

Massandra sits at 44.52°N, 34.19°E on the wooded slope just northeast of Yalta, above the Black Sea coast. From roughly 3,000 to 5,000 feet the terraced vineyards and the sharp rise of the Crimean Mountains behind them make useful landmarks, with the coastline as your guide. The nearest major airport is Simferopol International (UKFF), about 45 minutes inland by road; expect clear coastal air in summer.
