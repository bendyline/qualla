# Lesya Ukrainka Museum

A gingerbread-trimmed seaside house in Yalta where one of Ukraine's greatest writers lived, loved, and grieved, later turned into a museum of her memory.

**Location:** 44.4919, 34.1619

**Categories:** Culture, Literature, Crimea, Museums

## Introduction

In a small newspaper notice in Yalta, a young woman once advertised herself with disarming boldness: "A reader who speaks 6 languages is looking for something to do in the city." The reader was Larysa Kosach, who wrote under the name Lesya Ukrainka and would become one of the towering figures of Ukrainian literature. She lived for two years in her late twenties in a modest, wood-trimmed house near the Yalta seafront, and that building, the Lishchinsky dacha, later became a museum devoted to her memory.

## The House by the Sea

The museum's home is a piece of nineteenth-century Yalta craftsmanship. Built in 1884 and 1885 by the well-known Yalta architect Platon Terebenev for Baron A. G. Jomini, the Lishchinsky dacha was made of native Crimean stone and dressed in extensive wooden gingerbread, the lacy fretwork that gives so many resort houses their storybook air. Jomini later sold it to a merchant named Lishchinsky, whose name it still carries. Ukrainka was drawn to the inexpensive apartment and its convenient closeness to the sea. She took first a ground-floor annex and later moved up to the second floor, and from these rooms she poured out letters to her mother describing, room by room, her temporary home on the temperate Crimean coast. The house still stands as she knew it, its stone walls and carved wooden trim a rare survivor of the Yalta she lived in.

## The Cradle of Her Creativity

For this writer, Crimea was less a vacation than a wellspring. So much of her work was inspired, set, or written on the peninsula that it has been called the cradle of her creativity, the warm ground where her imagination took root. She lived here as far more than a passing guest. She gave lessons to two local students, grew especially close to the family of a pupil named Leoni Razumov, and quietly worked to nourish the town's cultural life, stocking the city reading room with Ukrainian books and once asking her mother to send a copy of Taras Shevchenko's Kobzar to the librarian "because people are asking for it a lot." It was here in Yalta, in 1897, that she met the great love of her life, the revolutionary Serhiy Merzhynsky, when both of them were being treated for the tuberculosis that shadowed her from youth. His death a few years later shattered and transformed her. In a single night of grief, keeping watch by his passing, she wrote the dramatic poem Oderzhyma, "The Possessed," one of the fiercest and most searing works she ever made, and a turning point toward the great dramatic writing of her later career.

## Saxifrage, the Stone-Breaker

The museum itself was born of stubborn devotion, and it took a name that fit. The push to honor Ukrainka in Yalta gathered around the centenary of her birth, drawing together local intellectuals, artists, historians, and admirers from Kyiv, along with one of her former students. After years of effort and official resistance, the Yalta City Council voted in 1993 to create the Lesya Ukrainka Museum. In 2001, on the 130th anniversary of her birth, its signature exhibition took the name Lomykamin, the Ukrainian word for saxifrage, a small alpine flower whose name means "stone-breaker" for its habit of rooting in bare rock. Ukrainka had chosen the word herself, and in her lifetime she was called the "Lomykamin of Ukrainian culture." The collection grew to hold first editions, family heirlooms, photographs, and embroidered rushnyky she stitched when too ill to write. After the events of 2014, the site was renamed the Yalta Historical and Literary Museum, most references to Ukrainka were stripped away, and the fate of many of her original exhibits became uncertain. Yet the stone-breaker's name endures elsewhere, in Ukraine and among her readers, as apt as ever for a writer who made beauty out of hard ground.

## Flight Context

The Lesya Ukrainka Museum stands at 44.492°N, 34.162°E in central Yalta, just uphill from the seafront promenade. From the air, the museum blends into the dense old-town rooftops near the harbor, with the wall of the Crimean Mountains rising close behind the coast. Fly the shore low, around 1,500 to 2,500 feet, using the Yalta waterfront as your reference. The nearest major airport is Simferopol International (UKFF), about 50 km north over the range.
