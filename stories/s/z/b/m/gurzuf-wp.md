# Gurzuf

A steep little resort town under Bear Mountain where an emperor's general built a fortress, a young Pushkin found his poetry, and a great choreographer chose to die.

**Location:** 44.5528, 34.2875

**Categories:** Cities, History, Crimea, Black Sea coast

## Introduction

In 1820, a restless young poet named Alexander Pushkin spent a handful of weeks in a small town on the Crimean coast, and later spoke of the time as among the happiest of his life. The town was Gurzuf, a cluster of houses tumbling down the hillside to the Black Sea beneath the great hump of Ayu-Dag, Bear Mountain. Something about the place has always drawn artists to its shore, and it wears its long history lightly, a resort town layered over Byzantine stone.

## A Fortress at the Empire's Edge

This modest resort began as a strongpoint on the frontier of a great empire. In the sixth century, the Byzantine emperor Justinian I raised a fortress here on the northern coast of the Black Sea, a stronghold the historian Procopius recorded as belonging to the Gorzoubitai, fixing the place in the written record more than a thousand years ago. Centuries later the Genoese, those far-ranging Italian merchants who studded this coast with trading posts, restored the fort and made it the seat of a chief magistrate, calling the place by a shifting handful of names, Garzuni, Grasni, Gorzanium. For a long span afterward it was a Crimean Tatar village, before the steady growth of the resort coast folded it into Greater Yalta. Even the town's name is a puzzle no one has fully solved. Some trace it to the Latin ursus, "bear," after the great mountain looming beside it; others hear older Taurian or Gotho-Alan roots meaning "valley among the mountains," a phrase that wore down slowly across the centuries, passing through forms like Gorzuvyti and Yurzuf before settling into the Gurzuf of today.

## Where Artists Came to Rest

Pushkin was only the first of many creative souls to fall for this coast, and the town became a quiet magnet for artists across generations. The great ballet master Marius Petipa, the man who shaped classical dance for the Imperial Theatres and choreographed The Nutcracker and Swan Lake, came here on his doctors' advice and chose to spend his final years in Gurzuf, dying in the town in 1910 at the age of ninety-two. The painter Konstantin Korovin set up his easel by the water and captured the harbor on canvas. Fountains and sculptures still dot the streets today, among them the graceful "Goddess of Night," small surprises tucked among the houses. Above the shore, on the headland of Cape Suuksu between the town and the looming mass of Ayu-Dag, stands a tower beside a medieval cemetery and a modest monument to Pushkin himself, marking the high ground where poetry and this landscape first met and where the town's long love affair with artists began.

## Many Tongues on One Shore

Gurzuf is a small town that holds a surprisingly large mix of peoples. At the 2001 census it counted 8,676 inhabitants, and that modest number contains a whole braided history. There is a slim majority of ethnic Russians, a large Ukrainian population making up more than a third, and smaller communities of Crimean Tatars, Belarusians, Poles, and Moldovans, all sharing one narrow strip of coast beneath the mountains. Russian serves as the common tongue between them, the interethnic language of the market and the street, though a significant minority speaks Ukrainian first. The town remembers its own children, too. Among them is the Crimean Tatar novelist Cengiz Dağcı, who lived from 1919 to 2011 and wrote with aching tenderness of a Crimean homeland from which he spent most of his life exiled, and Mansur Mazinov, celebrated as the first Crimean Tatar pilot. The politician Natalia Popovych was also born here. In their names, and in the several languages still heard along its lanes and its harbor, Gurzuf keeps faith with the many peoples who have, at one time or another, called this shore home.

## Flight Context

Gurzuf sits on the coast at roughly 44.553°N, 34.288°E, immediately west of the massive headland of Ayu-Dag, which is the key landmark from the air. Look for the town spilling down a steep slope to a small harbor, with Bear Mountain rising just to the east and the wall of the Crimean Mountains behind. Fly the shore at 2,000 to 3,000 feet. The nearest major airport is Simferopol International (UKFF), about 55 km to the northwest across the range.
