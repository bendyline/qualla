# Genoese Fortress of Sudak

Italian merchants raised this cliffside stronghold on Crimea's coast to guard a Silk Road port, and its tiered walls and sea-facing towers still crown the rock above Sudak.

**Location:** 44.8416, 34.9579

**Categories:** History, Architecture, Crimea, Fortress

## Introduction

Merchants from Genoa built this fortress, but they built it to protect money, not a homeland. Perched on a coastal mountain above the town of Sudak, its walls climb the rock in two great tiers, the lower ring bristling with towers, the upper huddled around a castle beside the Black Sea. The Italians who raised it in the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries were traders first and soldiers second, and the prize they guarded was a port that had funneled Silk Road goods to the Mediterranean for centuries. Spread across nearly 28 hectares of clifftop, it remains one of the most dramatic medieval strongholds anywhere on the Black Sea.

## The Prize Called Sougdaia

The Genoese were latecomers to a place that had been fortified for a thousand years. The Romans built the first defenses near Sudak in the third and fourth centuries, and the Bosporan Kingdom raised its own works at the port they knew as Sougdaia. That harbor was the reason everyone wanted the spot: it was a terminus of the great overland trade routes, a place where goods carried across Asia met ships bound for the wider Mediterranean world. When Genoese merchants began settling in Sougdaia in the thirteenth century, they were buying into one of the richest commercial nodes on the sea. The fortress they eventually built was the physical expression of that wealth, a statement that this profitable gateway now belonged to Genoa, and would be defended as such.

## Ruin and Return

Holding the port proved harder than taking it. The Genoese threw up stone fortifications around the city center in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, only to see the Mongols invade in the first half of the fourteenth and reduce much of that early work to rubble. But the harbor was too valuable to surrender. After the death of Khan Berdi Beg, the Republic of Genoa retook the town, now called Soldaia, and built a new and grander fortress in place of the ruined one. That is the stronghold whose bones survive today. Its stones record a pattern common all along this coast: a prize seized, lost to a stronger power, and then reclaimed, each cycle written into fresh walls raised over the wreckage of the old.

## Two Tiers Against the Sea

The fortress is built in two levels that follow the slope of the mountain. The lower tier forms the northern half, defended by a wall six to eight meters tall and up to two meters thick, studded with fourteen towers and pierced by the main gate, with a moat guarding the landward approach to the north. The upper tier climbs the southern half toward the sea and holds the fortress's citadel, the Consul's Castle, along with named towers that watched the horizon: the Watch Tower, the Corner Tower, and George's Tower. Within and around the walls lie the traces of the many peoples who held the place over the centuries, from a water cistern to the Padişah Cami Mosque, the Church of the Twelve Apostles, and even the ruined barracks of a later Russian garrison, layered together on the same clifftop.

## The Slow Fade and the Rescue

The fortress met its decline not through siege but through irrelevance. In 1475 the Ottoman Empire took Sudak, and in 1771 the armed forces of the Russian Empire occupied the town. Under Russian rule the stronghold lost its strategic purpose entirely, and in a fate that befell many old fortifications, its Genoese buildings were dismantled so their stone could be carted off for new construction. What survives is therefore a partial ruin, weathered and quarried, yet still commanding. Recognition eventually came: in 2007 the fortress was named a Tentative World Heritage Site, and in 2010 it joined a wider international nomination gathering the Genoese fortresses of the Black and Mediterranean seas. Standing on its towers today, with the sea below and the mountains behind, it is easy to understand why so many powers fought to hold this rock.

## Flight Context

The Genoese Fortress crowns a coastal mountain at 44.84 degrees north, 34.96 degrees east, on the western edge of the town of Sudak on Crimea's southeastern shore. From the air, the tiered stone walls and towers stand out sharply on the seaward cliff, with the Black Sea to the south and the town spreading inland to the east. The nearest major airport is Simferopol International (ICAO UKFF), roughly 90 kilometers to the northwest.
