# Sudak

An ancient Black Sea port whose Genoese fortress still crowns the cliffs, guarding a city that has changed hands, names, and empires for nearly two thousand years.

**Location:** 44.8514, 34.9725

**Categories:** history, coastal city, medieval fortress, crimea

## Introduction

The fortress walls climb a gray limestone crag above the sea, their crenellated towers stepping up the ridge as if the rock itself had been persuaded to grow battlements. This is the best-preserved Genoese fortress on the entire northern shore of the Black Sea, and it is only the most visible layer of a city that has been many cities. The Genoese called their colony here Soldaia. To the Byzantines it was Sougdaia. To medieval travelers on the Silk Road it was Surozh, a name so familiar that a whole sea was sometimes called after it. Today the town is Sudak, and its harbor still draws crowds to the Crimean coast, but the stones on the hill remember older masters.

## A City Older Than Its Records

No one knows exactly when Sudak began. The first written mention comes from a 7th-century geography, the Ravenna Cosmography, but local tradition reaches back further, placing the founding in 212 CE, and archaeological evidence supports a settlement here in Roman times. The city was most likely founded by the Alans, a people of the steppe, and its Greek name Sougdaia seems to carry the meaning pure or holy, drawn from an old Ossetian root. In its early medieval centuries the town sat under a loose Byzantine hand, passing at times into the sphere of the Khazars, whose empire once spread across these coasts. By the mid-11th century Byzantine control had returned, and an inscription from 1059 names an official who governed both distant Cherson and Sougdaia together, a reminder that this small port mattered to emperors far away.

## Between Empires

Sudak's fortune came from its position. Perched where the steppe met the sea, it was a natural gateway for goods moving between the Mediterranean and the interior of Asia, and empires kept reaching for it. Under Tatar rule the city and the eighteen villages around it were run by their own notables, men the Greek sources dignified with the Byzantine title sebastos and the Latin sources called simply the first men. Then came Genoa. As part of a network of Black Sea colonies the Genoese knew as Gazaria, Soldaia became a fortified trading post, and the fortress that survives today rose from that era of merchant power. The city changed rulers again and again over the centuries that followed, each leaving something behind, so that a walk through Sudak is a walk through the residue of a dozen sovereignties.

## What the Stones Remember

History left tender marks here as well as grand ones. Sudak was once an Orthodox bishopric and later a Catholic titular see, a place of monasteries and churches, including one dedicated to Saint Stephen of Surozh. It was also, like so many towns of this coast, a place of loss. During the Second World War German occupying forces murdered the Jewish community of Sudak, and their absence is among the histories the town carries, recorded now in the archives of Yad Vashem so that those lives are not forgotten. Modern Sudak wears its past lightly, a resort where visitors come for the beaches and the sun. But the fortress on the hill, the ruined churches, and the memory of the people who once filled these streets ask the traveler to look past the holiday and see the long, layered life of the place.

## Flight Context

Sudak sits at 44.85°N, 34.97°E on Crimea's southeastern Black Sea coast, 57 km west of Feodosia and 104 km east of the regional capital, Simferopol. From about 5,000 feet the Genoese fortress on its limestone crag and the curve of the resort bay are the clearest landmarks, with Perchem mountain rising just to the west. Nearest airport is Simferopol (UKFF), roughly 55 nautical miles northwest.
