# Scythian Neapolis

On a rocky plateau at the edge of Simferopol lie the ruins of a Scythian royal capital, where nomadic horsemen built a walled city of Greek columns and buried their kings beneath gold.

**Location:** 44.9428, 34.1206

**Categories:** History, Archaeology, Crimea, Ancient

## Introduction

The Scythians are usually met on horseback, glimpsed at the edges of Greek histories as fierce nomads of the open steppe. Yet on a rocky plateau at the edge of modern Simferopol, they built something that contradicts the caricature: a walled capital with Greek columns, painted plaster, and mausoleums fit for kings. This was Scythian Neapolis, also called Kermenchik, and for some five centuries it was the seat of a kingdom. The Greek geographer Strabo knew of it, describing a fortress and palace where Scythian rulers held court. Today its stone foundations lie exposed among apartment blocks, a lost royal city hiding in plain sight.

## Capital of the Late Scythians

Scythian Neapolis rose at the end of the third century BC and endured until the middle of the third century AD, when the Goths destroyed it. For that long span it served as the capital of the Late Scythian Kingdom, a realm that ruled the lands stretching from the lower Dnieper River down into Crimea. Its most storied rulers were the kings Skilurus and his son Palacus, whose names survive on carved reliefs found at the site. Perched on the Petrovsky rocks above the valley, the city commanded a strategic height in a landscape of dry, windswept summers and forests of oak, pine, poplar, and alder. This was no mere camp but a fixed royal center, the political heart of a people the Greeks and Romans regarded as barbarians, and who nonetheless built to last.

## Where Steppe Met Greece

The most striking thing about Neapolis is how thoroughly it mingled two worlds. Behind a great stone defensive wall, pierced by a gate flanked with two towers, stood public buildings shaped by Greek hands and Greek ideas: columns crowned with capitals, painted plaster, statues, and tiled roofs. The city traded widely, importing amphorae of wine from Rhodes, Cnidus, Cos, and Sinope, along with fine tableware and bronze mirrors, even as its people cooked over open fires and played dice with sheep knucklebones, dozens of which turned up in one of the palace halls. Archaeologists have found evidence of at least two distinct populations living side by side. Neapolis was a meeting point where the nomadic culture of the steppe absorbed the craft and luxury of the Hellenistic Mediterranean, producing something neither purely Scythian nor purely Greek.

## The Tombs of Kings

The city's grandest structures were built for its dead. Just outside the walls, near the central gates, stood the mausoleum of King Skilurus, raised around 115 BC. When excavators reached the crypt, they found the king laid out on the floor, his head crowned with a semi-spherical cap sewn with gold plaques, silver and bronze brooches scattered across his chest. Nearby stood an even older mausoleum, built for a Scythian noble named Argotas after a fire ravaged the city in 128 BC, and it was here that the richest concentration of Greek-style reliefs, sculptures, and inscriptions came to light. An inscription found within still sparks debate among scholars over whether its lines honor Skilurus or Argotas. Either way, the tombs make plain that these were rulers who saw themselves as sovereigns of Scythia, and who wished to be remembered as such.

## A City Rediscovered

For centuries the capital lay forgotten, its walls tumbled and buried, until systematic excavation began in 1945 under the archaeologists Schultz and Golovkina. The mausoleum of Skilurus followed in 1946, and the Argotas tomb was uncovered as recently as 1999, each dig peeling back another layer of a civilization known mostly through outsiders' accounts. What has emerged is a fuller, more surprising portrait of the Late Scythians than the old image of roving horsemen allowed. The ruins now form a historical and archaeological preserve on the outskirts of Simferopol, and to walk among the exposed foundations is to stand where a nomadic people once built a permanent throne, traded with the wider ancient world, and buried their kings in gold, only two thousand years before the modern city grew up around them.

## Flight Context

Scythian Neapolis occupies a plateau on the Petrovsky rocks at 44.94 degrees north, 34.12 degrees east, on the southeastern edge of Simferopol. From the air, the site sits on rising ground just above the modern city, best appreciated at low altitude where the exposed stone foundations become visible against the plateau. The nearest airport is Simferopol International (ICAO UKFF), a few kilometers to the northwest.
