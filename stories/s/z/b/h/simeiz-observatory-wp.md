# Simeiz Observatory

A mountaintop observatory on Crimea's Mount Koshka where astronomers found 150 minor planets and mapped some of the sky's most beautifully named clouds.

**Location:** 44.4180, 33.9974

**Categories:** Observatories, Crimea, Astronomy, Science

## Introduction

One hundred and fifty small worlds were found from this hilltop. On Mount Koshka, above the coastal town of Simeiz, astronomers turned their telescopes to the dark Crimean sky and pulled 150 previously unknown minor planets out of the blackness, one faint moving point of light at a time. The observatory that made those discoveries still stands on its mountain, though it long ago handed off the hunt for asteroids to newer facilities. Its work is written into the permanent catalogues of the sky, and its name is attached to some of the loveliest objects a stargazer can seek.

## A Mountain Named for a Cat

The observatory rose on Mount Koshka, whose name means "cat," a rocky height above the resort town of Simeiz on Crimea's southern coast. From below, the ridge really does suggest a crouching animal, and it makes a fitting perch for an institution whose whole purpose was watching. Simeiz operated as a research observatory into the mid-twentieth century and became part of the larger Crimean Astrophysical Observatory. The southern-Crimean location was no accident: clear skies, mountain air, and distance from city lights make this coast well suited to watching the heavens. Over its working decades, Simeiz built a distinguished record in the patient, painstaking discipline of finding minor planets, the small bodies that drift through the solar system between the more familiar worlds. Discovery here meant long nights in the cold, careful photography on glass plates, and the sharp eyes needed to notice when a single faint dot had shifted against the fixed stars from one exposure to the next. It was slow, exacting work, closer to detective labor than to spectacle, and it demanded a temperament as steady as the mountain itself.

## The Planet Hunters

The credit for those 150 discoveries belongs to a roster of dedicated astronomers whose names the Minor Planet Center still records: Grigory Neujmin, Sergey Belyavsky, Vladimir Albitsky, Grigory Shajn, Nikolaj Ivanov, Pelageya Shajn, Praskov'ja Parchomenko, Alexander Deutsch, and Evgenij Skvorcov. They worked in an era before computers automated the search, when finding a new world meant comparing photographic plates by hand and trusting your own perception. Notably, the list includes women among the discoverers, Pelageya Shajn and Praskov'ja Parchomenko, working at the frontier of a science that too rarely credited them by name. Each entry on that roster represents countless hours at the eyepiece and the measuring bench, the unglamorous labor behind every celestial discovery. A minor planet, when first spotted, is nothing more than a smear of light that has moved; only after nights of tracking can its path be pinned down and its existence confirmed. To find one is patience rewarded; to find 150 is a whole institutional culture of patience, sustained across decades by people willing to sit in the dark and look.

## Naming the Clouds of Space

Simeiz gave its name to more than asteroids. Astronomers Grigory Shajn and V. F. Gaze compiled a catalogue of glowing gas clouds, the emission nebulae scattered through our galaxy, published in the observatory's own bulletin and known ever after as the Simeis catalogue. Two of its entries have become favorites of astrophotographers everywhere. Simeis 57, in the constellation Cygnus, is popularly called the Propeller Nebula for its twin-bladed shape. Simeis 147, sprawling across the constellation Auriga, is the Spaghetti Nebula, a vast tangle of glowing filaments, the shredded remains of a star that exploded long ago. Today the observatory has found a new calling, using lasers to track the orbits of satellites. But its legacy is fixed in the sky: a scattering of minor planets and a handful of beautifully named clouds, all first noticed from a cat-shaped mountain above the Black Sea.

## Flight Context

Simeiz Observatory sits on Mount Koshka at roughly 44.418 N, 33.997 E, above the town of Simeiz on Crimea's southern coast. Best viewed from low altitude in clear conditions; the distinctive rocky ridge of Mount Koshka above the coastline and the nearby sea are the visual landmarks, with Ai-Petri (1,234 m) rising to the northeast. Nearest major field is Simferopol International (UKFF), inland to the north.
