# Koreiz

A Crimean seaside settlement of grand palaces, where Stalin kept his favorite dacha and hosted the powers dividing the postwar world.

**Location:** 44.4331, 34.0872

**Categories:** Towns, Crimea, Seaside Resorts, History

## Introduction

The name simply means "villages," plural, as if the town could not decide to be just one place. Koreiz sits on the warm southern coast of Crimea, southwest of Yalta, a settlement whose Greek name, chorea, gestures at the scattered hamlets it once was. Today it is remembered less for its villages than for its palaces, two of them, wildly different, each a monument to the vanished aristocracy that once summered on this shore. And in one of them, in the winter of 1945, the man who reshaped half the world came to rest between meetings that would draw the map of the postwar age.

## Two Palaces on One Shore

Koreiz became famous as the site of two very different palaces, each an argument for a different idea of beauty. The first is Dulber, built between 1895 and 1897 for Grand Duke Peter Nikolaevich, an asymmetrical Moorish Revival fantasy of silver domes, crenellated walls, and more than a hundred rooms, its design borrowed from the medieval architecture of fifteenth-century Cairo. The second is the Yusupov Palace, built in 1909 for Prince Felix Yusupov by an architect who had worked on the imperial Livadia Palace nearby. Where Dulber looked east to Cairo, the Yusupov Palace looked west to the Renaissance and to Rome, and it came with a romantic park of exotic plants and a wine cellar founded in the nineteenth century by Prince Lev Galitzine, a pioneer of Crimean winemaking. Two families, two dreams, standing within a short walk of each other above the same blue sea. That such extravagance clustered in one small town was no coincidence: once one prince built here, others followed, drawn by the mild climate and the fashion for a Crimean summer retreat.

## Stalin's Favorite Dacha

When the revolution came, the palaces changed hands but not their grandeur. The Soviet authorities nationalized the Yusupov Palace after 1917, and in time it became the favorite dacha of Joseph Stalin. In February 1945 that choice put Koreiz at the center of world events. The Yalta Conference, the meeting where Stalin, Franklin Roosevelt, and Winston Churchill negotiated the shape of the world after the Second World War, was held on this coast, and Stalin lodged at the Yusupov Palace throughout. Within these walls, and in the palaces nearby, the Allied leaders debated the fate of nations, the future of Germany, the borders of Europe. The quiet resort of "villages" had become, for a few February days, one of the most consequential addresses on earth.

## Sea, Spa, and Cypress

Beyond its palaces, Koreiz is at heart a place of sun and salt water. In 1958 it absorbed the neighboring spa of Miskhor, folding one more resort into its embrace, and tourism remains the mainstay of its economy today. The coastline here is the classic southern-Crimean blend: pebble beaches, warm subtropical air, dark cypress and pine climbing the slopes toward the mountains, and the great grey wall of Ai-Petri, 1,234 meters high, looming above it all. From the shore, the gondola lift that climbs that mountain is visible tracing its long line up the rock. Visitors come for the sea and stay for the strangeness, wandering from a Cairo of silver domes to a Renaissance villa and its wine cellar, all within a single small town. There is a peculiar pleasure in it, the sense of a place that has quietly outlived the fortunes that made it, where the mansions of vanished princes now open their doors to holidaymakers in swimsuits. Koreiz wears its layered history lightly, a resort that happens to have hosted grand dukes, a prince famous across Russia, and, once, for a few cold February days, the architects of a new world order.

## Flight Context

Koreiz lies at roughly 44.433 N, 34.087 E on Crimea's southern coast, southwest of Yalta. Best viewed from a few thousand feet in clear conditions; the silver domes of Dulber Palace and the coastal parks mark the town, with the face of Ai-Petri (1,234 m) rising sharply to the north and the sea to the south. Nearest major field is Simferopol International (UKFF), inland to the north.
