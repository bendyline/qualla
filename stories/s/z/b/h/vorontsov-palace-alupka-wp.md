# Vorontsov Palace

An English architect's Crimean masterpiece that shows a Scottish castle to the mountains and a Moorish palace to the sea, built by the hands of thousands of serfs.

**Location:** 44.4198, 34.0555

**Categories:** Palaces, Crimea, Architecture, History

## Introduction

Walk around the Vorontsov Palace and it seems to change its mind about what it is. Approach from the land side and you meet a grey Scottish castle, all towers and battlements, crouched against the Crimean Mountains. Circle to the sea and the same building becomes a Moorish palace, its great southern arch inscribed in Arabic, opening toward the Black Sea like a doorway to Cairo. This is one of the oldest and largest palaces in all of Crimea, and its deliberate refusal to be any single thing is precisely the point. An English architect built it for a Russian prince who loved England, and it faces two worlds at once.

## A Castle That Faces Both Ways

The palace was built between 1828 and 1848 for Prince Mikhail Semyonovich Vorontsov, the powerful governor of Russia's southern lands, at the staggering cost of nine million roubles. Vorontsov was a committed anglophile, a friend and admirer of the novelist Sir Walter Scott, and he turned to the English architect Edward Blore, who had worked on Scott's own baronial house at Abbotsford. Blore never visited Crimea, yet he produced a building faithful to no single style and richer for it: Scottish Baronial towers, Gothic Revival detail, and Indo-Saracenic arches all fused into one structure. The stone itself was quarried nearby, chosen so the palace would blend with the grey mountains behind it. The result is a house that reads as a medieval English fortress from one side and an Eastern fantasy from the other, a bridge between the Britain Vorontsov admired and the exotic "Taurica" that so entranced the Romantic poets of his age.

## Built by Unfree Hands

Behind the palace's grandeur lies a harder truth that the marble and the tilework can obscure. To raise it, Vorontsov brought thousands of his serfs from his estates in the Moscow, Vladimir, and Voronezh provinces, hundreds of miles to the north. These were unfree laborers, bound to the prince, and they performed the entire enormous work by hand, aided only by primitive tools, cutting and hauling the local stone, shaping the terraces, carving the ornament. No machines eased their burden. The palace that dazzled the Russian aristocracy, and moved so many wealthy nobles to build their own summer retreats along this coast, stands as a monument to their labor as much as to Blore's design. It is worth remembering, walking its cool galleries, that the beauty was quarried and lifted by people who had no choice in the matter and whose names history did not trouble to record.

## Lions, Poets, and a Rising Revolution

The palace has always drawn artists. Its famous Lion Terrace, guarded by marble Medici lions sculpted in the poses of sleeping, waking, and rising, achieved a strange immortality in 1925, when Sergei Eisenstein filmed the sculptures for his masterpiece Battleship Potemkin. Through the magic of montage, he cut the three lions together so they appear to be a single beast leaping to its feet, an image of revolution awakening. Long before the cameras came, the poet Ivan Bunin visited in 1900 and wrote verses about the long alley leading down to the shore. The Russian poet Alexander Pushkin haunts the story too, for it was Vorontsov's wife, Countess Yelizaveta, whom the exiled Pushkin famously courted in Odessa, addressing poems to her until her husband arranged for his troublesome rival to be sent away.

## Where Churchill Slept

The palace's most famous guest arrived amid the wreckage of a world war. During the German occupation, the palace was spared destruction, handed by Hitler as a prize to a field marshal who made it his headquarters, which is why it survived so intact. Then, in February 1945, it played host to history. When the Allied leaders met on this coast for the Yalta Conference to decide the shape of the postwar world, the Vorontsov Palace served as the residence of Winston Churchill and his British delegation. Churchill is said to have admired the marble lions so much he wanted to take one home. Today the palace is a museum and one of the most visited sights on the southern coast, its library still holding thousands of volumes, though coastal erosion and long neglect leave one wing perilously close to the sea. It endures, as it always has, facing two directions at once: back toward the empires that built it, and out toward the endless Black Sea.

## Flight Context

The Vorontsov Palace stands at roughly 44.420 N, 34.056 E at the foot of the Crimean Mountains near Alupka. Best viewed from low altitude in clear conditions; the palace's grey battlemented silhouette, its terraced gardens and Lion Terrace, and the sheer face of Ai-Petri (1,234 m) rising directly behind make it one of the coast's most recognizable landmarks. Nearest major field is Simferopol International (UKFF), inland to the north.
