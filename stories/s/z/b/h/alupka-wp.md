# Alupka

A small Black Sea resort named for a fox, where a British-designed castle briefly made it more fashionable than Yalta itself.

**Location:** 44.4197, 34.0431

**Categories:** Cities, Crimea, Seaside Resorts, History

## Introduction

The town is named after a fox. Alupka takes its name from the Greek word alopex, and if you stand on its shoreline and watch the dusk climb the slopes of the Crimean Mountains, the choice feels apt: something quick, russet, and quietly clever seems to have slipped away between the cypresses. This is one of the oldest settlements on Crimea's southern coast, a subtropical strip where the Black Sea laps warm against pebble beaches and the great grey mass of Ai-Petri rises straight up behind the rooftops. It is small. It is also, once you learn its story, out of all proportion to its size.

## A Fox, a Byzantine, a Tatar Village

Alupka was born Greek. Colonists gave it the fox's name, and after the Greeks came the Byzantines; the first written mention of the place appears in a document from the year 960, in connection with the Byzantine emperor Romanos II. For long centuries afterward it belonged to the Crimean Tatars, a farming and fishing hamlet clinging to the warm shelf between mountain and sea. When Russia annexed the Crimean peninsula in 1783, the land passed to Prince Grigori Potemkin, and by 1798 a census counted just 211 residents, most of them farmers. Nothing about the place suggested grandeur. It was a scatter of houses, vineyards, and gardens under a mountain, the kind of coastal village that history usually forgets. History was about to do the opposite.

## The Season It Outshone Yalta

In the middle of the nineteenth century, something remarkable happened: for a brief, glittering spell, tiny Alupka was more fashionable than Yalta, seventeen kilometers to the east. The reason was one man's ambition. Count Mikhail Vorontsov, the powerful governor of Russia's new southern lands, chose Alupka for his summer residence and poured a fortune into a palace so extravagant that visitors came simply to gawk. A French traveler had already described the coast admiringly in letters written in 1811, but it was Vorontsov's building that put Alupka on the map of the Russian imagination. By the turn of the twentieth century the town had become a celebrated resort, its subtropical air and 2,150 hours of annual sunshine drawing the leisured and the ailing to a shoreline that stays swimmable from March into October.

## The Ace Who Came Home Here

Alupka's most consequential son was not a prince but a pilot. Amet-khan Sultan, a Crimean Tatar flying ace of the Second World War, was born in this town, and it was to Alupka that he returned in May 1944 on a brief leave to see his parents. He arrived at a terrible moment. That same month the Soviet state deported the entire Crimean Tatar people from their homeland; only his own decorated status and his father's non-Tatar heritage kept his family from the cattle cars. He went on to become one of the Soviet Union's most daring test pilots. The town also gave the world the film producer Alexander Khmelik and the footballer Evgeni Aldonin, but it is the aviator, twice honored as a Hero, whose memory the town most carefully keeps.

## At the Foot of the Sea-Mountain

Everything in Alupka is arranged around the mountain and the sea. Ai-Petri, the 1,234-meter "St. Peter" of the Crimean chain, stands directly above the town, and since 1987 a gondola lift, one of the longest in Europe and built in two stages, has carried visitors up its face for a view that takes in the whole coast and the Black Sea beyond. Below, the streets slope down through gardens toward the water, past the pale bulk of the Vorontsov Palace and the small Saint Michael church. Resort hotels line the shore, and buses climb the corniche road from Yalta. It is still, at heart, a fox of a town: modest in size, unexpectedly rich, easy to underestimate and hard to forget.

## Flight Context

Alupka sits at about 44.420 N, 34.043 E on Crimea's southern coast, 17 km west of Yalta. Best appreciated from a few thousand feet with the sea to the south and the wall of Ai-Petri (1,234 m) rising sharply to the north. The unmistakable Vorontsov Palace and its dark-cypress park mark the town center. Nearest major field is Simferopol International (UKFF) inland to the north.
