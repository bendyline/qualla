# Dulber Palace

A silver-domed fantasy of a palace in Koreiz whose fortress walls, built for beauty, ended up sheltering the Romanovs from revolution.

**Location:** 44.4294, 34.0944

**Categories:** Palaces, Crimea, Architecture, History

## Introduction

Its name means beautiful, and it was built to be nothing else. Dulber, from a Crimean Tatar word rooted in the Persian for "heart-stealing," rises above the seaside settlement of Koreiz as an asymmetrical fantasy of silver domes and crenellated white walls, more than a hundred rooms conjured from the memory of medieval Cairo. A grand duke sketched it himself while traveling the Middle East. Yet the crenellations he added for their romance, the high blank walls, the fortress silhouette, would one day prove startlingly practical, when the family that built this pleasure-palace found themselves hiding inside it for their lives.

## A Palace Drawn From Memory

Dulber was built between 1895 and 1897 for Grand Duke Peter Nikolaevich of Russia, a grandson of Emperor Nicholas I and a relative of the reigning Nicholas II. The architect of record was Nikolay Krasnov, but the vision was the Grand Duke's own. On his travels through the Middle East he had filled sketchbooks with the Mamluk architecture of fifteenth-century Cairo, and he oversaw the construction himself so that those sketches became stone. His wife, Grand Duchess Militza, was no passive partner in the project; drawing on her own study of Persian architecture, she designed much of the interior decoration herself. The result was a Moorish Revival extravaganza unlike anything else on the coast, deliberately asymmetrical, its white walls broken by ornamental patterning and crowned with domes that catch the Crimean light and turn silver against the blue. It is the kind of building that looks less designed than dreamed, an Eastern mirage set down on a European shore. For more than two decades it was simply what it was meant to be: the family's summer residence, a beautiful house above a beautiful sea, its hundred rooms filled with the ordinary life of a wealthy family at leisure.

## When the Walls Mattered

History turned Dulber from an ornament into a shelter. After the revolution of 1917, Grand Duke Peter brought his family here and stayed, and in 1918 they were joined by other members of the imperial family fleeing danger elsewhere on the peninsula. Among them was the Dowager Empress Maria Feodorovna, mother of the last two tsars, relocated from another Crimean palace when a genuine threat to her life emerged, along with her daughter Grand Duchess Xenia. Of all the imperial residences scattered along this coast, Dulber's fortress-like walls offered the best protection against the Bolshevik forces active in the region. The palace built as a daydream of Cairo became, for a time, exactly what its silhouette suggested: a stronghold. It is a strange fate for a house named "beautiful," to be valued at last for being defensible.

## Escape by Sea

The refuge held until rescue came from the water. In 1919 the sheltering Romanovs left Dulber and Russia altogether, evacuated from the Crimean coast aboard the British battleship HMS Marlborough, a departure that carried the Dowager Empress and her relatives out of the country that had been theirs. Many of them would never return. Behind them the palace was nationalized, its private life ended, the family that had built it scattered into exile across Europe. Dulber was not the only imperial dwelling in Koreiz; nearby stood the Yusupov Palace, home of the family famous for the killing of Rasputin, and the two mansions still keep company on the same slope above the sea. But it was Dulber, with its improbable domes and its hard walls, that had done the sheltering when it mattered most. The revolution swept away the world that built these palaces, yet the buildings themselves remained, suddenly emptied of the people whose names they carried, their crenellations now guarding nothing but memory.

## Flight Context

Dulber stands at roughly 44.429 N, 34.094 E in Koreiz, on Crimea's southern coast southwest of Yalta. Best viewed from low altitude in clear conditions; its silver domes and white crenellated walls make it unusually easy to identify from the air against the dark coastal parks. The wall of Ai-Petri (1,234 m) rises to the north. Nearest major field is Simferopol International (UKFF), inland to the north.
