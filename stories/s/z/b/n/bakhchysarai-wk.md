# Bakhchysarai

The old capital of the Crimean Tatar Khanate, a valley town of palaces and cliff cities that lost its people to deportation in 1944 and has been reclaiming its heritage ever since.

**Location:** 44.7528, 33.8608

**Categories:** city, history, culture, Crimea

## Introduction

In front of the Khan's Palace, vendors sell samsa and honey-soaked baklava, and the smell of grilling shashlyk drifts from cafes named for Pushkin. It is an ordinary market scene with an extraordinary weight beneath it, because for nearly half a century almost none of the people who made this culture were here to sell it. Bakhchysarai was once the capital of the Crimean Tatar Khanate, and it is now a town in the slow, determined act of becoming itself again, since the Tatars were allowed to return from exile in the early 1990s.

## Capital of the Khans

The town's history is inseparable from the Crimean Khanate, a state that emerged from the Golden Horde, one of the fragments left when Genghis Khan's vast empire splintered. The Khanate won its independence in the 15th century, its first capital at Staryi Krym and its first ruler a descendant of Genghis Khan himself. Soon the capital shifted to Bakhchysarai, and the town grew into the political and cultural heart of the Crimean Tatars. In time it became an Ottoman protectorate, rising and falling with that empire's fortunes. The declining Ottomans lost Crimea in 1774, and Russia annexed the peninsula in 1783, after which the town lost its administrative importance but kept its palaces, its mosques, and its deep Tatar identity, waiting in the valley for the world to turn again.

## The Year the Town Was Emptied

In 1944, Bakhchysarai lost its Crimean Tatar population entirely. In an operation the Tatars remember as the Sürgün, Stalin's regime carried out the brutal mass deportation of the Crimean Tatar people to Uzbekistan, uprooting families from a homeland they had inhabited for centuries. The town's cultural life did not simply pause; it was severed. For decades the palace, the mosques, and the surrounding streets stood without the community that had given them meaning. Only in the 1990s were the Crimean Tatars permitted to return, and they came back to a place at once familiar and estranged, its monuments neglected, its memory interrupted. The rebirth since has been real but unhurried, a matter of restoring buildings and, harder still, of reweaving a way of life that was nearly erased.

## A Living Culture in a Mountain Valley

Today Bakhchysarai is by no means overrun with tourists, and that is part of its charm. Most visitors come from Russia, Ukraine, or Turkey, and English is rarely spoken. The rewards are tangible and specific. Near the palace, a workshop called Usta, Crimean Tatar for "master," gathers artisans whose silverwork draws television crews. The cafes serve the full range of Tatar and Central Asian cooking, plov and manty and lagman, cheburek and Tatar baklava. Beyond the town, the landscape opens into wonders. A short ride out lies Kachi-Kalyon, a Greek cliff city honeycombed with cave churches, and the nearby hiking country of Tash-Air. For the ambitious, a spectacular two- or three-day trek climbs from the valley through orchards, reservoirs, and mountain villages where old people tend their cows and a shop may hide, unmarked, inside a private house, before descending toward the Grand Canyon of Crimea. Buy a topographic map from a street vendor first, because you will not find one outside the peninsula. To visit is to walk a valley where a culture, deported and returned, is patiently reclaiming its own ground.

## Flight Context

Bakhchysarai lies at 44.75°N, 33.86°E in a river valley among the Crimean foothills, roughly 166 meters in elevation. From 2,500 to 5,000 feet, follow the valley to spot the Khan's Palace and its minarets, with the dramatic cliff plateau of Chufut-Kale and the cave monastery on the surrounding ridges. Nearest major airport is Simferopol International (UKFF), about 40 minutes east; Sevastopol lies southwest along the rail line.
