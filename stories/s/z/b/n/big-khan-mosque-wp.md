# Big Khan Mosque

One of Crimea's largest mosques and among the first buildings of the Khan's Palace, its twin octagonal minarets rising twenty-eight meters over Bakhchysarai since 1532.

**Location:** 44.7487, 33.8820

**Categories:** mosque, architecture, history, Crimea

## Introduction

Two slender octagonal towers, each twenty-eight meters tall and capped with a conical crown, rise above the rooftops of the Khan's Palace and mark the spot from across the valley. They belong to the Big Khan Mosque, and they have been pointing skyward over Bakhchysarai since 1532, when a Crimean Khan raised this prayer hall as one of the very first buildings of his new palace. Nearly five centuries on, the minarets still frame the same square, a fixed point in a town that has seen empires arrive and depart around them.

## Built by a Khan Who Meant to Stay

The mosque was commissioned in 1532 by Sahib I Giray, and for a time in the 17th century it bore his name. Its placement was deliberate: it stands on the Palace Square, just east of the northern gate, among the earliest structures of the Khan's Palace, a declaration that this valley would be a permanent seat of Crimean Tatar power rather than a passing camp. It grew into one of the largest mosques in all of Crimea. To build it, the Khans drew on the same currents of Ottoman and Islamic architecture that shaped the wider palace, producing a place of worship monumental enough to serve a capital and dignified enough to represent a dynasty. The minarets were both spiritual signal and civic landmark, calling the faithful to prayer while announcing the court to everyone within sight of the town.

## Inside the Prayer Hall

The mosque is a study in balanced geometry. Its core is a three-aisle square prayer hall covered by a hipped roof, fronted by a narthex and flanked by porticos facing east and west. Through those porticos rise the two symmetrical minarets, octagonal in section, finished with conical caps and finials. A domed ablution kiosk, square in plan, is tucked against the northeastern corner, where worshippers performed the ritual washing before prayer. You enter through a portal on the north side into a space wrapped on three of its four walls by a balcony, part of it screened off as the Khan's private lodge, so that the ruler could pray apart yet among his people. Scholars believe the hall was originally roofed not with the present hipped form but with a cluster of domes of varying sizes, a grander silhouette lost to time.

## Fire, Restoration, and Survival

A building that has stood for five hundred years accumulates its share of wounds. In 1736, the mosque was badly damaged by fire, during the Russo-Turkish War of 1735 to 1739, when conflict swept through the Crimean capital. It was restored afterward during the reign of Khan Selâmet II Giray, who ruled from 1740 to 1743, and the structure that survives still resembles the original despite the repairs. Around 1750, a madrasah, an Islamic school built by Khan Arslan Giray, is thought to have adjoined the eastern wall, extending the mosque's role from prayer into learning and binding worship to scholarship in the heart of the Tatar capital. What remains today is not a museum piece frozen in one moment but a layered survivor. The hipped roof that now covers the hall may not match the cluster of domes that once crowned it, and the beams and tiles have been mended more than once, yet the mosque still carries the scorch marks and repairs of centuries in the very stones its founder laid, a working monument to a dynasty that meant to endure.

## Flight Context

The Big Khan Mosque stands at 44.75°N, 33.88°E within the Khan's Palace complex in Bakhchysarai, in a valley among the Crimean foothills. From 2,500 to 5,000 feet, the twin twenty-eight-meter minarets rising from the palace grounds are the clearest marker, with the river valley and the nearby cliff of Chufut-Kale as further references. Nearest major airport is Simferopol International (UKFF), about 40 minutes east by road.
