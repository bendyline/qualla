# Orta Cami Mosque

One of Crimea's oldest mosques fell to ruin and rose again, its minaret rebuilt stone by stone on a Bakhchysarai street that still bears a Soviet name.

**Location:** 44.7498, 33.8772

**Categories:** religion, architecture, crimea, history

## Introduction

The address tells a small story all by itself: one of the oldest mosques in Crimea stands today on Lenina Street. A Friday mosque older than the United States, on a road named for the founder of an atheist state - the layers of history in Bakhchysarai rarely sort themselves neatly. This is Orta Cami, the "middle mosque," tucked into the old town of what was once the capital of the Crimean Khanate. For centuries the faithful gathered here for Friday prayer. Then, for a while, they could not - because there was scarcely a mosque left to gather in.

## The Middle Mosque of the Capital

Orta Cami - also known as Orta Juma Jami - was no minor neighborhood prayer room. It served as the main Friday prayer mosque in the capital of the Crimean Khanate, the congregational heart of a district in a city ruled by the Giray khans. Its earliest known mention comes from 1674, described as a ma'ale mosque, meaning the principal mosque of its quarter, and that date is now generally taken as the year of its completion. In a town whose skyline was once punctuated by minarets, the "middle mosque" held a central place in the daily and weekly rhythm of Muslim life - a fixed point around which a whole neighborhood organized its week.

## Ruin

Time was not kind. By the early twenty-first century, Orta Cami had fallen into bad condition - and worse than merely neglected, it was diminished. Its minaret, the tower from which the call to prayer had once carried over the rooftops of Bakhchysarai, was completely destroyed, along with several of the small buildings that had clustered around it. A mosque without a minaret is a mosque with its voice cut off, standing quietly on its old street while the town went on around it. For a place that had once been among the most important in the city, it was a long, silent decline - the kind that ends, more often than not, in disappearance.

## Stone by Stone

But this story turns. At the end of 2012, reconstruction began, financed by the family of Hajji Enver Umerov - Omer Kirimli. This was no cosmetic touch-up. The lost minaret was rebuilt entirely from scratch, raised again from the ground, and the smaller structures that had once stood beside the mosque were restored as well. By 2013 the work had transformed the ruin back into a living house of worship. To rebuild a minaret from nothing is an act of faith in more than one sense - a wager that the community that needs it will still be there to answer the call once the tower can sound again.

## A Center Restored

Today Orta Cami is, in the words of those who describe it, once again one of the most important places in Bakhchysarai - restored to something close to the role it played more than three centuries ago. Its survival is part of a larger pattern across Crimea, where mosques and shrines closed or ruined in the twentieth century have, one by one, been repaired and reopened. For a building to be lost and then deliberately raised back up says something about the people around it: that the thread of memory was never fully cut, only frayed, and that a community chose to knot it back together. The middle mosque stands in the middle of things again.

## Flight Context

Orta Cami sits in the old town of Bakhchysarai at 44.750 N, 33.877 E, along the valley of the Churuk-Su. From about 3,500-5,000 feet, follow the green river valley between limestone ridges; the mosque lies among the dense old-town streets near the Khan's Palace complex, its rebuilt minaret a slender vertical marker. Simferopol International (UKFF) is roughly 20 nm to the northeast.
