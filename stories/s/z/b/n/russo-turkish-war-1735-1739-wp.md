# Russo-Turkish War (1735–1739)

In the war that first carried a Russian army into Crimea, the deadliest enemy was never the Tatar cavalry - it was thirst, hunger, and the plague that emptied the ranks.

**Location:** 44.7500, 33.8600

**Categories:** history, military, crimea, 18th-century

## Introduction

Of the roughly thirty thousand men Russia lost in its 1736 campaign into Crimea, only about two thousand died in battle. The rest were killed by dysentery, by hunger, by the failure of fresh water and fodder on a burning peninsula that could not feed an invading army. That grim arithmetic - fewer than one death in ten from an enemy's hand - is the truest thing to know about the Russo-Turkish War of 1735 to 1739. It was the war that first brought a Russian army over the Perekop wall and into Crimea, all the way to the khan's capital at Bakhchysarai. And it was a war whose battlefields killed far less efficiently than its camps.

## A Peninsula Behind a Wall

Crimea was built to be hard to enter. The Ottoman Empire and its Crimean vassals held a chain of forts around the northern Black Sea, and the peninsula itself hung behind the Perekop trenches, guarded by the fortress of Or Qapi. On 20 May 1736, Field Marshal Burkhard Christoph von Munnich, a German in Russian service, breached that wall; two days later, the small garrison surrendered. For the first time, a Russian army stood inside Crimea. On 17 June they took Bakhchysarai, and the khan's palace burned - whether by accident or design, the sources cannot say. The war's stated grievance was the Crimean Tatars' long history of raiding the lands to the north and selling captives to the Turks; behind it lay Russia's deeper hunger, older than any single insult: access to the Black Sea.

## The Enemy in the Camp

Munnich won his battles and still could not stay. Dysentery appeared in the ranks on 7 June, and within weeks a third of his army was sick and much of the rest weakened. The Crimean interior offered little to an invader - the Tatars had scattered into the hills, the towns were torched, and there was not enough water or food to sustain tens of thousands of men and horses. So the field marshal turned around. This became the war's bleak rhythm: in 1736, 1737, and 1738, Russian armies broke into Crimea, and each time disease and starvation drove them back out again before winter. Between 1737 and 1739 the plague itself stalked the whole theater, reported from Moldavia to Poland, forcing the abandonment of hard-won forts because troops too weakened to fight could not have held them.

## The Widening War

The conflict spread far beyond the Crimean steppe. In 1737 the Habsburg monarchy joined against the Ottomans, opening a second front in the Balkans, and the fighting rippled from Ochakov at the mouth of the Dnieper to the fortresses of Bosnia. One Ottoman account, later translated into English, records Bosnian Muslim women fighting in the defense of their fortresses, said to have "acquired the courage of heroes" - a reminder that behind the grand maneuvers of field marshals stood ordinary people defending their homes. Munnich pushed on to victory at Stavuchany in 1739 and took the fortress of Khotyn, his advance units reaching as far as Iasi in Moldavia. On the battlefield, Russia had the better of it almost everywhere. The cost, as always, was counted in the sick and the dead behind the lines.

## Much Blood, Little Gained

In the end, everyone wanted out. Austria's campaign collapsed into a costly defeat and the surrender of Belgrade, sealed by the Treaty of Belgrade in September 1739. Russia, wary of a looming war with Sweden and negotiating through French mediation, settled soon after in the Treaty of Nis. For all its battlefield successes and its tens of thousands of dead, Russia kept remarkably little: a demilitarized Azov, control of the Zaporizhia, and no fleet permitted on the Black Sea it had fought to reach. Its claims on Crimea were, for now, abandoned. Yet the war had proved something. Russia had reached Bakhchysarai and could reach it again. The peninsula behind the wall was no longer beyond its grasp - only postponed.

## Flight Context

The war's pivotal moment - the capture and burning of the khan's palace - unfolded at Bakhchysarai, near 44.750 N, 33.860 E, in the Crimean foothills. From about 4,000-6,000 feet, look for the wooded valley of the Churuk-Su between limestone ridges; to the north, the flat land narrows toward the historic Perekop isthmus that guarded the peninsula's only land approach. Simferopol International (UKFF) lies roughly 20 nm northeast.
