# Sudan

Africa's third-largest country, home to more pyramids than Egypt, some of the continent's warmest hospitality, and a present shaped by decades of conflict - approach with respect, patience, and updated travel advisories.

**Location:** 15.0000, 30.0000

**Categories:** travel, country, africa, sudan

## Introduction

The pyramids of Meroë rise in a tight cluster at the edge of the Bayuda Desert, 200 kilometers north of Khartoum. There are more than 200 of them, and almost none of them are famous. Their shape is sharper than the Egyptian pyramids, their style distinctly Nubian, and on most days you can walk among them with no tour groups and no ropes and no voices but the wind. If Sudan's present were kinder, Meroë would be one of the great destinations on the continent. Its present is not kind. Travel here is for the patient, the well-prepared, and the informed - and right now, because of the civil war that began in April 2023, for almost no one at all.

## An Older Map Than Egypt

Civilization along this stretch of the Nile is older than almost any history the traveler already knows. The archaeological record goes back to around 8000 BCE. Kerma, in what is now northern Sudan, was the capital of an early centralized Nubian state. In the eighth century BCE the Kingdom of Kush, based at Napata and later Meroë, conquered Egypt and established the 25th Dynasty - pharaohs born in Sudan, ruling the Nile from delta to cataracts. When Rome tried to subdue Nubia, the warrior queen Amanirenas - known as the One-Eyed Queen after losing an eye in battle - fought them to a negotiated peace. Christianity arrived in the medieval kingdoms of Nobadia, Makuria, and Alodia. Islam followed Arab trade and settlement from the seventh century onward, and the Funj Sultanate ruled much of the region from the sixteenth. For the traveler with archaeological interests, Sudan offers what Egypt offers with orders of magnitude fewer crowds - when travel is possible.

## The Country You Might Visit

The three regions at the heart of Sudan's travel itinerary are all northern. Khartoum sits at the confluence of the Blue and White Niles - the Y-shape that defines the country's map. Omdurman, across the river, is older and more traditional, and the Friday Sufi dhikr ceremonies at the Hamed al-Nil tomb just before sunset are one of the great cultural spectacles in Africa: drumming, trance dancing, whirling robes in green and red, a community welcoming strangers. Meroë is the spectacular Nubian pyramid field about 2.5 hours' drive north, best reached early before the Khartoum traffic builds. On the way, the ruins at Naqa and Musawarat es-Sufra - smaller temples in the desert - require permits and small cash payments but offer almost complete solitude. Further north, the pyramids of Nuri and El Kurru and the sacred mountain at Jebel Barkal mark the old Kushite heartland. Tuti Island, in the middle of the Nile confluence right in central Khartoum, can be walked around in four hours and has a coffee stall under a tree on the west side that is worth the trip alone.

## What to Eat, What to Drink

Sudanese food is unpretentious and restorative. Foul medames - mashed fava beans with oil, cumin, and often a splash of lemon - is the breakfast of the country, eaten from a shared bowl with bread torn by hand. The breads matter: kissra is a thin sorghum flatbread, gurassa is a thicker wheat pancake often served under meat stew, and aseeda is a starchy porridge that is classed locally as bread. Western Sudan has agashe - meat coated with ground peanuts and chilli and grilled over open flame. The camel market north of Omdurman - Souq al-Naga - lets you choose your cut and hand it to a cook to prepare on the spot. For drinks, tea comes black and sweet, and Sudan's hibiscus infusion, karkade, is served hot or chilled and dyes the glass ruby red. Aradeeb (tamarind) and gongleiz (baobab fruit) are other specialties. Alcohol has been illegal since the 1980s; do not push against this. The coffee - Turkish-style, often spiced with cardamom or ginger - is excellent.

## People, and the Realities of Travel

Two things about Sudan can both be true at once. Sudanese people are consistently described by travelers as among the most hospitable on earth - shopkeepers refusing payment, strangers inviting you home for tea, a population whose generosity is embedded in its Arab-African-Muslim traditions of karam. And the country's political situation has, since independence in 1956, produced four decades of civil war, the 2003-ongoing Darfur conflict, the 2011 secession of South Sudan, and the April 2023 outbreak of war between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces that has destroyed much of Khartoum and displaced millions. These two realities coexist. Sudanese people are not their government. But the security situation is real: travel to Darfur and the Sudan-South Sudan border region is extremely inadvisable; Khartoum itself has been a combat zone since 2023. Solo women travelers should dress conservatively and travel with male companions where possible; indecency laws are arbitrarily applied. Photography requires permits, and photographing military installations is dangerous. Alcohol is illegal; possession carries serious penalties.

## Before You Go

Visas are expensive (US$150 at time of writing) and difficult to obtain for some nationalities, especially anyone with an Israeli stamp in their passport. Cairo and Aswan are the most efficient places to obtain one. Registration with the authorities is mandatory within three days of arrival - do not skip this, or you may be prevented from leaving the country. The Sudanese pound has been subject to high inflation and a significant black-market rate; bring US dollars in pristine condition (banks refuse worn notes). ATMs are unreliable for foreign cards outside top hotels in Khartoum. Check the situation carefully before traveling: Khartoum International Airport closed on 15 April 2023 following the outbreak of fighting, and at the time of writing many government services, banks, and tourism infrastructure have been disrupted. Sudan is in crisis. Future travelers to Meroë, to the Sufi dhikrs of Omdurman, to the Nile's confluence, to the generosity of the Sudanese people - will be visiting a country that has endured, and that will endure again. Check current advisories. Go when you can. Go with respect.

## Flight Context

Sudan covers roughly 1.86 million square kilometers in northeast Africa. Khartoum, the capital, sits at the Blue/White Nile confluence (15.5°N, 32.5°E). From cruising altitude the Nile itself is the primary navigation reference - a ribbon of green through the surrounding desert, widening at the confluence and narrowing to the famous cataracts further north. Major airports include Khartoum International (HSSS - closed since April 2023), Port Sudan (HSPN), Nyala (HSNN), El Fasher (HSFS), and Merowe (HSMR). Sudan's airspace has been subject to significant restrictions during the 2023-present civil war. The country's geographic range is striking: the Red Sea coast in the east, the Sahara in the north, and savanna transitioning toward the Sahel in the south and west.
