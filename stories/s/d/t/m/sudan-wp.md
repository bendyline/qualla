# Sudan

Africa's third-largest country by area, site of the world's earliest known war and more pyramids than Egypt, home to 51.8 million people caught today in one of the worst humanitarian crises on earth.

**Location:** 15.0000, 30.0000

**Categories:** history, country, africa, sudan

## Introduction

The word for war in human history may have been spoken first at Jebel Sahaba. Around 13,000 to 15,000 BCE, in what is now northern Sudan, a community of fishers and hunter-gatherers buried their dead in a cemetery where the arrowheads in their bones tell the story of the oldest known war on earth. The land is not done being a place of conflict. At the time of writing, Sudan is in the grip of a civil war that has killed at least 150,000 people, displaced more than 13 million, and pushed eighteen million into severe hunger. Between that first war and this one, thirteen millennia of civilization - Nubia, Kush, Christian kingdoms, the Funj, the Mahdiyya - have come and gone here.

## Before Egypt

The archaeological record in Sudan reaches further back than almost anywhere else on the Nile. Khormusan, Halfan, Sebilian, Qadan - these are names of prehistoric cultures that flourished here between 40,000 and 9,000 BCE. By the fifth millennium BCE, people from the drying Sahara migrated into the Nile Valley bringing agriculture, and the cultural mixing produced the Kingdom of Kerma around 2500 BCE. Kerma grew to rival Egypt itself, absorbing the Saï kingdom in its later phase and becoming a substantial Nile Valley empire before Egyptian pharaohs of the New Kingdom, starting with Thutmose I around 1500 BCE, broke it apart and annexed Nubia. Egyptian rule lasted centuries. Then, as Egyptian power ebbed, the Kingdom of Kush emerged at Napata. In the eighth century BCE, Kushite pharaohs - King Kashta and his successors - invaded Egypt and became the 25th Dynasty, ruling the Nile from delta to cataracts for nearly a century. The Assyrians eventually drove them back. Kush retreated south and built its second capital at Meroë, where more than 200 pyramids still stand today.

## Three Christian Kingdoms, Then Islam

After the decline of Meroitic Kush around 350 CE, three Christian kingdoms formed in its place: Nobadia in the north (capital at Faras), Makuria in the middle (capital at Old Dongola), and Alodia in the south (capital at Soba, now a suburb of Khartoum). They converted to Christianity in the sixth century. When the Arab armies of the Rashidun Caliphate swept across Egypt in the 640s, they tried to continue south into Nubia and were defeated - one of the very few cases of successful resistance to early Islamic expansion. Makuria signed a non-aggression pact with the caliphate that lasted nearly six centuries, with annual exchanges of gifts and slaves. Christian Nubian civilization peaked between the eighth and eleventh centuries, developing the Old Nobiin writing system and a distinctive Afro-Byzantine culture in which royal succession was matrilineal and women held high social status. From the fourteenth century, Arab tribes migrated south into Sudan; in 1504 the Funj founded the Islamic Sultanate of Sennar, which ruled central Sudan for three centuries while the Keira Sultanate ruled Darfur in the west. By the nineteenth century, Arabic had become the dominant language of central riverine Sudan.

## Conquest, Revolt, Reconquest

In 1821 Muhammad Ali of Egypt invaded Sudan and incorporated it into his expanding domains. Turco-Egyptian rule was characterized by heavy taxation, brutal suppression of dissent, and the industrial-scale extraction of enslaved people from the south. European pressure on Egypt to end the slave trade in the 1870s added economic grievance to religious and political resentment. In 1881, Muhammad Ahmad proclaimed himself the Mahdi - the prophesied redeemer of Islam - and launched a revolt that defeated Egyptian armies and ultimately captured Khartoum in January 1885, killing General Charles Gordon and establishing the Mahdist State. The Mahdi died six months later; his successor Abdallahi ibn Muhammad ruled the Mahdiyya until 1898, when Herbert Kitchener's Anglo-Egyptian army crushed the last Mahdist force at Omdurman with Maxim guns. In 1899 Britain and Egypt established the Anglo-Egyptian Condominium, which governed Sudan as a British possession in all but name. Independence came peacefully on 1 January 1956.

## The Long Postcolonial Struggle

The seventy years since independence have been shaped by recurring civil war and military rule. The First Sudanese Civil War, between the Arab-Muslim north and the African-Christian-Animist south, ran from 1955 to 1972. Gaafar Nimeiry's 1969 coup brought military rule; his 1983 imposition of sharia restarted the war. Between 1989 and 2019, Omar al-Bashir's regime ruled through the National Congress Party, supported by the National Islamic Front, and is estimated to have killed 300,000 to 400,000 people through its domestic policies and the Darfur genocide of 2003 onward. South Sudan became an independent country on 9 July 2011 following a referendum. Sudan was designated a state sponsor of terrorism by the United States in 1993 for harboring figures including Osama bin Laden, and American cruise missiles struck the Al-Shifa pharmaceutical factory in Khartoum in 1998. In 2009 the International Criminal Court issued the first warrant for a sitting head of state, charging Bashir with war crimes and crimes against humanity in Darfur; a 2010 warrant added genocide. Bashir continued to travel.

## Revolution and the Third War

The December 2018 protests over bread prices grew into the 2019 Sudanese Revolution. On 11 April 2019, the military overthrew Bashir. On 3 June 2019, security forces massacred more than 100 protesters at the sit-in outside the military headquarters in Khartoum. In August 2019 a transitional civilian-military government took power under Prime Minister Abdalla Hamdok. It did not last. In October 2021 General Abdel Fattah al-Burhan led a coup that undid the transition. In April 2023, a power struggle between Burhan and his deputy Hemedti - head of the Rapid Support Forces, the paramilitary successor to the Janjaweed militia of Darfur - erupted into open war. The battles began in central Khartoum and spread across the country. As of late 2025, more than 150,000 people have been killed, over 13 million displaced, 18 million face severe hunger, and reports of ethnic targeting of Black indigenous communities in Darfur - particularly the Masalit around El Fasher, where at least 60,000 may have died in the late-2025 RSF capture of the city - have triggered formal warnings of genocide. The country's future is, again, to be rebuilt by the Sudanese who remain.

## Flight Context

Sudan covers 1,886,068 square kilometers in northeast Africa, with an 853km Red Sea coastline and land borders with seven countries. From cruising altitude the Nile system is the central navigation reference: the Blue Nile from Ethiopia joins the White Nile at Khartoum (15.5°N, 32.5°E) to form the main Nile flowing north to Egypt. The Deriba Caldera in the Marrah Mountains of Darfur rises to 3,042m. The Red Sea Hills run along the eastern coast. Most of the country is flat plains, with the Nubian Desert in the northeast and the Bayuda Desert in the east. Major airports include Khartoum International (HSSS, closed since April 2023), Port Sudan (HSPN), Nyala (HSNN), El Fasher (HSFS), and Merowe (HSMR). Sudan's airspace has been subject to significant restrictions during the 2023-present civil war.
