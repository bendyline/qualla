# Khartoum

A city founded for war and remade for trade, where every generation rebuilds on the rubble of the last.

**Location:** 15.6031, 32.5265

**Categories:** cities, capitals, Sudan, history, Africa

## Introduction

In 1821, an Egyptian prince named Isma'il Kamil Pasha rode south with his father Muhammad Ali's army and planted a garrison on a narrow tongue of land between two rivers. The Dinka who had lived there called the place something that sounded like khar-tuom, place where rivers meet. The Egyptians kept the name, or most of it. What they founded was a military outpost. What grew from it became one of Africa's defining cities, a capital built four times over, each iteration rising from the wreckage of the one before.

## A City Built for the Slave Trade, Then Against It

Khartoum's first century was shaped by something the British traveler Samuel Baker called, in 1862, "the industry that kept Khartoum going as a bustling town": slavery. Caravans brought captured people from the Nuba Mountains, from the Dinka and Shilluk lands along the White Nile, through Khartoum and onward to Egypt and the Ottoman world. European pressure forced Egypt to close the public slave market in 1854, but the trade continued underground for decades. The flood of 1854 destroyed most of the mud-brick town, and what was rebuilt in stone carried the moral weight of what had come before. Charles Gordon, appointed Governor-General in 1877, tried to suppress the trade entirely. He made enemies doing it, and those enemies would eventually kill him.

## The Siege and the Silence

On March 13, 1884, troops loyal to Muhammad Ahmad al-Mahdi surrounded the city. Gordon had been sent to evacuate Khartoum's Egyptian garrison. Instead, convinced that abandoning the garrison would stain his honor, he dug in. For 317 days Khartoum held out, supplies dwindling, fortifications manned by soldiers who had not been paid in months. A British relief column marched across the desert but arrived on January 28, 1885, two days too late. The city had fallen on January 26. The defenders were killed, the survivors deported to a new Mahdist capital at Omdurman, and Khartoum was left essentially abandoned for thirteen years. When Herbert Kitchener reconquered Sudan in 1898, he had the ruins cleared and rebuilt the street plan in the shape of the Union Jack, a piece of cartographic arrogance still visible from satellites.

## Independence and the Refugees

On January 1, 1956, a new flag went up over Khartoum and Anglo-Egyptian Sudan became the Republic of Sudan. The city grew fast. By 1964 the population had ballooned to 185,000; by 1973, to 334,000. Through the 1970s and 80s, Khartoum became a destination for hundreds of thousands of refugees fleeing Chad, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Uganda, and the Second Sudanese Civil War in the south. Many built lives and assimilated. Others ended up in slums on the city's edges. In November 1991, President Omar al-Bashir's government rounded up roughly 425,000 people, mostly southerners deemed potential rebel sympathizers, and trucked them to five camps an hour into the desert, with burlap sacks for housing and the nearest food four miles' walk away. It was one of the twentieth century's largest forced displacements, and it was executed by a government that would, over the following three decades, do much worse.

## The Massacres on Khartoum's Streets

On June 3, 2019, the Rapid Support Forces, paramilitaries evolved from the Janjaweed militias of Darfur, opened fire on peaceful protesters who had gathered at a sit-in outside the military headquarters demanding civilian rule. More than 100 people were killed. Hundreds were injured. At least 70 women were raped. The bodies were dumped in the Nile. The sit-in had been organized by young Sudanese, doctors and students and teachers, people who thought the revolution that had ousted Bashir two months earlier might deliver a democratic future. The massacre ended that hope. Four years later, the same RSF would turn on the Sudanese Armed Forces itself. From April 2023 to March 2025, Khartoum became one of the most violent urban battlefields on Earth, with the National Museum looted, the airport destroyed, and much of the population driven into exile.

## What Endures

The Sudanese Armed Forces retook Khartoum on March 26, 2025, and found the city they recovered largely uninhabitable. But Khartoum has been here before. The population has returned before. The people who built this city from desert garrison to metropolis of seven million did so through Egyptian colonization, Mahdist siege, British reconstruction, independence, refugee influx, coup after coup. The University of Khartoum, founded as Gordon Memorial College in 1903, still stands. The Great Mosque still draws its Friday crowds. The two Niles still meet at Al-Mogran, indifferent to whose flag flies over the palace on Nile Street. A Sudanese proverb says that the Nile has seen empires rise and fall and knows better than to be surprised. The people of Khartoum, by necessity, have learned to take the same long view.

## Flight Context

Khartoum sits at 15.60°N, 32.53°E at the confluence of the Blue and White Niles. Recommended viewing altitude: 5,000-10,000 feet for the distinctive Y-shape of converging rivers and tripartite metropolis (Khartoum, Omdurman, Bahri). Khartoum International Airport (HSSK, formerly HSSS) is embedded centrally in the city, southeast of the Mogran confluence. Nearby: Wadi Seidna Air Base (HSSW) to the north of Omdurman. Climate is hot desert (BWh) - temperatures routinely exceed 40°C April-June; dust storms (haboobs) common. Visibility can drop sharply. Merowe Airport (HSMN) lies some 350 km north.
