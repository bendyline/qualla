# Kavieng

The capital of New Ireland sits on Balgai Bay, with a history that runs from Dutch mariners to German copra planters to the wartime rubble from which the modern town was rebuilt.

**Location:** -2.5667, 150.8000

**Categories:** Papua New Guinea, New Ireland Province, Provincial capitals, Port cities, World War II history, German colonial history, Pacific Islands

## Introduction

Dutch explorers sketched this coastline in 1616 and moved on. For nearly three hundred years, what is now Kavieng remained a wooded point at the top of a long island, known to the Tolai and Nalik peoples who lived there but charted only in fragments by Europeans passing through. When the German colonial administration finally established a settlement in the early 1900s, the entire town had fewer than eighty residents. A century later, Kavieng is the provincial capital of New Ireland, with 17,248 people as of the 2009 census, a deep-water wharf shipping copra to Asian ports, and a reputation as one of the best-preserved windows into twentieth-century Pacific history.

## A German Town on Balgai Bay

The town that grew at Balgai Bay in the early 1900s was a German creation. Under the colonial administration, copra plantations spread across New Ireland, and some planters made fortunes - but Kavieng itself stayed small, more administrative post than boomtown. The First World War changed that. When Australian forces took over German New Guinea in 1914, Kavieng passed to Australian control as part of the mandate that would eventually become Papua New Guinea. German missionaries were deported. German property was seized. But much of the physical town - the grid of streets, the plantation infrastructure, the colonial clubhouse - outlasted the empire that built it. The Boluminski Highway, running 293 kilometers south down the island to Namatanai, still carries the name of Franz Boluminski, the German administrator who ordered its construction.

## The War Came From the Sky

On 21 January 1942, Japanese aircraft hit Kavieng with a massive aerial bombardment. By the end of the day, most Australians on the island had been evacuated. Three days later, on 24 January, Japanese troops landed and occupied New Ireland. For the next two years, Kavieng became a logistics hub in Japan's defensive chain, a staging point for aircraft moving between Truk and Rabaul. Almost all the Europeans who stayed behind were killed. In March 1944, as Allied forces bombarded the island ahead of what Rear Admiral Ryukichi Tamura believed was an imminent invasion, Tamura ordered the execution of all European prisoners held in Kavieng. At least 23 were killed in the Kavieng Wharf Massacre. The perpetrators were later tried for war crimes. By the time Allied forces retook New Ireland in 1945, the town Tamura had commanded was almost completely destroyed.

## What the Wrecks Remember

Kavieng's wartime wounds are still visible, and the divers who come here every year treat them as something between archaeology and memorial. Plane wrecks and shipwrecks lie in the harbor itself. More are scattered through the reefs and channels between Kavieng and New Hanover. A large Japanese gun still sits above a bunker beside Harbour Road, never officially opened to the public but accessible through openings that have quietly become well-worn. Stand at the gun position and you get the same view that Japanese artillerymen watched from: the harbor, the small offshore islands, the open sea beyond. The bunker is a piece of recent history but feels older than it is, the concrete weathered to match the tropical landscape around it.

## Modern Life

Today Kavieng functions as a typical provincial capital with the infrastructure of small Pacific cities: government offices, shops, hotels including the Kavieng Hotel and the Malagan Beach Resort, a general hospital, a weekly market. The large wharf on the edge of town loads copra and oil directly onto deep-water ships, continuing the plantation economy that German planters began a century ago. Kavieng Airport handles daily flights to Port Moresby. Just offshore, Nango Island was home to a fish cannery in the 1960s and 1970s and now hosts a marine research center for the PNG National Fisheries Authority, opened in July 2009 by Governor-General Paulius Matane. The dive, trek, and surf tourism industries - all dependent on the biological diversity of New Ireland - have entered into partnerships with customary landowners, paying traditional resource owners directly for access to reefs and breaks.

## Climate and Sea

Kavieng has a tropical rainforest climate, classified Af under the Köppen system. Temperatures stay warm day and night, humidity runs high year-round, and rainfall is heavy across every month of the calendar. This is not a place with a clear dry season. What it has instead is constant biological abundance - the nutrients that drive the local fisheries, the coral growth that makes the diving exceptional, and the rain-fed rivers that feed into Balgai Bay and the wider channels. Visible from the town are the islands of the archipelago between New Ireland and New Hanover: Nusa Lik, Nusa Lawa, Nango, and dozens of smaller cays. The surrounding sea is the reason the town exists in the first place, and remains the reason most people find their way here.
