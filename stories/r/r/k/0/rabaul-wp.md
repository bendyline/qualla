# Rabaul

A town built twice on a volcano's rim, capital of three different empires in turn, and buried in ash in 1994 with only nineteen hours of warning and almost no one lost.

**Location:** -4.1981, 152.1681

**Categories:** papua new guinea, world war ii, volcanoes, pacific islands, german colonial history

## Introduction

Nineteen hours. That was the warning the Rabaul Volcanic Observatory gave the city on 18 September 1994 before Tavurvur and Vulcan erupted together the next morning and buried Rabaul in ash thick enough to collapse roofs across the southeastern half of town. The city had rehearsed this. It had drilled for this. And when the time came, the 17,000 residents simply walked out - down the coast road to Kokopo, onto boats, up into the hills - and left Rabaul to the volcanoes. Five people died: four from the immediate disaster, one struck by lightning in the eruptive column. It was the third time a volcano had destroyed Rabaul. It was the first time the city was ready.

## A Town on a Mangrove Swamp

Rabaul was not an accident of settlement but a deliberate choice. In 1910, the German colonial government under Governor Albert Hahl decided that Herbertshoehe, their original administrative seat, was insufficient for what they imagined would be the capital of a Pacific empire. They moved the offices, the district court, the hospital, and the postal facilities north to Simpsonhafen - the great enclosed harbor at the head of New Britain's Gazelle Peninsula. A mangrove swamp was reclaimed and built upon. The Germans named the expanded settlement Rabaul, which was not a German word but a Tolai one, taken from the Kuanua language, meaning mangrove. The town that rose from the drained swamp was meant to be the crown jewel of German New Guinea, and for five years it was. Then World War I came, and Australian troops walked ashore at Bita Paka in September 1914, and the German flag over Simpsonhafen came down forever.

## Between Two Wars

From 1920, Rabaul was the capital of the Australian-mandated Territory of New Guinea, a League of Nations Class C mandate that functioned in practice as a colony. It became one of the more exotic postings in the British imperial system. Margaret Mead came through. Writers and administrators filled the Pacific Islands Monthly with dispatches. Gunantambu, the house of Emma Forsayth - Samoan-American, imperious, remembered as Queen Emma of the South Seas - held a piano that had belonged to Robert Louis Stevenson, a souvenir of her Samoan years. In 1929, local plantation workers staged the Rabaul Strike, the first industrial strike in Papua New Guinea, an early sign that the colonial labor system was pushing against its limits. Then on 29 May 1937, the volcano spoke for the first time in modern memory. An eruption destroyed much of the town and killed over 500 people. The Australian administration moved the capital inland to Lae. Rabaul rebuilt, but it never reclaimed its status, and the volcano had announced itself as the thing the town would always have to reckon with.

## Fortress Rabaul

In January 1942, thousands of Japanese naval landing forces came ashore. The small Australian and local garrison, the Lark Force, was overwhelmed, and in the months that followed the fate of most of its members became one of the great tragedies of the war. Military personnel and remaining civilians were loaded onto the Montevideo Maru, which was sunk by an American submarine off the Philippines on 1 July 1942 with over 1,000 prisoners aboard who did not know they were being taken anywhere. Rabaul itself became the largest Japanese base in the South Pacific, with 110,000 troops at its peak by 1943. The Imperial Japanese Army dug kilometers of tunnels into the ridges as shelter from Allied bombing. Around 2,000 local women from New Britain and nearby islands were forced into sexual slavery in what the Japanese military called comfort stations - a trauma that Tolai communities carried forward for generations and that only began to surface publicly in the 1990s. On 18 April 1943, Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto took off from Rabaul on an inspection tour, and American P-38 Lightnings, acting on decrypted radio intercepts, shot down his aircraft over southern Bougainville. The architect of Pearl Harbor died a few minutes' flight from the base he had helped build.

## Bypassed

The Allies decided not to take Rabaul. Operation Cartwheel in 1943 and 1944 threw a ring of airfields and naval bases around the fortress - New Georgia, the Admiralties, Emirau - and then, rather than mount an amphibious assault on the caldera, simply left the Japanese garrison to wither. The 70,000 men trapped in the Rabaul tunnels were pounded continuously from the air, cut off from resupply, and reduced to growing vegetables in volcanic soil to extend their rations. They never surrendered the base until Japan surrendered the war in August 1945. After the war, Rabaul flourished again as the principal port of the New Guinea Islands region, its population climbing to 17,044 by 1990. Port Moresby had become the capital, but Rabaul was still the trading heart of East and West New Britain, New Ireland, Manus, and Bougainville - a role it kept even after Papua New Guinea's independence from Australia in 1975.

## The Ash

In 1983 and 1984, the volcanoes started to heat up, and Rabaul was evacuated and re-evacuated and then settled back in. Nothing happened. A decade passed. Then on 19 September 1994, Tavurvur and Vulcan erupted simultaneously and sent ash thousands of meters into the air. Eighty percent of the buildings in the southeastern half of Rabaul collapsed under the weight of it on their roofs. The airport was gone, buried. The provincial capital was moved to Kokopo, 20 km down the coast - the same Herbertshoehe the Germans had left behind in 1910. People still live in what remains of Rabaul, and they are slowly rebuilding inside the danger zone, because this is home and has been for longer than the threats have been measured. Tavurvur has continued with intermittent small eruptions - most recently a notable one on 29 August 2014 - while Vulcan has stayed quiet. The observatory on the northern ridge of the caldera watches both of them every hour of every day. Rabaul is still here because somebody always is.

## Flight Context

Rabaul sits at 4.20 degrees south, 152.17 degrees east on the northeastern tip of New Britain's Gazelle Peninsula. Simpson Harbour, the flooded caldera of a large pyroclastic shield volcano, is an ellipse 14 km by 9 km ringed by a volcanic ridge several hundred meters high. Tavurvur's active cinder cone on the eastern rim is the defining landmark - often visible from altitude as a dark scar with a thin ash plume. The airport is Tokua (ICAO: AYTK, code RAB), about 50 km southeast; the original Rabaul Airport was destroyed in 1994 and never rebuilt. Ash plumes can close the airport. Climate is tropical rainforest year-round, averaging 26.9 degrees C and 2,201 mm of rainfall annually.
