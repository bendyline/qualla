# 1936 Bundaberg distillery fire

When lightning struck the Bundaberg Rum distillery in 1936, burning spirit poured into the Burnett River and set the water itself alight.

**Location:** -24.8543, 152.3670

**Categories:** 1936 disasters in Australia, 1930s fires in Oceania, 1936 fires, Bundaberg, Disasters in Queensland, Building and structure fires in Australia, Industrial fires and explosions in Australia, 1930s in Queensland, Explosions in 1936, November 1936 in Australia

## Introduction

It began with a single bolt of lightning. At about a quarter past six on the evening of 21 November 1936, in a gathering storm over Bundaberg, lightning struck the rum distillery on the bank of the Burnett River. Inside the bond store stood 63 vats, each holding on average 10,000 gallons of rum and spirit, more than half a million gallons of alcohol in all. The strike set it alight, and within minutes the place was an inferno. Burning rum did not stay put. It flowed, a river of fire running out of the building and down into the Burnett itself, until the surface of the water was ablaze. The glow could be seen from Childers and Gin Gin, towns far enough away that their residents stepped outside to watch a distant sky turn orange.

## Half a Million Gallons Alight

There is a particular terror to a fire made of liquid. Solid fuel burns where it stands; spilled spirit goes wherever gravity takes it, and it took the burning rum straight toward the river. Explosions punctuated the blaze as vats let go. The heat was so fierce that police had to cordon the area off and hold the crowds back, and a crowd there certainly was, drawn by a spectacle few of them would ever see again. Brisbane's Sunday Mail caught the scale of it in a single breathless headline two days later: half a million gallons of rum, it reported, had gone up in smoke, the explosions violent, the river surface on fire. By midnight the flames were finally brought under control, but by then the distillery building stood gutted, a ruin against the glow.

## A Toll Paid by the River

The remarkable thing about a disaster of this size is who survived it. No one was killed. No one was even seriously hurt, though one fireman managed to sprain an ankle in the chaos, the sole human casualty of a fire that lit up the night for two towns. The cost was counted instead in money and in fish. The damage ran to around £200,000, an enormous sum for the era. And the Burnett River paid dearly. As the burning, alcohol-laden water poured in, it killed marine life along the adjacent reach in staggering numbers; thousands of dead fish washed up on the banks afterward, sharks and cod and salmon and barramundi together, a grim tideline left by the night the river caught fire. The general manager, surveying the wreckage of a works owned by the Millaquin Sugar Company, called it one of the biggest fires in the history of the state.

## Three Years to Rise Again

A distillery is not rebuilt overnight, and this one took three years. Through the late 1930s the site was cleared and reconstructed, and the Bundaberg Distilling Company was back in production by 1939. That persistence is the quiet point of the whole story. Bundaberg Rum had been made on this stretch of the Burnett since the 1880s, a by-product of the surrounding sugar industry turned into something the region built an identity around. The fire of 1936 could have ended it. Instead the company simply rebuilt on the same ground and carried on, and it distills there to this day, on the very site where the vats once burned. Few brands can point to the exact spot where they nearly went up in flames and say, truthfully, that they never moved.

## What the Records Remember

The fire passed into local legend, but it also left a paper trail. The State Library of Queensland holds some of the original correspondence and financial records of the Bundaberg Distilling Co. relating to the 1936 blaze, the ledgers and letters that tallied the loss and planned the recovery. They survive alongside the photographs taken at the time, images of the distillery wholly ablaze, of its blackened ruins the morning after, of the fish lined up dead along the riverbank. Together they preserve a night that Bundaberg never quite stopped talking about: the evening a storm rolled in off the coast, a single strike found the most flammable building in town, and for a few hours the Burnett River ran with fire.

## Flight Context

The Bundaberg Rum distillery stands at roughly 24.85 degrees south, 152.37 degrees east, on the south bank of the Burnett River at Bundaberg in Queensland's Wide Bay-Burnett region, near the river mouth where it meets the Coral Sea. From the air the city sits amid a vast checkerboard of sugarcane, the Burnett winding through it in broad meanders toward the coast; the distillery is an industrial cluster beside the water, still working on its historic site. Nearest airport is Bundaberg (ICAO YBUD) just west of the city; Hervey Bay (YHBA) lies to the south. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 3,000 feet shows the river, the cane country and the port. The towns of Childers and Gin Gin, from which the 1936 fire's glow was visible, lie to the south and southwest respectively, giving a sense of just how far that blaze could be seen across the flat coastal plain.
