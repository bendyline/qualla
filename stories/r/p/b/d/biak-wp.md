# Biak

An island shaped by forced wintering, forced labor, and a massacre still denied - and yet a place where coral gardens, endemic paradise kingfishers, and a dance called Yosim Pancar persist.

**Location:** -0.9450, 135.9361

**Categories:** island, Indonesia, Papua, biodiversity, World War II

## Introduction

In 1526 a Portuguese ship's captain named Jorge de Menezes, carried east by a storm while trying to reach the Spice Islands, wound up wintering on Biak. He stayed long enough that Portuguese charts started calling the island simply Ilha de Dom Jorge - Jorge's Island. Four and a half centuries later, on 6 July 1998, Indonesian soldiers opened fire on a peaceful demonstration in Biak City just before dawn. Many survivors were loaded onto naval vessels and disposed of at sea; in the days that followed, bodies washed up on the island's shores. The authorities blamed a distant tsunami. Between these two dates stretches most of what can be said about Biak - a Pacific stepping stone with a coral coast, a tangle of colonial and postcolonial histories, and a Papuan population whose lives have repeatedly been reshaped by outsiders.

## Geography of a Stepping Stone

Biak covers 2,455 square kilometers - 72 kilometers long, 37 across at the widest - rising to 740 meters in the northwest. The island forms the main body of the Biak Archipelago, sometimes called the Schouten Islands after Dutch navigator Willem Schouten, or Geelvink Islands in older Dutch usage. Supiori sits immediately to the northwest, separated by a shallow channel narrow enough to bridge. The Padaido Islands scatter to the south and southeast, and Numfor rises farther off to the southwest. The whole group encloses the northern mouth of Cenderawasih Bay. Kota Biak - Biak City - is the only real urban center; the rest of the island is villages scattered along the coast, with the interior covered in tropical rainforest.

## Who Lives Here

The Biak people are predominantly Melanesian, speaking predominantly Austronesian languages - a combination common in coastal New Guinea but still strange when you first encounter it. The main religion is Christianity, carried in by Dutch missionaries and shaping the island's churches and school system. Biak and Yapen host the largest local Chinese-descent population in Papua, the product of centuries of trade with the Tidore Sultanate and with Chinese merchants who worked the archipelago's waters. These families intermarried with locals for generations; many took on Biak clan names. A tradition called Mansorandak - stepping on a Chinese porcelain plate after returning from a long voyage - shows how deeply the trade settled into ritual. Many Biak family names carry Tidore roots: rum, the Tidore word for house, survives in surnames like Rumbiak, Rumbewas, and Rumbekwan. Administratively, Biak is divided into 12 kecamatan; the 2020 census counted 122,166 people.

## Wor and Yosim Pancar

Biak Numfor culture organizes itself around ceremonies collectively called Wor. There is Wor Kapapnik, the first-haircut ceremony; Wor Famarmar, marking a child's transition toward adulthood; and Wor Yakyaker Farbakbuk, the wedding. All of them involve singing, dancing, and offerings to ancestral spirits, woven into daily life even as most Biak people are now Christian. The friendship dance called Yosim Pancar spread from Biak across coastal Papua and has itself evolved into something modern - traditional ukulele-and-guitar rhythms beside pop beats, women in woven chest coverings and feathered headdresses beside men in bare-chested moves like Pancar Gas, Gale-Gale, and Jef. The Pancar half of the dance uses tifa drums; the Yosim half has foreign strings. The original purpose was to summon ancestral spirits to join the circle. Now it often just summons a crowd.

## The Battle and the Airfields

In World War II, Biak's flat southern coast offered exactly what Japanese, then American, military planners wanted - ground suitable for long runways. The Japanese built Mokmer, Sorido, and Borokoe airfields here, using Romusha forced labor. In the Battle of Biak from 27 May to 17 August 1944, US forces took the island at the cost of 435 killed and over 2,300 wounded; the Japanese lost an estimated 6,125 soldiers, most fighting to the last in limestone caves above Mokmer. Many islanders received clothing and food from the Americans - a small relief after brutal Japanese occupation. Postwar, Mokmer became Frans Kaisiepo International Airport. The name honors the fourth Governor of Papua (as West Irian), one of several Biak-born political leaders who shaped the island's difficult negotiation with Indonesian rule after 1962.

## Biodiversity and the Spaceport That Wasn't

The rainforest-covered islands are designated the Biak-Numfoor rain forests, holding the largest number of endemic bird species of any single area in the New Guinea region. Green tree pythons and amethystine pythons slip through the branches. The Biak glider - a gliding marsupial recently elevated from subspecies to full species - parachutes between trees at dusk. A palm species called Manjekia maturbongsii was only recently described, endemic to Biak. The island also holds large reserves of copper and nickel, though full-scale mining has not taken hold. Indonesia's National Institute of Aeronautics and Space once planned to build the Biak Spaceport to launch satellites from the island's near-equatorial location - a detail that rarely gets told, and may or may not still happen.

## Flight Context

Biak Island is centered at 0.945 degrees south, 135.936 degrees east, inside Cenderawasih Bay on the northwest side of New Guinea. From altitude, the island appears as an elongated green mass, with the flat southern coast holding the main airfield strip at Frans Kaisiepo Airport (ICAO WABB) and the higher interior rising toward the northwest. Supiori Island lies just north across a narrow channel; Yapen Island stretches to the south across a broader channel. The Padaido Islands dot the waters just south and southeast of Biak. Equatorial climate; expect regular afternoon convective buildup.
