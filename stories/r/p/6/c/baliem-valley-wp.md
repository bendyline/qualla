# Baliem Valley

In 1938 a zoological expedition accidentally discovered a Neolithic civilization of 200,000 farmers hidden in a valley the world had not yet named.

**Location:** -4.0218, 138.8960

**Categories:** Valleys, Highland Papua, Indigenous culture, Historical discovery, Anthropology

## Introduction

On 21 June 1938, a reconnaissance plane flying south from Hollandia photographed what its crew was not supposed to find. The aircraft belonged to Richard Archbold's third zoological expedition to New Guinea, hunting specimens of tree kangaroos and orchids, and the pilots were looking for a route south across the Central Highlands. What they saw instead, beneath them, was a green valley eighty kilometers long and twenty wide, its floor divided into a geometric quilt of fenced gardens and irrigation ditches. Fenced villages stood at regular intervals. Smoke rose from cooking fires. The expedition named it the Grand Valley and flew home. The civilization they had just photographed - two hundred thousand sweet-potato farmers who had lived here for at least nine thousand years - had not been on any map of the Western world.

## The Grand Valley

The Baliem Valley, sometimes spelled Balim, sits at the heart of Highland Papua in the Central Highlands of Western New Guinea. The valley floor lies at about 1,600 to 1,800 meters elevation, held between ridges that climb past 3,000 meters on both sides. Ice-age geology carved the shape of it; the Baliem River carved the rest. More than 200,000 people farm these valleys now, in a region whose agricultural productivity had been understated by every outsider who tried to describe it. Archbold's expedition followed up their aerial discovery by landing seaplanes on Lake Habbema, fifteen miles west, and sending ground parties of Dutch soldiers and Dayak porters recruited from Borneo. The first contact went badly. A scouting party killed a Papuan man without firing a warning shot - the porters did not speak any local language and could not understand what they were being asked.

## A Society Mapped from the Sky

What the expedition observed from the air turned out to be socially intricate. Ten to fifteen villages formed a neighborhood within an hour's walk of each other. Several neighborhoods formed a confederation. Several confederations formed an alliance of four to five thousand people, bound by shared language, ethnicity, and ritual. Between alliances ran feuds - cycles of revenge killing that could continue for generations, even though the alliances involved shared the same culture. The anthropologist Karl Heider and the naturalist-novelist Peter Matthiessen, who both worked in the valley in the early 1960s, would later describe this warfare as deeply integrated into Dani cosmology, a ritual practice rather than a Clausewitzian war aim. Matthiessen's 1962 book Under the Mountain Wall remains the best general-reader account. Robert Gardner's 1963 documentary Dead Birds - now in the U.S. National Film Registry - captured the battle dances, the funerals, and the women at the salt pools.

## Shangri-La, Crashed

On 13 May 1945, a U.S. Army C-47 named the Gremlin Special flew over the valley on a sightseeing flight for airbase staff and crashed into a mountain near Pass Valley, just north of the Baliem. Three survivors - Margaret Hastings, Kenneth Decker, and John McCollom - made it down the mountain alive. There was no landing strip, no road, no way to reach them by ground. Eleven Filipino-American paratroopers jumped in to help. For six weeks the survivors and their rescuers camped in the valley, trading visits with the Dani who lived there, while the Army engineered an extraction by having a glider land and be snatched back into the air by a C-47 tow. The story made Hastings briefly famous. Mitchell Zuckoff's 2011 book Lost in Shangri-La tells the whole account. For the Dani who hosted them, the crash added to their already substantial catalog of sky-people visitors.

## Who Lives Here

When Western anthropologists first worked in the valley in the 1940s and 1960s, they described the population as the Dani. Further work made the picture more complex. The eastern reaches of the valley are home to the Yali, the Mek, and the Nduga. The northern slopes belong to the Walak. The western highlands are Lani territory. In 2002, a traditional council called the Dewan Adat Papua formalized these distinctions: the people of the central Baliem Valley proper should be called Hubula, the northerners Walak, and the westerners Lani - not all Dani. The linguistic situation matches the ethnic one: the Baliem Valley languages are a small family, all related, all distinct. Every cluster of villages has its own variety.

## Tourism, Coffee, Festival

The valley gradually opened to limited tourism after Indonesian administration stabilized. The Baliem Valley Festival, held each August, brings together Dani, Yali, and Lani performers for mock battles, traditional dance, and the pig-roasting ceremony called bakar batu - cooking hot stones with sweet potato, pork, and vegetables wrapped in banana leaves. Treks from Wamena into the remoter valleys take three days to three weeks. Kopi Wamena, arabica coffee grown on the valley's slopes, is now an Indonesian specialty export. From the air, at cruising altitude, the valley still looks the way it must have looked to Archbold's pilots in 1938 - a green corridor walled by mountains, stitched with geometric gardens, a place that took eighty years to reach the rest of the world and that still, in important ways, is keeping its own time.

## Flight Context

Baliem Valley centered near 4.02 S, 138.90 E in Highland Papua, Indonesia. Valley floor 1,600-1,800 m elevation, ringed by peaks exceeding 3,000 m - notably Puncak Trikora (4,760 m) to the east and Puncak Mandala (4,750 m) farther east. Optimal aerial viewing from 15,000-25,000 ft AGL in early morning before cumulus build-up over the ridges. The Baliem River is visible as a brown snake threading the valley floor. Wamena Airport (WMX / WAVV, 1,660 m) is the only fully-serviced runway in the valley. Flights from Jayapura's Sentani Airport (DJJ / WAJJ) take about 40 minutes. All approaches visual, weather-dependent; arrive before 1000 local if possible.
