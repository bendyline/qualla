# New Guinea

The world's second-largest island shelters more plant species than Madagascar, more languages than any other place on Earth, and a rainforest older than anyone can remember.

**Location:** -5.5000, 141.0000

**Categories:** Islands of Oceania, Islands of Indonesia, Islands of Papua New Guinea, Biodiversity hotspots, Melanesia

## Introduction

A Spanish navigator named Yñigo Ortiz de Retez sailed along a coast in 1545 and named it for what it reminded him of: Africa's Guinea, whose peoples he thought looked like these. The name stuck, and for the next four centuries Europeans used it to map a landmass they barely understood. The people living on it had their own names - Papua, from a Malay word, or possibly from the Biak phrase *sup i babwa*, "the land below the sunset." Irian came later, imposed by Jakarta. Underneath the shifting names, the same extraordinary island has held its shape: 786,000 square kilometers, the second largest in the world, cut east-to-west by a mountain spine taller than anything in Oceania and held together by rainforest older than memory.

## A Continent That Was

Geologically, New Guinea is not a separate place from Australia. The two sit on the same tectonic plate, and during the low sea levels of the Pleistocene they formed a single landmass called Sahul - an almost-continent that only fragmented when the Torres Strait flooded about 8,000 years ago. You can still see the kinship in the fauna: marsupials, including wallabies and possums, and the egg-laying echidna, an animal so strange it sometimes seems like a bureaucratic error of evolution. Place names still use the echo of the island's shape - a "Bird's Head" peninsula in the northwest, a "Bird's Tail" in the southeast - compared, the locals say, to the outstretched form of a bird of paradise, the creature so emblematic of these forests that Europeans first thought the birds must be mythical.

## The Spine and the Glaciers

A wall of east-west mountains, the New Guinea Highlands, dominates the island. It runs more than 1,600 kilometers, with many peaks above 4,000 meters. Puncak Jaya, the highest, reaches 4,884 meters and still holds what may be the last equatorial glaciers in Asia - though they have been retreating continuously since at least 1936. Above 4,000 meters, the tree line gives out and you get alpine tundra; below it, montane rainforest that drapes the ridges in moss. Vast southern and northern lowlands spread from the mountains to the sea: the world's largest mangrove forests in places, lowland rainforest elsewhere, the Trans-Fly savanna in the south. Lorentz National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, is the only nature reserve on Earth to contain the full altitudinal range from tidal sea to equatorial ice.

## A Thousand Languages

New Guinea is the most linguistically diverse place in the world. Ethnologue lists roughly 826 languages in Papua New Guinea and 257 in the Indonesian half - more than 1,000 distinct tongues, most of them unrelated to one another. The reason is geography: valleys separated by ridges become linguistic islands, and ridges abound here. The Papuans, descendants of the first humans to arrive on Sahul roughly 50,000-60,000 years ago, speak languages grouped loosely into what linguists call Papuan families - a catch-all category meaning, essentially, "not obviously related to anything." About 3,500 years ago, Austronesian seafarers arrived and established coastal communities; their languages now share the island with the older Papuan tongues. The Indonesian province of West Papua is estimated to still contain around 44 uncontacted tribal groups.

## The Botanical Crown

In 2020, an international team of 99 experts cataloged 13,634 species of vascular plants on New Guinea and its satellite islands - more than Madagascar (11,832), Borneo (11,165), or the Philippines (9,432). It is the most floristically diverse island on Earth. Between 1998 and 2008, conservationists described 1,060 species new to science. Between 2011 and 2017, researchers added 465 more plants. The island has 284 mammal species (69 percent of them endemic), 578 breeding birds (324 endemic), 282 known frog species with the total likely to triple, and around 1,200 fish species in the surrounding reefs. Roughly 600 species of reef-building coral live in New Guinea's waters - about 75 percent of the world's total. Less than 1 percent of Earth's land area holds between 5 and 10 percent of Earth's species.

## Gardens That Predate Pyramids

The Highlands were an independent center of agriculture. Irrigation systems at Kuk Swamp in the Wahgi Valley go back at least 10,000 years - among the oldest continuous agricultural landscapes anywhere. Highland permacultures evolved to cope with constant rainfall of up to 10,000 millimeters a year, hilly terrain, earthquakes, and occasional frost. Complex mulches, crop rotations, and terraced irrigation are still used in ways Western agronomists have not fully understood. A distinctive feature is the cultivation of *Casuarina oligodon*, a native ironwood that fixes nitrogen through root nodules - effectively a home-grown soil fertilizer. Sugarcane was domesticated here around 6000 BCE. The sweet potato arrived much later, probably within the last several hundred years, and it remade the demographic map of the island almost overnight.

## Flight Context

Centered near 5S, 141E, New Guinea extends from 0S to 10S and is crossed by the Central Range (to 4,884m). Major airports: Port Moresby (AYPY/POM) and Mount Hagen (AYMH/HGU) in PNG; Jayapura (WAJJ/DJJ), Timika (WABP/TIM), and Sorong (WASS/SOQ) in Indonesia. Torres Strait separates the island from Queensland, Australia. Trade winds and monsoon variations create complex weather; rainfall exceeds 10,000mm/yr in some highland basins. Overflight at FL350+ reveals the full island in clear conditions; most regional traffic remains below FL250 and routes around the central spine.
