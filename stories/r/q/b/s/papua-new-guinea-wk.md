# Papua New Guinea

A country of over 800 languages, roadless mountains, and the shortest cultural learning curve in human history, occupying the eastern half of the world's largest tropical island.

**Location:** -6.0000, 147.0000

**Categories:** Countries, Melanesia, Pacific islands, Papua New Guinea, Tropical destinations

## Introduction

More than 800 languages are spoken here, which is around a tenth of every language on earth, concentrated in a country smaller than Texas. That number is the first thing most writing on Papua New Guinea reaches for, and it is the right place to start, because almost nothing else about the country makes sense until you account for what it means. The central highlands were not even mapped until the 1930s. They were not effectively administered until the late 1960s. When Australian patrol officers walked into the Wahgi Valley in 1933, they met people who had never heard of Australia and did not know that the outside world existed. Those people's grandchildren now carry smartphones and follow rugby league. The shift from stone tools to silicon took, in some valleys, a single lifetime.

## The Shape of the Ground

Papua New Guinea occupies the eastern half of New Guinea, the world's largest tropical island, together with hundreds of smaller islands scattered across the Bismarck and Solomon Seas. A spine of mountains, the New Guinea Highlands, runs the length of the mainland. Mount Wilhelm rises to 4,509 metres, high enough for frost and, historically, small glaciers. Tropical rainforest covers most of the lowlands. The Sepik and Fly rivers drain vast wetlands that barely show up on most maps. The country sits astride the Pacific Ring of Fire, and its volcanoes are in no mood to retire: Rabaul was largely destroyed by eruption in 1994, Manam Island erupted from 2004 to 2020, and earthquakes are frequent enough that locals barely look up. The ground here is still being built.

## The Language Problem and Its Solutions

When you have 800 languages and need to buy fish from someone whose grandmother would not have understood your grandmother, you invent a third language. Papua New Guinea produced two. Tok Pisin, based on English vocabulary with its own grammar and syntax, spread inland from coastal plantations and eventually became the creole first language of many urban children. Hiri Motu, based on the Motu language of the Port Moresby area, served the southern coast. Tok Pisin sometimes looks like phonetic English, which is a trap. The Tok Pisin sentence yu dring, yu draiv, yu dai works because of a grammar that has more personal pronouns than English does, and because vocabulary has drifted to mean things it never meant in either parent language. Signs across the country often run in both English and Tok Pisin. In conversation, it is polite to try English first. Using Tok Pisin immediately can read as an assumption that the other person doesn't know English, which is often wrong.

## Modern Papua New Guinea

The country took its modern shape through a series of colonial handovers. Germany and Britain divided the eastern half of New Guinea in 1884; Australia captured the German portion in 1914 and administered both halves after the Second World War. Independence came on 16 September 1975. The fifty years since have been harder than the dreams of that day allowed for. Economic stagnation, corruption, and a long secessionist war on Bougainville that ran from 1988 to 1998 have all slowed the growth that independence was supposed to unlock. Bougainville, after a referendum, is scheduled to become independent around 2027. The national parliament has 111 members. The country keeps subdividing provinces into smaller ones, partly in the belief that closer administration will reach people the old system never touched.

## A Country Where Planes Do the Work of Roads

Papua New Guinea has some of the most demanding flying in the world, and some of the oldest. In the 1920s, Lae was the busiest airport on earth by tonnage, because gold miners in the Bulolo valley discovered it was cheaper to fly freight in than to build a road. Amelia Earhart departed Lae on the leg from which she never returned. Even now, most settlements are built around an airstrip, and many of those strips are short, sloped, and ended in rock. The Highlands Highway, running from Lae through Goroka to Mount Hagen, is the country's one long road. Port Moresby, the capital, has almost no road connection to anywhere else. Everywhere else goes by plane or by banana boat, those 30- to 40-foot fibreglass hulls with outboard motors that tie together every archipelago in the country.

## The Collision With Global Culture

Papua New Guinea markets itself as The Last Unknown, which is a phrase that needs careful handling. It is true that some ethnographically documented societies here were, until recently, isolated from the outside world. It is also true that calling any Papua New Guinean a Stone Age person to their face will cost you the conversation and possibly the room. The interesting thing about the country is not the isolation but the dynamism of what happened when it ended. Ritual garb combining bird plumes with rolled-up instant noodle wrappers. Rap in Tok Pisin. Highlands warriors taking names like Rambo. Clan-based politics operating inside a Westminster parliamentary system. The annual Goroka Show and Mount Hagen Show bring dozens of sing-sing groups together in a competition of dance, costume, and music, with the winner invited to perform at hotels and restaurants for the following year.

## Where to Look From the Air

Flying into Papua New Guinea, even at cruising altitude, the country reveals its structure immediately. The central highlands form a long green wall bisecting the mainland. North of it lie the Sepik wetlands. South, the wide floodplain of the Fly. East, the Owen Stanley Range and the Kokoda Track, a 96-kilometre path across the mountains that became famous for the Australian defensive campaign against Japanese advance in 1942. The northern coast shows the cones of Manam, Karkar, and the buried ruins of old Rabaul. The reefs fringing the south coast of New Britain and the islands of Milne Bay province glow a distinct pale turquoise when light catches them right. You can see a lot from the window. Seeing everything would take the rest of your life.

## Flight Context

Papua New Guinea's centroid sits near 6°S, 147°E. The main international gateway is Jacksons International Airport at Port Moresby (AYPY). Lae's Nadzab Airport (AYNZ) is the main domestic hub for highlands and north coast flights. Other significant airports include Madang (AYMD), Mount Hagen (AYMH), Goroka (AYGA), Rabaul/Tokua (AYTK), and Alotau (AYGN). The Highlands Highway between Lae, Goroka, and Mount Hagen is the only extensive paved road; most inter-town travel is by air. Weather over the mountains is often cloudy; dry season (May-September) offers the best visibility. Cruise altitudes of 10,000-14,000 feet show the dramatic ridge-and-valley structure of the highlands.
