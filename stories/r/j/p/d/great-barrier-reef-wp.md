# Great Barrier Reef

The largest living structure on Earth, built grain by grain by tiny coral polyps over thousands of years - and now the front line of a warming ocean.

**Location:** -16.4000, 145.8000

**Categories:** Great Barrier Reef, Reefs of Australia, World Heritage Sites in Australia, Marine ecoregions, Tourist attractions in Queensland

## Introduction

It is the only living thing on the planet that astronauts can pick out from low orbit. Strung along the Queensland coast for roughly 2,300 kilometres, the Great Barrier Reef is not a single reef at all but a vast mosaic - some 3,000 individual reefs and 900 islands sprawling across an area larger than Italy. And every scrap of it was built by an animal smaller than your fingernail. Coral polyps, soft-bodied relatives of the jellyfish, secrete limestone skeletons one upon another, generation after generation, until the accumulated labour of billions becomes a structure visible from space. Nothing else made by living organisms comes close.

## An Architecture of Small Animals

Walk a reef flat at low tide and you are walking on a graveyard that is also a city. The hard coral you see is mostly skeleton - calcium carbonate laid down by colonies of polyps, each a translucent sac of tentacles no bigger than a match head. What gives living coral its colour is not the animal but a guest: microscopic algae called zooxanthellae that live inside the polyps' tissue. The algae photosynthesise, feeding their hosts sugars; the coral, in return, offers shelter and nutrients. It is one of the most consequential partnerships in nature. This quiet exchange built the entire reef. The current living structure began growing only about 9,000 years ago, on the drowned limestone platforms of far older reefs - but the polyps' patient, blind construction has shaped a coastline you can see from the Moon.

## A Riot of Life

The reef is one of the richest ecosystems on Earth, and the numbers strain belief. More than 1,500 species of fish patrol its corridors, from the inch-long clownfish darting into its anemone to coral trout and emperors prized by anglers. Six of the world's seven sea turtle species haul ashore here to nest - green, hawksbill, loggerhead, flatback, leatherback, olive ridley. Dugongs, the gentle 'sea cows' that may have inspired mermaid legends, graze the seagrass meadows. Thirty kinds of whales and dolphins pass through, including the dwarf minke whales that draw divers north each winter. Over 4,000 species of mollusc cling to the coral, among them the giant clam, its mantle electric blue and green. Add 215 bird species, 17 sea snakes, and saltwater crocodiles in the coastal mangroves, and the reef becomes less a place than a teeming metropolis - one whose residents have been refining their relationships for millennia.

## Older Than Memory, Known Far Longer

Europeans were latecomers. The Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have known these waters for thousands of years, weaving the reef into story, ceremony and law; for some seventy clan groups it remains a cultural and spiritual heartland, a living map of country that extends out beneath the sea. The first European to feel the reef did so the hard way. On 11 June 1770, James Cook's HMS Endeavour ground onto a shoal near present-day Cooktown and nearly sank; his crew spent seven weeks patching the hull. The reef earned its name from Matthew Flinders, who charted it in detail in 1802 and grasped, as Cook had so painfully learned, that this was one continuous barrier - a wall of coral guarding the coast and wrecking the unwary. More than 1,600 ships are known to have foundered in these waters.

## The Heat Is the Threat

There is a sentence scientists dread having to write, and they have had to write it five times since 2016. When the sea grows too warm, corals expel the algae living in their tissue - the very partners that feed them and lend them colour. Stripped bare, the coral turns ghostly white. This is bleaching. A bleached coral is not dead, but it is starving, and if the heat does not relent it dies. Mass bleaching struck the reef in 1998 and 2002, then with brutal frequency in 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022 and again in 2024. The 2016 event killed huge stretches of the northern reef outright. The 2024 bleaching, part of a global marine heatwave, left the widest footprint ever recorded here, reaching even the southern reefs that had largely escaped before; on some monitored reefs, the majority of colonies died. The reef has shown a stubborn capacity to recover between blows - the fast-growing Acropora corals can rebound within years - but the blows are landing closer together, and the ocean keeps warming.

## A Living Thing, Not an Obituary

In 2016 an American magazine published an obituary for the Great Barrier Reef. Scientists pushed back hard, and rightly - not because the danger is exaggerated, but because writing off a living system can become a way of abandoning it. The reef is changing, unmistakably and not for the better, yet it is still vast, still wild, still building. Around two million visitors a year come to snorkel its gardens and drift over its walls, and the warm, clear water off Cairns and the Whitsundays still delivers the encounter people travel across the world for: a wall of colour, a turtle banking past, a reef that goes on further than the eye can follow. What happens to it next will be decided less on the reef itself than in the atmosphere above it.

## Flight Context

The Great Barrier Reef Marine Park spans roughly 14 to 24 degrees south latitude along the Queensland coast; this article's reference point sits near 16.4 degrees S, 145.8 degrees E, offshore from Cairns. The reef is best appreciated from low level on a clear, calm day, when sun angle reveals the turquoise reef tops, deep blue channels, and white sand cays against the darker Coral Sea. The primary gateway is Cairns Airport (ICAO YBCS), with Cooktown (YCKN) serving the far northern reaches near Lizard Island. Expect rapidly building cloud and showers in the November-April wet season; haze and afternoon storms are common. Marine park rules govern low-flying tour aircraft and helicopters over popular reef sites.
