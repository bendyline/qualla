# Munga-Thirri–Simpson Desert National Park

Australia's largest national park - a vast field of parallel red dunes that the Wangkangurru people called home for thousands of years and that the wider world long believed no one could cross.

**Location:** -26.2461, 137.8730

**Categories:** National parks of South Australia, 2021 establishments in Australia, Protected areas established in 1967, Far North (South Australia), Deserts of Australia

## Introduction

Munga-Thirri means 'big sandhill country,' and the name is an act of plain honesty. Declared in November 2021, this is the largest national park in Australia - around 3.6 million hectares of parallel red dunes in the state's far north, double the size of Kakadu and four times the size of Yellowstone. It was assembled by joining the older Munga-Thirri–Simpson Desert Conservation Park to the neighbouring Regional Reserve, capping a decade-long campaign to protect one of the planet's finest examples of dunal desert. To the outside world this was long imagined as empty country. It was never empty.

## Country Older Than the Idea of Australia

The dunes that fill this park are part of the Simpson Desert, the largest sand-ridge desert on Earth, built from more than eleven hundred parallel ridges of fine, iron-stained quartz running roughly north to south. The wind aligned them over thousands of years, and some run unbroken for as much as two hundred kilometres - a corduroy of red sand stretching beyond the curve of the horizon. Between the dunes lie the swales: flat corridors of spinifex grassland, hardy Acacia woodland, and broad white playa lakes that hold salt far more often than water. Some of the trees here send roots down to underground springs, drinking from the artesian water hidden beneath the sand. It is austere country, but it is structured country, every ridge and hollow shaped by the same patient physics.

## The People of the Sandhills

Long before the desert was mapped or proclaimed, it was home. The Wangkangurru people lived deep within these dunes, in a place outsiders would later insist was uninhabitable, and they did it by knowing the land with extraordinary precision. Their survival turned on the mikiri - freshwater soaks hidden in claypans and between the dunes, narrow shafts of life-giving water that linked one part of the desert to the next. Following these soaks and the songlines that connected them, families moved through country that to a stranger looked like nothing but sand. When the surveyor David Lindsay entered the western desert in 1886, he documented nine of these native wells - and he found them only with the guidance of a Wangkangurru man who already knew exactly where each one lay. The 'discovery' of the desert was, in truth, a sharing of knowledge tens of thousands of years deep.

## A Refuge Held at Arm's Length

For most of the year the park keeps people out by simple physics. Its conservation core carries the strictest possible protection - an IUCN Category Ia strict nature reserve - and the heat enforces what the rules begin. In an unprecedented step, authorities closed the park and the adjoining reserve to all public access from December 2008 through March 2009, when summer temperatures made the desert lethal; that summer shutdown is now an annual rhythm, the gates closing each December and reopening in mid-March. The protection matters because the desert is alive in ways that reward being left alone. Varied dune systems, salt lakes, and grasslands shelter a surprising range of desert flora and fauna, including a rich community of birds - so rich that the surrounding desert is recognised as an Important Bird Area. The emptiness outsiders once saw is, on closer inspection, a finely balanced abundance.

## Why It Took a Century

Protection came slowly. The land first gained reserve status in December 1967 as the Simpson Desert National Park, was reconstituted as a conservation park in 1972, and was renamed Munga-Thirri–Simpson Desert in 2018 to honour the country's traditional name. Only in 2021 did the pieces finally join into the single, continent-leading park that exists today - the result of years of patient campaigning. The number is staggering on paper, but the meaning is simpler than the hectares suggest. One of the world's great deserts, ancestral country to the people who read its every soak and ridge, is now held intact: not as a blank space on the map, but as a living landscape protected for the long future.

## Flight Context

The park centres near 26.25°S, 137.87°E, in the far north of South Australia near the corners of Queensland and the Northern Territory, roughly 970 km north of Adelaide. From altitude it is one of the most legible deserts on Earth: hundreds of long, parallel red dunes running nearly north-south, ruler-straight to the horizon, separated by pale swales and the white glint of playa salt lakes. The uniformity of the dune field is itself the landmark - few places on the planet show such regular linear texture from the air. Nearest airfield is Birdsville (YBDV) off the northeastern edge; Oodnadatta (YOOD) lies to the southwest. Best viewing is the low, raking light of early morning or late afternoon, when shadows deepen between the ridges and the iron-red sand burns at its richest. The park is closed to ground visitors each December–mid-March; in any season, watch for heat shimmer and airborne dust when inland winds rise over this exceptionally arid region.
