# Munga-Thirri National Park

Queensland's largest national park protects 10,120 square kilometres of the Simpson Desert, where ancient dunes, a sleeping seed bank, and tens of thousands of years of Wangkangurru and Yarluyandi life all share the same red sand.

**Location:** -25.1425, 138.2400

**Categories:** National parks of Queensland, Protected areas established in 1967, Important Bird Areas of Queensland, Simpson Desert, Shire of Diamantina

## Introduction

Munga-Thirri means, roughly, the country of sandhills, and the name is an act of restoration. For decades this place was filed away on Queensland maps as Simpson Desert National Park, a colonial label for country that already had a far older one. The renaming returned the desert to the language of the Wangkangurru and Yarluyandi people, who knew these dunes intimately long before any surveyor drew a line through them. At 10,120 square kilometres, it is the largest national park in Queensland, a single unbroken expanse of dune fields wrapping around Poeppel Corner in the continent's arid heart.

## A Sea Frozen in Sand

The Simpson Desert is a fossil seascape, its waves made of sand and held still. The dunes here began forming around 30,000 years ago, shaped by prevailing winds into long parallel ridges that run with remarkable regularity. Most rise between 10 and 35 metres, though some reach 50, and they line up roughly a kilometre apart, marching in formation across the park. The longest sand ridge in the system extends some 200 kilometres without a break, one of the longest in the world. Between the crests lie the swales, sheltered corridors of spinifex, gibber, and clay where most of the desert's life concentrates. The whole arrangement is so orderly from the air that it looks engineered rather than blown.

## The Watercraft of a Dry People

Survival in a desert is a problem of water, and the Wangkangurru solved it with extraordinary knowledge. Across this country they maintained a network of native wells, or mikiri, hand-dug soakages sunk into the depressions between dunes to reach water trapped beneath the sand. Some descended as much as seven metres. These wells were not random; their locations were held in memory, in song, and in the lines of travel that connected them, allowing people to cross country that to an outsider looked utterly waterless. To understand Munga-Thirri only as wilderness is to miss the truth of it. This was a lived-in landscape, mapped and named and travelled, for tens of thousands of years.

## Life That Waits

The desert's wildlife is built for patience. Red kangaroos drift through the swales, dingoes leave their tracks across the dune crests at dawn, and an astonishing variety of geckos and other reptiles thrive in country that seems to offer them nothing. Feral camels, descendants of animals brought to inland Australia in the colonial era, now roam the dunes in herds. But the most dramatic biology here is the kind that hides. When significant rain finally falls, the bare sand answers within days, dormant wildflowers erupting into bloom and a seed bank that may have waited years for the moment seizing it all at once. The desert does not so much come alive as wake up.

## Drawing the Lines

The park's modern history is brief beside its deep one. David Lindsay became the first non-Aboriginal person to penetrate the central and southern Simpson Desert in 1886, guided in part by the very wells the Wangkangurru had dug, though he was ultimately forced to turn back before completing the full crossing. Formal protection came on 14 December 1967, when the area was proclaimed as Simpson Desert National Park. In 1991 the park was extended northwards to take in arid lands with a more diverse range of vegetation, broadening the sweep of habitat under protection. Today the head ranger is Don Rowlands, a Wangkangurru elder and traditional owner based in Birdsville, so that the country is once again managed, in part, by the people whose ancestors knew it best.

## At the Mercy of the Sky

Munga-Thirri belongs to the Lake Eyre basin, the great internal drainage system where rivers run inward toward the lowest point of the continent rather than out to any sea. Out here the climate sets every rule. Summer temperatures can exceed 50 degrees Celsius, hot enough to make the park's December-to-March closure a matter of survival rather than comfort, while winter nights can plunge below freezing. The country receives its water erratically, sometimes from monsoonal systems and decaying cyclones that push rain far inland, sometimes not for years. When floodwaters do reach the dune fields, they can leave the park impassable for months, which is why the rangers' decisions about when to open and close are governed less by the calendar than by the weather the desert chooses to send.

## Flight Context

Munga-Thirri National Park is centred near 25.14 degrees south, 138.24 degrees east, about 1,495 kilometres west of Brisbane, surrounding Poeppel Corner where Queensland, South Australia, and the Northern Territory meet. From altitude the defining feature is the dune field itself: hundreds of parallel red ridges running roughly north-northwest to south-southeast, the longest stretching some 200 kilometres, with the QAA Line traced faintly across them. The nearest substantial airfield is Birdsville (ICAO YBDV) to the east, with Boulia (YBOU) to the north and Mount Isa (YBMA) the closest larger airport. Recommended viewing altitude is 8,000 to 12,000 feet to take in the geometry of the dunes. Visibility is usually superb in the dry months; wind raises dust, and surface temperatures exceed 50 degrees Celsius in the closed summer season.
