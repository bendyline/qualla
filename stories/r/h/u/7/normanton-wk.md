# Normanton

The largest town in Queensland's Gulf Country, where a life-size replica of the biggest crocodile ever shot guards the park, a purple pub anchors the main street, and the one-of-a-kind Gulflander rolls out across flood-proof steel.

**Location:** -17.6667, 141.0667

**Categories:** Normanton, Queensland, Gulf Country, Towns in Queensland, Tourist attractions in Far North Queensland

## Introduction

He is the size of a bus, painted dull green, jaws cracked open in the council park where the kids climb on him for photos. This is Krys the Savannah King, a life-size replica of a saltwater crocodile measuring 8.63 metres - twenty-eight feet and four inches - the largest croc ever recorded shot. The original was killed on a sandbank of the Norman River in July 1957 by Krystina Pawlowski, a Polish-born hunter the outback remembered as 'One Shot Krys.' Stand beside the model and the scale stops being a number. Then look around: this is Normanton, the biggest town in Queensland's Gulf Country, where the savannah meets the sea and everything, including the crocodiles, runs to outsized proportions.

## One Shot Krys

Krystina Pawlowski's story is more complicated than the trophy suggests. By legend she shot some five thousand crocodiles across the Gulf and missed only three - a hunter of formidable skill in an era when croc-shooting was simply how people made a living on these rivers. But she came to regret it. In her later years she turned from hunter to conservationist, arguing for the protection of the very animals she had once stalked, and her change of heart mirrors the broader shift that eventually made the saltwater crocodile a protected species in Australia. The replica in the park is a record-book curiosity, yes, but it is also a memorial to that turn - a monument to the moment the Gulf decided its great reptiles were worth more alive than dead. The wild crocodiles, incidentally, are still out there in the river. Take the warning signs seriously.

## The Purple Pub and the Painted Town

Normanton's main street has a sense of colour that catches travellers off guard. Its most photographed building is the Purple Pub - a hotel drenched, walls to roofline, in vivid lavender. The story behind it is pure Gulf improvisation: the building began life as the Exchange Hotel in Croydon and was hauled the long road west to Normanton, where it was reborn in its unmistakable coat of paint. A short walk away stands the Big Barramundi, a six-metre statue raised in 1995 to honour the fish that draws anglers from across the country. Between the crocodile, the pub and the barra, the town wears its identity loudly. This was a wealthy port once, in the gold-rush 1880s, and a few grand heritage buildings - a timber bank with its old gold scales, a vast corrugated-iron merchant's warehouse - still hold the line on the high street as reminders of that boom.

## A Train From Nowhere to Nowhere

Down at the edge of town waits one of the strangest railways in the world. The Gulflander runs the historic Normanton to Croydon line, a stretch of track connected to no other railway in Australia - an island of steel laid across the flood plain in 1891. Its genius is that it was built to drown: special steel sleepers sit directly on the bare ground, and when the wet season floods roll over the line, the track simply waits underwater and returns to service once the waters drop. Today a restored 1950 railmotor carries passengers up to Croydon and back on a leisurely weekly schedule, rolling out beneath the terminus's elegant curved iron carriage shade. Catching the Gulflander is less about getting somewhere - Croydon, locals will cheerfully tell you, is in the middle of nowhere - and more about riding a living museum across the savannah at an unhurried clip.

## The Far North They Reached

Some 35 kilometres south-west of town, on the banks of the Little Bynoe River, the bush still holds a quiet, melancholy landmark. This is Camp 119 - the most northerly point the explorers Burke and Wills ever reached on their doomed 1860-61 crossing of the continent. So close to the Gulf they could almost smell it, mangroves and tidal mud finally turned them back short of the open sea. The party blazed fifteen trees to mark the spot, and some of those scarred old trunks, now signposted and protected, can still be found. This is Gkuthaarn and Kukatj country, and the traditional owners' deep connection to these rivers and wetlands was formally recognised in a native title agreement in 2020. For travellers, the wetlands between Normanton and the Gulf port of Karumba are a paradise of a different kind - among the best birdwatching country in Australia, especially in the brilliant weeks just after the wet.

## Flight Context

Normanton sits at 17.667°S, 141.067°E, in the heart of Queensland's Gulf Country, roughly 70 km up the Norman River from the coast. From the air it reads as a compact grid on a flat ironstone ridge surrounded by vast savannah and seasonal wetlands - the curved roof of the railway terminus and the bulky Burns Philp warehouse are the easiest landmarks. Best appreciated from 2,000-4,000 ft AGL, where the river's serpentine course and the patchwork of wetlands toward Karumba come into view. Normanton Airport (ICAO YNTN) lies right at the town's edge. Karumba (YKMB) is about 70 km north-west on the Gulf; Mount Isa (YBMA), the nearest major airport, is roughly 370 km south, and Cairns (YBCS) lies some 700 km east along the Savannah Way. Fly in the dry season (May-October) for reliable visibility; the wet (December-March) can flood the plains, the roads, and even the famous railway.
