# Little Bighorn Battlefield

Custer's last stand - where Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho warriors annihilated five companies of the 7th Cavalry in 1876, the Native American victory that sealed their defeat.

**Location:** 45.5696, -107.4256

**Categories:** montana, indian-wars, custer, battlefield, national-monument

## Introduction

On June 25, 1876, Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer led approximately 210 soldiers of the 7th Cavalry against a combined Lakota Sioux, Northern Cheyenne, and Arapaho encampment along the Little Bighorn River in Montana Territory. None of them survived. Within hours, every man under Custer's direct command was dead - killed by warriors led by Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, and other leaders defending their people and their way of life. The Battle of the Little Bighorn was the greatest Native American victory over the United States Army, and it achieved nothing. Within a year, the victorious tribes were scattered or confined to reservations. Crazy Horse would be murdered at Fort Robinson. Sitting Bull would flee to Canada before returning to die at Standing Rock. The battlefield where they triumphed became a monument first to Custer's 'heroic sacrifice,' later and more honestly to all who fought there - a contested landscape where American myths meet American realities.

## The Campaign

The Great Sioux War of 1876 erupted when Lakota and Cheyenne bands refused to cede the Black Hills - sacred territory that the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie had guaranteed to the Sioux 'forever.' When gold was discovered there in 1874, 'forever' became negotiable. The Grant administration ordered the bands onto reservations by January 1876; when they refused, the Army launched a three-pronged campaign to force compliance. Custer's 7th Cavalry formed part of Brigadier General Alfred Terry's column. On June 25, Custer's scouts located an enormous Lakota and Cheyenne village along the Little Bighorn - perhaps 7,000 people, including 1,500 to 2,000 warriors. Rather than wait for reinforcements, Custer divided his regiment and attacked. He expected the Lakota and Cheyenne to scatter and flee as they had in previous campaigns. He was catastrophically wrong.

## The Battle

Custer divided his 647 soldiers into three battalions. Captain Frederick Benteen was sent south to ensure warriors didn't escape in that direction. Major Marcus Reno advanced directly toward the village with 140 men, expecting Custer to support him. Instead, Custer took five companies north, apparently intending to strike the village's far end. Reno's attack stalled when he encountered unexpected resistance; his men retreated in disorder to a hilltop where they dug in. Benteen joined them there. They heard heavy gunfire to the north but couldn't reach Custer through the warrior forces between them. What happened on Last Stand Hill remains partly reconstructed from archaeological evidence and Native accounts. Custer's command was overwhelmed in perhaps an hour. Warriors later recalled the soldiers' confusion, their attempts to form defensive positions, their final desperate firing. By late afternoon, every man was dead.

## Aftermath

The news reached the eastern states during the nation's Centennial celebration, transforming Custer from a controversial figure into a martyr and the battle into justification for total war against the Plains tribes. Reinforced Army columns pursued the scattered bands through the following winter. Crazy Horse surrendered in May 1877 and was killed while allegedly resisting arrest that September. Sitting Bull fled to Canada, finally returning to surrender in 1881. The Black Hills, the trigger for the war, were seized. The great horse culture of the Plains was finished. Yet the Little Bighorn victory entered Native American memory as proof that resistance was possible - that for one afternoon, the people had defeated their destroyers. The battlefield became a contested memory site where American triumph narratives collided with Native survival narratives.

## Changing Memory

For nearly a century, the battlefield commemorated 'Custer's Battlefield' - celebrating the soldiers' sacrifice and largely ignoring the Native perspective. In 1976, the American Indian Movement protested the centennial commemoration, arguing that the site 'celebrated an act of genocide.' This challenge gradually transformed interpretation. In 1991, Congress renamed the site Little Bighorn Battlefield National Monument and mandated an Indian Memorial. Dedicated in 2003, the memorial features Spirit Warriors, a bronze sculpture by Oglala and Sicangu artist Colleen Cutschall. Red granite markers now stand alongside white marble markers, honoring warriors like Lame White Man and Noisy Walking alongside the soldiers they killed. Senator Ben Nighthorse Campbell, a descendant of Cheyenne leaders who fought Custer, spoke at the dedication. The battlefield has become a site of reconciliation - however incomplete - where all who fought are remembered.

## Visiting the Battlefield

Little Bighorn Battlefield National Monument lies in southeastern Montana, within the Crow Indian Reservation near Crow Agency. The visitor center provides exhibits and films explaining both military and Native perspectives on the battle. A 4.5-mile road winds through the battlefield, passing key sites including Last Stand Hill, where Custer fell and where the granite memorial and most soldier markers are concentrated. The Reno-Benteen Battlefield, three miles southeast, marks where the other battalions survived by holding their hilltop. Walking trails allow closer exploration. The landscape remains remarkably unchanged - the same grass-covered ridges and coulees where desperate men fought. Crow guides offer tours providing both historical and cultural perspectives. The battle anniversary on June 25 draws large crowds. Spring and fall offer better conditions for experiencing the battlefield's powerful isolation. Come prepared for wind, weather, and the weight of history.

## Flight Context

Located at 45.57°N, 107.43°W in Big Horn County, southeastern Montana. The battlefield is visible from altitude as a cluster of monuments on Last Stand Hill overlooking the Little Bighorn River valley. The Crow Agency community is nearby. Interstate 90 passes just north of the monument. Billings Logan International Airport (BIL) is 65 miles northwest.
