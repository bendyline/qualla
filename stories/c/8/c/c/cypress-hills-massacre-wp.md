# Cypress Hills Massacre

A whisky-fueled tragedy at a remote trading post in 1873 killed thirteen Assiniboine people and sparked the creation of Canada's legendary North-West Mounted Police.

**Location:** 49.5492, -109.8681

**Categories:** North-West Mounted Police, 1873 in Canada, Conflicts in 1873, June 1873, Massacres in 1873, Canada-United States relations, Disasters in Saskatchewan, Conflicts in Canada, Maple Creek No. 111, Saskatchewan, Massacres of First Nations

## Introduction

George Hammond fired first. On the morning of June 1, 1873, after a night of drinking whisky at Abe Farwell's trading post in the Cypress Hills, Hammond led a group of American wolfers toward an Assiniboine camp, convinced that someone had stolen his horse. Farwell had assured them the band led by Little Soldier had no stolen horses. The search confirmed it. But Hammond's horse went missing again overnight, and by morning his anger had overcome all reason. When Farwell tried to restrain him, tried to negotiate with Little Soldier and prevent bloodshed, Hammond raised his rifle. The shot that followed would transform Canada's frontier forever.

## Stolen Horses, Borrowed Trouble

The violence that morning grew from grievances accumulated over weeks on the trail. Thomas Hardwick and John Evans had been leading a mixed party of Canadian Metis and American wolfers back from their winter hunt when their horses vanished overnight on the Teton River. Assuming Native people had taken them, the men walked to Fort Benton in Montana Territory to seek help. The authorities refused. Furious, the group headed north across the border in pursuit, eventually arriving at Farwell's small trading post in the Cypress Hills of what was then Canada's North-West Territories. There they found George Hammond, an old friend who had been selling whisky in the area. Hammond joined their search. And then the drinking began.

## Fifty Yards

The wolfers lined up along a riverbank just fifty yards from the Assiniboine camp. They had the high ground, protected by the tall embankment. When Hammond fired and the others followed with volleys into the camp, Little Soldier's people found themselves outgunned and exposed. They returned fire with inferior weapons but could not sustain an attack against the wolfers' protected position. When the shooting stopped, at least thirteen Assiniboine warriors lay dead. One wolfer had been killed. Farwell, the trader who had tried to stop the massacre, later testified about Hammond's actions and his own desperate, failed attempts to prevent the violence. Historical accounts conflict on many details, but the basic horror remains clear.

## The Mounties Ride West

News of the massacre did not reach Ottawa until late August 1873. The Canadian government demanded extradition of those responsible, creating a diplomatic confrontation with the United States. But Canada had no means to enforce law on its western frontier. The newly formed North-West Mounted Police had been authorized but barely existed on paper. The Cypress Hills Massacre became one of the primary catalysts for accelerating the force's recruitment and deployment. By the following year, Assistant Commissioner James Macleod traveled to Helena, Montana Territory, to investigate. Seven arrests were made, but evidence proved contradictory. American commissioner W.E. Cullen acknowledged that while testimony suggested the Assiniboine fired first, they were provoked by an armed party menacing their camp.

## Justice Denied

No one was ever convicted. Two men escaped custody before examination. The American commissioner refused extradition due to conflicting testimony. When three suspects later crossed into Canada voluntarily in 1876, they were arrested and tried in Winnipeg, but the Crown's case collapsed under the weight of insufficient and contradictory evidence. All three were acquitted. The case was finally abandoned in 1882, nearly a decade after the massacre. Today the site is a National Historic Site of Canada, designated in 1964. Artifacts from the massacre are preserved at nearby Fort Walsh National Historic Site, along with reconstructions of Farwell's and Solomon's trading posts. The Cypress Hills themselves remain, their grass-covered slopes looking much as they did on that June morning when everything changed.

## Flight Context

The Cypress Hills Massacre site lies at 49.55N, 109.87W near Battle Creek in what is now southwestern Saskatchewan, close to the Alberta border. The Cypress Hills rise unexpectedly from the prairie, the highest point between the Rocky Mountains and Labrador. Fort Walsh National Historic Site preserves the area's history. Nearest airports include Medicine Hat Airport (CYXH) approximately 100 km west and Swift Current Airport (CYYN) roughly 150 km east. Best viewed at 3,000-5,000 feet AGL. The rolling, forested hills contrast sharply with surrounding prairie.
