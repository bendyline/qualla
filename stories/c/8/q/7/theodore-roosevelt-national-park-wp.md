# Theodore Roosevelt National Park

The only American national park named for a single person preserves the rugged North Dakota badlands where a grief-stricken young Roosevelt found healing and forged his identity as a conservationist.

**Location:** 46.9833, -103.5500

**Categories:** Theodore Roosevelt National Park, Badlands of the United States, Protected areas of Billings County, North Dakota, Protected areas of McKenzie County, North Dakota, National Parks

## Introduction

On February 14, 1884, Theodore Roosevelt's mother and wife died just hours apart in the same New York brownstone. The devastated young politician fled west to the Dakota badlands, seeking solitude in a landscape so harsh he later called it "an abode of iron desolation." What he found instead transformed him. The stark beauty of striped buttes, the thundering bison herds, the endless prairie sky - these wild lands shaped the man who would become America's greatest conservation president. Today, this national park bearing his name protects the very terrain that healed Theodore Roosevelt.

## A Landscape of Grief and Renewal

The Little Missouri River has carved this landscape for millions of years, cutting deep ravines into clay and sandstone, exposing layers of ancient time in bands of red, gray, and gold. Roosevelt arrived here at age 25, broken by loss, and found something unexpected in the brutal terrain: purpose. He threw himself into ranching, spending days in the saddle chasing cattle through coulees and badlands. The physical demands and stark solitude worked a transformation. "I never would have been President if it had not been for my experiences in North Dakota," Roosevelt later reflected. His Maltese Cross Cabin still stands at the South Unit visitor center, a small log structure that housed enormous ambitions.

## Wild Herds of the High Plains

Bison nearly vanished from these grasslands by the time Roosevelt arrived in the 1880s - he watched the great herds dwindle to scattered remnants. Today, roughly 350 bison roam the South Unit and 20 more range through the North Unit, their numbers carefully managed through a program that shares animals with Native American tribes to strengthen tribal herds. Mustang horses gallop across the park, descendants of ranch stock that escaped capture during a 1954 roundup. After years of attempting to remove them, the Park Service recognized in 1970 that these horses belong to the historical landscape Roosevelt knew. Pronghorn, elk, bighorn sheep, mule deer, and prairie dogs complete the cast of Great Plains wildlife that makes every drive through the park feel like traveling backward in time.

## Three Fragments of Wilderness

The park sprawls across three separate units, connected by the Maah Daah Hey Trail - a name meaning "an area that has been or will be around for a long time" in the Mandan language. The South Unit near Medora draws most visitors with its scenic loop drive and accessible badlands vistas. Eighty miles north, the smaller North Unit offers deeper solitude among its own maze of buttes and coulees. Between them lies the Elkhorn Ranch Unit, the remote site where Roosevelt built his second ranch and spent his most formative Dakota years. Only the foundation stones remain, but threats from oil development on adjacent lands make its preservation a continuing challenge. The Little Missouri River threads through all three units, the same waters that carved this improbable landscape from the prairie.

## Seasons of Iron Desolation

Winter transforms the badlands into the iron desolation Roosevelt described, snow softening the sharp terrain into white abstraction while temperatures plunge to minus 30 degrees. Spring brings sudden green to the coulees and hundreds of wildflower species erupting across the prairie. Summer bakes the clay hills until they crack, while bison wallow in dust and pronghorn seek shade in the cottonwood bottoms. The park's dark night skies offer some of the best stargazing in the Great Plains, with occasional displays of northern lights rippling overhead. By autumn, the cottonwoods along the Little Missouri blaze yellow against the rust and ochre of the badlands, and bull elk bugle their challenges across the ravines.

## The Conservation President's Legacy

Roosevelt called his Dakota years the "romance of my life," and the experience shaped his presidency. He established the United States Forest Service, signed the Antiquities Act into law, and protected roughly 230 million acres of public land through national forests, parks, monuments, and wildlife refuges. This park, established in 1978, preserves not just the landscape but the idea that wild places can remake a person. The wilderness designation covering over 29,000 acres protects the most rugged sections from development, maintaining the same untrammeled character that drew a grieving young man west 140 years ago. In the sharp angles of the badlands and the sweep of the prairie, visitors can still find what Roosevelt found - a place wild enough to demand everything and give back even more.

## Flight Context

Located at 46.98N, 103.55W in western North Dakota. The park's three units are visible from altitude as dark badlands terrain contrasting with surrounding agricultural land and grasslands. The South Unit is adjacent to Medora and Interstate 94. The North Unit lies roughly 80 miles north near Watford City. Lake Sakakawea is visible to the northeast. Nearest major airport is Bismarck Municipal Airport (KBIS), approximately 130 miles east. Dickinson Theodore Roosevelt Regional Airport (KDIK) is closer at about 35 miles east of the South Unit. Best viewed at 5,000-8,000 feet AGL for the carved badlands topography and meandering Little Missouri River.
