# Writing-on-Stone Provincial Park

Ancient sandstone deposited 84 million years ago along an inland sea now bears 9,000 years of Indigenous rock art, the largest concentration on the Great Plains.

**Location:** 49.0819, -111.6169

**Categories:** Provincial parks, Rock art, UNESCO World Heritage Sites, Indigenous heritage, Geology

## Introduction

Eighty-four million years ago, during the Late Cretaceous, this land lay at the edge of a vast inland sea. Sand accumulated along that ancient shoreline, eventually compressing into the Milk River Formation. When glaciers retreated at the end of the last Ice Age, meltwater carved the valley that now bears the river's name, and wind, water, and ice sculpted the sandstone into the hoodoos and cliffs that stand today. For 9,000 years, Indigenous peoples have been adding another layer of meaning to these formations, carving and painting their stories into rock that was already ancient beyond imagining.

## Messages in Stone

The petroglyphs tell stories of a changing world. Common scenes depict coups, the war honours that gave young Blackfoot men status and prestige. Horse raids, battles between tribes, encounters with Spirit Beings carved into sandstone that was once a seabed. The Blackfoot name for this place, Aisínai'pi, means 'it is pictured' or 'written.' Over 50 petroglyph sites hold thousands of individual works, making Writing-on-Stone the greatest concentration of rock art anywhere on the North American Great Plains. The art spans millennia. Early images show hunters on foot carrying body shields. After horses arrived around 1730, the images transformed: mounted hunters, warriors depicted without their heavy shields, their new mobility making such protection unnecessary.

## Spirit Homes in the Valley

The Milk River Valley has drawn people for 9,000 years. First Nations peoples, including the Blackfoot and Shoshone, found shelter in the coulees, game to hunt, and berries to gather. But they found something more in the towering hoodoos and cliff faces: homes of powerful spirits. These otherworldly formations, carved by the same forces that shaped the entire valley, inspired reverence and wonder. Evidence of longer-term habitation, including tipi rings and a medicine wheel, suggests some chose to remain near these sacred places. The rock art itself became a dialogue with the spiritual world, a way of recording visions and encounters with powers greater than human.

## The Lonely Mountie Post

In 1887, the North-West Mounted Police, forerunners of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, established a camp here to fight whiskey smuggling and horse-raiding. Neither problem ever became serious at this outpost. The Mounties spent their days fighting prairie fires, herding stray American cattle back across the border, and riding patrol through empty country. When settlers began arriving before World War I, the isolation eased somewhat. The outpost closed in 1918, its mission accomplished more by time than action. Shortly after, persons unknown burned it to the ground. The reconstructed outpost, rebuilt between 1973 and 1975 on its original site, now serves as a park attraction, a reminder of a brief chapter in a much longer story.

## Prairie Wildlife Refuge

Writing-on-Stone protects one of the largest areas of prairie in Alberta's park system. The coulee environment supports balsam poplars and narrow leaf cottonwoods, with peachleaf willows, chokecherry, juniper, and wild roses thriving in sheltered spots. Some of the northernmost cactus species, prickly pear and pincushion, push their limits here. Prairie falcons hunt above the valley while great horned owls claim the night. Pronghorn antelopes, mule deer, and bobcats move across the surrounding grassland. Tiger salamanders, leopard frogs, and plains spadefoot toads represent the amphibians. Reptiles include garter snakes, bull snakes, and the park's most notorious residents: prairie rattlesnakes, timid creatures that want only to be left alone.

## From Park to World Heritage

The park was established in 1957 and designated an archaeological preserve in 1977. Archaeologists catalogued the petroglyph and pictograph sites that year, beginning formal protection of art that had survived millennia. In 1981, the most sensitive areas were set aside for guided tours only, a response to increasing vandalism and graffiti. Parks Canada added the site to the tentative World Heritage list in 2004. In March 2005, it became a National Historic Site. Finally, on July 6, 2019, UNESCO inscribed Writing-on-Stone/Aisínai'pi as a World Heritage Site, officially recognizing what the Blackfoot have known for generations: this place, where ancient rock meets ancient art, holds value beyond measure.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.08N, 111.62W in southern Alberta, straddling the Milk River approximately 100 km southeast of Lethbridge. The distinctive hoodoo formations of the Milk River Formation are visible against the prairie landscape. To the south, the volcanic Sweetgrass Hills of Montana rise in the distance. Nearest airport: CYQL (Lethbridge). Best viewed at lower altitudes to appreciate the sandstone cliffs and river valley carved by glacial meltwater.
