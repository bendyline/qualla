# Alert Bay

A tiny island village where the Kwakwaka'wakw people preserved their outlawed potlatch ceremonies in secret for decades, now home to the world's tallest totem pole and a cultural centre that holds masks the Canadian government once stole.

**Location:** 50.5825, -126.9253

**Categories:** british-columbia, first-nations, cultural-preservation, indigenous, islands

## Introduction

In 1921, the Canadian government raided a potlatch ceremony on Village Island, arrested 45 people, and seized every mask, rattle, copper, and blanket they could carry. The confiscated regalia was shipped to museums in Ottawa and New York. For the Kwakwaka'wakw people of Alert Bay, it was both a criminal act and a cultural catastrophe. But it was not the end. On tiny Cormorant Island, off Vancouver Island's northeast coast, the ceremonies continued in basements and behind closed doors. Today, Alert Bay is where that survival is celebrated -- a village of 449 people with an outsized role in one of Canada's most important stories of cultural resistance.

## The Island Where Ceremonies Went Underground

Alert Bay sits in traditional Kwakwaka'wakw territory, and more than half its residents are First Nations, primarily from the 'Namgis First Nation. More than half the island itself is reserve land. The potlatch -- an elaborate gift-giving ceremony that served as the social, legal, and economic backbone of Northwest Coast cultures -- was banned by Canada in 1885. Chiefs validated their status by giving away wealth; names, songs, dances, and hereditary rights were all transmitted through potlatch. To Canadian authorities, it looked wasteful and heathen. To the Kwakwaka'wakw, losing it meant losing everything. So they refused to lose it. Families hid regalia, disguised ceremonies as Christmas celebrations, and passed knowledge down quietly through the decades of the ban.

## Masks Returned, Dignity Reclaimed

When the potlatch ban was finally lifted in 1951 -- largely because the government conceded it was unenforceable -- the Kwakwaka'wakw emerged with their culture battered but intact. The harder fight was getting back what had been taken. Museums resisted returning the confiscated regalia, claiming the objects were legally acquired, though "surrendered under duress" is more accurate. Negotiations stretched across decades. In 1979, the National Museum of Canada agreed to return Alert Bay's regalia on one condition: a proper museum had to be built. The U'mista Cultural Centre opened in 1980. Its name means "the return of something precious." Inside, masks and regalia are displayed not as ethnographic specimens under glass, but in the order they would appear in a traditional ceremony. The collection is one of the most complete of its kind in the world.

## A Shadow on the Hill

For decades, a decaying four-storey red-brick building glowered from a hill north of town. St. Michael's Indian Residential School, operated by the Anglican Church from 1929 to 1974, had housed up to 200 Indigenous children at a time, drawn from communities stretching from Campbell River to Prince Rupert to Haida Gwaii. Students were prohibited from speaking their languages and kept from their families for years. In February 2015, church leaders, politicians, Assembly of First Nations representatives, and former students gathered for a healing ceremony hosted by the 'Namgis First Nation as the building came down. The Canadian government contributed $1.8 million for the demolition and site remediation. Survivors described the removal as lifting a weight they had carried their entire lives.

## Cedar Standing Tall

At the northern end of Cormorant Island, near the 'Namgis Big House, stands the world's tallest totem pole: 52.7 metres of carved cedar, 173 feet of story reaching into the coastal sky. Thirteen figures climb the pole -- Sun Man, whale, Thunderbird, bear -- representing multiple families of the Kwakwaka'wakw nation. It is visible from the BC Ferries crossing from Port McNeill, a 40-minute ride across the strait. The village itself is small enough to walk in an hour. But between the U'mista Cultural Centre, the Big House where public ceremonial performances still take place, and the totem pole rising above the trees, Alert Bay packs a weight of meaning that has nothing to do with its size.

## Flight Context

Located at 50.58N, 126.93W on Cormorant Island, off Vancouver Island's northeast coast. From altitude, Alert Bay appears as a narrow village clustered along the southern waterfront of a small island in the channels of the Inside Passage. The 173-foot totem pole at the north end of the island is a distinctive landmark. Nearest airport with scheduled service is Port Hardy (CYZT), approximately 45 km northwest. Port McNeill, the ferry terminal town on Vancouver Island, is visible across the strait to the south. Malcolm Island and Haddington Island are nearby to the north and west.
