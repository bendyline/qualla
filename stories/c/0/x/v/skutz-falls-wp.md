# Skutz Falls

A twenty-foot drop on Vancouver Island's Cowichan River that once stopped every salmon in the watershed, Skutz Falls now has a concrete staircase built for fish and an audience that comes to watch them climb it.

**Location:** 48.7833, -123.9500

**Categories:** Waterfalls of British Columbia, Cowichan Valley, Rivers and salmon, Provincial parks, Coast Salish

## Introduction

Twenty feet is not much of a waterfall. Skutz Falls does not plunge so much as stagger -- a run of steep, broken rapids where the Cowichan River drops over a ledge of rock on its way to the sea. Photographers pass it over for the taller, prettier cascades elsewhere on Vancouver Island. But for several thousand years, those twenty feet were the hard ceiling of the salmon world. Everything upstream, the whole upper river and the lake behind it, lay beyond reach of fish that could not make the jump. Autumn after autumn, chinook and coho piled into the pool below and got no further. The river above ran clear and empty.

## A Name That Only Describes

The falls were called Skwets, or Squitz, long before the spelling settled into Skutz. In the Cowichan dialect of Halkomelem the word simply means waterfall -- no legend attached, no hero, no transformation story, just the plain noun for the thing that is there. That bluntness fits a river whose own name carries a different kind of warmth. Cowichan comes from Quw'utsun, a Coast Salish word usually rendered as warming your back, describing a valley that catches the sun. The Cowichan people have lived in this watershed for thousands of years, and the river they call Quw'utsun Sta'lo' has fed them for all of it. The names tell you what mattered: the sun on the valley, the water on the rock, and the salmon that connected the two.

## A Staircase Built for Fish

In the 1960s, members of the local fish and game association decided the ledge should stop being final. They built a fish ladder alongside the falls -- a series of stepped concrete pools, each one a modest rise, that lets a salmon climb what it could never leap. The engineering is unromantic. The result is not. Come in late autumn and you can stand a few feet from the ladder while spawning salmon hurl themselves up through the pools, dark-bodied and battered, some of them scraped raw from the gravel, working the staircase one surge at a time. Children lean over the rail. Nobody talks much. For a fish that has crossed the North Pacific to get here, this concrete flight of stairs is the last obstacle between it and the gravel bed where it was born.

## The Trap Below the Pools

In summer the river changes character entirely. Below the falls the water slows, warms, and turns the translucent green that draws people onto inner tubes by the hundreds. It is easy to reach from the road, easy to leave a car at each end, and easy to underestimate. Immediately downstream the Cowichan enters Marie Canyon, where the river narrows between rock walls and the current accelerates into standing waves and undercut ledges. The stretch between Skutz Falls and the canyon has killed swimmers and tubers, and local fire crews are called out to it with grim regularity. Nothing about the surface warns you. The same river that invites you in at the falls is, four hundred metres later, a piece of moving machinery that does not let go.

## A Working Heritage River

In 2003 the Cowichan was designated a Canadian Heritage River, recognition of forty-seven kilometres of water that have carried Coast Salish history, a working fishery, and a famous run of trout. Skutz Falls sits inside Cowichan River Provincial Park, with a campground nearby and a trailhead onto the Cowichan River Footpath, a twenty-kilometre route that threads the valley through second-growth forest and mossy bluffs. It is an ordinary place in the best sense -- a picnic table, a gravel lot, the smell of cedar and cold water. The drama here is seasonal and small-scale, measured in fish that make it up the ladder and fish that do not. Stand long enough and you understand why the people who named this place did not bother inventing a story for it. The falls were already doing all the work.

## Flight Context

Skutz Falls lies at 48.783N, 123.950W in the Cowichan Valley of southeastern Vancouver Island, roughly 15 km west of Duncan and 10 km east of Lake Cowichan. From the air the river is the landmark: a bright ribbon running east out of Cowichan Lake through dense second-growth Douglas fir, tightening into the dark slot of Marie Canyon just downstream of the falls. Cowichan Lake itself, a long finger of water to the west, is the easiest navigational anchor; the Island Highway corridor and Duncan's grid lie to the east. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 feet, low enough to pick out the canyon walls and the cleared river bars. Nearest airports: Duncan Airport (CAM3) about 8nm east, Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) about 20nm north, and Victoria International (CYYJ) about 35nm southeast. Valley fog is common on autumn mornings and can fill the river corridor while the ridges above stay clear.
