# Lake Cowichan

A Vancouver Island logging town at the outlet of a great lake, where Ts'uubaa-asatx territory, Punjabi sawmill families and a short-lived Gestalt commune all share the same riverbank.

**Location:** 48.8258, -124.0540

**Categories:** Towns, Lakes, Logging History, Indigenous Culture, British Columbia

## Introduction

In 1969 a German-born psychiatrist with a white beard and a talent for provocation left California and moved to a logging town on Vancouver Island. Fritz Perls, co-founder of Gestalt therapy and one of the more magnetic figures of the human potential movement, had been running workshops at the Esalen Institute in Big Sur. What he wanted next was something he called a Gestalt kibbutz: a place where people would live together and work on themselves full time rather than dropping in for a weekend. He chose Lake Cowichan. Around thirty of his followers came north with him. Less than a year later he was dead, and the commune dissolved - but for a few months, a mill town of a few thousand people was one of the stranger addresses in psychotherapy.

## Kaatza, the Great Lake

Long before any of that, the water had a name. The Ts'uubaa-asatx - the People of the Lake - have lived along its shores since time immemorial, and their traditional territory takes in the whole Cowichan Lake watershed: every tributary that feeds it, and the ridgelines of the mountains that ring it. Their word for the lake itself, Kaatza, means Great Lake, which is exactly what it is. Cowichan Lake stretches west into the island's forested interior, and the town sits at its eastern end, right where the Cowichan River begins its run down toward the sea. That outlet is the reason there is a town here at all. Everything the valley has ever done - fishing, logging, milling, floating, tubing - has depended on where the water leaves the lake.

## Rails In, Rails Out

The rails reached town in 1912, and for most of the next seventy years the Esquimalt & Nanaimo and the Canadian National hauled timber out of the Cowichan watershed toward coastal mills. The crews who cut and milled it came from a long way off. Scandinavian, Japanese and Punjabi loggers built the industry here, and the Cowichan Valley has held a significant Sikh Canadian community for more than 130 years, with families working the local sawmills from the early twentieth century into the 1980s. The town incorporated in 1944. The rails were lifted in 1984, which in most places would be the end of the story. Here the grade simply changed jobs: the old right-of-way is now the Cowichan Valley Trail, part of the Trans Canada Trail, carrying walkers and cyclists over restored timber trestles on the route that once carried logs.

## A Summer Town on a Heritage River

Come July, the river fills with inner tubes. The Cowichan is a designated BC Heritage River, and drifting its warm green water from town down through Cowichan River Provincial Park has become the valley's signature summer ritual, shared with fly fishers working the same runs for trout and steelhead. Gordon Bay Provincial Park, on the lake's south shore, handles the other half of the season - campers, kayakers, paddleboarders, and swimmers testing water that is genuinely warm by British Columbia standards. Around all of it stands Pacific temperate rainforest, the ecosystem that grows some of the largest and oldest trees on Earth, and which is the reason the railways came in the first place.

## Sheltered Weather, Long Reach

Tucked at low elevation in the island's interior, Lake Cowichan gets a climate slightly out of step with its neighbours. Summer afternoons run several degrees warmer than the coastal towns down the highway, which is what built the tourism economy. Winter reverses the deal: sheltered from the moderating ocean, nights get colder and the town sees more snow than Victoria or Duncan ever do. Warm, dry summers and mild, wet winters have also given the wider Cowichan Valley an expanding wine industry, an unlikely second act for a place defined by sawmills. The town's outward reach is unlikely too. Lake Cowichan, population 3,325 at the 2021 census, was the site of the first community twinning program between Canada and Japan, paired with the district of Ohtaki in Date City, Hokkaido. Nearby Youbou, Honeymoon Bay and Mesachie Lake - a few hundred people each - round out a shoreline that still runs on lake time.

## Flight Context

Lake Cowichan sits at 48.8258 N, 124.0540 W, at the eastern outlet of Cowichan Lake on southern Vancouver Island, roughly 28 nm northwest of Victoria. The navigation feature is the lake itself: a long, narrow, slightly bent body of water running about 30 km west-northwest into the island's forested interior, unmistakable against the surrounding green of the Cowichan watershed. The town is the built-up patch at the lake's eastern tip, where the Cowichan River drains east toward Duncan and Cowichan Bay. A viewing altitude of 4,000 to 6,000 feet MSL shows the lake end to end along with the river valley and the old rail grade now serving as the Cowichan Valley Trail. Victoria International (CYYJ) lies southeast, Nanaimo (CYCD) to the north, and Duncan Airport (CAM3) is the closest field, about 12 nm east. Terrain rises sharply on both flanks of the lake; low cloud and valley fog are common on winter mornings, and summer afternoons are typically the clearest window.
