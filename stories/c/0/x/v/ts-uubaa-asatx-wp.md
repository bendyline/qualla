# Ts'uubaa-asatx

The People of the Lake, canoe builders at the centre of Vancouver Island, whose nation now numbers a few dozen on a reserve of forty-two hectares.

**Location:** 48.8260, -124.0697

**Categories:** british-columbia, vancouver-island, first-nations, coast-salish, lake, indigenous-history

## Introduction

Twenty-six names. That was the entire registered membership of the Tsʼuubaa-asatx Nation in April 2022 - a roll small enough to fit on one sheet of paper, belonging to a people whose own name for themselves means People of the Lake. The lake is Cowichan, a long trough of cold green water gouged into the middle of Vancouver Island, and the Quwʼutsun name for it is Kaatza. Before contact, upwards of five hundred Tsʼuubaa-asatx lived along its shores. What happened between those two numbers is not a mystery. It is simply a history that the tourist brochures for the Cowichan Valley tend to start after.

## People of the Lake

Cowichan Lake sits in the island's interior, walled in by forested ridges, draining east through the Cowichan River toward the Strait of Georgia. It is the kind of place that rewards patience: morning fog that lifts off the water in sheets, loons calling across a surface too still to seem real, second-growth timber running up every slope.

The Tsʼuubaa-asatx have lived on these shores for millennia. Their reserve today, the Cowichan Lake Reserve, occupies about 42.7 hectares on the north bank - a little over a hundred acres, a fraction of a territory that once ran the length of the lake and into the country beyond it. The village of Lake Cowichan, a logging town with a railway museum named Kaatza after the lake, sits at the outlet end.

## Canoes for the Coast

Position is destiny on an island, and the Tsʼuubaa-asatx held a good one. Cowichan Lake lies almost exactly at the middle of Vancouver Island, with routes running east toward the Salish Sea and west toward the open Pacific. That made the lake a meeting ground, a place where groups from opposite coasts could reach each other without crossing the whole island.

What the Tsʼuubaa-asatx brought to that exchange was craftsmanship. They were known for their cedar canoes, and they traded them outward to neighbouring peoples including the Ditidaht, whose territory lies toward the island's west coast. Consider what that trade implies: the skill to fell and hollow a cedar log into something seaworthy, the reputation to make it worth another nation's while, and a standing relationship with people who spoke an entirely different language.

## A Borrowed Tongue, A Kept Identity

The Tsʼuubaa-asatx are their own distinct group with ties running west to the Ditidaht, yet the language spoken among them came from the other direction. Their powerful eastern neighbours, the Cowichan Tribes - the largest band government among the Coast Salish - speak the Island dialect of Halkomelem known as Hulʼqumiʼnum. Over time the Tsʼuubaa-asatx adopted it, and linguists now classify them within the Hulʼqumiʼnum group.

This is a common enough pattern along the Northwest Coast, where influence flowed along trade routes and marriage ties rather than borders. But it illustrates something easy to miss: a nation can change the language it speaks and remain entirely itself. The Tsʼuubaa-asatx never stopped being the people of this lake. They simply came to describe it in their neighbours' words.

## The Arithmetic of Absence

Five hundred before contact. A few dozen now. The documented record of that decline is thinner than it should be, and what exists comes mostly from outside observers: an 1860 prospector found many Tsʼuubaa-asatx already dead or dying of smallpox, and an 1887 colonial surveyor reported the people had been 'nearly wiped out' by conflict with neighbouring Cowichan and Ditidaht groups - an account worth reading with some caution, since it comes from a government surveyor rather than the nation itself. Behind those outside accounts sits a pattern documented again and again across this coast: laws that restricted where Indigenous people could live and work, and the steady conversion of traditional territory into reserve land, farms, and towns. Survivors left the lake because staying became impossible - for work, for family, for the simple reason that the land that had supported a community no longer belonged to it.

They have not stopped being a nation. The Tsʼuubaa-asatx maintain a government, an economic development arm, and a seat in the treaty process; roughly thirty people live on the reserve, and, by their own account, many more want to come home. Lake Cowichan today is better known for fishing and tubing the river than for any of this. The people who named the lake are still there, on the north bank, holding the ground they were left.

## Flight Context

The Cowichan Lake Reserve lies at 48.826N, 124.070W on the north shore of Cowichan Lake, in the interior of southern Vancouver Island. From 4,000 to 6,000 feet the lake is unmistakable - a long, narrow, roughly east-west body of water set in forested ridges, with the town of Lake Cowichan at its eastern outlet where the Cowichan River begins its run to the Strait of Georgia. Clear-cut patchwork on the surrounding slopes makes the logging history legible from the air. Nearest airfields are Duncan (CAM3) about 20 nm east, Port Alberni (CBS8) to the northwest, Nanaimo (CYCD) to the northeast, and Victoria International (CYYJ) some 40 nm southeast. Valley fog is common on cold mornings and can fill the lake basin to the ridgelines while the peaks stay in sun; afternoon westerlies funnel along the lake axis.
