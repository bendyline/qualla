# James Island (La Push, Washington)

A-ka-lat, the Top of the Rock, guarded the mouth of the Quillayute River as a fortified Quileute village and a burial place for chiefs, and after a century of federal ownership it was given back.

**Location:** 47.9062, -124.6474

**Categories:** Quileute, Indigenous history, Pacific islands of Washington (state), Olympic coast, Landforms of Clallam County, Washington

## Introduction

The Quileute name for it is A-ka-lat, Top of the Rock, and for a long time the people who gave it that name buried their chiefs on its summit. The island stands 160 feet above the surf at the mouth of the Quillayute River, a green-capped wall of stone that blocks the horizon when you look west from First Beach at La Push. It is close enough that on a calm evening you can hear water working the base of it. You will not get any closer than that. Since 1966, when the federal government returned the island to the Quileute Tribe, no one outside the tribe has been permitted to set foot on it.

## Top of the Rock

Height is defense, and the Quileute used it. A fortified village occupied the island into the second half of the nineteenth century, reachable only by a climb that attackers coming off the water would have had to make slowly and in full view. The same elevation served a gentler purpose. From the summit, lookouts could read the water for miles and spot the blows of migrating whales long before the canoes went out. The Quileute were among the Northwest coastal peoples who hunted whales, and a sighting from A-ka-lat was the beginning of something the whole village would take part in. The island was a fortress, a watchtower, and a calendar all at once, a place where the practical business of survival was conducted from the best seat on the coast.

## Whose Name Sticks

The English name is contested, and the disagreement is telling. Local historians hold that the island honors Francis Wilcox James, a lighthouse keeper who was a friend to the Quileute. The 1923 reference volume Origin of Washington Geographic Names credits instead a Quileute chief named Jimmie Howeshatta. Two accounts, one settler and one Indigenous, attached to the same syllable. Neither displaced A-ka-lat, which the Quileute have never stopped using, and which describes the place more honestly than any surname could. It is a small illustration of how the coast got its modern map: names layered over names, some recorded carefully, some guessed at by clerks a long way from the water.

## The River Moved

James Island was not always an island. It began as a sea stack still tethered to the mainland, one of the countless outcrops along this coast where softer rock eroded away and the hard core stayed standing. What finished the separation was not the ocean but the United States Army Corps of Engineers, which rerouted the Quillayute River and cut the last connection. Engineering did in a few seasons what the Pacific had been attempting for millennia. The result is a harbor entrance the fishing fleet at La Push still uses, and an island that now sits fully offshore, its isolation partly manufactured. The Coast Guard maintains an automated light and a foghorn there, a machine keeping watch on a summit where people once did.

## Given Back

For years the island was folded into the Quillayute Needles National Wildlife Refuge, managed by a federal agency as habitat, its history as a village and a burial ground largely beside the point of that designation. In 1966 the Department of the Interior removed James Island from the refuge and returned it to the Quileute people, on the grounds that it lay within the historic Quileute Indian Reservation and had never stopped being theirs in any sense that mattered. Restorations of this kind were uncommon, and the reasoning behind this one was refreshingly plain. The chiefs buried on the summit are there still. The tribe closed the island to outsiders, which is what you do with a cemetery.

## Looking West From First Beach

La Push sits at the end of a road that goes no further, a fishing village on Quileute land where the Quillayute River finally gives up on the continent. The view seaward is dominated by James Island and, beyond it, the scattered sea stacks of the Needles, dark shapes that go blue and then gold as the light fails. Storm surf here arrives with the whole Pacific behind it. On clear nights the red and green channel markers at the river mouth glow against the water while the automated light on the island turns above them. The rock is off limits, and it is more interesting for it. Some places are best understood from a respectful distance.

## Flight Context

James Island lies at 47.906N, 124.647W, immediately offshore at the mouth of the Quillayute River beside La Push, Washington, on the Olympic Peninsula's Pacific coast. From the air it reads as a steep-sided, flat-topped green rock roughly 160 feet high, separated from the beach by a narrow channel. Use the river mouth, the jetties, and the cluster of buildings at La Push as the visual anchor; the sea stacks of the Quillayute Needles scatter north and south along the surf line. Quillayute Airport (KUIL) sits about 8nm east near Forks, and William R. Fairchild International Airport (KCLM) at Port Angeles is roughly 45nm northeast. Best viewed between 1,000 and 2,500 feet with the coastline on your left heading north. Marine fog and low stratus are frequent here even when the interior is clear, and the island is tribal land closed to visitors, so approaches should stay offshore and high.
