# Canyon Creek Shelter

A one-room log shelter above Sol Duc Falls, the last of three built by Civilian Conservation Corps crews in 1939 and restored decades later with nothing but hand tools carried in on people's backs.

**Location:** 47.9518, -123.8199

**Categories:** Historic Sites, National Parks, Architecture, Civilian Conservation Corps, Washington, Hiking

## Introduction

Every morning the crew hiked in with chisels, slicks, gouges, and drawknives strapped to their packs, and every evening they hiked back out carrying the same tools. No generators. No power saws. Nothing that ran on anything but arms and patience. Those were the terms for repairing the Canyon Creek Shelter, a single-room log building standing in the dripping forest above Sol Duc Falls in Olympic National Park - and they were nearly the same terms accepted by the young men who built it in 1939.

## The Corps Arrives at the Elwha

Olympic National Park was barely a year old. Congress had converted the Forest Service's Mount Olympus National Monument into a national park in 1938, and the new park inherited hundreds of square miles of rain forest, ridgeline, and river valley with very little infrastructure to show for it. Into that gap came the Civilian Conservation Corps, the Depression-era work program that put unemployed young men into tents and boots and sent them out to build trails, bridges, and shelters. Camp Elwha, on the river of the same name, supplied the crews for this corner of the park. In 1939 they built three rustic shelters for hikers - one at Hoh Lake, one at Moose Lake, and one above the falls on the Sol Duc. The work was hard, the pay was small, and most of it went home to families who needed it more than the men did.

## Two Gone, One Standing

Of those three shelters, two are gone. Hoh Lake and Moose Lake sit high and exposed, and in a landscape that measures rainfall in feet rather than inches, wood surrenders. Snow load flattens roofs. Rot climbs up from the sills. The forest simply reclaims what people leave in it. Only the shelter above Sol Duc Falls survived, which makes it the last CCC trail shelter standing anywhere in Olympic National Park. That distinction earned it a place on the National Register of Historic Places on July 13, 2007 - not for grandeur, because it has none, but for what it represents: a moment when the federal government decided that unemployed young men and untouched backcountry might be good for each other.

## A Cupola in the Rain Forest

The building is small and stubbornly specific. One story, T-shaped in plan, built of logs joined at the corners with saddle notches, set on a concrete foundation and roofed with cross-gabled cedar shakes. A porch projects from the front, open to the weather, and above it sits a small cupola - a flourish that serves no structural purpose and was clearly included because someone thought the building deserved one. This is the rustic style the park service favored in that era: heavy timber, local materials, an insistence that a structure should look as though the forest produced it rather than a lumberyard. Nearby, the Sol Duc River narrows and drops into a mossy slot canyon in braided ribbons. The shelter looks out over that sound, which never stops.

## Chisels In, Chisels Out

By the twenty-first century the shelter was failing quietly. A restoration project assessed its structure and then set about repairing it the way it had been made - stabilizing and replacing portions of the log sill and frame, patching the roof gables, renewing the cedar-shake cladding. New saddle notches were cut to replicate the shape, orientation, and even the tool marks of the CCC original. Because the site sits deep in the backcountry, crews used only traditional hand tools and packed them in and out every day. The replacement material came from close by: Douglas fir logs and western red cedar shakes. Field teams also drew the building as it stood in 2011, producing measured architectural drawings for the Historic American Buildings Survey at the Library of Congress, so that even if the forest wins eventually, the shelter will still exist on paper.

## Two Kinds of Preservation

Not everyone agreed the work should have happened. The Canyon Creek Shelter was one of five aging structures at the center of a lawsuit brought by Wilderness Watch, which argued that rebuilding cabins and shelters inside a designated wilderness - particularly with helicopter support - violated the 1964 Wilderness Act's promise to protect a landscape's primeval character. The park service argued that historic buildings on park land carry their own obligation. Both sides were defending something real. In December 2016 a federal judge sided with the park, and the shelter stayed. Hikers reaching the falls today still find it there, open to the porch, smelling of wet cedar, offering the same thing it offered in 1939: a dry place to stand while the rain comes down.

## Flight Context

Canyon Creek Shelter sits at 47.9518°N, 123.8199°W, deep in the Sol Duc valley on the north side of Washington's Olympic Peninsula. The shelter itself is invisible from the air beneath old-growth canopy, so navigate by terrain: the Sol Duc River drainage cuts east-west between forested ridges, with the high country of the Bailey Range and the glaciated summit of Mount Olympus rising to the southeast. The Sol Duc Hot Springs road and resort clearing are the most reliable visual anchors upstream of the falls. Recommended viewing altitude is 6,000-8,000 ft MSL for valley context, or 10,000 ft and above to take in the whole Olympic massif at once. Nearest airport is William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) at Port Angeles, roughly 25 nm northeast; Quillayute Airport (KUIL) near Forks lies about 25 nm west. Expect persistent low cloud and rain in this drainage - the Olympic rain forest earns its name - with the clearest windows typically in July, August, and September.
