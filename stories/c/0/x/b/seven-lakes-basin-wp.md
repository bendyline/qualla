# Seven Lakes Basin

A glacier-carved bowl at the head of the Sol Duc River holding eight lakes that nobody renamed, reached by a nineteen-mile loop that climbs out of Olympic rainforest onto a ridge facing Mount Olympus.

**Location:** 47.9153, -123.7720

**Categories:** Landforms of Clallam County, Washington, Landforms of Olympic National Park, Alpine lakes, Hiking and backcountry

## Introduction

Count them and the arithmetic fails. There are eight lakes in Seven Lakes Basin — Sol Duc, Long, Lunch, Morgenroth, No Name, Clear, Round, and a final holdout nobody ever bothered to christen at all, which appears on maps as Lake No. 8. Scattered among them are dozens of ponds too small to argue about. The name stuck anyway, the way names do, and hikers who grind nineteen miles and four thousand vertical feet to stand above the basin rarely stop to recount. What draws them is not the tally. It is the moment the forest ends and the whole interior of the Olympic Peninsula opens up at once.

## Up Out of the Rainforest

The trail begins in a valley so wet and green it feels almost subterranean. From the trailhead near Sol Duc Hot Springs, the path follows the river upstream through old-growth forest — Douglas-fir and western hemlock, moss hanging in curtains, daylight arriving in narrow shafts. Less than a mile in, Sol Duc Falls drops through a narrow rock cleft in several channels depending on the season, and the roar reaches you well before the footbridge does. Past the falls the ground tilts. The loop gains roughly four thousand feet across its nineteen miles, and most of that arrives in a few relentless stretches of switchbacks where the only measure of progress is the changing timber. Hemlock gives way to subalpine fir. The trees shrink, thin, and eventually quit, and you step out onto open meadow with the entire Sol Duc drainage falling away behind your boots.

## Eight Lakes, One Name

Ice made this place. Glaciers scooped out the bowl and left a scatter of shallow tarns in rock hollows — Sol Duc Lake the largest, Morgenroth the smallest, with Long, Lunch, Clear, Round, No Name and Lake No. 8 filling the spaces between. Morgenroth shares its name with Chris Morgenroth, a Forest Service ranger who gave twenty-three years to this peninsula, blazed many of its first trails, and replanted the thirteen thousand acres the Sol Duc burn consumed in 1907. Two more lakes sit just below the divide but are excluded from the count by local convention: Deer Lake, passed on the way up, and Heart Lake, which earned its name honestly — seen from above, the shoreline draws a passable valentine. Anglers do well in some of these waters and find nothing whatsoever in others. Heart Lake, for all its charm, holds no fish at all.

## The Ridge Between Two Rivers

The High Divide is the reason people come. This ridge runs along the basin's southern rim, separating the Hoh River's watershed from the Sol Duc's, and the trail traces its spine for several exposed miles. Bogachiel Peak, at 5,478 feet, marks the high point and the finest vantage on the loop. Turn north and the lakes lie below like spilled mercury in a stone dish. Turn south and the ground falls into the Hoh Valley — one of the wettest places in the contiguous United States — with Mount Olympus standing beyond it, the highest summit in the range, its glaciers hanging in plain view across a gulf of empty air. There is no gradual reveal, no build. You walk a ridge with forest on both flanks, crest a modest rise, and the whole massif is simply there.

## A Short and Crowded Season

Winter owns this basin for most of the year. Even after the snow clears, the bowl itself stays a barren rockscape well into late summer, austere in a way that surprises people who came for postcard alpine lakes. What surrounds it compensates. The meadows below the divide run thick with wildflowers, then with wild blueberries heavy enough by late summer to slow a hiker's pace considerably. Black bears work the same berry fields, unhurried and largely indifferent to human company. Marmots whistle from boulder fields and vanish. Black-tailed deer browse the meadow edges at dusk, and Roosevelt elk — long called Olympic elk here — move through the valleys below. Overnight permits are required and go quickly, because this is a crowded wilderness by Olympic standards. Which is to say you might pass a dozen people in a day, and then see nobody until morning.

## Flight Context

Seven Lakes Basin lies at 47.92N, 123.77W in the northern interior of Olympic National Park, Washington, at the headwaters of the Sol Duc River. From the air the basin reads as a cluster of small dark tarns in a pale rock bowl, bounded on the south by the east-west line of the High Divide and Bogachiel Peak (5,478 feet). Use the Sol Duc River valley running northwest toward Lake Crescent as your approach line, or pick up Mount Olympus and its glaciers roughly 10nm to the south-southeast. Nearest airports: William R. Fairchild International at Port Angeles (KCLM) about 25nm northeast, and Quillayute State (KUIL) near Forks about 20nm west. Terrain here rises to over 5,000 feet with higher summits nearby, and the Olympics generate their own weather — marine cloud frequently fills the valleys while ridgelines stay clear. Best light is mid-morning in late summer, when snow has retreated and the lakes hold their color.
