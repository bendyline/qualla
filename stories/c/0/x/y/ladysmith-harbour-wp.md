# Ladysmith Harbour

A sheltered Vancouver Island inlet that lost its name to a colonial war on another continent, gained a coal port, and quietly recorded its own water temperature every single day for two decades.

**Location:** 48.9908, -123.8000

**Categories:** Ports and harbours of British Columbia, Strait of Georgia, Mid Vancouver Island

## Introduction

The houses came over the hill. In the closing years of the 19th century James Dunsmuir needed somewhere to settle the families of the men working his coal seams inland, and he chose a sheltered inlet on Vancouver Island's east coast that everyone then called Oyster Harbour. Buildings from Extension and Wellington were taken apart, or jacked up whole and dragged - by rail where there was rail, by oxen where there wasn't - and set down again on the slope above the water. Then in 1900 Dunsmuir renamed the place Ladysmith, marking the relief of a besieged British garrison in South Africa that February during the Second Boer War. The harbour took the new name along with the town. The oysters, which had been there considerably longer than any of it, kept their own counsel.

## Before It Belonged to Anyone

Long before the coal and the renaming, this was and remains Stz'uminus territory. Two of the Nation's reserves sit on the harbour itself: Oyster Bay Indian Reserve No. 12, on the south shore near the head of the inlet, and Chemainus Indian Reserve No. 13, the largest of them, occupying the headland on the outer southwest side where the harbour opens to the strait. The people here speak Hul'q'umi'num, a dialect of Halkomelem, and the Nation's own name in that language is shc'uminus. Until 2009 the federal government's paperwork called them the Chemainus Indian Band, a name applied from outside; the change restored what the community calls itself. Stz'uminus voices were also among those that pushed, successfully, for the waters north of here to be recognized as the Salish Sea - a designation made official in 2010. That is a long habit of insisting, politely and repeatedly, on the right words for a place.

## Coal in the Water

What made this inlet valuable to Dunsmuir was not the oysters but the shelter and the depth. Ladysmith Harbour became the coaling port for his mine at Extension, several kilometres inland - the coal came down by rail, and the wharves loaded it into the bunkers of ships bound up and down the coast. A working harbour organizes everything around it. The town incorporated on 3 June 1904, laid out on the hillside so that almost every street looked down at the ships. Coal built the place, and when coal receded, forestry and the log booms took over the same water. If you stand on the Ladysmith shore now you can still read the sequence in the shoreline: mine wharves, then mill wharves, then the pilings that outlasted both, standing in the shallows like a row of unanswered questions.

## A Bucket, a Thermometer, and Twenty Years

From 1938 to 1942, and again from 1949 through 1957, someone walked down to the water at Ladysmith Harbour every single day and took a reading. Surface temperature. Salinity. The numbers went into the British Columbia Shore Station Oceanographic Program, run for what is now the Department of Fisheries and Oceans, part of a network of coastal stations doing exactly the same unglamorous thing at the same unglamorous hour. Nobody collecting those figures in 1939 was thinking about century-scale ocean change. They were building a baseline, and a baseline is only worth anything if somebody shows up in the rain for years. Those daily observations, along with the rest of the shore-station record, now sit in an open federal dataset - one of the longer continuous windows anyone has on the temperature and saltiness of this coast's inshore water.

## The Harbour Now

Ladysmith today holds just under nine thousand people on twelve square kilometres of hillside, its heritage main street preserved well enough that a television production set up shop here for several seasons in the 2020s and hired locals as background. The harbour below is still doing work. Log booms drift at anchor; small craft move in and out; the Stz'uminus reserves hold their ground on the south shore and the outer headland. The name on the chart still commemorates a siege that was lifted on the far side of the world, an accident of a mine owner's timing and politics. But the water it describes is a Coast Salish inlet that was called something else for thousands of years before 1900, and is still called that by the people who never left it.

## Flight Context

Ladysmith Harbour lies at 48.9908 N, 123.8000 W, indenting the east coast of Vancouver Island opposite the southern Strait of Georgia. Best appreciated from 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL on a northwest-southeast pass down the island shore: the harbour reads as a narrow, well-protected finger of water with the town stacked on the hillside above its northwest side, log booms visible as pale rectangles on the surface, and the Chemainus headland closing the southwest entrance. Thetis and Valdes Islands lie just offshore to the east. Nearest airports: Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) roughly 5 nm north-northwest, Victoria International (CYYJ) about 25 nm southeast, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 27 nm northeast across the strait. Morning fog and low stratus are common in autumn and winter; summer afternoons are usually clear with good visibility across to the Coast Mountains on the mainland.
