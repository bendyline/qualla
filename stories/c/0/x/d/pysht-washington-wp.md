# Pysht, Washington

A Klallam village name, a vanished company town of five hundred, and a handful of buildings where the Pysht River meets the Strait of Juan de Fuca.

**Location:** 48.1960, -124.1160

**Categories:** washington, olympic-peninsula, company-town, logging, indigenous-history, coastal

## Introduction

Say it aloud and it sounds like a sneeze, or a word invented to win at Scrabble. Pysht comes from the Klallam language - pəšc̕t, a word that may carry the sense of against the wind, or against the current - and the people who gave it to this stretch of shoreline were living at the river's mouth long before anyone tried to spell it in Roman letters. Drive State Route 112 today and you could miss the place entirely: a bend in the road, a river sliding out into the Strait of Juan de Fuca, trees on every side. A century ago, five hundred people lived here. They had a hospital, a movie hall, an electric plant, and a company store, and every plank of it belonged to a lumber firm headquartered two thousand miles away in Michigan.

## The Word for Wind

The Klallam knew this coast as a place where the weather has an opinion. The Strait of Juan de Fuca funnels air between Vancouver Island and the Olympic Peninsula, and at the Pysht River mouth that air arrives with nothing to slow it down - which is very likely what the name was recording in the first place.

The river itself drains the forested country south of the strait and runs northeast to the saltwater. Its mouth gave the village everything a Coast Salish community needed: a beach to land canoes, a river for fish, and a back door into the timbered interior. Directly east, the bluff at Pillar Point rises out of the water, a landmark visible for miles and today a county park. The geography has not changed. Only the number of people living in it has.

## Five Hundred People, One Landlord

Merrill and Ring got its start in 1886, a Michigan timber partnership looking west after the Great Lakes forests thinned out. The company bought up homesteads in the Pysht Valley, set logging camps in behind the beach, and built a town around the port that shipped the logs out.

At its peak Pysht had roughly five hundred residents, a post office, a company store, a schoolhouse, a hospital, an electric plant, and a movie hall - a full municipal kit, none of it municipal. A company town meant your house, your doctor, your groceries, and your Saturday night all arrived from the same ledger. In 1920 a county road finally reached the place, the route that became State Route 112, and the isolation eased a little. Merrill and Ring is still in business on the Olympic Peninsula, more than a century and a quarter after it started buying Pysht Valley ground.

## What Was Taken While They Were Away

The Klallam village did not fade out. It was destroyed. In the 1930s, loggers who wanted the site for a lumber mill tore the village down while its residents were away working elsewhere - seasonal work, the kind of absence that had never before cost anyone their home. The people came back to find nothing to come back to.

There is no monument at the spot, and the account survives largely because Klallam families kept telling it. The Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe still carries the story, and a 1992 account recorded how the demolition happened. It is worth sitting with the arithmetic: a village occupied for generations, removed in the time it takes to clear a building lot, by people who did not think they needed to ask. The Klallam remain on this coast - at Lower Elwha, at Jamestown, at Port Gamble - and the name of the river is still theirs.

## The Road Along the Strait

State Route 112 is one of the great underrated drives in Washington, a two-lane ribbon that traces the southern shore of the strait from Port Angeles out toward Neah Bay. It runs close enough to the water in places that you can watch freighters inbound for Puget Sound while keeping one eye on the centre line.

Pysht sits about midway along it. Pillar Point County Park, just east, offers the pull-out where most people finally stop: gravel beach, driftwood, and Vancouver Island lying across fifteen miles of open water. On a clear evening the light comes low down the strait and turns the whole channel to hammered metal. The logging still goes on in the hills behind, quieter than it was, and the town of five hundred has shrunk back to a scatter of buildings and a signpost with a name most drivers read twice.

## Flight Context

Pysht lies at 48.196N, 124.116W on the south shore of the Strait of Juan de Fuca, roughly 35 nm west of Port Angeles. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet the river mouth is easy to pick out: a break in the timbered shoreline with State Route 112 threading past it, and the blunt headland of Pillar Point immediately to the east. Vancouver Island fills the horizon to the north across about 15 nm of water, with the Olympic foothills rising to the south. Nearest airfields are William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) at Port Angeles to the east, Sekiu (10S) to the west, and Quillayute (KUIL) on the outer coast to the southwest; Victoria International (CYYJ) sits across the strait. Expect strait-channelled winds, frequent low marine cloud, and fog that can fill the shoreline while the ridges above stay clear.
