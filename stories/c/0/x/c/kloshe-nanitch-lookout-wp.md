# Kloshe Nanitch Lookout

A ridge on the Olympic Peninsula whose name is an instruction in Chinook Jargon - keep a good lookout - and which has now outlived two buildings that tried to obey it.

**Location:** 48.0809, -124.0790

**Categories:** Fire Lookouts, Olympic Peninsula, Washington, Forest History, Hiking, Viewpoints

## Introduction

The name is not a description. It is an order. Kloshe Nanitch comes from Chinook Jargon, the trade language that once let Pacific Northwest nations, French-Canadian voyageurs, and English-speaking newcomers do business with each other, and it means roughly keep a good lookout, or stand watch. Someone climbed this ridge above the Sol Duc Valley, decided what it was for, and named it with a verb. Two buildings have since stood here and both are gone. The ridge, unhelpfully, is still watching.

## What the 1907 Fire Taught

To understand why anyone hauled lumber up Snider Ridge, look at what burned. In 1907, fire tore through the Sol Duc country and left a scar so large it got a name of its own - the Solduc Burn - miles of standing dead timber where dense forest had been. A decade later the Forest Service put its first structure on Kloshe Nanitch, right in the heart of that burn: a D-6 wooden cupola groundhouse, raised in 1917 on a low mortared rock foundation. The logic was blunt. If a fire could do that once, someone had better be up here with a pair of eyes and a map. The first fire reported from this site went out over the wire in 1918, which means the building justified itself in roughly a year.

## Twelve Feet Square

The D-6 was a small thing to stake a forest on. Its lower story measured twelve feet by twelve - a single balloon-framed room that served as kitchen, bedroom, office, and refuge - with a cupola only six feet square perched on top, windowed on all sides. That cupola was the actual job. A lookout climbed into it, sat with a fire finder, and spent the dry months reading the horizon for the one wrong thing: a smudge of grey that wasn't cloud, a column rising where no column should be. Summers meant heat trapped under a wooden roof and lightning that arrived at eye level. Winters meant leaving. Whoever lived here carried water, carried food, and carried down whatever they had failed to see coming.

## Two Buildings, Both Gone

Aircraft, radio, and eventually satellite imagery made the human cupola obsolete, and the Kloshe Nanitch lookout was abandoned and destroyed in the early 1960s along with hundreds of others across the West. Then, in the 1990s, someone decided the ridge shouldn't be blank. A replica went up - a tribute rather than a working station - and it did not last. It was never strongly built, by most accounts, and it drew the particular attention that a remote unguarded structure attracts. Vandalism accumulated. Repair money did not. In October 2012, the Forest Service took the replica down rather than keep patching a building nobody could defend. The site remains open, maintained now as what it always essentially was: a place to stand and look.

## The View That Outlasted Both

What's left is the reason for everything else. From the ridge the Sol Duc Valley opens below, Lake Crescent's long glacial trench lies east, Lake Pleasant sits north toward the lowlands, and on a good day Mount Olympus itself shows its snow to the south. When the marine layer stays offshore, the view runs all the way to the Pacific and the sea stacks off James Island. The Forest Service keeps picnic tables, a vault toilet, and garbage service here, and pointedly no water - bring your own. You can drive most of the way on Forest Road 3040 out of the Snider Work Center, or earn it on the Kloshe Nanitch Trail, about 6.4 miles round trip and 2,200 feet of climbing through Douglas-fir and thick undergrowth to a high point near 3,160 feet. The last stretch crosses a meadow just beneath where the building used to be. There is nothing to go inside. That turns out not to matter much.

## Flight Context

Kloshe Nanitch sits at 48.0809°N, 124.0790°W on Snider Ridge, in the northwest quadrant of the Olympic Peninsula inside Olympic National Forest. The navigation cues are unmistakable from the air: Lake Crescent's long, deep blue trench runs east-southeast just a few miles away, the Sol Duc River valley threads west below the ridge, and US 101 traces the corridor between them. Mount Olympus and the glaciated Olympic core fill the horizon to the south; the Strait of Juan de Fuca and Vancouver Island lie north. Recommended viewing altitude is 4,500-6,000 ft MSL to hold the ridge, Lake Crescent, and the Sol Duc drainage in one frame, or 9,000-10,000 ft to set the whole peninsula against the Pacific. William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) at Port Angeles is roughly 30 nm east; Quillayute (KUIL) near Forks is about 20 nm southwest. Expect low stratus and valley fog on summer mornings, heavy rain and ridge-obscuring cloud from October through spring, and the clearest air in late summer - the same conditions that once made the fire season worth watching.
