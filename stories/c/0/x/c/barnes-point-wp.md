# Barnes Point

A landslide delta on Lake Crescent that became a trailhead, a plant nursery, and a refuge for a Dutch resistance fighter the war had nearly killed.

**Location:** 48.0611, -123.7960

**Categories:** olympic-national-park, lakes, geology, washington-state, local-history

## Introduction

In 1947 a young Dutchman arrived at a flat spit of land on the south shore of Lake Crescent carrying a body the war had nearly ruined. Emil deWilde had served in the Dutch underground; the Nazis had captured him, tortured him, and starved him. There was no treatment for what that had done to him, only a place quiet enough to let him mend. His father had bought ten acres at Barnes Point five years earlier. The quiet there is close to absolute. Lake Crescent fills a glacier-gouged trough in the northern Olympics, 624 feet deep by official survey and rumored deeper still, and its shoreline rises so steeply that level ground is scarce. Barnes Point is one of the few exceptions: an ancient landslide delta shoved out into water so clear that the drop-off looks like the edge of something.

## The Mountain That Came Down

Barnes Point exists because the slope above it did not stay put. Roughly eight thousand years ago a massive slide off the Olympic ridges dammed this valley, splitting one long body of water into two - Lake Crescent to the west, Lake Sutherland to the east - and cutting the lake off from its outlet to the sea. The mountain was not finished. Around 1100 BCE an earthquake shook loose the Sledgehammer Point rockslide, some 7.2 million cubic meters of Mount Storm King, into water at least 140 meters deep. The wave it raised ran up the opposite shore an estimated 82 to 104 meters. None of this announces itself today. Barnes Creek slides out of the forest and into the lake, gravel sorted and glinting in the shallows, while the peak responsible for the catastrophe simply looms above the trailhead, dark and near-vertical.

## A Trout That Never Left

Sealed off from the Strait of Juan de Fuca, the lake's fish evolved on their own terms. The Crescent cutthroat trout lives nowhere else on Earth, and Barnes Creek is where it spawns - a cold, gravel-bedded stream running down through old-growth forest. Three of the most-walked routes in this corner of Olympic National Park start from the flat ground here. One follows the creek a gentle mile to Marymere Falls, ninety feet of water dropping into a mossy bowl. Another turns uphill and climbs Mount Storm King through switchbacks that eventually give out entirely, leaving hikers to haul themselves up a rope-assisted scramble along a knife-edge ridge. The third simply follows Barnes Creek into the trees and keeps going, deeper into forest that was never cut.

## Ten Acres, Thirteen People

The ten acres between Rosemary Inn and Lake Crescent Lodge belonged until 1940 to Thomas Aldwell, the Port Angeles promoter who dammed the Elwha River and lit the peninsula with its first hydroelectric power. There was a main house on the property, three guest cabins, a work shed, and a horse barn. In 1942 it passed to James C. deWilde, a retired Dutch banker lately of Shanghai, whose wife's father had served as a colonel of the Bengal Lancers in India. deWilde spent the war years at Barnes Point as the local blackout warden, watching a dark lake for an enemy that never came, and wrote two books on the resurgence of Islam in Asia. He was not the only author on the point. Margaret Merrill wrote her small classic "Bears in My Kitchen" here; her husband Bill, a park ranger, published a guide to fishing and camping and was the first ranger here to wear a sidearm. Indigenous residents nicknamed him Two Gun.

## Nursery, Then Nothing

Emil deWilde and his new wife came over from the Netherlands in 1947, and he and his father started the Lake Crescent Nursery on the site - a business built out of patience, which was the one thing the place had in surplus. By 1949 thirteen people were living on those ten acres: two families, field hands up from Oklahoma, a nanny from Scotland. It was a household improvised out of the century's dislocations, planted on a delta at the foot of a mountain that occasionally falls into the lake. In 1953 the Park Service bought the property. The main house and the barn came down. Two of the cabins were floated whole across the water to Log Cabin Resort, where guests still sleep in them. What remains at Barnes Point is the lodge, the Storm King Ranger Station, a handful of private homes, and the name, left behind by Paul Barnes, who settled the point in 1890.

## Flight Context

Barnes Point lies at 48.061 degrees north, 123.796 degrees west, on the south shore of Lake Crescent in Washington's Olympic Peninsula, roughly 17 miles west of Port Angeles. A viewing altitude of 4,000 to 6,000 feet holds the full crescent of the lake in frame while keeping Mount Storm King, which rises past 4,500 feet immediately southeast of the point, safely below and to the side. Navigate by the lake itself: it is an unmistakable eight-mile arc of deep blue in a dark forested trough, with US 101 tracing its southern shore. The delta reads as the one pale, flat interruption in an otherwise sheer shoreline. Nearest fields are William R. Fairchild International at Port Angeles (KCLM) to the east and Sekiu (KNHW area traffic) to the west; Victoria International (CYYJ) sits across the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the north. Expect low marine cloud filling the trough on summer mornings and burning off by midday; the surrounding ridges make this poor terrain for low flight in reduced visibility.
