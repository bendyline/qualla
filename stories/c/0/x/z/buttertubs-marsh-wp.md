# Buttertubs Marsh

A rebuilt wetland in the middle of Nanaimo that is the only documented breeding site for American bitterns on all of Vancouver Island.

**Location:** 49.1708, -123.9730

**Categories:** Nanaimo, Bird sanctuaries of Canada, Important Bird Areas of British Columbia, Wetlands, Wildlife

## Introduction

You will almost certainly not see the bird that makes this place matter. The American bittern is a heron that has given up on being seen: vertically striped in buff and brown, it stands among the cattails with its bill pointed straight at the sky and sways gently when the reeds sway, and a person can look directly at one from ten metres away and register nothing but marsh. What you might hear, on a spring evening, is the call - a deep, glottal, almost plumbing-adjacent pumping sound that carries across water. Buttertubs Marsh, forty hectares of reeds and open water in the middle of the city of Nanaimo, is the only documented breeding site for American bitterns anywhere on Vancouver Island.

## A Lake, Undone and Remade

Before the mid-1800s this was a shallow lake. European settlers drained it, as settlers drained wetlands everywhere, to get at what lay beneath - and what lay beneath was good bottomland for dairy cattle and potatoes. For more than a century the water was the enemy of the enterprise. Then the calculation reversed. The marsh that exists today is human-made, a deliberate re-wetting of ground that had been deliberately dried, and it is worth sitting with that for a moment: almost nothing you see from the observation deck is original. The cattails, the open water, the herons standing in the shallows - all of it is the second draft of a landscape whose first draft was erased on purpose.

## Forty Hectares, Surrounded

The marsh covers roughly 100 acres, about 40 hectares, bordered by the Millstone River and hemmed in on every side by Nanaimo - subdivisions, roads, the ordinary sprawl of a mid-sized coastal city. That containment is exactly what gives the place its weight. Birds moving down the Pacific coast need somewhere to stop, and on an island where lowland wetland has been drained, filled and built over with remarkable thoroughness, the options are few. Buttertubs functions as breeding ground, wintering ground and rest stop all at once, a single green comma in a sentence of pavement. Within the larger marsh sits the 46-acre Buttertubs Marsh Conservation Area, owned by The Nature Trust of British Columbia, which bought its first piece of the ground in 1970.

## The Regulars

Great blue herons work the margins with the stiff, deliberate patience of birds that have all afternoon. Wood ducks - the drake is arguably the most absurdly decorated waterfowl in North America, painted in green, chestnut, white and red as though by committee - nest in cavities near the water. Belted kingfishers rattle across the open channels and stop hard on a branch. Mergansers dive. Red-winged blackbirds claim cattail stems and shout about it from March onward, and common yellowthroats, small and masked like tiny bandits, work the low vegetation. None of this is rare. All of it is the sound and motion of a wetland functioning, five minutes from a shopping centre.

## Turtles, Native and Otherwise

The marsh's reptiles tell a more complicated story. Western painted turtles bask on logs here - a species listed as endangered, its coastal populations squeezed by exactly the kind of wetland loss that created this place's need to exist. Sharing those logs are red-eared sliders, which are not from here at all. They arrived the way red-eared sliders arrive everywhere: bought small and green in pet shops, outgrowing their tanks, released by owners who meant well. They compete with the painted turtles for basking sites and food, and they are now a permanent feature of the marsh. Two turtles on the same log, one native and imperilled, one introduced and thriving, is as neat a summary of North American wetland ecology as you are likely to find.

## Made Permanent

In 2012 the Buttertubs West Marsh was purchased by Ducks Unlimited Canada together with the City of Nanaimo. Because the acquisition went through Canada's ecological gift program, the land cannot be developed or significantly altered - a legal permanence that ordinary parkland does not enjoy. Since 2013, Vancouver Island University has run an avian monitoring program on the marsh, which means the birds here are not just present but counted, banded and tracked across years. Meanwhile a trail loops the whole property, with observation decks positioned where the sightlines are good. Walk it slowly at dusk in April. Stop at the reeds. Listen for something that sounds like a bad pipe, and look for the bird that is standing perfectly still, pretending to be a cattail.

## Flight Context

Buttertubs Marsh lies at 49.1708 N, 123.973 W, inland from Nanaimo Harbour on the east coast of Vancouver Island. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500-3,000 ft AGL; the marsh is small and reads as a dark, irregular patch of open water and reedbed set inside residential Nanaimo, with the thread of the Millstone River running along its edge. Useful nearby landmarks: Nanaimo Harbour and the BC Ferries terminal at Departure Bay to the northeast, the wooded bulk of Newcastle Island and Protection Island just offshore, and Mount Benson rising to the west. Nearest airports are CYCD (Nanaimo Airport, at Cassidy, about 15 km south), CAC8 (Nanaimo Harbour Water Aerodrome, a busy floatplane base serving Vancouver), and CYQQ (Comox) further north. Low cloud and drizzle are common from October through March; clear mornings in late summer give the best contrast between water and surrounding city.
