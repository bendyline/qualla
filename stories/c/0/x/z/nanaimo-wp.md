# Nanaimo

Vancouver Island's Harbour City, built on Snuneymuxw ground and a century of coal, where the most famous mayor raced bathtubs dressed as a pirate.

**Location:** 49.1642, -123.9364

**Categories:** Cities, British Columbia, Mining History, Indigenous History, Maritime History, Food and Drink

## Introduction

There is a statue on the Nanaimo waterfront, at Swy-a-Lana Lagoon, of a man in a pirate costume. He is not a fictional character. He is Frank J. Ney, who served as mayor of this city, sat as a member of the provincial legislature, and dressed as a buccaneer more or less constantly in public because he had decided the town should be known for racing bathtubs across the Strait of Georgia. It worked. But the pirate is the recent layer. Underneath him lies a far older story about coal, water, and a nation that was here first.

## The Great People

Nanaimo sits on the traditional territory of the Snuneymuxw First Nation, a Coast Salish people whose name is usually rendered as "the great people" or "gathering place of a great people." The city's own name descends from theirs. This stretch of the island's eastern shore made sense as a gathering place: sheltered harbour, protected waters behind Newcastle and Protection islands, salmon runs, and a mild lee-side climate quite unlike the drenched Pacific coast on the other side of the mountains. In 1849 a Snuneymuxw leader told the Hudson's Bay Company about the black rock that outcropped nearby and burned. It was an act of trade and diplomacy. It also set in motion everything that followed, and much of what followed came at the Snuneymuxw's expense - land taken, villages displaced, an economy rearranged around somebody else's furnaces.

## The Octagon by the Water

The Hudson's Bay Company moved on the coal seams in 1852, and by 1853 a settlement existed here along with the Bastion, an octagonal wooden tower built to guard the company's miners and stores. It still stands downtown, small and improbable among modern buildings, a reminder of how provisional the whole enterprise once was. Coal made the town. Shafts ran out beneath the harbour and out under the seabed itself, and for the better part of a century Nanaimo's identity was measured in tons brought up and shipped out. Mark Bate became the first mayor in 1875 and then, apparently finding the work agreeable, served fifteen more one-year terms spread across the next quarter century.

## May 3, 1887

On the afternoon of May 3, 1887, an explosion tore through the Number One Coal Mine beneath Nanaimo. Explosives had been laid improperly. The blast and the fires killed some men outright, but a great many more survived the initial explosion and then died over the following hours from the poisonous gas that filled the workings. The death toll is now counted at about 150 - one of the worst mining disasters in Canadian history. Fifty-three of the dead were Chinese workers. In the official inquest and the Minister of Mines' annual report they were not recorded by name at all, only as "Chinamen, names unknown," followed by a payroll number. They had families. They had sent money home. The record of who they were survives, when it survives, mostly through the patient work of descendants and historians who went looking a century later.

## Coal Out, Pulp In

The mines eventually gave out, and forestry took over. In 1958 MacMillan Bloedel opened a pulp mill at Harmac, south of the city, named for Harvey MacMillan, and by the early 1960s timber had displaced coal as the engine of the local economy. The mill is still running, now owned by its own employees and local investors, and it still pushes well over half a million dollars a day into the surrounding area. It also, several times a year, makes the entire region smell distinctly of sulfur - a fact residents state with the resigned precision of people who have had the conversation before. These days the provincial government is the largest single employer, with service, retail, tourism, and a growing technology sector filling in around it. Vancouver Island University's main campus sits above the harbour, drawing students from across the Pacific.

## Bathtubs, Custard, and a Fast Boat

Nanaimo has roughly 99,900 residents and an unusual number of things it can claim outright. The Nanaimo bar - a no-bake square of crumb base, custard filling, and chocolate top - is named for the city and has become a national dessert. The Pacific Biological Station on Departure Bay, established in 1908, is the oldest fisheries research centre on Canada's Pacific coast. The Nanaimo Hornets rugby club dates to 1888, making it the second-oldest in Western Canada. North America's first legal, purpose-built bungee jumping bridge is here. And Ney's bathtub race still runs each summer as part of the Marine Festival, out of the downtown harbour, around Entrance Island, up to the Winchelsea Islands and back into Departure Bay. Since 2023 a high-speed passenger ferry has crossed to downtown Vancouver in seventy-five minutes, which makes the city, for the first time, a genuinely plausible commute.

## Flight Context

Nanaimo lies at 49.1642°N, 123.9364°W on the east coast of Vancouver Island, facing the Strait of Georgia. From the air the harbour is easy to identify: a sheltered basin screened by Newcastle and Protection islands, with Gabriola Island forming the long outer wall to the east and Departure Bay opening to the north. The Nanaimo Parkway (Highway 19) traces a clear arc around the western edge of the built-up area, and three BC Ferries terminals - Departure Bay, Duke Point, and downtown - mark the shoreline. Recommended viewing altitude is 3,500-5,000 ft MSL for the harbour and island screen, or 8,000-10,000 ft to take in the whole strait with the Coast Mountains of the mainland rising beyond. Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) sits about 10 nm south of downtown; Nanaimo Harbour Water Aerodrome handles floatplanes right off the waterfront. Vancouver International (CYVR) is roughly 30 nm east across the strait, Victoria International (CYYJ) about 50 nm southeast. This is the island's dry side, so conditions are generally better than on the Pacific coast, but expect low cloud and marine layer in winter and occasional smoke haze in late summer.
