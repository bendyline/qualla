# Wellington, British Columbia

A fishing trip at Diver Lake in 1869 turned into the richest coal seam on Vancouver Island, a company empire, and one of Canada's worst mine disasters.

**Location:** 49.2142, -124.0400

**Categories:** Ghost towns in British Columbia, Mining communities in British Columbia, Neighbourhoods in Nanaimo, Populated places in the Regional District of Nanaimo, Coal mining disasters, Labour history of Canada

## Introduction

Robert Dunsmuir went fishing at Diver Lake in 1869 and came back a coal baron. He was a former Hudson's Bay Company man, working in a district where the good coal was supposed to already be spoken for, and while he was out at the lake about eight kilometres northwest of the Nanaimo mines, he saw evidence of a seam. What made the find extraordinary was not just its quality, which turned out to be the highest grade on the island. It was the location. The deposit lay just past the northern boundary of the Vancouver Coal Mining and Land Company's property. Dunsmuir named it the Wellington seam, and for the next half century the town that grew on top of it produced enormous wealth, a castle in Victoria, a private railway, and a body count that the province has never entirely reckoned with.

## Just Past the Property Line

This was Sneneymuxw and Nanoose territory long before anyone sank a shaft into it. Europeans came late and came for one reason. The Hudson's Bay Company opened the Douglas Mine around 1860 in what is now downtown Nanaimo, the first coal mine in the region, and sold its holdings to the Vancouver Coal Mining and Land Company in 1862. That corporate boundary is why Wellington exists. In 1872, Dunsmuir and his partners secured a Crown grant of 1,032 acres covering their lease, pushing the northern edge out far enough to take in the north shore of Long Lake. They formed Dunsmuir, Diggle & Company and began mining west of Diver Lake. Dunsmuir bought out two partners around 1879 and the last one, Diggle, in 1883. From then on the Wellington Collieries were his alone.

## Troops in the Coal Towns

Labour trouble arrived almost immediately. In 1877, disputes between strikers and strikebreakers turned violent, and the British Columbia government responded by declaring martial law and sending in troops to restore order, an early and unsubtle demonstration of whose side the state was on. The following year, sabotage on a company tramway caused the death of a Chinese employee, killed in a manner that the records describe as horrific and do not bother to name him for. That omission is itself part of the story. Chinese workers were the cheapest labour in the colliery and the most expendable in its accounting, hired in large numbers, paid a fraction of white wages, and blamed by white miners for the wage pressure the company itself had engineered. In 1879, eleven miners died in an explosion of coal dust, or from breathing what the blast left behind.

## The Morning of January 24, 1888

At roughly half past eight on a January morning, methane ignited in No. 5 pit. One hundred and sixty-eight men were underground. Seventy-seven of them did not come out. About two thirds of the dead were Chinese, men whose names in many cases never made it into the official lists in any recoverable form, though they had families in Guangdong counting on the remittances and families in Nanaimo waiting at the pithead. Only seven men caught in the blast escaped, among them the foreman Richard Gibson, who was also the mayor and who found his way out through an airshaft into the mule stables. Gas and wreckage made the recovery work nearly impossible. It was May before the last bodies were brought up. Four months is a long time to wait to bury someone.

## Coal, Rails, and a Castle

Getting the coal to market built the transport network that still shapes north Nanaimo. A wagon road gave way to a horse-drawn wooden tramway by 1873, replaced the next year by a two-foot narrow gauge line running 4.6 miles to the company wharf at Departure Bay, where Dunsmuir's own steamers loaded for San Francisco. The grade was awkward enough that the engineers had to build a long loop into the line simply to lose elevation at a survivable rate. Dunsmuir went further and built the Esquimalt and Nanaimo Railway, which opened between Victoria and Nanaimo in 1886 and reached Diver Lake the following year. The profits from all of this went south to Victoria and rose again as Craigdarroch Castle, a Highland-style mansion Dunsmuir did not live to occupy. He died in 1889, a year after the explosion.

## A Neighbourhood on Top of a Ghost Town

Wellington is now simply part of north Nanaimo, a residential district along Jingle Pot Road where the original town grew north of Diver Lake. The first Wellington school opened in 1875, and the school district's Island ConnectEd buildings stand on that same property today. The census tract that covers most of the neighbourhood shows a younger population than the city around it, with a median age of 37.1 against Nanaimo's 43.3, and more children under fifteen. It reads like any other suburb on the island's east coast. The coal is still down there, along with the workings, and the ground occasionally remembers what it used to be.

## Flight Context

Wellington sits at 49.214N, 124.040W, in north Nanaimo on the east coast of southern Vancouver Island. From the air the orienting features are the chain of small lakes the story turns on, Diver Lake and Long Lake, sitting just inland of the built-up area, with Brannen Lake to the west. Departure Bay, the old colliery's shipping point and today the BC Ferries terminal for the Horseshoe Bay run, is about five kilometres east on the coast. The Strait of Georgia is beyond it, with the mainland Coast Mountains rising on the far side in clear weather. Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) lies about 20 kilometres south, and Nanaimo Harbour Water Aerodrome (CAC8) sits downtown. Victoria International (CYYJ) is roughly 90 kilometres southeast. Best viewed at 2,500 to 5,000 feet AGL. This part of the island is in a partial rain shadow, so ceilings here are often better than on the west coast of the island.
