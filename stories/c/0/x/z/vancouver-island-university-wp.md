# Vancouver Island University

A Nanaimo university that heats its buildings with water from the flooded coal mine beneath it, began in a converted hospital, and eventually let go of the name of an explorer who never came here.

**Location:** 49.1573, -123.9664

**Categories:** universities, vancouver-island, british-columbia, architecture-and-engineering, education

## Introduction

Underneath the Nanaimo campus of Vancouver Island University there is a coal mine full of water. The Wakesiah mine worked from 1918 to 1930, and when the last crew came up the shafts filled and stayed filled, leaving a body of brackish water sitting at a constant eleven degrees Celsius year-round, indifferent to the weather ninety feet above it. In 2018 the university started pumping it. A district geo-exchange system - the first of its kind in Canada - now draws on that flooded mine to heat and cool roughly a fifth of the buildings on campus. It is an open loop, so no groundwater is consumed; the only energy required is the electricity to run the pumps, and on this coast that comes from hydro. A century of extraction left a hole in the ground. The hole turned out to be worth more full than empty.

## It Started in a Hospital

Malaspina College enrolled its first students in September 1969, in the old Nanaimo Hospital building at 388 Machleary Street. Planners had expected a modest intake. More than six hundred people registered, nearly double the projection, and the college spent seven years crowded into wards and corridors built for patients. In 1971 it merged its administration with the Nanaimo Vocational Training School, which had been teaching trades since 1936, and in 1976 it finally moved onto a new campus on Fifth Street - former Department of National Defence land next door to that same vocational school. The site remains the main campus today: ninety-two acres and more than a million square feet of built space, stacked up a hillside above the harbour.

## Four Names in Forty Years

The institution changed what it was called almost as often as it changed what it did. A 1988 provincial initiative aimed at widening access to degrees gave five British Columbia community colleges - Malaspina, Fraser Valley, Kwantlen, Cariboo, and Okanagan - the authority to offer bachelor's degrees and the new label of university college; at first they granted those degrees through one of the three established provincial universities. In 1995 the province renamed the school Malaspina University-College and let it confer degrees in its own right. Its coat of arms was registered with the Canadian Heraldic Authority that May. Thirteen years later, on 1 September 2008, an amendment to the University Act made it a full university, and it opened that morning as Vancouver Island University.

## The Explorer Who Never Arrived

Alessandro Malaspina was an Italian-born officer in the Spanish navy who led one of the great scientific voyages of the eighteenth century, a five-year circuit of the Pacific between 1789 and 1794. He reached Nootka Sound on the far side of Vancouver Island in August 1791 and stayed about a month. He never came anywhere near Nanaimo. His name attached to this side of the island only because, in 1792, he sent the schooners Sutil and Mexicana under Dionisio Alcala Galiano and Cayetano Valdes to chart the Strait of Juan de Fuca and the Strait of Georgia. So for thirty-nine years a college on the Salish Sea carried the name of a man who got no closer than Yuquot. When the name went in 2008, the institution took the island's instead.

## Gardens, Firsts, and Fourteen Names

VIU's first chancellor was Chief Shawn A-in-chut Atleo, who in 2009 became national chief of the Assembly of First Nations; his appointment made him the first Indigenous person to hold a chancellorship in British Columbia. Deborah Saucier, appointed president in 2018, was the university's first woman and first Metis president, and resigned in 2025. The campus reflects some of this in built form. Shq'apthut: A Gathering Place houses the Elders-in-Residence and the Office of Indigenous Education and Engagement. The Kwulasulwut Garden honours the Coast Salish elder Ellen White, a retired Elder-in-Residence. The university offers a degree in Indigenous/Xwulmuxw Studies developed with First Nations of the island and coastal British Columbia. And in a quieter corner grows the Jardin des quatorze, the Garden of the Fourteen, planted for the fourteen women - engineering students, most of them - who were murdered at the Ecole Polytechnique in Montreal in 1989 for the offence of studying engineering while female.

## Flight Context

The Nanaimo campus sits at 49.157 degrees north, 123.966 degrees west, on a hillside about two kilometres southwest of downtown Nanaimo on the east coast of Vancouver Island. A viewing altitude of 2,500 to 4,000 feet takes in the campus, Nanaimo Harbour, Newcastle and Protection Islands, and the ferry lanes running east across the Strait of Georgia to the mainland. Navigate by the harbour: Nanaimo is the obvious urban notch on the island's east shore, with Departure Bay to the north and the long green wedge of Gabriola Island lying offshore. Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) is about 16 kilometres south near Cassidy; Nanaimo Harbour Water Aerodrome (CAC8) handles floatplanes downtown; Victoria International (CYYJ) is 100 kilometres southeast and Vancouver International (CYVR) 55 kilometres east across the strait. Expect winter stratus and rain, summer afternoon sea breeze, and heavy floatplane and helicopter traffic over the harbour at commuter hours.
