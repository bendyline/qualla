# Snake Island (Nanaimo)

An uninhabited rock northeast of Nanaimo where nesting seabirds, a colony of unusually sociable harbour seals, and the largest artificial reef in the Pacific Northwest all share the same square kilometre of water.

**Location:** 49.2158, -123.8908

**Categories:** Uninhabited islands of British Columbia, Regional District of Nanaimo, Shipwrecks and diving, Seabird colonies

## Introduction

At low tide, a shape surfaces in the rocks along the shore — a blunt head, a suggestion of jaw, the long curve of a body running back into the water. There are no snakes on Snake Island. There is only this trick of stone and tideline, exposed twice a day and covered again, which is the likeliest explanation for how a bare rock northeast of Nanaimo got its name. Boaters pass it constantly on the run between Departure Bay and the Strait of Georgia and think nothing of it. What happens around that unremarkable rock is considerably stranger than what happens on it.

## A Minor Member of a Famous Group

Snake Island belongs, technically, to the Gulf Islands — the scattered archipelago east of southern Vancouver Island that includes Salt Spring, Galiano, Mayne and the rest of the well-loved names. It is not one of them in any practical sense. Nobody lives here. There is no dock, no cabin, no trail, and very little soil. The island is a low sandstone and conglomerate outcrop, wave-scoured and salt-burned, ringed by reefs that drop away steeply into cold green water. Its whole identity comes from what it is not: not developed, not visited on foot, not part of anyone's commute. In a strait crowded with ferries, log booms, seaplanes and weekend traffic, Snake Island is the rare piece of the Gulf Islands that people mostly leave alone — which turns out to matter a great deal to the animals that use it.

## The Nesting Rock

Because nobody lands here, birds nest here. Snake Island is a sanctuary for glaucous-winged gulls and pelagic cormorants, and it carries formal recognition as an Important Bird Area. The gulls take the flat ground, a sprawling and vocal colony that fills the air with noise through the breeding season. The cormorants take the ledges — dark, slender, prehistoric-looking birds that build on impossibly narrow shelves above the surf and stand with wings spread to dry, since their feathers are not fully waterproof. From a boat the colony reads as motion more than detail: white bodies wheeling over the high ground, black ones stitched into the cliff face below. Wildlife tour operators out of Nanaimo make the island a regular stop for exactly this reason. You stay offshore. The birds stay put.

## Seals That Know the Rules

The harbour seals hauled out on the surrounding rocks have made a different arrangement with people. Seals elsewhere on this coast slide off into the water when a boat gets close and resurface at a careful distance. The Snake Island colony has been visited by divers for so long, and so consistently, that its members have stopped treating them as a threat. Divers in the water here describe seals approaching deliberately — circling, hovering just out of reach, occasionally tugging at fins or nosing a camera dome before wheeling away. It is not tameness exactly. It is a wild animal's considered judgment that this particular intrusion is interesting rather than dangerous, and it makes for an encounter most divers remember longer than anything they see on the reef itself.

## Six Decks Down

The reef offers competition, though. Just off the island lies HMCS Cape Breton, a Second World War Victory ship built in 1944 for the Royal Navy as HMS Flamborough Head, transferred to the Royal Canadian Navy in 1952, and worked hard as a fleet repair and escort maintenance ship until she was paid off into reserve in 1964. She spent her retirement as a towed support and accommodation vessel at Esquimalt, a role that lasted until a shore building replaced her in 1993, and might have gone quietly to the scrappers. Instead the Artificial Reef Society of British Columbia acquired her, stripped every hazardous material to meet Environment Canada standards, cut access openings through the hull and mapped safe routes through her interior for divers. On October 20, 2001, she was sunk here. At 134 metres long and standing six decks high, the Cape Breton is the largest artificial reef in the Pacific Northwest, and she settled upright roughly a hundred metres off the bow of HMCS Saskatchewan, already on the bottom — a paired-wreck dive site with few equals in Canada. Her stern section and engines were spared and went to the Maritime Museum in North Vancouver, so a piece of her never went under at all.

## Flight Context

Snake Island sits at 49.22N, 123.89W in the Strait of Georgia, roughly 2nm northeast of Nanaimo on Vancouver Island's east coast. From the air it is a small, pale, treeless rock with a surrounding apron of shallow reef that shows bright green against the darker deep water — easy to pick out against the Nanaimo waterfront and Departure Bay to the southwest. Newcastle Island and Protection Island lie between it and the harbour, and the Winchelsea Islands lie north. Nearest airports: Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) about 12nm south, Qualicum Beach (CAT4) about 20nm northwest, and Nanaimo Harbour Water Aerodrome (CAC8) 2nm southwest, with heavy seaplane traffic on the Vancouver run crossing this water all day. Keep above 2,000 feet to avoid disturbing the nesting colonies and to stay clear of the harbour circuit. Morning light off the strait is the best time to see the reef structure and the wreck's outline in clear water.
