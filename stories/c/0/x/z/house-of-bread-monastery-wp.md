# House of Bread Monastery

A Benedictine community in Nanaimo took its name from the Hebrew words behind Bethlehem, and built a life around quiet, bread, and the island around it.

**Location:** 49.1646, -124.0047

**Categories:** monasteries, religious-communities, vancouver-island, british-columbia, catholic-heritage

## Introduction

In Hebrew, beth lehem means house of bread. A town in Judea carried that name for centuries before anything happened there worth writing down. When a small community of Benedictine women settled in Nanaimo, on the sheltered eastern shore of Vancouver Island, they reached for those same two words in English and meant them without much metaphorical hedging. Bread, in their reading of the Rule of St. Benedict, is Scripture and Eucharist and also simply what you set on the table when somebody knocks. The monastery they founded is not large or old or architecturally famous. It is a working answer to a question a bishop asked in 1972.

## The Bishop's Word Was Return

In 1972, Bishop Remi J. De Roo of the Diocese of Victoria went before the General Chapter of the Federation of St. Gertrude with a request. He asked that the Benedictines return to Vancouver Island, he said, so that their spirituality might be shared with the people in the area. The verb is worth pausing on. De Roo was not asking for something new; he was asking for something back. De Roo had been made a bishop in 1962 at the age of thirty-eight, the youngest in the world at the time, and he sat through all four sessions of the Second Vatican Council; he then spent thirty-seven years in Victoria pressing the Church outward toward ordinary working life. The request fit the pattern exactly. What he wanted on the island was not an institution but a presence -- people living a particular rhythm where others could see it.

## Two Sisters and a Dependent Priory

The answer came from Oregon. Jean Ann Berning and Augusta Raabe, Benedictines of Queen of Angels Monastery at Mount Angel, took up the invitation and established a Benedictine presence in Nanaimo in 1974. For its first two decades the house existed formally as a dependent priory of Mount Angel -- a daughter, not yet a household of its own. Independence came slowly and then all at once. In 1993 the House of Bread Monastery was incorporated into the Federation of St. Gertrude in its own right, and Sister Jean Ann Berning, O.S.B., was elected its first prioress. Nineteen years elapsed between the arrival and the election. In monastic time, that is brisk.

## An Oratory, a Library, and Agreed-Upon Quiet

The community describes its inheritance -- the Rule of St. Benedict filtered through a Swiss Benedictine lineage -- as fundamentally Eucharistic, and the chosen name is meant to say so out loud: to be, as they put it, sacrament persons sharing the bread of Scripture and Eucharistic presence with others. What that looks like day to day is unglamorous and specific. Personal prayer and lectio, the slow meditative reading of Scripture, require an oratory and a library to be possible at all. They also require a shared agreement about which hours and which rooms stay silent, because silence in a house of several people is a negotiated thing rather than a natural one. Corporate prayer anchors the day, and the labor of preparing it rotates. Nobody outsources the liturgy to a specialist.

## Stewardship in an Island Setting

Nanaimo was a coal town before it was a ferry town, and it still has the shape of a working port: docks, log booms, the Strait of Georgia opening east toward the mainland mountains, Newcastle and Protection islands sitting just offshore. The sisters have written their attention to that setting into their own description of themselves, calling the community mindful of its natural environment and of the need to hold itself to good stewardship of what they regard as God's gifts in their island place. It is a modest, almost administrative sentence, and it carries fifteen centuries behind it. Benedictines have historically been the monastics who stayed put -- who took a vow of stability, farmed the same ground, and therefore had to live with what they did to it. On an island, that logic is harder to escape than most places. There is a finite amount of ground, and the sea is always in view.

## Flight Context

Located near 49.16 N, 124.00 W on the eastern side of Vancouver Island at Nanaimo, British Columbia, essentially at sea level. The monastery itself is a small, low-profile property and is not a visual landmark from altitude; navigate instead by Nanaimo Harbour, the distinctive pair of Newcastle and Protection islands enclosing it, and the Departure Bay ferry terminal just to the northwest. The Strait of Georgia runs east toward the Coast Mountains of the mainland. Best viewed at 2,000-3,500 ft AGL, ideally in late-afternoon light when the strait goes metallic. Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) lies about 10 nm south; Nanaimo Harbour Water Aerodrome (CAC8) sits in the harbour itself, and Victoria International (CYYJ) is roughly 55 nm to the southeast. This coast is in the rain shadow of the island's mountains, so conditions are frequently better here than on the west coast, though winter stratus and morning fog off the strait are common.
