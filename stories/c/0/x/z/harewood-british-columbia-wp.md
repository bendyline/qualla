# Harewood, British Columbia

A south Nanaimo neighbourhood still carries the name of an English earl's seventh son, a naval officer who came looking for coal and left a harder legacy on this coast.

**Location:** 49.1333, -123.9500

**Categories:** Ghost towns in British Columbia, Populated places in the Regional District of Nanaimo, Neighbourhoods in Nanaimo

## Introduction

Being the seventh son of an earl means inheriting nothing. Horace Douglas Lascelles was born in Yorkshire in 1835 into the family of the 3rd Earl of Harewood, entered the Royal Navy at thirteen, and by his mid-twenties was commanding a gunboat on the far side of the world. He needed a fortune and he thought Vancouver Island coal would provide one. It did not — but his family name stuck to the ground south of Nanaimo and never came off. Today Harewood is a neighbourhood of houses, a university campus, walking trails along old dam reservoirs, and the ghost of a mining village that the city simply grew over.

## A Fortune in the Chase River Valley

In 1861 Lascelles's friend Dr. Alfred Benson acquired some 3,000 acres in the Chase River Valley on the strength of coal showing at the surface. Lascelles put in money — the initial investment ran to $30,000, serious capital for a colony barely a decade old — and by 1864 the Harewood Coal Mining Company was formally at work on the seams south of Nanaimo. The resident manager was a Scottish miner named Robert Dunsmuir, then unknown, later the richest and most feared industrialist in British Columbia. Harewood was Dunsmuir's apprenticeship in ownership. The mine itself struggled from the start: the coal was awkward to reach, the money ran thin, and the original company had ceased operations by 1869. Lascelles never saw his fortune. The name outlived the venture by a century and a half.

## What the Gunboat Did

Lascelles commanded HMS Forward on the BC coast, and the vessel's work was not only surveying and supply. The colonial administration used gunboats to project force against Indigenous communities, and on 20 April 1863 the Forward shelled the Lamalchi village on Kuper Island — known today as Penelakut Island — in a punitive expedition following the killing of three settlers in the Gulf Islands. A firefight that day killed a British sailor and the ship withdrew; it returned the next day to find the village emptied, and levelled it anyway, burning houses, canoes, and winter stores. The Navy then hunted down the people who had lived there; four were eventually captured and hanged in Victoria. This is the same man, in the same years, whose family name now marks a quiet residential district. Both facts belong to Harewood, and a place is not honestly described with only one of them.

## Absorbed

The mining village persisted in name at least into 1913, a cluster of workers' housing distinct enough to be mapped and referred to on its own terms. Then Nanaimo grew, and the seam between town and village closed. There is no Harewood townsite to visit, no preserved main street, no interpretive plaque marking where the pithead stood. Schools and fire halls carried the name for decades before amalgamating into the City of Nanaimo and losing it from their signage. What remains is the word itself, applied now to a neighbourhood, a street, a lake, and a plain — the sort of survival that happens when a name is convenient enough that nobody bothers to replace it.

## The School on Howard Street

For a hundred and two years there was one solid piece of evidence. Harewood School opened at 505 Howard Street in 1914, and it kept teaching until 2004, by which point it was the oldest school building still standing anywhere in Nanaimo. Closed buildings decay quickly. Its registered historical value could not compete with a leaking roof and a decade of no one inside it, and in the spring of 2016 the crews came and took it down. The detached gymnasium was left standing — an outbuilding that outlived the building it served. It is still there, and it is the last physical thing in Harewood that the mining village's descendants would have recognized.

## Trails, Lectures, and Old Water

Modern Harewood is unexpectedly full. Vancouver Island University climbs the slope here, putting several thousand students in what was once mine housing. The Nanaimo Armoury sits within the area, as does the headquarters of Nanaimo Search and Rescue — the volunteers who go looking for people lost on this island's very unforgiving terrain. And the Colliery Dams trails wind through second-growth forest around reservoirs built to serve the coal industry that gave the district its name. Walk them on a wet morning and the water is black-green and perfectly still under the cedars. The colliery is a century gone. The dams are still holding.

## Flight Context

Harewood occupies the slope south of downtown Nanaimo at approximately 49.133°N, 123.950°W, on the east coast of Vancouver Island. A good look comes at 2,000 to 3,500 feet on a southbound pass down the Strait of Georgia shoreline. Visual anchors are easy: Nanaimo Harbour with Newcastle and Protection islands enclosing it, the Nanaimo River delta to the south, and the Vancouver Island University campus terracing up the hillside inland of the harbour — the Colliery Dam reservoirs show as two small dark ponds in the forest just south of it. Mount Benson rises to 1,019 metres immediately west and is the dominant terrain feature for miles. Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) lies about 10 nautical miles south near Cassidy; Nanaimo Harbour Water Aerodrome (CAC8) handles the floatplane traffic from Vancouver; Victoria International (CYYJ) and Vancouver International (CYVR) are the nearest major fields. Expect frequent low cloud and drizzle from November through March, with summer afternoons often clear and offering long views across the strait to the Coast Mountains on the mainland.
