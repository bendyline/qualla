# Extension, British Columbia

A Vancouver Island coal town born from a Black pioneer's unsolved death, scarred by an explosion that killed thirty-two and a strike that brought in troops, now a quiet Nanaimo bedroom community with a slag heap hidden in the trees.

**Location:** 49.0986, -123.9570

**Categories:** Mining communities in British Columbia, Mid Vancouver Island, Labour history, Black Canadian history, Populated places in the Regional District of Nanaimo

## Introduction

In late February 1895, Louis Stark was found dead at the foot of a cliff near his home in the Cranberry district south of Nanaimo. Stark was a Black rancher, the son of a woman who had been enslaved in the American South, and his family had come north to British Columbia in the great Black migration of 1858 before settling on Salt Spring Island and then here. Beneath his land lay a rich coal seam. Men who wanted to mine it had made offers, and Stark had refused them, reportedly in the face of threats. His neighbour Ephraim Hodgson was arrested for the death, then released for lack of evidence, and shortly afterwards bought the Stark property for $800. He sold it to the coal baron James Dunsmuir, who opened three large mines on it that same year. The town that grew above those mines was called Extension.

## Who Gets the Credit

Even the discovery of the seam is contested. One account holds that Stark himself found the coal on the southern slope of Mount Benson; another says only that he owned the ground it lay under and would not part with it. What is not in doubt is that after his death, Hodgson claimed the credit and collected the reward. Dunsmuir needed the find badly. His Wellington mine to the north was nearly worked out, and the company was hunting for a replacement seam with the urgency of a business that had built everything on one. He got it. The name Extension itself is a company word, describing what the new workings were meant to be: a continuation of the Wellington operation by other means, in other ground, belonging to other people.

## The Town That Refused to Commute

Dunsmuir did not want a town here. He had watched labour militancy take root in Nanaimo and wanted no repeat of it on his own doorstep, so he planned for his miners to live at Oyster Bay and ride a train up to the pithead each morning. Hundreds of them built houses at Extension anyway. The settlement filled in around the works, and by 1900 it had a post office and its present name. Five hotels operated in the early years, the Central, the Extension, the Overton, the Pretoria, and the Queen's, alongside a scatter of stores. Saturday nights meant dances, and special trains ran between Extension and Ladysmith to carry people to whichever hall had the music. For a company town that was never supposed to exist, it developed a remarkably good social calendar.

## Dust and Gas

In 1909 an explosion of coal dust tore through the workings. Thirty-two men did not come out, killed by the blast itself or by the poisonous gases it left behind in the tunnels. Coal dust is the peculiar terror of a mine like this one: fine enough to hang in the air, combustible enough that a single spark converts an entire drift into a pressure wave. The dead were neighbours in a village small enough that everyone knew everyone, and the funerals would have emptied the place. Extension went back underground afterwards, because the alternative was no wages, and because that is what coal towns did. Peak production came in the years surrounding the disaster, 1908 through 1912, with the mines running at full stretch.

## August 1913

Sir William Mackenzie bought the whole Dunsmuir operation in 1910 through Canadian Collieries, and within two years the Vancouver Island coalfields erupted into one of the bitterest labour disputes in Canadian history. Strikes shut the mines through 1912 and 1913. That August the anger at Extension broke into violence: strikers destroyed company property and burned out the homes of strikebreakers, with the damage falling heaviest on the Chinatown neighbourhood, where Chinese workers who had been brought in to keep the mines running lost their houses and possessions. It was a labour struggle and a racial attack in the same hours, and the Chinese families caught between the two had the least power of anyone involved. The province declared martial law and sent troops to restore order and walk strikebreakers to work under escort. The union lost. Vancouver Island miners would not win back recognition until 1938.

## Scratching the Leftovers

The Extension mines closed for good in 1931, having yielded eight million tons. What followed was scavenging. Canadian Collieries leased small unworked pockets to contractors, the largest being the Beban mine, which ran from 1935 to 1941 with about thirty-five men and pulled out 75,000 tonnes, chronically flooding wherever it broke into the old abandoned workings. Three men drowned in a 1937 flood. Through the Depression, ordinary residents crawled up to 300 feet into the dead Extension tunnels to hack coal out by hand. A 1948 account described warped, unpainted shanties with walls angling crazily, a ghost town except that people still lived in it. Logging carried the place through the following decade, then developers discovered that land just outside Nanaimo's boundary came with much lower taxes. Extension is a commuter village now, modern houses among small farms, the general store gone, the volunteer fire hall still there. Behind the trees, the slag heap stands bare.

## Flight Context

Extension sits at 49.099N, 123.957W, about 10 km south of Nanaimo and roughly 107 km by road northwest of Victoria, just off BC Highway 19 on southeastern Vancouver Island. From the air, look for the wooded ridge country between Nanaimo and Ladysmith, with Mount Benson rising to the northwest as the primary landmark; the old slag heap shows as a bare grey scar amid second-growth forest. Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) at Cassidy is roughly 5nm south, Nanaimo Harbour Water Aerodrome (CAC8) about 10nm north, and Victoria International (CYYJ) some 45nm southeast. Best viewed at 1,500 to 3,000 feet on a transit between Nanaimo and Ladysmith. The Strait of Georgia lies a few miles east, and low morning cloud tends to sit in these valleys well after the coastline has cleared.
