# Hucuktlis Lake

The wettest place in North America is a narrow lake hidden behind the mountains of Vancouver Island, where more than nine metres of rain once fell in a single year.

**Location:** 49.0936, -125.0380

**Categories:** Alberni Valley, Lakes of Vancouver Island, Clayoquot Land District

## Introduction

In 1997, 9,307 millimetres of rain fell on this lake. That is thirty and a half feet of water — deep enough to submerge a three-storey building — arriving one drop at a time over twelve months, and it stands as the all-time Canadian record. It was not a freak year so much as an emphatic one. Hucuktlis Lake averages 6,903 millimetres annually, roughly 272 inches, which makes this narrow gash of water behind the mountains of Vancouver Island the wettest place in North America. The people who have always known it call it Hucuktlis, pronounced "who chook-tlis," and for a century of maps and bureaucracy it was called something else entirely.

## Why the Sky Empties Here

The mechanism is simple and relentless. Pacific storms roll east across open ocean with nothing to slow them, hit the wall of Vancouver Island's mountains, and are forced upward. Rising air cools, cooling air cannot hold its water, and the water comes down — on this particular basin, harder than anywhere else on the continent. The lake sits in a steep-sided valley draining toward Uchucklesit Inlet, an arm of the Alberni Inlet, a funnel aimed almost perfectly at the incoming weather. Winter is when the numbers get absurd, month after month of it, the rain arriving less as weather than as a condition. The forest responds accordingly: moss on every surface that holds still, cedar and hemlock running to enormous size, creeks that go from trickle to torrent in an afternoon and back again.

## Place Way Inside

Hucuktlis means "place way inside" — a description rather than a label, naming the lake by its relationship to the inlet and the people who reached it from the water. The Uchucklesaht used this lake, and the lands and mountains ringing it, for spiritual practice. That is the part of the record that predates every survey, every gazetteer entry, every colonial cartographer who came through with a notebook. The name is not decorative and it is not a translation exercise. It carries a sense of direction and belonging: the place you come to when you go far enough in.

## A Mistake, Then a Correction, Then a Return

In 1912 a regional map got the name wrong. In 1917, rather than untangling the error, officialdom made it permanent: Henderson Lake became the adopted name, a tidy English surname fastened to a body of water that had carried a Nuu-chah-nulth name for as long as anyone could say. It held for a hundred and one years. Then, in 2018, on the recommendation of the Uchucklesaht First Nation and with the backing of the Alberni-Clayoquot Regional District, British Columbia changed the official name back to Hucuktlis Lake. It is a small administrative act on paper — a database field, a line in a gazetteer. It is also the difference between a place described by outsiders and a place named by the people whose spiritual life is bound to it.

## The Long Narrow Water

From the air the lake reads as a dark slot pressed between ridges, about seventeen miles southwest of Port Alberni and effectively invisible from any road a visitor is likely to drive. Clemens Creek feeds the northern tip. The Hucuktlis River drains the southern end into Uchucklesit Inlet, and from there the water joins the Alberni Inlet and runs out to the open Pacific through Barkley Sound. A fish hatchery operates on the lake, and it is the hatchery crew's weather station — an instrument shelter, a gauge, and the patience to read it in the rain — that produced the numbers that made this valley famous. Records like these do not come from satellites. Somebody stood out there in a downpour, year after year, and wrote down what the sky had done.

## Flight Context

Hucuktlis Lake lies at 49.0936°N, 125.038°W, tucked into the mountains roughly 17 miles southwest of Port Alberni on Vancouver Island. Fly it at 4,500 to 7,000 feet — high enough to see the whole drainage in one frame, low enough to read the terrain that makes the rain. The navigation picture is straightforward once you find the Alberni Inlet: follow that long fjord southwest from Port Alberni, watch for Uchucklesit Inlet branching north, and the lake sits directly up-valley from its head, a narrow dark ribbon aligned roughly north-south between steep forested ridges. Barkley Sound and the Broken Group Islands open to the southwest. Nearest fields are Port Alberni Regional (CYPB) about 20 miles northeast, Tofino/Long Beach (CYAZ) on the west coast, and Nanaimo (CYCD) or Comox (CYQQ) for full services. Plan conservatively: this is the wettest airspace on the continent, with low cloud, embedded rain, and mountain obscuration common from October through March. Late July and August offer the most reliable visual conditions, and even then morning fog fills the inlets.
