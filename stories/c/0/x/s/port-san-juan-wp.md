# Port San Juan

A deep Pacific inlet on Vancouver Island carved along two fault lines, home of the Pacheedaht First Nation and the southern gate to the Graveyard of the Pacific.

**Location:** 48.5497, -124.4511

**Categories:** Coastal, British Columbia, Indigenous History, Maritime History, Geology, Hiking

## Introduction

Port San Juan is not really a bay. It is a crack in the earth that filled with seawater. Two faults - the San Juan and the Leech River - run parallel through this corner of southwestern Vancouver Island, and the valley between them has been ground down, flooded, and opened to the Pacific. Sail in from the Strait of Juan de Fuca and you are steering up a geological seam, with steep forested slopes rising on either hand and the San Juan and Gordon rivers pushing fresh water down from the northeast to meet the swell.

## People of the Sea Foam

The southern shore of the inlet, near the mouth of the San Juan River, is home to the Pacheedaht First Nation and the community of Port Renfrew. Pacheedaht is usually translated as "people of the sea foam," a name that makes immediate sense to anyone who has watched a winter swell break across the mouth of this inlet and send spume drifting inland over the trees. The Pacheedaht are part of the broader Nuu-chah-nulth Nation of the island's west coast, and their villages, trails, and fishing sites occupied the San Juan Valley and its rivers for centuries before any European chart carried a name for the place. The trail network that linked them up the coast toward Nitinaht and beyond was not scenery. It was infrastructure, and it would later be borrowed for a purpose its builders never imagined.

## The Schooner and the Smallpox

Among the oral histories of Vancouver Island's First Nations is an account of a Spanish trading schooner that appeared off this coast in 1777. The Spanish anchored in the harbour and traded with the Nitinaht people (today the Ditidaht First Nation), then found gold in the San Juan River and attempted to take it. The Nitinaht defeated the expedition. Two Spanish women were captured and held in slavery, later released to a subsequent Spanish party that found them - and that second expedition carried smallpox ashore. What followed for the Nitinaht was what followed for coastal nations everywhere the disease landed: whole villages emptied in a season, knowledge and kinship severed in weeks. Documentary evidence is thin and the details are debated, though Spanish vessels such as the Santiago did probe this coast in the 1770s and records of conflict with First Nations do exist. If the account holds, it contains the first reported discovery of gold on the San Juan River - and a far heavier reckoning alongside it.

## The Name the Post Office Changed

The settlement founded on the inlet's southern shore was originally called Port San Juan, the same as the water it sat on. That turned out to be a problem. Mail bound for the little Vancouver Island settlement kept going astray among the San Juan Islands just across the international boundary in Washington, and in 1895 the town took a new name: Port Renfrew, after Lord Renfrew, who had a scheme to settle Scottish crofters here. The crofters never arrived in the numbers imagined. The name stuck anyway, and the result is a place where the town and the bay it faces carry entirely different labels - Port Renfrew on the land, Port San Juan on the chart.

## Southern Gate to a Graveyard

The coastline running northwest from this inlet has been called the Graveyard of the Pacific, and it earned the title honestly. In January 1906 the steamer Valencia went onto the rocks north of here, and 136 people died within sight of shore while rescuers could do almost nothing. The public outrage that followed pushed the Dominion government to upgrade an existing First Nations route into a lifesaving trail, completed in 1907, running from Port Renfrew north to Bamfield with telegraph line and shelters along the way. It exists today as the West Coast Trail, and hikers finish it here, at the inlet - muddy, rain-soaked, and generally elated. Getting to Port San Juan is easier than it once was. Highway 14 runs out from Victoria, the Pacific Marine Circle Road drops down from Lake Cowichan, and boats still come in the way everything once did, off the Strait of Juan de Fuca.

## Flight Context

Port San Juan opens to the Pacific at 48.5497°N, 124.4511°W, on the southwest coast of Vancouver Island. The inlet is unmistakable from altitude: a straight, deep notch cut northeast into the coastline, aligned with the San Juan and Leech River fault traces, with the San Juan and Gordon river valleys feeding into its head. Port Renfrew's cluster of buildings and its marina sit on the southern shore near the river mouth. Recommended viewing altitude is 4,000-6,000 ft MSL to follow the fault-aligned valley inland, or 9,000 ft and above to see the inlet in the context of the full Strait of Juan de Fuca and Washington's Olympic Mountains beyond. Victoria International (CYYJ) lies roughly 50 nm southeast; across the strait, William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) at Port Angeles is about 35 nm to the southeast. Marine fog, low stratus, and heavy Pacific rain are routine here from autumn through spring; summer afternoons offer the best chance of clear sightlines down the open coast.
