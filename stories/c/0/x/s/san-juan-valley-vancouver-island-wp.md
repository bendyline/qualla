# San Juan Valley (Vancouver Island)

A rainforest valley on Vancouver Island's southwest coast holding the largest Douglas fir on Earth, the wreckage of a crushed continental fragment, and a two-century-old story about Spanish gold that nobody can quite verify.

**Location:** 48.6000, -124.2167

**Categories:** vancouver-island, british-columbia, old-growth-forest, geology, first-nations

## Introduction

The story has been told on this coast for more than two centuries and nobody has ever been able to settle it. In 1777, the account goes, a Spanish trading schooner came in past the headlands and anchored in Port San Juan, at the mouth of a river running out of the mountains. The crew traded. Then they found gold in the riverbed and decided to stay and take it, and the people whose river it was drove them off. Two Spanish women were left behind and enslaved until a later Spanish expedition found them and carried them away - and that expedition, unknowingly, carried smallpox in the other direction. Whether any of this happened is genuinely unclear. Spanish ships did work this coast in the 1770s; the Santiago was among them, and colonial records do note clashes with First Nations. What is certain is the name. The valley, the river, and the bay all still answer to San Juan.

## Everything Drains West

Geography here is simple once you see it from above. The San Juan Valley is a trough on the southwest shoulder of Vancouver Island, walled off from the Cowichan Valley to the north by the Seymour Range and from the Loss Creek and Jordan River drainages to the south by an unnamed ridge that never earned a label. The San Juan River runs the length of it and empties into Port San Juan at Port Renfrew, which along with the communities of the Pacheedaht First Nation makes up the only permanent settlement in the whole valley. Three small lakes sit in the folds: Fairy, Lizard, and Pixie - names that sound like a joke until you see the moss. The Pacheedaht, whose name refers to the sea foam that breaks on the rocks at the harbour mouth, take it from the old village of p'aachiida at the head of the bay.

## Wreckage Between Two Continents

The rock under all this arrived from somewhere else, badly damaged. The valley sits on the remains of the Pacific Rim Terrane - a slab of crust that was caught and crushed between two larger arriving fragments, Wrangellia pressing from the north and Siletzia from the south. Terranes are the debris of plate tectonics, pieces of island arc and seafloor that ride the subduction conveyor until they jam against a continent and weld on. The Pacific Rim Terrane got the worst of it, mashed into a narrow band that runs from the open Pacific eastward as far as Victoria. What the geologists read as a collision, hikers experience as topography: steep, broken, chaotic ground where the drainages rarely run where you expect them to.

## Rain, Then More Rain

The valley's west end opens onto the Strait of Juan de Fuca, and the ocean sets the terms there - an oceanic climate, cool and wet, with the marine layer rolling up the inlet. Push inland and the pattern shifts to warm-summer Mediterranean, dry enough in August to raise fire concern in a place that otherwise feels permanently soaked. Between the two extremes lies temperate rainforest of the classic Pacific Northwest sort. Western hemlock, western red cedar, Pacific silver fir, yellow cedar, Douglas fir, grand fir, Sitka spruce, and western white pine make up the canopy. Below them grow bigleaf maple and red alder, and below those a floor of sword fern and red huckleberry so dense that stepping off-trail means wading. Little of the light that lands on the canopy reaches the ground at all.

## The Largest Douglas Fir on Earth

Some of the oldest and largest surviving patches of old growth on Vancouver Island are in this valley, and one tree in particular draws people from around the world. The Red Creek Fir, standing east of Port Renfrew, is the largest Douglas fir known anywhere: about 74 metres tall, 13.3 metres around, with a trunk roughly 4.2 metres across and a volume near 349 cubic metres - the greatest of any tree in the pine family. It seeded around the year 1000, which means it was already a century old when the first European ships were still four hundred years from the Atlantic crossing. Nearby stands the San Juan Spruce, once ranked the second-largest Sitka spruce in the world. Both survived largely by accident of terrain; the forest around them did not. Logging has run through this valley for a century, and the arguments over what remains have been loud, long, and unresolved.

## Flight Context

The San Juan Valley centres near 48.60 degrees north, 124.22 degrees west, on the southwest coast of Vancouver Island. A viewing altitude of 5,000 to 7,000 feet gives the full trough - the Seymour Range walling the north side, the unnamed ridge to the south, and the river threading west to tidewater at Port Renfrew. The clearest navigation feature is Port San Juan itself: a deep, straight-sided inlet biting northeast off the Strait of Juan de Fuca, the last significant harbour before the coast turns wild toward Cape Flattery's opposite number at Bonilla Point. Clearcut blocks show as pale rectangles against the dark old growth and make the valley easy to identify even in haze. Nearest fields are Victoria International (CYYJ) about 70 nautical miles southeast, Port Alberni (CBS9) to the northeast, and William R. Fairchild International at Port Angeles (KCLM) across the strait in Washington. Expect low stratus and drizzle much of the year, with the marine layer pouring up the inlet from the west most mornings.
