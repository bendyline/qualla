# San Juan River (Vancouver Island)

A short river on southern Vancouver Island carrying two lost-gold stories, the largest Douglas-fir on Earth, and the watershed where Canada's biggest civil disobedience campaign over old-growth logging played out.

**Location:** 48.5594, -124.3986

**Categories:** Juan de Fuca region, Rivers of Vancouver Island, Old-growth forests, Spanish history in the Pacific Northwest

## Introduction

For a decade an American prospector named Foster walked back into the San Juan Valley every summer looking for a place he had already found. In 1885 he had pulled gold out of this river on southern Vancouver Island. When he returned in 1907 to work the spot properly, he could not locate it — not that season, not the next, not in any year that followed. He died in Salt Lake City in 1917 with the river still keeping its secret. Port Renfrew heard the story and took up the search themselves, combing the gravel bars and side creeks for a claim that was, by all accounts, real. Nobody has found it since.

## East to West, Then the Open Pacific

The San Juan rises in the Seymour Range and runs the length of its own valley from east to west before emptying into Port San Juan, the deep inlet at Port Renfrew where the Strait of Juan de Fuca finally surrenders to the open ocean. By continental standards it is a short river. What it has instead is depth of a different kind — a valley floor of rich alluvial soil, rainfall measured in metres rather than millimetres, and a climate that has been growing trees here without serious interruption for thousands of years. The upper river threads through timber. The lower reaches braid across gravel bars and pile up against log jams left by winter floods. Near the mouth the water spreads into an estuary where salt and fresh trade places twice a day and the current slows enough for salmon to gather before the climb inland.

## Two Gold Stories

Foster's is the second gold story this river carries. The first is older and far harder to pin down. First Nations on Vancouver Island preserved an account of a Spanish trading schooner that anchored in the harbour in 1777 and traded with the Nitinat — as traders of that era wrote the name — before the crew found gold in the San Juan and moved to take it. In the telling, the expedition ended in violence, two Spanish women were held and later handed to a second Spanish party, and that second party carried smallpox ashore. The documentary support is fragmentary. Spanish vessels including the Santiago did survey this coast in the 1770s, and records of coastal conflict exist. What is not in question is the epidemic. Smallpox swept the Northwest Coast repeatedly in the late eighteenth century and killed a devastating share of the people living along it, whatever route carried it here.

## The Valley of Giants

Whatever the river is hiding, the valley's real treasure has always stood in plain sight. The Red Creek Fir grows on a bench above the San Juan about half an hour from Port Renfrew: 73.8 metres tall, 13.3 metres around, some 349 cubic metres of wood. It is the largest Douglas-fir known anywhere on Earth and the largest tree in the pine family by volume. Core estimates put its germination near the year 1000, give or take three centuries, meaning it was already an old tree when Foster first came prospecting. Nor does it stand alone. The same watershed holds the San Juan Spruce, the largest Sitka spruce in Canada, and Big Lonely Doug, the country's second-largest Douglas-fir, spared by a single logger and left standing in the middle of a clearcut where it remains, unmistakable and slightly absurd, a giant with nothing left around it.

## Fairy Creek

Fairy Creek, a tributary of the San Juan, became the most contested patch of forest in Canada. Beginning in August 2020, activists blockaded logging roads inside Tree Farm Licence 46 to halt cutting in roughly 2,000 hectares of unprotected old growth — the largest unbroken stand left in the region outside a park. The blockade held for eight months, and more than 1,100 people were arrested defying court orders to clear it. None of the politics ran clean. The watershed sits in Pacheedaht territory, and the Nation's leadership asked the blockaders to leave; at the same time individual members, among them Elder William "Bill" Jones, backed the protesters and warned that logging would endanger spiritual bathing pools upstream. In June 2021 the Pacheedaht asked British Columbia for a two-year deferral on old-growth logging in Fairy Creek and the nearby Central Walbran. The province granted it.

## Where the River Ends

The estuary is protected as an ecological reserve under the strictest designation the IUCN applies — Category Ia, a place set aside for nature and science rather than for visitors. Red alder and black cottonwood crowd the wet ground, with Sitka spruce and western hemlock standing behind them. Underfoot the tangle is salmonberry, stink currant, piggyback plant, lady fern and deer fern. Black bears fish the shallows in season. Roosevelt elk browse the open flats, river otters work the side channels, mink slip along the cut banks, and bald eagles hold the dead snags above everything, waiting on the salmon run that brings the whole system back to life each autumn.

## Flight Context

The San Juan River runs east to west across southern Vancouver Island, reaching tidewater at Port San Juan near Port Renfrew, British Columbia, at roughly 48.56N, 124.40W. From the air the valley is an obvious green corridor cut between forested ridges, with the Seymour Range at its eastern head and a wide, braided estuary at the western mouth. Port San Juan itself is a clean V-shaped inlet opening southwest onto the Strait of Juan de Fuca — an unmistakable coastal fix. Look for the checkerboard of clearcuts and second growth flanking the valley and the darker unbroken canopy of the Fairy Creek headwaters to the north. Nearest airports: Victoria International (CYYJ) about 45nm east-southeast, Port Alberni Regional (CBS8) about 35nm north, and Sooke/Jordan River seaplane traffic along the strait. Best viewed between 2,500 and 5,000 feet. This coast is fog-prone in summer mornings and the valley funnels low cloud; the clearest flying is often mid-afternoon on an outflow day.
