# Port Renfrew

A village of 262 at the end of Vancouver Island's south coast highway, where the Graveyard of the Pacific meets the largest Douglas firs on Earth.

**Location:** 48.5586, -124.3992

**Categories:** Coastal Communities, British Columbia, Vancouver Island, Old-Growth Forests, Maritime History, First Nations

## Introduction

The town changed its name because of the mail. It was Port San Juan first, after the inlet it sits on, and letters kept ending up in the San Juan Islands on the American side of the strait - close enough on a map, useless in practice. So the colonists renamed the settlement for Lord Renfrew, a Scottish peer who intended to resettle crofters on this coast and, in the end, did not. The inlet kept its Spanish name. The town took the name of a man who never arrived. Nearly two centuries later, Port Renfrew has 262 residents, a government wharf, and the somewhat grander title it now advertises on its own signage: the Tall Tree Capital of Canada. Both names were given by outsiders. Only one of them has trees to back it up.

## A Coast That Kept Score

Between 1830 and 1925, 137 major shipping disasters occurred near the entrance to the Strait of Juan de Fuca - the corridor that funnels every vessel bound for Puget Sound and the Fraser past this stretch of shore. Fog, reefs, and a lee coastline did most of the work. Sailors called it the Graveyard of the Pacific, and the name was not decorative. The wreck of the Valencia in 1906, which killed most of the people aboard within sight of land, finally pushed the Canadian government to act. Crews cut the Dominion Lifesaving Trail through the rainforest north of Port Renfrew, stocking shelters with rations, blankets, and telegraph equipment so that survivors who reached shore had somewhere to walk to. That trail is still there. Hikers now pay for permits to walk it and call it the West Coast Trail.

## People of the Sea Foam

Long before the postal confusion, this inlet was Pacheedaht territory, and it still is. The nation's name translates as People of the Sea Foam, and its traditional village sites sit in and around what is now the town centre - not adjacent to the community but underneath it. The San Juan River mouth, the estuary, the beaches at the head of the inlet: these were living places with houses, fishing stations, and generations of accumulated knowledge about when the salmon ran and where the sea foam gathered. The Pacheedaht remain the landholders and decision-makers for much of the surrounding territory, including some of the old-growth forest that draws visitors here, and questions about which of those trees stand and which fall run through their council as much as through any provincial ministry.

## The Tall Tree Capital

The San Juan Valley east of town holds trees that strain the vocabulary. The Red Creek Fir is the largest Douglas fir known on Earth. Twenty kilometres west of it stands Big Lonely Doug, roughly 66 metres tall, about 12 metres around, and something close to a thousand years old - a tree that survived because a logging engineer flagged it to be left standing. Everything around it did not. Doug now rises alone out of a twenty-hectare clearcut felled in 2012, surrounded by stumps three metres wide, and the photographs of that contrast did more for old-growth campaigns than any speech. Nearby, the Ancient Forest Alliance pushed to protect Avatar Grove, a stand of gnarled, burl-covered redcedars that became the argument's showpiece: proof that a big tree left upright can be worth more to a small town than a big tree cut down.

## Where the Pavement Stops

Highway 14 runs west out of Victoria along the Juan de Fuca shoreline and ends at the Port Renfrew government wharf. The other way in is the Pacific Marine Road from Lake Cowichan, over the hills through logged and replanted country. Either approach takes a while, which is part of the appeal and all of the constraint. The climate is genuinely gentle for Canada - oceanic, wet, with some of the mildest winters in the country - and the town has learned to sell what that produces: fishing charters, kayaking, birding, the Juan de Fuca Marine Trail heading southeast and the West Coast Trail heading north. Since 2010 the Tall Tree Music Festival has taken over the last weekend in June, briefly multiplying the population several times over before the road empties again.

## Two Economies, One Forest

Port Renfrew's history is a forestry and fishing history, like most of this coast, and the town's present is an awkward negotiation with that past. The same valleys that produced generations of logging paycheques now produce a tourism economy built on the trees those crews did not reach. Nobody here treats that as a settled question. The visitors who drive out to photograph Big Lonely Doug pass through cutblocks on the way; the residents who guide them often come from families that worked those cutblocks. What makes the place worth the drive is not resolution but the fact that you can see the whole argument in one afternoon - the stumps, the survivor, the grove, the wharf, and the grey water of Port San Juan running out toward the strait that swallowed 137 ships.

## Flight Context

Port Renfrew lies at 48.559 N, 124.399 W on the south shore of Port San Juan, a west-facing inlet on the southwest coast of Vancouver Island. A viewing altitude of 3,000-5,000 feet MSL works well: low enough to pick out the government wharf and the San Juan River estuary, high enough to hold the whole inlet and the Strait of Juan de Fuca in one view. The inlet itself is the primary landmark - a clean rectangular notch in an otherwise unbroken coastline, with the San Juan Valley running northeast behind the town and mountains closing it on three sides. Across the strait, the Olympic Peninsula fills the southern horizon on clear days. Nearest airports are Victoria International (CYYJ) about 50 nm east-southeast, Nanaimo (CYCD) to the northeast, and William R. Fairchild International at Port Angeles (KCLM) across the strait in Washington. Expect frequent low cloud, marine fog, and heavy rain - this coast earned its shipwreck reputation on visibility, and the flying conditions have not changed.
