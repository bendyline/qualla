# Lighthouse Pub

A Sunshine Coast pub assembled from the salvage of Expo 86, wearing a fast-food spaceship as its lighthouse and doubling as a floatplane terminal.

**Location:** 49.4811, -123.7580

**Categories:** Landmarks, Sunshine Coast, British Columbia, Expo 86, Aviation

## Introduction

The lighthouse on the roof was never a lighthouse. It was a spaceship. When Expo 86 closed in Vancouver and the site went under the hammer, the space-themed McDonald's gave up its flying saucer, and a Sechelt developer looked at the thing and saw a beacon. Some modification, a coat of paint, and the saucer became the most recognisable roofline on Porpoise Bay. The building underneath it has a similar provenance: it, too, spent the summer of 1986 as part of a world's fair, and it, too, ended up on the Sunshine Coast because somebody bid on it.

## Lot for Sale, Purpose Unstated

The first public hint appeared in the Coast News on 24 March 1986, a notice about a proposed pub on the shores of Porpoise Bay. It named no operator and described no building. There was, at that point, no building to describe. What existed on the site was the Tyee float plane base and a very small marina, a workaday corner of Sechelt Inlet where aircraft came and went and nobody lingered. The transformation would come from eighty kilometres away, out of a world exposition that was still running at the time the notice was printed, and whose pavilions were already understood to be temporary. Expo structures were built to be admired for six months and then disposed of. A handful escaped that fate.

## China Gate, Dismantled

The building now standing over the water at Porpoise Bay was the China Gate Restaurant at Expo 86, one of the very few Expo restaurant buildings anywhere that still exists. Sechelt developer Len Van Egmond bought it in the auction that followed the exposition's closing. Moving it was the hard part. The pilings went into the ground in June 1987, and the structure travelled north in pieces, loaded onto the Langdale ferry and trucked up Highway 101 to Sechelt, where it was reassembled half on land and half out over the water. It reopened as the Lighthouse Pub in June 1988. The building has since been misidentified in print more than once, most notably in Bill Cotter's book on Expo 86, which records it as the Munich Festhaus.

## Adding Rooms to a Souvenir

A salvaged building rarely stays as salvaged. In December 1994 a restaurant opened beneath the lighthouse motif, enclosing what had been an open deck and turning ornament into floor space. The bigger change came in 2006, when the pub was substantially reworked: the liquor store moved offsite, the front of the building was opened up, and the modest deck was expanded to run the full width of the frontage. That work wrapped in 2007. The result is a building whose interior no longer particularly resembles a pavilion, though everyone who walks in knows what it used to be. The spaceship on the roof makes it difficult to forget.

## Pub as Terminal

What makes the Lighthouse genuinely unusual is not the architecture but the function. This is a neighbourhood pub with big-screen televisions, weekend live music and a menu built on local seafood, and it is also, simultaneously, a transportation hub. The marina serves boat traffic on Sechelt Inlet. The float plane base operates scheduled flights to Vancouver, Richmond and Nanaimo. The complex houses the pub itself, Buccaneers Restaurant, Lighthouse Marine and the Harbour Air Sechelt base, which means it is entirely possible to finish a pint, walk outside, and be airborne within minutes. Few pubs anywhere double as a gateway. On the Sunshine Coast, where the highway is interrupted by water and the ferry timetable governs everything, a place that is both bar and terminal makes complete sense.

## What Survives a World's Fair

Expo 86 remade Vancouver's waterfront and then vanished almost entirely, leaving a handful of landmarks and a great deal of nostalgia. Most of the pavilions were scrap within a year. That a Chinese restaurant and a fast-food spaceship should have been rescued, barged and trucked up the coast, welded into a single structure and kept in continuous commercial use for decades is a small and genuinely improbable act of preservation. Nobody set out to save a piece of the fair. Somebody wanted a pub with a good roofline on Porpoise Bay, and the cheapest way to get one was to buy it secondhand.

## Flight Context

The Lighthouse Pub sits at 49.4811 N, 123.758 W on Porpoise Bay, at the southern end of Sechelt Inlet on British Columbia's Sunshine Coast. Best viewing altitude is 1,000 to 2,000 feet, low enough to pick out the white lighthouse structure on the roof, which is the single most reliable visual marker on this shoreline. Key visual landmarks: the long straight trench of Sechelt Inlet running northeast into the Coast Mountains, the narrow isthmus of Sechelt separating the inlet from the Strait of Georgia, and the floatplane dock and marina immediately in front of the building. The site is an active water aerodrome served by scheduled floatplane flights, so expect low-level seaplane traffic in the bay and on approach up the inlet. Vancouver International (CYVR) lies roughly 30 nm southeast across the Strait of Georgia, and Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is the usual downtown floatplane terminus. Inflow winds funnel strongly up Sechelt Inlet on summer afternoons, and low cloud frequently caps the inlet walls.
