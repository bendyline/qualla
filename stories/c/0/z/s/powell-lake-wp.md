# Powell Lake

A fifty-kilometre fjord on British Columbia's Sunshine Coast that the land itself lifted out of the sea, trapping ancient ocean water in its depths where it has sat, undisturbed, for eleven thousand years.

**Location:** 50.0833, -124.4170

**Categories:** Sunshine Coast (British Columbia), Lakes of British Columbia, Meromictic lakes

## Introduction

At the bottom of Powell Lake there is seawater that has not touched the ocean since the last ice age. It lies in the deepest fifty metres of two of the lake's basins, too dense and too salty to mix with the fresh water above it, and it has been sitting there for roughly eleven thousand years. This was a fjord once, open to the Strait of Georgia. Then the land, released from the crushing weight of the glaciers, began to rise. The bedrock sill at the mouth came up out of the water like a lid closing, and what had been an arm of the Pacific became a lake with an ocean sealed inside it.

## Fossil Water

Scientists call a lake like this meromictic: one that never fully turns over. Most lakes mix top to bottom with the seasons, cold surface water sinking and stirring the whole column. Powell Lake cannot. The salt layer at the bottom is heavy enough to stay put permanently, and because nothing mixes down into it, nothing carries oxygen down either. That deep water is anoxic, dark, and effectively unchanged since the Pleistocene - a sample of the post-glacial Pacific held in place by nothing more complicated than density. The lake itself is enormous by coastal standards: fifty kilometres long, about 120 square kilometres of surface, an average depth of 150 metres and a maximum of 360. Six interconnected basins make up its length, and two of them hold the trapped sea.

## Thaʔyɛɬ

In ʔayʔaǰuθəm, the language of the Tla'amin, the lake is θaʔyɛɬ - thaʔyɛɬ - which means, plainly and accurately, freshwater lake. The Tla'amin lived at the lake's outflow in a village called tiskʷat, and they were removed from it in the 1880s to make room for industry. The name now on the chart is thought to honour Dr. Israel Wood Powell, B.C.'s first superintendent of Indian Affairs and an architect of policies that included residential schools and the banning of the potlatch, who is also taken as the namesake of the river and the city. The city has treated him as such since at least 1946, when his daughters presented a portrait of their father to a local high school. Two names, then, for one body of water: one that describes what it is, one that commemorates an official who never lived beside it. Both are still in circulation, and the older one is not going anywhere.

## The Lid and the Dam

The same geology that trapped the salt water made the place irresistible to engineers. A low rise of land separates the lake from the Strait of Georgia, holding the water roughly forty-six metres above the tide - a natural dam requiring almost no improvement, with a very short river running down to the sea. Construction on a hydroelectric dam began in 1910, and in 1924 the lake's surface was raised to fifty-six metres above sea level. The station was never built to light a region; it was built to run a paper mill, and to supply that mill with water. The city of Powell River grew on the rise between the two, mill below, lake above. Few industrial sites anywhere have been handed so much head and so little distance to work with.

## Goat Island and the Long Arms

From the water the lake still behaves exactly like the fjord it used to be. Steep forested walls climb straight from the shoreline, the basins bend out of sight from one another, and the whole thing runs deep into the Coast Mountains rather than sprawling across open ground. Goat Island, large and mountainous, sits in the middle of it, big enough to read as mainland until you come around the end and discover the water continues on the other side. The arms run north and northeast into country with no roads. The outflow at the south end is short enough that the sea is close the entire time you are on the lake, which is a strange sensation on fresh water - and a fair reminder that the ocean got here first.

## Flight Context

Powell Lake centres near 50.0833 N, 124.4170 W on the northern Sunshine Coast, running roughly north-northeast from the city of Powell River into the Coast Mountains. Fly it at 3,000 to 5,000 feet AGL along the axis for the best read: the lake appears as a long, narrow, steep-sided fjord with Goat Island splitting the lower basins, and the very short outflow river dropping to the Strait of Georgia past the mill site at the south end. Vancouver Island and the Comox Valley lie across the strait to the southwest; the Coast Mountains rise sharply on both flanks of the lake's upper arms. Powell River Airport (CYPW) is about 15 nm south-southwest, Comox Valley Airport / CFB Comox (CYQQ) roughly 29 nm southwest across the strait, and Campbell River (CYBL) about 34 nm west. Expect strong channelled winds along the lake axis and mechanical turbulence near the walls; low cloud and rain are the default from October through March, and summer mornings often start with a thin valley fog that burns off by mid-morning.
