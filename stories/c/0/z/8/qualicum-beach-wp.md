# Qualicum Beach

A Vancouver Island town named in a language that no longer has fluent speakers, sitting in the mildest climate in Canada, with the oldest population in the country and a habit of refusing to let its old buildings fall down.

**Location:** 49.3500, -124.4330

**Categories:** Towns, Vancouver Island, Coastal British Columbia, Indigenous Heritage, Heritage Preservation, British Columbia

## Introduction

The name means where the dog salmon run. It comes from Pentlatch, a language that once carried the whole inventory of this coastline - the runs, the beaches, the camas meadows - and that has no fluent speakers left. The chum salmon still come. The town that inherited the word is now a tidy community of about 9,300 people spread along the Strait of Georgia at the foot of Mount Arrowsmith, with a farmers market, a restored 1912 schoolhouse full of artists, and the highest median age of any town in Canada. Very little about present-day Qualicum Beach suggests what the word originally described. The salmon are the through-line.

## Camas in Spring, Chum in Fall

Qualicum Beach lies in the traditional territory of the Qualicum First Nation, and for a very long time this stretch of shoreline ran on a calendar that had nothing to do with tourist seasons. People came from across the region each spring for the camas meadows - the bulbs are sweet, and they were valuable enough to trade as a delicacy - and again in the fall for the chum. Between those runs the beach itself provided: clams, butter clams, horse clams, geoducks pulled from tidal sand. That diet left a physical mark still detectable in the ground. The soils here are acidic, gravelly and poor, except near the shore, where centuries of shell middens loaded them with calcium and organic matter. Then, in 1862, smallpox swept the coast. When the botanist Robert Brown led an exploring expedition through the area in 1864, he found it empty. Settlers arrived in the 1880s to a landscape whose people had been decimated two decades before. The residential school system, in the generations that followed, is among the reasons the Pentlatch language did not survive.

## The General's Resort

Qualicum Beach was built to be visited. The E&N Railway reached town in 1914, and with it came the machinery of an Edwardian seaside resort: the Qualicum Beach Hotel, managed by the British-Canadian brigadier-general Noel Money, and the Crown Mansion, Money's own Georgian Revival residence, raised the same year above the Memorial Golf Course. That course had opened in 1913 and is the fourth-oldest in British Columbia, still open year-round in a climate that permits it. Not everyone who arrived fit the resort template. In the 1920s Giuseppe Roat - remembered locally as the Hermit of Qualicum Beach - opened the town's first museum, the Crystal Springs Museum of Nature Art, and charged visitors twenty-five cents to drink from his spring. He bought seventy-five lots, forty-four acres in all; the Hermitage Park subdivision now stands on them. In 1938 Sam Little built St. Andrews Lodge in Tudor style, and it filled with Canadian service personnel through the Second World War. The town incorporated as a village in the early 1940s and became a town in 1983. The trains stopped running in 2011.

## The Mildest Corner of the Country

Geography is generous here in a way it is not fifty kilometres west. Qualicum Beach sits on the Nanaimo lowlands, a narrow plain between the Georgia Basin and the Beaufort Range, shaped by ice that last advanced across it some 18,000 to 19,000 years ago. The Vancouver Island Ranges, Mount Arrowsmith among them, block the weather coming off the Pacific, and what lands here is the leftovers - about 131 centimetres a year, four-fifths of it between October and April. July and August average 27 millimetres of rain and highs around 23 degrees. The freeze-free season runs roughly 180 days, the longest in Canada. All of this puts the town inside the Coastal Douglas-fir biogeoclimatic zone, the mildest climate in the country and one of its most biologically distinctive, where Garry oak and arbutus grow alongside the firs. Each spring the Brant geese stop on their way north and the town throws a festival for them. At the west end, where the Little Qualicum River meets the sea, the Marshall-Stevenson Wildlife Sanctuary protects the tidal wetlands they use.

## A Town With Time

In 2021 the median age in Qualicum Beach was 67.5, and more than half the residents - 56.3 percent - were sixty-five or older. No town in Canada is older. It is easy to make that a punchline and harder to notice what it produces, which is a place with an unusual quantity of civic energy and an unusual reluctance to demolish anything. The 1912 schoolhouse was slated for the wrecking ball; residents formed a cultural association, restored it, and reopened it in 1988 as The Old School House arts centre, now full of galleries and resident artists. The Village Theatre, built as a movie house in 1948, hosts the ECHO Players, a company founded in 1934 that still stages four main productions a season. When St. Andrews Lodge came up for demolition, a society formed in late 2020 to save it, and did; the Qualicum First Nation gifted the park there a new name, Saahtlam. The Saturday farmers market, twenty vendors in 1997, now runs about a hundred. And on Father's Day, a town of nine thousand takes in something on the order of thirty thousand people for a classic car show.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.35 N, 124.433 W on the northeast coast of Vancouver Island, British Columbia, at an elevation of about 8 m (26 ft). The town fronts the Strait of Georgia along Highway 19A and is easy to identify from the water side: a low, green, built-up strip on the Nanaimo lowlands with the Beaufort Range rising immediately southwest and Mount Arrowsmith (1,819 m) the dominant summit inland to the south. The mouth of the Little Qualicum River and the Marshall-Stevenson tidal wetlands mark the west end of town; French Creek Harbour lies about 5 km southeast. Lasqueti Island sits across the strait to the northeast. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,500-4,000 ft MSL for the shoreline and estuary, 6,000 ft and above if you want Arrowsmith and the Cameron valley in the same frame. Qualicum Beach Airport (CAT4) is in town; Nanaimo (CYCD) is roughly 45 km southeast and Comox (CYQQ) about 45 km northwest. The rain-shadow position means visibility here is often far better than over the island's west coast, but winter easterlies off the mainland can bring abrupt cloud and freezing conditions that do not last.
