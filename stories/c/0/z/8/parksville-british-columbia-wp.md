# Parksville, British Columbia

A Vancouver Island beach city where the tide walks a kilometre out, comes back warm over sunbaked sand, and has drawn a higher concentration of retirees than anywhere else in Canada.

**Location:** 49.3150, -124.3120

**Categories:** Parksville, British Columbia, Cities in British Columbia, Populated places on the British Columbia Coast, Regional District of Nanaimo, Beaches of British Columbia

## Introduction

Twice a day the ocean leaves. At Rathtrevor Beach the water pulls back nearly a kilometre, uncovering a plain of ribbed sand that people walk out onto as though someone had rolled back a carpet. Herons work the channels. Children find sand dollars. Then the tide turns and comes sliding back in across flats that have been sitting in the sun for hours, and the water arrives noticeably warm — warm enough to swim in on a coast that is otherwise famous for taking your breath away. That trick of physics is most of the reason Parksville exists in its present form.

## The Warmest Cold Ocean

Rathtrevor Beach Provincial Park protects about two kilometres of this shoreline, and every campsite in it is within a five-minute walk of the water. The sand flats are the attraction, but the climate makes the case. Parksville sits in a Csb warm-summer Mediterranean zone — an odd classification to find on the British Columbia coast — with July and August each receiving less than 40 millimetres of rain. The Vancouver Island ranges to the west wring the weather out before it arrives, leaving this stretch of the Strait of Georgia in a rain shadow. Dry summers, a long sandy beach, and water you can stand in: that combination is rare enough in Canada to build a town around.

## River of Cranes

Coast Salish peoples have lived along this shore for thousands of years — the Qualicum, the Snaw-naw-as of Nanoose, and the Snuneymuxw. The first Europeans through were Spanish, in 1791, followed almost immediately by George Vancouver's Royal Navy survey, and for a brief moment the Spanish names stuck to the charts. What is now the Englishman River appeared as Rio de Grullas, the River of Cranes, near present-day San Pareil; the birds in question were almost certainly herons, which still stalk the same estuary. French Creek was marked Punta de Leonardo. Two empires named the same water within a couple of years of each other, and neither asked the people already fishing it.

## A Post Office in a Cabin

The first European landholder here never bothered to move in. John Hirst pre-empted 120 hectares on both banks of the Englishman River in 1873 and stayed in Nanaimo. For decades the place was known simply as The River, an outpost reachable only by water until a road finally came up from Nanaimo around the turn of the century. A post office arrived in 1877, run out of the cabin of the first postmaster, Nelson Parks, and the town took his name. What changed everything was the Esquimalt and Nanaimo Railway's 1901 extension to McBride Junction. Logging came first, then something nobody had planned on: Islanders riding the train up to spend a week on the beach. Motels, stores, campgrounds, and resorts followed. Parksville incorporated as a village on 19 June 1945, became a town in 1978, and a city in 1981.

## Castles With a Deadline

Since 1982 the city has spent a month each summer turning its beach into a gallery. Parksville Beachfest runs from mid-July to mid-August, and it is the only World Championship Sand Sculpting official qualification event in Canada — meaning the sculptors on this beach are competing for a place on the world stage, not building for the fun of it. The work is astonishing and completely doomed, which is the point. Sculptors haul in graded sand, compact it in forms, and carve downward with palette knives and drinking straws, producing figures several metres tall that hold their edges through weeks of wind off the strait and then go back to being a beach.

## The Oldest City in Canada

At the 2021 census Parksville counted 13,642 residents, up 9.5 percent in five years, and the median age was 56.7 — against 42.8 for British Columbia as a whole. Roughly 44 percent of the population is 65 or older, compared with under 19 percent nationally, the highest concentration of seniors of any city in the country. The effect on daily life is not what the statistics suggest. On the first of January, residents wade into the winter strait for the Polar Bear Splash. On summer Tuesdays the Summer By the Sea night market fills Craig Street with as many as 150 vendors. There is a Canada Day parade down the Island Highway and fireworks over the community park. A town full of people with time on their hands turns out to be a town where things happen.

## Flight Context

Parksville lies at 49.315°N, 124.312°W on the east coast of Vancouver Island, facing the Strait of Georgia. Best viewed from 1,500 to 4,000 feet on a northwest–southeast coastal track. The signature feature is the tidal flat: at low water, Parksville Bay and Rathtrevor Beach expose a pale apron of sand extending close to a kilometre offshore, unmistakable from the air and unlike anything else along this shoreline. The Englishman River enters the sea at the southeast end of town and French Creek at the northwest, bracketing the built-up area; Craig Bay sits just east of the Englishman. Qualicum Beach (CAT4, elevation 190 feet, single 3,564-foot asphalt runway) is the nearest field, about 7 kilometres northwest; Nanaimo (CYCD) is roughly 20 nautical miles southeast and Comox (CYQQ) about 30 nautical miles northwest. The Vancouver Island ranges rise sharply inland to the west, with Mount Arrowsmith prominent on the southwest horizon. This is a rain-shadow coast — expect better ceilings here than over the mountains, summer haze, morning fog in the fall, and brisk southeasterlies ahead of winter fronts running up the strait.
