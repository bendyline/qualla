# Nanoose Bay

A Vancouver Island bay whose Coast Salish name means the way in the harbour, and whose sheltered water has since held a dynamite works, a vanished sawmill town, and one of the busiest torpedo ranges on the Pacific.

**Location:** 49.2500, -124.1830

**Categories:** Vancouver Island, British Columbia, Indigenous History, Military History, Ghost Towns, Industrial History

## Introduction

The Halkomelem word behind this place, Naus, describes a way in - the opening of a harbour, a route toward shelter. It is an invitation of a name. The irony is what the twentieth century did with it: the water off Nanoose Bay is now one of the most closed stretches on the British Columbia coast, a restricted zone mariners know by its radio designation, Whiskey Golf, where torpedoes are fired for a living. A bay named for entry became a place you are not allowed to go.

## The Name of a Survivor

The Snaw-naw-as, also called the Nanoose First Nation, are Coast Salish people and among the northernmost nations on Vancouver Island's east coast. Their name carries a memory of violence: it commemorates the sole survivor of a battle in the nineteenth century, a person who came through something that almost everyone else did not. Oral accounts hold that the attack fell on people who had gone out to gather berries - ordinary work on an ordinary day. That a nation should name itself for a survivor rather than a victory says a good deal about what was endured here. In 1876 the Joint Reserve Commission set aside a reserve at Nanoose Bay, an act that formalized on paper the loss of a far larger traditional territory. The Snaw-naw-as remain here, still tied to these lands and waters.

## Powder Point

In 1911 the Giant Powder Company - the first American firm to manufacture dynamite under licence from Alfred Nobel - set up shop on the peninsula, and the place took the plain, honest name of Powder Point. It was a full industrial settlement: a plant, a community for the people who worked it, and a narrow gauge railway about three miles long stitching the complex together. Everyone involved understood the arithmetic. In 1918 an explosion tore through the works and caused extensive damage. The plant kept going for a few years afterward, then relocated in 1925, leaving behind a road name that still tells you exactly what used to happen there.

## Pilings in the Mudflats

Nanoose Bay accumulated and discarded settlements at a steady rate. Southeast along what is now Highway 19 stood Red Gap, where Joe and Max McKercher built a modern lumber mill in 1912; it passed through owners before Frank Pendleton took it over as the Straits Lumber Company in 1917, and it cut boards until 1942. What is left of Red Gap is a rank of worn pilings protruding from the bay's mudflats at low tide. The community now called Nanoose Bay went by Arlington for a decade, after the Arlington Hotel, and the post office used that name from 1906 until 1916. Down NW Bay Road, Brynmarl had a post office of its own from 1886 to 1935, plus a stage stop and a train station. The trains outlasted the towns, barely. When VIA Rail's Vancouver Island service ended in 2011, Nanoose Bay was still technically a flag stop - though by the end, not even a signpost at the Nanoose Beach Road crossing marked where a train was supposed to stop for you.

## Whiskey Golf

On May 18, 1965, Canada and the United States signed an agreement establishing an underwater weapons range in the deep, sheltered water off Nanoose. It became the Canadian Forces Maritime Experimental Test Range (CFMETR), with its range operations facility on Winchelsea Island and its shore terminus at the end of Powder Point Road. Since it opened, something over 31,000 torpedo tests have been run here - exercise weapons, tracked by hydrophones on the seabed, fired and recovered and fired again. The restricted area that covers it is Area Whiskey Golf, closed to traffic when the range is hot. Not everyone welcomed it. Through the 1970s and 1980s a protest camp sat along the highway opposite the facility, part of the broader anti-nuclear and peace movement of those decades, keeping a long argument going in full view of the people driving past.

## Flight Context

Nanoose Bay lies at 49.2500°N, 124.1830°W, on the east coast of Vancouver Island between Nanaimo and Parksville. The identifying feature is the peninsula itself: a blunt finger of land reaching northeast into the Strait of Georgia, with the bay tucked in behind it on the south side and Schooner Cove on the north. Highway 19 runs the length of the community; Winchelsea Island and the Ballenas Islands sit offshore to the northeast, with Lasqueti and Texada beyond. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,500-4,000 ft MSL to read the peninsula, the mudflats where Red Gap's pilings stand, and the naval wharf at Powder Point; 8,000 ft sets the whole strait against the Coast Mountains. Nanaimo (CYCD) is roughly 17 nm southeast and Qualicum Beach (CAT4) about 11 nm northwest; Comox (CYQQ) lies some 32 nm north. Important: the offshore water here is Area Whiskey Golf, an active military range with associated restrictions - check NOTAMs and range activity before operating low over the strait. Expect good summer visibility, winter southeasters, and morning fog over the bay in autumn.
