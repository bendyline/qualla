# Errington, British Columbia

A Vancouver Island farming community named after a line in a Walter Scott ballad, built around a hall the neighbours paid for themselves.

**Location:** 49.2833, -124.3670

**Categories:** Populated places in the Regional District of Nanaimo, Designated places in British Columbia, Vancouver Island, Rural communities, Farmers' markets

## Introduction

Duncan McMillan named this place after a poem. He was a Scot by way of Ontario, one of the first two settlers to take up land here in 1885, and the story passed down is that he lifted the name from Sir Walter Scott's ballad "Jock of Hazeldean," which mentions a village called Errington in Northumberland. It is an unusually literary origin for a rural post office on Vancouver Island, and it fits the community that grew around it better than anyone could have planned. Errington sits off Highway 4 just south of Parksville, a scatter of small farms and forested acreage in Electoral Area F of the Regional District of Nanaimo, with 2,677 residents at the 2016 census and a village centre that amounts to a post office, a general store, a cemetery, and a hall.

## The First Two

McMillan and Alva Swayne arrived in 1885 and started clearing. The log house McMillan built is still standing, which is a rarer thing on this coast than it sounds, given how efficiently damp and rot dismantle untended timber here. In 1891 the Errington Post Office opened with McMillan as its first postmaster, and that single institution is what converted a few farms into a named place. The land around Parksville is a rarity on Vancouver Island, low and relatively flat and workable, sitting on the leeward side of the mountains where the rainfall drops to something a farmer can plan around. People who wanted to farm rather than log came here for exactly that reason, and their descendants are still at it.

## The Hall the Neighbours Paid For

The Errington War Memorial Hall opened in 1922, built to remember the men the district lost in the First World War. Nobody wrote a cheque for it. The hall was financed through bazaars, donated materials, and volunteer labour, which is to say that a community of a few hundred people organized bake sales and raised a building with their own hands and then owned it outright. A century later it is still the centre of everything. The hall and the adjacent park host the farmers' market, Hi Neighbour Day, the Pumpkin Trail, a vaudeville show, craft sales, regular coffee houses, and a professional concert series that brings touring musicians to a rural hall in a place with no traffic light.

## Wooden Stalls Under the Trees

Geraldine Shaw and her family started the Errington Farmers' Market in 1972. It has been running ever since, and it does not look like the farmers' markets that have spread everywhere since. There is no parking-lot grid of pop-up canopies. The market sits in the woods, open-air, with permanent wooden stalls built among the trees, so that shopping for vegetables here means walking through dappled forest shade with the smell of cedar in it. The setup is deliberately unglamorous and has become the thing people drive from Nanaimo and Courtenay to see. More than fifty years in, it is old enough that the vendors include grandchildren of the original ones.

## Where the River Cuts Down

Two parks press up against the community. Englishman River Regional Park follows the river, and just upstream, Englishman River Falls Provincial Park protects 97 hectares of old-growth forest around a pair of waterfalls where the river has sawed a narrow canyon into bedrock. The province set it aside on December 20, 1940, specifically to keep the big trees standing, and the upper falls drop into a pool the colour of green glass. The river's name has a grim local legend attached, that Indigenous people found the skeleton of an English man near the falls. Whether that is where the name came from is anybody's guess. It has been repeated long enough to become part of the place regardless.

## Eagles in Recovery

On eight acres off Leffler Road, the North Island Wildlife Recovery Centre has been taking in sick, injured and orphaned animals since 1985. It is a volunteer-driven non-profit whose goal is to put as many patients as possible back into the woods they came from. Eagles, owls, falcons, hawks and ravens occupy the flight cages, and there are black bears in care. The centre counts more than 23,400 birds and animals rescued on Vancouver Island over its history. Some of the residents cannot be released, and those become the teaching animals for the school groups that come through, which is a hard trade but an honest one.

## Flight Context

Errington lies at 49.283N, 124.367W, inland on the east coast of Vancouver Island, about six kilometres southwest of Parksville. From the air the orienting features are the Strait of Georgia to the northeast, the broad sandy flats of Parksville Bay that empty spectacularly at low tide, and the Englishman River running northeast out of the mountains to meet the sea there. Southwest of the community, Mount Arrowsmith rises to 1,819 metres and is the dominant terrain feature of the region, usually snow-capped into early summer. Highway 19 and Highway 4 cross near Parksville; Highway 4 is the route west toward Port Alberni and the open Pacific. Qualicum Beach Airport (CAT4) is about 15 kilometres northwest and Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) about 45 kilometres southeast. Best viewed at 2,500 to 5,000 feet AGL. This is the driest quadrant of Vancouver Island, so summer visibility here is often excellent while the west coast is socked in.
