# Nelson Island (British Columbia)

British surveyors wrote the entire life of Horatio Nelson onto this stretch of the Sunshine Coast — his island, his favourite ship, the officer at his deathbed, even his bargeman.

**Location:** 49.7167, -124.1114

**Categories:** Islands of British Columbia, Sunshine Coast (British Columbia), Quarrying, Sunshine Coast Regional District

## Introduction

Read the chart around this island and you are reading a biography. Nelson Island carries the name of Horatio Nelson, 1st Viscount Nelson. Hardy Island, immediately alongside, honours the officer who was the last man Nelson spoke to as he lay dying after Trafalgar. The channel along the eastern shore is named Agamemnon, for Nelson's first command and his favourite ship. And the island's southern tip is Fearney Point, named for William Fearney, Nelson's bargeman — the sailor who gathered up the swords of the surrendered Spanish officers at the Battle of Cape St Vincent, a moment carved in bas-relief at the foot of Nelson's Column in Trafalgar Square. Somebody surveying this coast decided to commemorate not just the admiral but his crew, and the names have outlasted every settlement built beneath them.

## Where the Inlets Meet

Nelson Island sits at the junction of three bodies of water on British Columbia's South Coast — Hotham Sound to the north, Agamemnon Channel to the east, and Malaspina Strait to the west — at the mouth of Jervis Inlet, one of the deepest fjords on the coast. It is mountainous and mostly forested, its interior shaped by lakes and steep rocky hills, with clear-cut scars where logging has reached inland. Hardy Island lies immediately off its southeastern shore, separated by Blind Bay, a sheltered pocket of water that boaters have used as an anchorage for as long as boats have come this way. The nearest communities are Pender Harbour and Egmont, both across the water on the Sechelt Peninsula. There is no ferry and no bridge. Getting to Nelson Island has always meant getting into a boat.

## The Stone That Built Victoria

Before the summer cabins, there were quarries. Granite from Nelson Island went into buildings all over British Columbia, and the most consequential of them stands in Victoria: the provincial Parliament Buildings, designed by Francis Rattenbury and constructed between 1893 and 1897, opened in 1898. The great five-hundred-foot facade that faces the Inner Harbour is andesite quarried at Haddington Island, but the granite that went into the structure came from here, at the mouth of Jervis Inlet. It is worth standing on the island's rocky shoreline with that in mind. The same grey stone that tourists photograph beneath the dome — the seat of a province's government, lit up at night with thousands of bulbs — was cut out of these headlands by quarrymen living in camps on an island with no road to anywhere.

## Sunray

In 1929, Harry Roberts retired to Nelson Island and built himself a house. He called it Sunray, and the name described the architecture exactly: a cottage whose south-facing walls were made of glass, angled outward from the centre so that they spread like the rays drawn on a child's sun. On a coast where the standard dwelling was a shake-roofed cabin with small windows to keep the weather out, Roberts built something that invited the light in and organised the whole structure around it. It still stands, in a cove at Cape Cockburn, and it remains the single most recognisable building on the island — a piece of idiosyncratic homemade modernism finished a full decade before most Canadians had seen anything like it.

## A Population You Could Fit in a Room

Nelson Island was never densely settled. A scattering of homesteads and small logging shows once worked its shoreline, and most of those buildings have since become summer cabins, which means the island's permanent population is very small and its seasonal one is much larger. The rhythm this produces is particular to the BC coast: docks quiet and green with weed through the winter, then busy from June, with boats crossing from Pender Harbour and Egmont loaded with groceries and children. The forest has grown back over most of the old workings. The quarries are silent. What remains is an island where the loudest sounds are a floatplane on approach and the wake of a passing tug, and where every name on the chart still points back to a naval battle fought off the coast of Spain.

## Flight Context

Nelson Island lies at 49.7167 degrees north, 124.1114 degrees west, at the mouth of Jervis Inlet on British Columbia's Sunshine Coast. Best viewed from 3,000 to 5,000 feet — high enough to take in the full geometry that makes this place legible from the air: the island's mountainous forested bulk with Hotham Sound to the north, the narrow slot of Agamemnon Channel separating it from the Sechelt Peninsula to the east, and Malaspina Strait opening west toward Texada Island. Hardy Island and the sheltered water of Blind Bay sit off the southeastern shore; Fearney Point marks the southern tip. Clear-cut patches and old quarry faces give the interior a mottled look. Nearest airports: Powell River (CYPW) about 40 km northwest across Malaspina Strait, Vancouver International (CYVR) roughly 85 km southeast, and Vancouver Harbour water aerodrome (CYHC), the usual origin for floatplane traffic through this inlet. Jervis Inlet funnels wind and can produce strong outflow conditions in winter; low cloud and mountain obscuration are common in the fjord even when the strait is clear.
