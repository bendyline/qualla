# Sunshine Coast (British Columbia)

A populous stretch of the British Columbia mainland that no road reaches - fifty thousand people, a highway that starts and ends on a ferry, and the fastest tidal rapids on the coast.

**Location:** 49.6833, -124.1833

**Categories:** Sunshine Coast (British Columbia), Sunshine Coast Regional District

## Introduction

Look at a road map of British Columbia and the Sunshine Coast appears to be part of the mainland, because it is. Try to drive there and you discover the catch. Fifty thousand people live along this shore, and not one of them can reach the rest of their own province without putting a vehicle on a boat or a body on a floatplane. The fjords running inland from the Strait of Georgia cut too deep and the Pacific Ranges rise too steeply behind them for any road to get around or across. The result is a mainland that behaves, in every practical sense, like an island.

## A Highway With Salt Water in the Middle

British Columbia Highway 101 runs the length of the region between Langdale and the village of Lund, where it simply stops at the northern terminus. To drive it end to end you board three ferries: Horseshoe Bay to Langdale to get on at all, Earls Cove to Saltery Bay to cross Jervis Inlet, which bisects the region, and Little River to Westview if you are coming from Vancouver Island. Pacific Coastal Airlines flies into Powell River Airport (CYPW), and charter and private traffic uses the Sechelt Aerodrome (CAP3). Water taxis handle the smaller islands. Living here means checking a sailing schedule the way people elsewhere check traffic, and a missed ferry is not an inconvenience so much as a change of plan.

## Strong Water

At the entrance to Sechelt Inlet the tide has to squeeze an entire fjord's worth of ocean through a gap, and it does so with spectacular ill grace. The Sechelt Rapids at Skookumchuck Narrows push around 760 million cubic metres of seawater on a typical three-metre tide, and current speeds there exceed 16 knots - roughly 30 km/h - with measurements as high as 17.68 knots on record. Standing waves, whitecaps and whirlpools form in dead calm weather, purely from the water arguing with itself. Chinook Jargon supplied the name: skookumchuck, strong water. A trail through Skookumchuck Narrows Provincial Park leads to viewpoints where you can watch it, and the timing matters. At slack tide it is a quiet channel. An hour later it is a river running uphill.

## Two Districts, Older Names

Administratively the Sunshine Coast is two regional districts, and the northern one changed its name in 2018. The Powell River Regional District became the qathet Regional District, from q̓at̓ᶿɛt in the ʔayʔaǰuθəm language, meaning "working together, bringing together" - a name chosen with the region's four Indigenous nations rather than about them. It covers 5,075 square kilometres and 21,496 people as of the 2021 census; the Sunshine Coast Regional District to the south holds another 32,170, for a regional total of 53,666. Powell River is the largest community at 13,943, followed by Sechelt at 10,847, Gibsons at 4,758 and Roberts Creek at 1,949. In Sechelt, the tems swiya Museum tells the history and culture of the shíshálh people, whose territory this is.

## Mediterranean, Technically

The lowlands here are classified Csb - warm-summer Mediterranean - which sounds absurd for a coast at 49 degrees north until you spend a rainless August week on it. Summers run warm and dry; winters are cool and wet; climb a few hundred metres inland and the classification shifts to marine west coast, with rain distributed more evenly through the year. The wildlife list reads like the whole province compressed into one shoreline: cougars, black bears and wolves in the timber, orcas and sea lions offshore, great blue herons standing in the shallows, bald eagles on every second snag, and marbled murrelets nesting improbably high in old-growth canopy. Tide pools do the rest of the work, full of anemones, molluscs and small fish waiting for the water to come back.

## Where the Road Runs Out

The far end of the Sunshine Coast is where it gets interesting. Beyond Lund lies Desolation Sound Marine Provincial Park, a maze of deep, sheltered anchorages at the region’s northwest boundary. The Copeland Islands sit just offshore. Inland, the Powell Forest Canoe Route links a chain of lakes over 57 kilometres of paddling and portages, while the Sunshine Coast Trail runs 180 kilometres from Saltery Bay to Sarah Point. Smuggler Cove, Spipiyus Provincial Park and the Sechelt Heritage Forest fill in between. Forestry and tourism carry the economy now, where logging and mining once did, and purple flags along the highway mark the studios of artists who work here and welcome visitors in.

## Flight Context

The Sunshine Coast stretches along the British Columbia mainland shore of the Strait of Georgia, with a representative point at 49.683°N, 124.183°W near Powell River. Fly it at 3,000-5,500 ft northwest-bound from Howe Sound and the structure reads itself: Anvil, Gambier and Keats Islands at the southeast end, the long Sechelt Peninsula, then the deep gash of Jervis Inlet running northeast into the Pacific Ranges, then Powell River, Savary Island's pale sand, and Desolation Sound at the far end. Skookumchuck Narrows shows as a visible line of disturbed white water at the throat of Sechelt Inlet on a running tide. Powell River Airport (CYPW) and Sechelt Aerodrome (CAP3) serve the region directly; Vancouver International (CYVR) lies southeast and CFB Comox (CYQQ) west across the strait. Expect strong outflow winds funnelling down the inlets in winter, mountain wave and turbulence close to the Pacific Ranges, and summer morning marine stratus along the water.
