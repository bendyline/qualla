# Maurelle Island

A Discovery Island bounded on all four sides by water that moves, including a tidal rapid called Hole in the Wall and another that runs up to 16 knots.

**Location:** 50.2814, -125.1436

**Categories:** Islands, Discovery Islands, British Columbia, Tidal Rapids, Marine Parks

## Introduction

Hole in the Wall. Surge Narrows. Okisollo. Calm Channel. The four passages that define Maurelle Island were named by people who had to get boats through them, and the names are less description than warning. This is an island in the Discovery Islands of British Columbia, wedged into the archipelago between Vancouver Island and the mainland, at the point where the wide Strait of Georgia begins to constrict toward Johnstone Strait. Everything that flows between those two bodies of water has to squeeze past here. Twice a day, in each direction, it does.

## Four Edges, Four Channels

Maurelle is easiest to locate by what surrounds it. Quadra Island lies to the southwest, separated by Okisollo Channel. Sonora Island sits to the northwest, and the gap between them is so tight that the chart simply calls it Hole in the Wall. Read Island is to the south, across Whiterock Passage. To the northeast, Calm Channel divides Maurelle from the mainland of British Columbia and, by the standards of this neighbourhood, lives up to its name. Administratively the island falls within Electoral Area C of the Strathcona Regional District, a jurisdictional fact that conveys almost nothing about the experience of being here. The channels are the real organising structure. You do not arrive at Maurelle Island so much as arrive at one of its edges, and which edge you choose determines the kind of day you are going to have.

## Sixteen Knots

At the island's southern tip, Antonio Point, the water between Maurelle and Quadra narrows into Surge Narrows, where tidal currents can run as fast as 16 knots on the flood and 14 on the ebb. That is faster than many small boats can make against it. At full ebb the surface here stops behaving like a channel and starts behaving like a river in flood: standing waves, boils that rise and flatten without warning, whirlpools that open and close on their own schedule. Mariners do not fight water like this. They wait for slack, the brief interval when the flood has spent itself and the ebb has not yet committed, and they go through then. Local knowledge in this archipelago is largely a matter of knowing the tide tables, and knowing them better than the weather.

## A Spanish Name, Awarded Late

The island carries the name of Francisco Antonio Mourelle de la Rua, a Spanish naval officer whose career took him into the northeastern Pacific during the era when Spain was contesting this coast. He never lived here and the island was not named in his lifetime. The Geographical Names Board of Canada assigned the name in 1903, long after the Spanish presence on the coast had ended, and in the process the spelling drifted: Mourelle became Maurelle. It is a small mutation, easily missed, and it is now permanent. The Discovery Islands are full of this kind of layering, where Spanish, British and Indigenous names sit alongside one another on the same chart, each recording a different claim about who was here and what they thought they were looking at.

## The Park at the Point

Surge Narrows Provincial Park occupies the southern tip of Maurelle Island, at the very place where the current runs hardest. It is a marine park in the fullest sense: the point of it is the water. Protected shoreline, small islets, the tidal rapids themselves. In channels that flush this vigorously, the nutrient exchange is relentless, and the rock walls beneath the surface support the kind of dense invertebrate life that draws divers from a long way off. Above the waterline the forest runs to the edge and stops. There is no gradual transition on this coast, no beach to speak of in most places, just trees and then rock and then several hundred feet of very cold, very fast water.

## Threading the Archipelago

Vessels working north out of the Strait of Georgia toward Johnstone Strait have to thread this archipelago, and the route they take depends entirely on the hour. Some passages are usable for only a few minutes either side of slack. The result is an odd rhythm of traffic: nothing, nothing, nothing, and then a small procession of boats going through together because they all read the same table. Maurelle Island sits in the middle of that pattern, quiet and forested and largely unbuilt, defined less by what is on it than by what moves past it. There are islands whose story is their settlement. This one's story is its water.

## Flight Context

Maurelle Island lies at 50.2814 N, 125.1436 W in the Discovery Islands of British Columbia, northeast of Quadra Island and southeast of Sonora Island. Best viewing altitude is 3,000 to 5,000 feet, high enough to hold the whole island and all four of its defining channels in one frame. Key visual landmarks: the extremely narrow slot of Hole in the Wall between Maurelle and Sonora to the northwest, Okisollo Channel along the southwest shore, Antonio Point and Surge Narrows at the southern tip, and Calm Channel opening northeast toward the mainland fjords. On a strong tide the rapids at Surge Narrows and Hole in the Wall are visible from the air as distinct white disturbance lines across otherwise dark water, which is a genuinely useful navigational cue. Campbell River Airport (CYBL) lies roughly 20 nm south and is the practical base for this area; Campbell River Water Aerodrome handles floatplane traffic. Expect strong channel-aligned winds, mechanical turbulence downwind of the steep island ridges, and rapid deterioration in visibility when marine cloud pushes in from Johnstone Strait.
