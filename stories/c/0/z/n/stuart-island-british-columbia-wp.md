# Stuart Island (British Columbia)

A privately owned island at the mouth of Bute Inlet with no ferry, exclusive fishing lodges, and a paved runway that appears on no aeronautical chart.

**Location:** 50.3883, -125.1228

**Categories:** Islands of the Discovery Islands, Private islands of Canada, John Stuart, 3rd Earl of Bute

## Introduction

There is a paved runway on Stuart Island, roughly 2,100 feet of it, and you will not find it on an aeronautical chart. It is absent from the Canada Flight Supplement too. The only official acknowledgement that anyone lands here at all is the Big Bay Water Aerodrome, which appears in the Water Aerodrome Supplement - a listing for floatplanes, on an island whose actual runway has been deliberately left off the books. That single detail tells you nearly everything about the place: Stuart Island is reachable, comfortable, and expensively private, and it would prefer not to be found by accident.

## Named for a Very Short Premiership

The island sits among the Discovery Islands, the archipelago wedged between northern Vancouver Island and the mainland coast, just east of the larger Sonora Island at the mouth of Bute Inlet. Administratively it falls in Electoral Area C of the Strathcona Regional District, which is about as much government as it requires. Both the island and the inlet carry the name of John Stuart, 3rd Earl of Bute, who served as Prime Minister of Great Britain from 1762 to 1763. His term lasted under a year and ended in general unpopularity. His name has now been attached to a deep-water fjord and an island on the far side of the Pacific for well over a century and a half - a durability his politics never managed.

## The Door to Bute Inlet

Stuart Island stands at the entrance to one of the most dramatic waterways in British Columbia. Bute Inlet runs about 80 kilometres inland, averaging only four kilometres wide, and reaches depths of 660 metres, making it one of the deepest fjords in the province. The mountains along its upper reaches climb to 2,700 metres, so the walls rise more than nine thousand feet above water that drops nearly half that far below. The inlet lies within territories claimed by the Homalco, Klahoose, Kwiakah and We Wai Kai First Nations. Anyone arriving by floatplane sees the whole arrangement at once: a narrow green corridor between snowfields, and a scatter of small islands guarding its mouth.

## The Road That Started a War

In 1864 the entrepreneur Alfred Waddington set out to build a wagon road from the head of Bute Inlet up the Homathko River to the Cariboo goldfields - a shortcut that would have made him rich. Relations with the Tsilhqotin people through whose country it ran were already strained, and matters broke when Waddington's foreman threatened the warriors with smallpox, a disease that had recently devastated Indigenous communities along this coast. Warriors led by Klattasine attacked the road party. The conflict that followed is known as the Chilcotin War. Klattasine eventually came in to talk, was betrayed, and was hanged. The road was never finished, and when the Canadian Pacific Railway later weighed Bute Inlet as a route to the sea it declined, the memory of the war among the reasons.

## Lodges, Estates, and Granite

Today the island is mostly exclusive fishing lodges and large private estates. There is no ferry. Most visitors come by floatplane or helicopter, and Nanook Lodge is the only fishing and adventure lodge open to the public, served by scheduled daily seaplane flights from Seattle - a commute that makes more sense than a highway ever could. Among the estate owners is a Montana industrialist who owns the Seaspan Marine Corporation; on his property, a nine-hole golf course was carved out of the island's granite. The image is worth sitting with. This is a rock in a tidal channel at the mouth of a fjord, and someone has made fairways on it.

## Why Anyone Comes

The fishing is the honest answer. The waters around the Discovery Islands funnel enormous volumes of tide between the Strait of Georgia and Johnstone Strait, and that moving water concentrates food, and the food concentrates salmon, and the salmon have drawn people to these channels for a very long time and drawn money to them for a rather shorter one. Everything else on Stuart Island - the unlisted runway, the helipads, the seaplane schedule, the granite golf holes - is infrastructure built around that single fact. The island produces nothing and ships nothing. It exists to put people within casting distance of the tide.

## Flight Context

Stuart Island lies at 50.3883 N, 125.1228 W, at the mouth of Bute Inlet among the Discovery Islands. Treat the island's roughly 2,100 ft paved strip as unavailable: it is private and appears on no aeronautical chart or in the Canada Flight Supplement. Big Bay Water Aerodrome is the listed facility, in the Water Aerodrome Supplement. Campbell River Airport (CYBL) is about 50 km south-southeast and is the practical base for the area. Fly the approach at 2,000-4,000 ft and use the obvious features: Sonora Island immediately west, the long dark trench of Bute Inlet running northeast between 2,700 m peaks, and the Homathko River delta at its head. This is serious mountain and fjord terrain - expect strong channelled winds and outflow down the inlet in winter, rapid cloud build-up on the ridges, and turbulence in the narrows even on fair days.
