# Malaspina Peninsula

A mountainous finger of Tla'amin territory reaching north to Desolation Sound, carrying the name of a Spanish-navy explorer who was tried, imprisoned and erased from the record he helped write.

**Location:** 50.0128, -124.7683

**Categories:** Peninsulas, Coastal, Exploration History, Indigenous Culture, British Columbia

## Introduction

Alessandro Malaspina charted more of the Pacific than almost anyone of his generation and was rewarded with six years in a fortress. Born into Italian nobility in 1754, he took a commission in the Spanish navy and between 1789 and 1794 led an expedition that mapped the American coast from Cape Horn to Alaska and carried on to the Philippines, New Zealand and Australia, gathering scientific material that later observers judged to surpass Cook's. Then he went home, suggested that Spain might let its American colonies go, and was arrested. His findings were suppressed and scattered through archives for the better part of two centuries. Alexander von Humboldt said he became more famous for his misfortunes than for his discoveries. And yet his name is written all over this coast - on a strait, an inlet, an Alaskan glacier, and on this long mountainous peninsula running up toward Desolation Sound.

## Named at a Distance

Malaspina himself never walked here. The naming came later and second-hand. In 1792 the Spanish officers Galiano and Valdes - both of whom had served under him - put his name on a nearby inlet. Sixty-seven years after that, Captain George Henry Richards of HMS Plumper was surveying this stretch of coast for the Royal Navy and, needing labels for a strait and a peninsula, borrowed the name already sitting on the chart beside them. By then Malaspina had been dead for nearly half a century, having died in exile at Pontremoli in 1810, stripped of rank, his expedition still unpublished. The geography of British Columbia remembers him rather better than Spain did.

## Tla'amin Ground

None of these names are the oldest ones here. The Malaspina Peninsula is the traditional territory of the Tla'amin - the Sliammon, one of the Mainland Comox peoples - and has been for far longer than any chart has existed. Their village at the peninsula's northern end, ƛaʔamɩn, gave the nation its name and stood on the harbour long before European sails appeared in the strait. The land itself is steep and forested, a mountainous spine rather than a low coastal shelf, its shoreline broken into coves and small islands. To read it only through the names a passing navy handed out in the 1790s and 1850s is to read the last page of a very long book.

## Road's End

At the top of the peninsula, Highway 101 simply stops. The Sunshine Coast Highway runs out at Lund, a harbour of fewer than 300 people, and there is no more pavement going north. Two Swedish brothers, Frederick and Charles Thulin, founded the settlement in December 1889 and named it after their hometown, reasoning that Lund was Swedish and easy enough for non-Scandinavians to say. Their hotel opened in 1905, burned in a forest fire in 1918, and was succeeded by the Malaspina Hotel, later renamed the Lund Hotel - still the village landmark. The road south to Vancouver was not paved until 1954. Powell River, the peninsula's main town, sits 22 kilometres down the coast near the isthmus where the peninsula joins the mainland.

## Where the Water Takes Over

North and west of Lund the road ends but the geography does not. Boats leave the harbour for Desolation Sound Marine Provincial Park, the Copeland Islands, and the water-taxi run out to Savary Island. Desolation Sound opens across the mouth of Toba Inlet, a fjord cutting inland between the peninsula and the Discovery Islands, and the protected warm water of the sound has made it one of the most sought-after cruising grounds on the west coast. From the air the transition is abrupt: forested ridgeline, a scatter of docks, then a maze of islands, inlets and deep green channels running back into the Coast Mountains.

## The Trail Down the Spine

There is a way to travel the peninsula on foot, and it is a good one. The Sunshine Coast Trail runs 180 kilometres from Sarah Point on Desolation Sound, at the peninsula's northern tip, south to the ferry terminal at Saltery Bay on Jervis Inlet. Fourteen public huts stand along it, with more planned; most are free and maintained by volunteers, which makes it the longest hut-to-hut hiking trail in Canada. It exists because a group of Powell River residents formed a parks and wilderness society in 1992 to protect old-growth forest and decided the most durable form of protection was a trail people would actually use. The route was finished in 2000, and the huts began going up in 2009 - a piece of public infrastructure built almost entirely by people who wanted somewhere to walk.

## Flight Context

The Malaspina Peninsula centres near 50.0128 N, 124.7683 W, extending northwest from Powell River on British Columbia's upper Sunshine Coast to Sarah Point on Desolation Sound. From the air it reads as a steep forested finger between the Strait of Georgia to the west and Okeover and Malaspina Inlets cutting in from the northeast; Savary Island and the Copeland Islands lie off its western shore, the Discovery Islands sit across the water to the northwest, and the deep trench of Toba Inlet runs inland beyond Desolation Sound. Powell River, with its waterfront mill site and ferry terminal, marks the southern isthmus, and Lund is the small harbour at the top where Highway 101 ends. Powell River Airport (CYPW) is the local field, about 5 nm south of the peninsula's neck; Campbell River (CYBL) and Comox (CYQQ) lie across the strait on Vancouver Island. A viewing altitude of 4,500 to 6,500 feet MSL shows the full peninsula with Desolation Sound and the Coast Mountains behind it. Inflow winds funnel hard up the inlets on summer afternoons, and cloud often hangs on the peninsula's spine when the strait below is clear.
