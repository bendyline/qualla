# East Redonda Island

A Spanish name meaning round, attached to an island that is nothing of the kind - and half of it set aside as a 6,212-hectare ecological reserve above Desolation Sound.

**Location:** 50.2422, -124.7106

**Categories:** british-columbia, discovery-islands, island, wilderness, conservation, coastal

## Introduction

The Spanish got it wrong, and the name stuck anyway. When Galiano and Valdés worked their way up this coast in 1792, they took a distant look at two large islands north of what is now Desolation Sound and entered them on the chart as Isla Redonda - the round island. Sail into the middle of East Redonda and you find something closer to the opposite: a hooked, lobed mass of rock with a long sound driven deep into its flank, a shape that maps rather better onto Orcas Island down in the San Juans than onto anything circular. Two centuries on, the misnomer is the official name of an island where almost nobody lives and half the land is legally off limits.

## Round, They Said

Charting this coast from a small vessel in 1792 was a matter of glimpses. Galiano and Valdés were working a shoreline of near-identical forested walls, in weather that hides as much as it shows, and from certain angles a mountainous island does read as a dome on the horizon. Redonda, they wrote. Round.

What they had actually found was two islands, now East and West Redonda, split by Waddington Channel. East Redonda is the taller and stranger of the pair. Pendrell Sound cuts into it from Waddington Channel and runs northeast far enough to nearly bisect the island, leaving arms of high ground on either side. Standing at the head of that sound, walled in by timber on both sides, the word round is the last one that would occur to you.

## Channels on Every Side

East Redonda is defined by the water around it more than by anything on it. Waddington Channel separates it from West Redonda to the west. Homfray Channel, deep and dark, runs down its eastern side between the island and the mainland coast at the mouth of Toba Inlet. Pryce Channel closes off the north end.

These are not ordinary straits. They are drowned glacial trenches, gouged out when ice filled this country and then flooded when the sea came back, which is why the water goes down so abruptly so close to shore. The northern limit of Pendrell Sound marks part of the northern boundary of the Salish Sea itself - the point where that great inland sea gives way to the mainland fjords. Cross it and you are in a different kind of coast: steeper, colder, emptier, running back into the Coast Mountains through inlets that go on for days.

## Mount Addenbroke

The island's high point is Mount Addenbroke, 1,591 metres - 5,220 feet - and the number understates it, because there is no foothill, no valley floor, no plateau to start from. The mountain begins at sea level and stops at 1,591 metres, and everything in between is forest clinging to a slope that would rather shed it.

This is the standard arrangement on the BC coast and it never stops being startling. Peaks that would be unremarkable inland become vertiginous when their base is the tideline. Boats passing through Waddington Channel sit in the shadow of a full kilometre and a half of rising ground, close enough that the summit is out of sight above the gunwales. Snow lingers up there into early summer while the shoreline below stays green and dripping.

## Half an Island, Left Alone

The eastern half of East Redonda is the East Redonda Island Ecological Reserve, 6,212 hectares protected for forestry research and the study of forest growth. No logging, no development, no resort - just a very large piece of coastal rainforest permitted to do what it would have done anyway, observed rather than managed.

That makes it a control sample in a landscape where almost nothing else is. The BC coast has been cut over, replanted, cut again; clear-cut geometry shows up on hillsides in every direction from here. Inside the reserve boundary the forest simply keeps going, on slopes too steep and ground too broken for anyone to have argued hard about. The island falls administratively under Electoral Area C of the Strathcona Regional District, which is a very bureaucratic way of saying that hardly anyone is here to administer.

## Flight Context

East Redonda Island lies at 50.242N, 124.711W in the Discovery Islands, just north of Desolation Sound and west of the mouth of Toba Inlet. The best altitude is 5,000 to 8,000 feet, high enough to see the whole shape at once: Mount Addenbroke at 5,220 feet forming the summit, Pendrell Sound driving northeast into the island from Waddington Channel, and Homfray Channel running along the eastern shore against the mainland wall. West Redonda sits immediately across Waddington Channel; Pryce Channel closes the north end. There are no airstrips on the island - nearest fields are Campbell River (CYBL) some 35 nm southwest across the Discovery Passage, Powell River (CYPW) to the south, and Comox (CYQQ) further down the Strait of Georgia; floatplane traffic serves Refuge Cove and the Desolation Sound anchorages in summer. Watch for inflow and outflow winds accelerating through the channels, rapid cloud build on Addenbroke, and mechanical turbulence in the lee of the ridges.
