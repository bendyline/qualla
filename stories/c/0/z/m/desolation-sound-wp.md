# Desolation Sound

George Vancouver found nothing here that pleased his eye and named it accordingly; the Tla'amin, Klahoose, and Homalco peoples had been living in it for thousands of years, and it is now one of the most sought-after cruising grounds on the Pacific coast.

**Location:** 50.1222, -124.7583

**Categories:** Sounds (geography) of British Columbia, Salish Sea, Marine provincial parks, Coast Salish, Exploration of the Pacific Northwest

## Introduction

In the summer of 1792, Captain George Vancouver worked his way into a deep-water sound at the top of the Salish Sea and did not care for it. "There was not a single prospect that was pleasing to the eye," he wrote, and then, with the finality available to men holding charts and pens, he named the place Desolation Sound. The verdict has puzzled readers ever since. Steep granite walls drop straight into green water, waterfalls come off the Redondas after rain, and the Coast Mountains stack up behind everything. Today boaters queue for the anchorages. Whatever Vancouver was seeing in that particular week, it was not the landscape everyone else has seen since.

## The Worst Review on the Coast

Two surveying expeditions met here that summer and, unusually for the era, decided to cooperate. Vancouver's British party and the Spanish vessels under Dionisio Alcala Galiano and Cayetano Valdes y Flores compared notes, shared boats and soundings, and mapped the sound together -- an outbreak of professional courtesy between empires that were, in most other respects, rivals for the whole coast. The sound they charted is genuinely difficult water for a sailing ship. It is deep almost to the shoreline, with little bottom to hold an anchor and mountains that block and funnel the wind. Vancouver's men had spent months in small boats in the rain. Read his sentence as a survey note rather than an aesthetic judgment and it makes more sense: from the deck of a becalmed ship with no safe place to lie for the night, desolation is a reasonable word.

## Older Names, Older Claims

The sound was not empty and never had been. It lies within the traditional territories of the Klahoose, Tla'amin, and Homalco First Nations, and Tla'amin people have lived in and around this water for thousands of years -- harvesting clams from managed beaches, taking salmon at the river mouths, moving between winter villages and summer camps along shorelines that European charts would later describe as uninhabited. Vancouver named what he was looking at after his own mood. He was, at that moment, sailing past an occupied and thoroughly known landscape whose inhabitants had their own names for every channel, cove, and headland he was busy relabelling. Both sets of names survive. Only one of them is printed on the chart.

## A Maze of Islands and Channels

Geographically, Desolation Sound is a junction. Cortes Island lies to the west, East Redonda and West Redonda to the north and east, and the water splits into Lewis Channel, Waddington Channel, and Homfray Channel, with Okeover Inlet reaching southeast and the Strait of Georgia opening south. The result is a nested set of protected basins -- Prideaux Haven off Homfray Channel, Tenedos Bay, Grace Harbour, Gorge Harbour on Cortes -- where a boat can lie still while the open strait a few miles away is whitecapping. British Columbia recognized what it had: Desolation Sound Marine Provincial Park, roughly 84 square kilometres of shoreline and seabed, was created on May 18, 1973, and Malaspina, Okeover Arm, and Roscoe Bay parks followed the same logic of protecting the edges where land meets water.

## Refuge Cove and the Long Way In

Only one community exists in the whole sound. Refuge Cove, on West Redonda Island and administered from the Strathcona Regional District, is a store, a fuel dock, and a cluster of buildings on floats and pilings -- the central supply stop for everyone working or cruising these waters. Getting there takes commitment. By car it means two BC Ferries crossings, from Vancouver or from Courtenay, to reach Powell River, then a short drive north to Lund where the highway simply stops. From Lund you go by boat or by kayak, past the Copeland Islands and up the coast. Shellfish farming, forestry, and ecotourism keep the place economically alive, an unusual combination of industries that all depend on the same water staying clean. The difficulty of arrival is the point. Desolation Sound has stayed what it is largely because it is hard to get to and always has been.

## Flight Context

Desolation Sound is centred near 50.122N, 124.758W, at the northern end of the Strait of Georgia where the Salish Sea narrows into the coastal channels. From the air it is unmistakable: a dark, deep-water basin ringed by steep forested islands, with Cortes Island to the west, the two Redonda Islands forming a rough horseshoe to the north and east, and the snowfields of the Coast Mountains rising immediately behind Homfray Channel. The fjord-like channels radiate outward like cracks. Lund and the Copeland Islands mark the mainland approach to the south; Refuge Cove appears as a small cluster of docks on West Redonda's western shore. Best viewed at 3,000-5,000 feet to hold the island maze and the mountain backdrop in one frame. Nearest airports: Powell River Airport (CYPW) about 20nm southwest, Campbell River Airport (CYBL) about 25nm west across Vancouver Island, and Comox Airport (CYQQ) about 35nm southwest. Expect mechanical turbulence near the channel walls and rapid cloud formation against the Coast Range on summer afternoons.
