# Sechelt Peninsula

A mainland coast reachable only by boat, where a tidal rapid moves 760 million cubic metres of seawater a day and the oldest tree ever recorded in Canada was felled before anyone counted its rings.

**Location:** 49.5991, -123.9240

**Categories:** peninsulas, tidal-rapids, old-growth-forests, indigenous-nations, british-columbia

## Introduction

Skookumchuck is Chinook Jargon for strong water, and on this peninsula it functions as a warning label rather than a name. Twice a day the Pacific tries to fill Sechelt Inlet through a gap barely wide enough to allow it, and the result is a standing wall of whitewater in the middle of what is otherwise placid saltwater fjord country. On a three-metre tide, roughly 760 million cubic metres of seawater force through the Skookumchuck Narrows. The current can exceed sixteen knots. Kayakers paddle out to surf a wave that exists nowhere else on the coast, and boaters who misjudge the tide table learn a permanent lesson about the difference between a channel and a drain.

## The Only Door Into Sechelt Inlet

Sechelt Rapids -- the Skookumchuck -- is the sole water entrance to the entire Sechelt Inlet system, which means every vessel that goes in must wait for slack and every vessel that comes out must do the same. At full flow the rapids become a field of boiling cauldrons with overfalls dropping eight feet, standing waves that reverse direction with the tide, and whirlpools large enough to turn a small boat broadside. The strangest part is the timing. Slack water lasts a matter of minutes, and the transition from impassable to glassy happens fast enough to feel like a trick. Most visitors arrive on foot instead, walking the four-kilometre trail through second-growth forest to the viewpoints at Roland Point and North Point, where the roar reaches you well before the water does.

## At the Isthmus, a Nation Wrote Its Own Law

The peninsula's only incorporated district sits at the isthmus, on the narrow neck of land between Sechelt Inlet and the Strait of Georgia -- a few hundred metres of gravel separating an inland sea from an outer one. This is shishalh territory, and in 1986 the nation did something no other First Nation in Canada had managed. Members voted in a referendum on 15 March of that year to accept the terms of what became the Sechelt Indian Band Self-Government Act, and with it the band stepped outside the Indian Act entirely. The legislation transferred lands to the nation's own control, recognized a band constitution, and granted governmental powers comparable to those of a municipality. It was the first agreement of its kind in the country, and the federal statute now carries the name the nation uses for itself: the shishalh Nation Self-Government Act.

## The Oldest Tree in Canada Is a Stump

The Caren Range runs north to south along the Sechelt Inlet shore, and its upper slopes hold something rare: a high-elevation old-growth forest that the industrial logging of the twentieth century reached late. Spipiyus Provincial Park now protects 2,979 hectares of it, including the oldest documented specimens of yellow cedar, mountain hemlock, and western hemlock anywhere in the world. That superlative comes with a wound attached. In 1993, researchers in the range counted the rings on a yellow cedar stump and arrived at 1,835 years -- a tree that had been standing since the Roman Empire, and that had been cut down in a large clearcut in the 1980s. Canada's oldest known tree was identified only after it had been logged. The park exists in large part because of what that stump revealed, which is a bleak way to found a protected area but not an unusual one.

## A Mainland You Cannot Drive To

For roughly 350 square kilometres, the Sechelt Peninsula does a convincing impression of an island. Malaspina Strait separates it from Texada Island to the west; Agamemnon Channel and Jervis Inlet cut it off to the north; Sechelt Inlet runs down its eastern flank; the Strait of Georgia opens south toward Vancouver Island. It is attached to the mainland, and it is not reachable from the mainland highway network -- you arrive by ferry or you do not arrive. About 28,000 people live here, strung out along Highway 101 as it traces the southern and western shore through Halfmoon Bay, Secret Cove, Madeira Park, Pender Harbour, and Egmont, before ending at the Earls Cove terminal where another ferry carries traffic north to Powell River and Lund. Tourism and logging carry the economy. The forest shifts between drier and wetter temperate rainforest as you cross the interior, the lakes are numerous and mostly unsigned, and the whole peninsula rewards the specific kind of traveler willing to wait for a boat.

## Flight Context

Centered near 49.60 N, 123.92 W on British Columbia's Sunshine Coast, roughly 30 nm northwest of Vancouver. From altitude the peninsula is unmistakable: a wedge of forested land nearly severed from the mainland, with the long dagger of Sechelt Inlet running up its eastern side and the narrow isthmus at Sechelt pinching almost through. Texada Island lies west across Malaspina Strait, Nelson Island north across Agamemnon Channel, and the deep trench of Jervis Inlet runs northeast into the Coast Mountains. The Skookumchuck Narrows at the head of the inlet system are visible as a churned white line on a running tide -- time your overflight to the tide table for the best view. Terrain rises to the Caren Range along the inlet shore. Best viewed at 3,000-5,000 ft AGL. Sechelt Aerodrome (CAP3) sits on the isthmus; Powell River Airport (CYPW) is about 25 nm northwest, and Vancouver International (CYVR) roughly 30 nm southeast -- check Vancouver terminal airspace before transiting south. Expect marine stratus, inflow winds funneling up the inlets on summer afternoons, and mechanical turbulence near the Coast Mountain walls.
