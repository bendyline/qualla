# Pender Harbour, British Columbia

ḵalpilin was one of the most powerful places in shíshálh territory before settlers renamed it for a naval surveyor; today its sixty kilometres of hidden shoreline hold longboard racers, a folk legend, and a much older story.

**Location:** 49.6250, -124.0330

**Categories:** Populated places in the Sunshine Coast Regional District, Unincorporated settlements in British Columbia, Marinas in Canada, Transport buildings and structures in British Columbia

## Introduction

The harbour reaches only five kilometres inland, yet it has more than sixty kilometres of shoreline. That arithmetic explains almost everything about this place. Pender Harbour is not one bay but a puzzle of them — coves folding into coves, rocky islets, narrow passes that open into sheltered water where a canoe or a boat can lie still while a southeaster tears down Malaspina Strait outside. The shíshálh people call it ḵalpilin, and long before any European saw it, this intricate geometry made the harbour one of the most populous and powerful regions in the shíshálh swiya — their world, their territory.

## The Gateway Village

Several villages shared ḵalpilin, each with its own name and its own ground: sex̱wʔamin at what is now Garden Bay, p'ukwp'akwem at Bargain Harbour, salalus at Madeira Park, smishalin at Kleindale, kwayahkuhlohss at Myer's Creek. Of these, sex̱wʔamin mattered most. It was occupied year-round and it sat at a hinge in the coastal road — the water road — where routes along sinkwu, the Georgia and Malaspina straits, met the passage into lekw'emin, Jervis Inlet, by way of lilkw'emin, Agamemnon Channel. Whoever lived at that hinge controlled movement. Together with klayahkwohss on Thormanby Island to the south, sex̱wʔamin was also where the winter dances and ceremonials were held, when the work of summer was done and the longhouses filled with people, firelight, and song.

## The Islets Without Water

As European settlement expanded through the harbour in the early twentieth century, many shíshálh residents were forced out of the villages their families had held for generations and moved to sexaliten, the Skardon Islands — small islets at the mouth of the harbour. The islets have no fresh water. Every drop had to be carried in by boat. It is worth pausing on the ordinariness of that arrangement, because nothing about it was an accident or an oversight: people who had lived at the centre of a territory were relocated to its least habitable edge, and the paperwork that accomplished it would have looked routine to the men who signed it. The shíshálh Nation is still here, still governing this territory, and the village names above are the ones its own land use plan uses.

## Named for a Man Who Passed Through

In 1860, Captain G.H. Richards of the Royal Navy attached a new name to the harbour, honouring Daniel Pender, who had arrived on this coast on 9 November 1857 as second master of a survey vessel. Pender went on to serve as master of the Plumper and the Hecate, and from 1863 to 1870 commanded the Beaver — the old Hudson's Bay Company steamer, hired for hydrographic work — charting the inlets and reefs that ships had until then navigated largely by nerve. He finished his career at a desk in the Hydrographic Office in London between 1871 and 1884. His charts were genuinely good. His name is on a harbour he surveyed rather than lived in, which is how most of this coastline got labelled.

## Steamers, Sawdust, and the Doctor

For a long stretch of the twentieth century Pender Harbour was a working place: a scheduled steamer stop on the coastal run, a fishing village whose boats filled the coves, and a logging centre. It was also a medical waypoint — the place people came from up and down a roadless shore when something went wrong, in an era when the nearest hospital might be a day's travel by water. That practical role shaped the community's geography. Docks, stores, and services clustered where boats could reach them, which is why the modern settlements of Madeira Park, Garden Bay, Irvines Landing, and Kleindale still read as separate villages rather than one town.

## Under Three Thousand

The permanent population now sits under 3,000, and more than 40 percent of property owners do not live here — a pattern that has hollowed out plenty of BC coastal communities and given this one a summer-and-winter double life. The arts community is real, not decorative: several music festivals run annually, and the harbour hosts the second-oldest May Day celebration in British Columbia. It also hosts the loudest thing on the Sunshine Coast calendar — Attack of Danger Bay, the biggest and longest-running downhill longboard race in Canada, where riders tuck through corners on a closed road at speeds that make spectators wince. Tourism pays a good share of the bills. The water still does the rest.

## Flight Context

Pender Harbour lies at 49.625°N, 124.033°W on the east side of Malaspina Strait, roughly midway up British Columbia's Sunshine Coast. Fly it at 2,000 to 4,000 feet: high enough to resolve the whole labyrinth of bays at once, which is the entire visual point of this place. From the air the harbour is unmistakable — a lacework of inlets biting into the shore, with the Skardon Islands and Francis Peninsula guarding the entrance and Thormanby Island lying offshore to the south. Look northeast for Agamemnon Channel, the narrow slot leading into Jervis Inlet and, beyond it, the walls of Princess Louisa Inlet. The Coast Mountains rise sharply inland and Texada Island fills the strait to the west. Nearest fields are Sechelt Aerodrome (CAP3) about 20 nautical miles southeast and Powell River (CYPW) to the northwest; floatplanes serve the harbour directly from Vancouver Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYHC), and Vancouver International (CYVR) is the major gateway. Watch for strong southeast winds funnelling up Malaspina Strait in winter storms and for afternoon inflow in summer; morning fog is common in the inlets from late August onward.
