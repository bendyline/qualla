# Kitty Coleman Provincial Park

A ten-hectare beach park on the Comox Valley shore, named for a We Wai Kai woman who spent her last years living alone on this sand, selling fish and berries.

**Location:** 49.7889, -124.9960

**Categories:** Provincial Parks, British Columbia, Vancouver Island, Beaches, First Nations, Old-Growth Forests

## Introduction

She died in 1918, around seventy years old, and within a year the neighbours had named the beach after her. That is an unusual thing for a settler community to do in British Columbia in 1919. Kitty Coleman was from Cape Mudge, a member of the We Wai Kai nation, and the surviving account of her life is short and mostly told by other people: she left her community to marry a white man, he was later jailed, and afterward she lived alone on this stretch of shoreline, selling fish and berries to whoever came by. That is the whole documented record of a life that ran seven decades. What remains is a name on a provincial park sign, a beach, a creek, and a stand of very old trees - a memorial assembled by people who apparently thought she mattered, even if they left almost nothing behind about why.

## The Edges of a Record

It is worth sitting with how little we know. The phrasing that survives in most accounts - that she "left her tribe" to marry - is the language of the people writing it down, not hers, and it flattens what was almost certainly a complicated decision made under colonial pressure that no one bothered to describe. Marrying a non-Indigenous man in that era carried legal consequences for Indigenous women in Canada, including the loss of status and the right to live in their own communities. Whether that shaped her years on this beach, nobody wrote down. What the record does say is that she supported herself, that she was known well enough by name to be remembered, and that when she died the community she lived beside chose to attach her name permanently to the place. In a province where nearly every feature was named for a naval officer or a colonial administrator, that is a small anomaly worth noticing.

## A Park the Neighbours Run

The beach became a community park in 1919 and a provincial park on 14 November 1944. Its official designation is Class C, which in British Columbia means something specific: the park is managed not by the province directly but by a local community board. Fewer than a handful of BC parks carry that status. The practical effect is visible the moment you arrive. There are pit toilets and no sani-station, no hookups for motorhomes, and about 0.1 square kilometres of ground to work with. It has stayed modest because the people running it never had a reason to make it otherwise. In 2014 the campground marked seventy years as a provincial park, still operating under the same local arrangement.

## The Old Tree

The southern section of the park holds Douglas firs of a size the Comox Valley has mostly lost. One of them, known locally as the Kitty Coleman Fir, is estimated at over five hundred years old and ranks among the largest trees left standing in the valley - a remnant of the forest that covered this coastline before the logging boom reorganized it. Around it grow western red cedar and western hemlock, the standard cast of a coastal Douglas fir ecosystem, and wild onions come up through the understory in season. The tree was already centuries old when Kitty Coleman was born. It is the one thing in the park that predates every human story attached to the place.

## Facing the Strait

The beach here is rocky rather than soft, sloping into the Strait of Georgia with the campsites set directly behind it - close enough that you can hear the water from a tent. Look east and the far shore is the Powell River coast on the mainland, close enough on a clear day to pick out the mill site and the settlements strung along the water. Ships bound up and down the strait pass in the middle distance. The park sits just south of the mouth of the Oyster River, northeast of Courtenay, on a shoreline that stays reliably mild and reliably grey for much of the year. It is not a dramatic landscape. It is a working one - a beach where someone once made a living on fish and berries, now a place where families come to sleep beside the same water.

## Flight Context

Kitty Coleman Provincial Park sits at 49.789 N, 124.996 W on the east coast of Vancouver Island, facing the Strait of Georgia. A viewing altitude of 1,500-3,000 feet MSL works best for a small coastal park like this: the campground clearing, the rocky beach, and the dark block of mature forest to the south are all resolvable, with the Powell River mainland shore across roughly 15 nm of open water. The mouth of the Oyster River a short distance north is the cleanest navigational landmark, and the Courtenay-Comox urban area lies to the southeast. CFB Comox / Comox Valley Airport (CYQQ) is about 8 nm southeast and is an active military field - check airspace before descending. Campbell River (CYBL) lies about 15 nm northwest and Powell River (CYPW) sits directly across the strait. Expect low cloud and rain from autumn through spring; summer brings reliable visibility and frequent afternoon inflow winds up the strait.
