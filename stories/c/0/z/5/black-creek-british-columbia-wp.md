# Black Creek, British Columbia

Mennonite families fleeing the Soviet Union were handed a stump field on Vancouver Island where some of the world's largest Douglas firs had stood, and turned it into dairy farms.

**Location:** 49.8333, -125.1330

**Categories:** settlement-history, mennonite-heritage, logging-history, vancouver-island, agriculture

## Introduction

By the time the first Mennonite families reached Black Creek in 1932, the land had already surrendered the thing that made it famous. Some of the largest Douglas firs on Earth had grown on this stretch of eastern Vancouver Island, and the Comox Logging & Railway Company had taken them. What remained was stony ground studded with stumps the size of small rooms, a grid of abandoned rail grades, and a price low enough that people arriving with almost nothing could afford it. The settlers who came were German-speaking Mennonites, most of them a step or two removed from villages in the Soviet Union. They looked at a clearcut and saw farmland, which required a particular kind of stubbornness.

## Camp Number Three

In the early years of the twentieth century, Black Creek was a company address rather than a town. Several logging camps operated here, among them Comox Logging & Railway Camp #3, which stood on what is now Endall Road. The camps were temporary by design: bunkhouses on skids, a cookhouse, rails that moved as the cutting face moved. Almost nothing of that world was built to outlast the timber it existed to harvest. One fragment did. Part of the old camp bunkhouse still stands, absorbed into a local estate home -- a wall of logger's lodging now doing quiet service inside somebody's house. Endall Road ends where it always ended, and a public easement stitches it through to Hamm Road, which is how you get the shape of a place that was mapped by a railway and then re-mapped by farmers.

## Arriving After the Trees

The Mennonites who settled Black Creek came directly from the Canadian Prairie provinces, and some by way of Mexico, but they belonged to a larger displacement: the exodus of Mennonite communities from the Soviet Union, where collectivization and religious repression had made a centuries-old agricultural way of life impossible. They arrived in the depth of the Depression and were offered land nobody else wanted. Henry and Elisabeth Schulz, Jacob and Margaret Brucks, Aron and Justina Enns, William and Elizabeth Hamm, Jacob and Maria Dirks, and a handful of other families broke that ground into dairy pasture and fruit orchards. They founded the United Mennonite Church in 1935, three years after the first arrivals -- a congregation before a general store. Farming alone did not pay at first, so the men also worked the logging camps and lumber mills that had stripped the valley in the first place.

## German Until the Sixties

The community grew by invitation. Through the 1950s, Black Creek Mennonites sponsored waves of Austrian and German immigrants, and the valley's daily life ran on a working mixture of German and English well into the 1960s -- sermons, kitchen arguments, and schoolyard negotiations all shifting between the two. A settlement that conservative and that church-centered might have turned inward. Instead it turned musical. Black Creek families became a disproportionate presence in the academic and musical life of the Comox Valley, and especially in its high schools, where choir rooms and honour rolls filled with names that had crossed an ocean and a continent within living memory. There is an old Mennonite pattern in that: four-part singing is not a hobby in these communities, it is closer to a native language.

## Along the Old Island Highway

Two Mennonite churches still operate in Black Creek, the United Mennonite and the Mennonite Brethren, though only a few of the founding families remain in the area. What the rest left behind is visible from the road. The Old Island Highway runs past small, meticulously kept farms and gardens on ground that took generations of stump-pulling to make arable, and the effort shows in the geometry -- fields squared off against a landscape that did not want to be squared off. Black Creek today sits sixteen kilometres north of Courtenay and about twenty south of Campbell River, which makes it a bedroom community as much as an agricultural one, part of Electoral Area C in the Comox Valley Regional District. The climate is mild oceanic, saved from a Mediterranean classification by a driest month that still delivers just enough rain. Dry enough for fruit trees, wet enough for pasture. The settlers could not have known that when they bought in. It turned out to be the one thing the place gave them for free.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.83 N, 125.13 W on the eastern coastal plain of Vancouver Island, at low elevation between the Strait of Georgia and the Vancouver Island Ranges. From the air Black Creek reads as a patchwork of small rectangular farm fields and pasture -- a conspicuous break in the otherwise continuous forest and second-growth of this coast -- threaded by the Old Island Highway running northwest to southeast. Miracle Beach and the shoreline of the strait lie a short distance east, with Mitlenatch Island and the mainland Coast Mountains beyond. Best viewed at 2,000-3,500 ft AGL. Campbell River Airport (CYBL) is roughly 11 nm northwest; CFB Comox / Comox Valley Airport (CYQQ) sits about 12 nm southeast, and its control zone should be checked before transiting. This eastern shore lies in the island's rain shadow, so VFR conditions here are typically far better than on the west coast, though winter stratus and morning fog off the strait are routine.
