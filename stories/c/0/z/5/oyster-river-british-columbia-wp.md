# Oyster River, British Columbia

A community of about 1,500 on Vancouver Island's dry eastern shore, where volunteers rewrote a salmon run's two-year clock and the whole town shops at one grocery store.

**Location:** 49.8728, -125.1300

**Categories:** Small Towns, British Columbia, Rivers and Estuaries, Wildlife, Fishing, Coastal

## Introduction

Pink salmon are creatures of a strict two-year cycle. They hatch, go to sea, come back, spawn, and die on a schedule so rigid that most rivers on this coast see them only in odd-numbered years, or only in even ones - never both. The Oyster River is an exception. Here, thanks to decades of volunteer hatchery work, the pinks come home every single year, and in late summer the estuary at the south end of this small Vancouver Island community fills with fish that biology alone would not have delivered.

## Resetting a Two-Year Clock

The Oyster River Enhancement Society formed in 1983, a volunteer outfit set up to do something about declining stocks in a watershed that people here had fished and lived beside for generations. The work is unglamorous: rearing fry, releasing them, mending spawning habitat, keeping records. It also worked. Since 1996 a typical year brings something in the range of 30,000 to 50,000 spawning salmon back to the river - pink, coho, chinook, and chum together - and the annual release of pink fry has effectively overwritten the species' usual biennial rhythm. Eagles know this. So do black bears, river otters, and a fair number of people standing in waders in August. The estuary and the whole oceanfront of the community turn over to the run when it arrives.

## Three Bridges and a Boundary

Geography here is a matter of lines that only locals can see. The river itself draws the community's southern border, separating Oyster River from Black Creek and, more formally, marking the edge of the Comox Valley Regional District. Three northbound bridges cross the water, and crossing them means leaving one district and entering another. To the north, between Oyster River and the Campbell River city limits, sits an even smaller place called Stories Beach. Together the two make up Area D of the Strathcona Regional District - unincorporated, independent of the city, and reliant on steady cooperation with it for the ordinary services a community needs. Campbell River lies 12 kilometres north; Black Creek is 3 kilometres south. Highway 19A threads through the middle of all of it.

## The Store That Anchors the Place

Roughly 1,500 people live here, and the commercial sector is exactly as small as that number suggests. Campgrounds and resorts do most of the economic work - Salmon Point Resort on the shore, Pacific Playgrounds down on the banks of the river - filling up in summer and emptying out after Labour Day. The one substantial business is a grocery store, Discovery Foods, owned and run by three local families, which employs a meaningful share of the working residents of both Oyster River and Black Creek. A restaurant, a liquor store, and a medical clinic share the same complex. In a place without a town centre, that complex is the town centre, which is a distinction worth noticing rather than apologizing for.

## In the Rain Shadow

Vancouver Island's west coast is one of the wettest places in North America. This side is not. The mountain spine running down the island wrings the Pacific moisture out before it gets here, and the result at Oyster River is a warm-summer Mediterranean climate - both July and August average under 40 millimetres of rain. That number does more to explain the campgrounds, the summer swimming holes, and the retirees than any tourism brochure could. The river is accessible at several points, including the old Highway 19B bridge and York Road, and on a hot August afternoon it is full of people cooling off in water that will, in a few weeks, be full of salmon instead. Rain returns in force between November and March, and it is that winter water - pouring off the Vancouver Island Ranges, scouring gravel, carrying nutrients down to the tideline - that keeps the whole system working. The dry summers people move here for and the wet winters the fish depend on are the same climate, viewed from opposite ends of the calendar.

## Flight Context

Oyster River sits at 49.8728°N, 125.1300°W on the eastern shore of Vancouver Island, facing the Strait of Georgia. From the air, look for the river's distinct estuary fan pushing into the strait, with the ribbon of Highway 19A hugging the coastline and the Inland Island Highway (Highway 19) running parallel a few kilometres inland. Campbell River's larger urban footprint lies 12 km north; the broad agricultural flats of the Comox Valley open to the south. The snow-streaked peaks of Strathcona Provincial Park rise to the west, and Quadra and Cortes islands fill the water to the northeast. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,500-4,000 ft MSL for the estuary and shoreline detail, or 8,000 ft to frame the community against Strathcona's summits. Campbell River Airport (CYBL) is roughly 8 nm north and Comox (CYQQ) about 15 nm southeast. This is the island's rain-shadow side, so summer visibility is often excellent; winter brings low cloud and frequent southeast wind down the strait.
