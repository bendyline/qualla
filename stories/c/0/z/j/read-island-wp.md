# Read Island

A Discovery Island named after a clerk who never saw it, briefly notorious for whisky and killing, now home to about eighty people and some of the fastest water on the coast.

**Location:** 50.2314, -125.0864

**Categories:** Islands of the Discovery Islands, Islands of British Columbia, Strathcona Regional District, Coastal British Columbia

## Introduction

On June 24, 1893, a man calling himself Ben Kennedy sailed his sloop into Read Island carrying sixty cases of whisky, two Colt revolvers, a hunting knife, and a dog. He had bought the liquor at the Coleman and Evans wharf in Vancouver for a hundred and twenty dollars, and he was headed for a logging camp at White Rock Bay on the island's northwest shore, where several dozen men had money and nothing to spend it on. Kennedy also went by Jack Myer and by Bart McKenzie, depending on who was asking. Over the following months Read Island and neighbouring Savary Island became the setting for a series of killings ugly enough that they were still being written about a century later. That was the island's brief moment of notoriety. Before and after it, Read has mostly been a quiet place at the edge of some very loud water.

## Named From a Desk in London

Daniel Pender, commanding the steamer Beaver on hydrographic survey duty, put the name Read Island on the chart around 1864. He was honouring Captain William Viner Read, a naval assistant at the United Kingdom Hydrographic Office, who as far as anyone knows never came within six thousand miles of the place. The island's southern tip became Viner Point to complete the tribute. This was standard practice for British surveyors on this coast, who distributed the names of superiors, colleagues, and the occasional patron across a landscape that already had names, given by the Coast Salish peoples who had used these waters for thousands of years and who are not commemorated anywhere on the modern chart of this particular island.

## Whisky, Timber, and Distance

Loggers and the first settlers showed up in the late 1880s, drawn by standing timber and by the fact that nobody was watching. Remoteness has a way of attracting people who want it, and Read Island in that decade collected more than its share of the flamboyant and the unaccountable. There were no police within a day's travel by boat. Liquor was technically controlled and practically available to anyone willing to sail it in, which is what Kennedy was doing with his sixty cases. The killings of 1893 and 1894 shocked Victoria mostly because they revealed how little authority reached this far up the coast, and the details that survive come largely from newspaper accounts written by people who were not there.

## Sixteen Knots

Read Island sits in a maze. Hoskyn Channel separates it from Quadra Island to the west, Whiterock Channel from Maurelle Island to the north, and Sutil Channel from Cortes Island to the east, with Evans Bay cutting a deep notch into the eastern shore. All of that water has to move twice a day. At Surge Narrows, where Read, Quadra and Maurelle nearly touch, two flood tides converge and the current rips through at up to sixteen knots, easing to around fourteen on the ebb. Just east, Beazley Passage runs at twelve knots on a south-setting flood. Boats go through at slack water or they do not go through. The sound of it carries a long way on a still morning.

## Eighty People and No Ferry

About eighty people live on Read Island today, within Electoral Area C of the Strathcona Regional District. There is no ferry service and no public road system connecting it to anywhere, so life here runs on private boats, water taxis, and the rhythms of weather and tide. Residents generate their own power and haul their own supplies. The census-designated locality of Read Island occupies the east side near the southern end, facing Sutil Channel and the sunrise over Cortes. It is a demanding way to live and an extremely deliberate one. Nobody ends up on Read Island by accident, which is a change from the 1890s.

## What the Island Keeps

Read Island Provincial Park protects a stretch of the island's shoreline and forest, part of a network of small coastal parks scattered through the Discovery Islands. The country here is classic inside-passage terrain: steep rocky shorelines rising into Douglas fir and cedar, arbutus leaning out over saltwater, oyster and clam beaches in the sheltered bays. Orca and humpback whales work the channels. Sea lions haul out on the rocks in winter. The old logging scars have grown in enough that from a distance the island looks untouched, which it is not and never was. It simply has the patience to look that way.

## Flight Context

Read Island lies at 50.231N, 125.086W, in the Discovery Islands between Vancouver Island and the British Columbia mainland, roughly 30 kilometres northeast of Campbell River. From the air the island is unmistakable in context: it sits in a tight cluster with Quadra Island to the west, Maurelle Island to the north, and Cortes Island to the east, with the channels between them showing visible tide rips and standing waves when the current is running. Evans Bay indents the east shore. Look southeast down Sutil Channel to the Strait of Georgia; look north and the mainland inlets of Bute and Toba cut deep into the Coast Mountains, which carry snow most of the year. Campbell River Airport (CYBL) is the nearest significant field, with Campbell River Water Aerodrome (CAE3) handling the floatplane traffic that serves these islands. Comox (CYQQ) is about 60 kilometres southeast. Best viewed at 2,000 to 4,000 feet AGL. Low cloud and rain are common from October through April, and the channels funnel wind hard from the northwest in summer afternoons.
