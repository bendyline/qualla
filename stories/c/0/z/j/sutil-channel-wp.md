# Sutil Channel

A broad Discovery Islands strait carrying the name of a small Spanish schooner that circled Vancouver Island in 1792 — and the site of a whaling station that emptied the Strait of Georgia in eighteen months.

**Location:** 50.1333, -125.0670

**Categories:** Central Coast of British Columbia, Landforms of the Discovery Islands, Channels of British Columbia, Salish Sea, Maritime history

## Introduction

The same name sits on this chart twice, once in each language. Sutil is Spanish for subtle, and the scatter of small islands partway up the channel is marked Subtle Islands — the identical word, translated, applied by a British surveyor who evidently liked the joke. Both honour a schooner. In 1792 the Sutil, a goleta small enough that a modern fish boat would dwarf her, threaded these same narrows on her way to becoming half of the first expedition ever to sail all the way around Vancouver Island. The channel she passed through got her name seventy years later, and then the islands got it again.

## Two Schooners and a Rumour

Dionisio Alcalá Galiano commanded the Sutil; Cayetano Valdés y Flores had the Mexicana. They left Acapulco in March 1792 with orders to settle, once and for all, whether a strait connected the Pacific to the Atlantic somewhere behind the Strait of Juan de Fuca. The method was tedious and brave — work north up the inside passage, send the ship's launches days at a time into every inlet, chart what they found, rule out what they could. Off Point Grey on 27 June they met George Vancouver's expedition doing exactly the same work with far larger vessels. Instead of jostling for position, the three captains spent roughly two weeks in company, swapping charts and observations. The Spanish then rounded the island's north end, came down the outer coast to Nootka Sound, and closed the loop. It was the last Spanish exploration of this coast after two and a half centuries of Spanish primacy.

## The Surveyor's Long Memory

Captain George Henry Richards, running the Royal Navy's survey of these waters, put the name Sutil Channel on the chart around 1864. It is worth pausing on that. Britain and Spain had come close to war over this coast during the Nootka Crisis, the Spanish claim had since collapsed entirely, and the empire that won was under no obligation to commemorate the losers. Richards commemorated them anyway — and then, for the small islands in the middle of the channel, translated the ship's name into English and used it a second time. The Subtle Islands are the Sutil twice over. Hydrographers, it turns out, have a professional regard for good surveying that outlasts the question of whose flag was flying.

## Reading the Channel

Sutil Channel runs between Cortes Island on the east and Quadra and Read Islands on the west, linking Calm Channel in the north to the Strait of Georgia in the south. It is wide at the bottom and narrows as it climbs, finally merging with Calm Channel near the Rendezvous Islands off the northern tip of Cortes. The formal boundaries are drawn between points: Francisco Point at the southeast corner of Quadra across to Sutil Point at the south end of Cortes marks the southern limit, and a line from Rebecca Spit to Read Point separates Sutil from Hoskyn Channel, which branches away northwest. Von Donop Inlet cuts deep into Cortes from the channel's northern reach. Burwood, Carrington, Coulter, Evans, and Manson bays notch the shores, along with George Harbour. Up at the north end, the Penn Islands mark part of the outer boundary of the Salish Sea — the point where these protected waters stop being the Salish Sea and start being something wilder.

## Three Hundred Whales

Whaletown, on the Cortes shore, is named with grim literalism. In 1867 Governor James Douglas identified the deep cove there as a suitable place to render whales taken in the Strait of Georgia, and from 1869 James Dawson's Victoria-based whaling company set up try-works on the beach — great iron kettles for boiling blubber down to oil. In roughly eighteen months of operation the station processed about three hundred whales, most of them humpbacks. Within four years of the company's founding, the animals were effectively gone from these waters, and in 1871 the operation moved on to Hornby Island. Whaletown today is a ferry landing, a post office, and a few dozen people — a quiet place whose name records how fast a population of large animals can be removed from a sea.

## Who Was Here First

Long before either flag, these were Coast Salish waters, and the channel was a travel corridor rather than a boundary. The smallpox epidemic that swept the coast in 1862 killed a devastating share of the people living on Cortes and the surrounding islands — a catastrophe that arrived ahead of most settlers and reshaped who lived where. The Klahoose are still here, centred on Squirrel Cove on the east side of Cortes, running their own government, forestry operations, and a wilderness lodge. Kayakers paddling the Subtle Islands on a still August morning are crossing a route that was well travelled long before anyone wrote a name on it.

## Flight Context

Sutil Channel centres on roughly 50.133°N, 125.067°W in the Discovery Islands, at the north end of the Strait of Georgia. Best viewed from 2,000 to 5,000 feet. The channel is easy to identify from the pattern of islands: Cortes Island forms the eastern wall, Quadra Island the western, with smaller Read Island filling the northwest side and Hoskyn Channel splitting away behind it. Look for Rebecca Spit — a long, thin, hooked sandspit on Quadra's east coast, one of the most distinctive shapes in the Discovery Islands — and the Subtle Islands sitting mid-channel to the northeast of it. Marina Island lies off the southern entrance. The channel opens south into the Strait of Georgia between Francisco Point and Sutil Point, and narrows north toward the Rendezvous Islands and Calm Channel, with the fjords of Desolation Sound and the Coast Mountains beyond. Campbell River (CYBL) is the main field, about 15 nautical miles west across Quadra Island; Powell River (CYPW) sits roughly 25 nautical miles southeast across the strait, and Comox (CYQQ) about 35 nautical miles south. Floatplane traffic through here is constant in summer. Expect strong tidal streams visible as rips and boils on the surface, low cloud filling the channels in winter, and rapidly rising terrain on the mainland side.
