# Refuge Cove, British Columbia

A dozen people winter over at this roadless general store on West Redonda Island, in the middle of a sound George Vancouver named for how much he disliked it - and hundreds of boats a day tie up here every summer.

**Location:** 50.1247, -124.8450

**Categories:** British Columbia, Inside Passage, Boating, Small Towns, Remote Communities, Coastal History

## Introduction

In 1968 the general store at Refuge Cove burned to the ground. The owners got the cash box out and nothing else. What they did next tells you most of what you need to know about this place: they hauled an 87-foot barge from the wharf up onto the land and reopened the store inside it. There is no road to Refuge Cove. There has never been a road. When something here is destroyed, whatever replaces it has to arrive by water or not at all.

## "Not a Single Prospect Pleasing to the Eye"

George Vancouver anchored in these waters in the summer of 1792 and hated them. The mountains crowded the shoreline, the water dropped away too deep to anchor comfortably, the wind died, and the surveying went slowly. He wrote that there was not a single prospect that was pleasing to the eye, and he named the place Desolation Sound. Two centuries of boaters have found this one of the funniest misjudgments in the history of Pacific exploration. The same geography that trapped and irritated Vancouver - deep fjords, sheltered basins, water that warms in summer to the highest temperatures north of Mexico - now draws thousands of pleasure craft every season. Refuge Cove sits at the northern edge of it, tucked into the southwest corner of West Redonda Island, looking across at Squirrel Cove on Cortes.

## Boomtown on a Steamer Route

The settlement began with paperwork. George Black took out a Crown grant on 63 hectares at Refuge Cove in 1913, and a store opened here in 1918 to serve the loggers, fishermen, and coastal steamers working the Inside Passage. It worked. By 1931, when J.R. Tindall bought the store, Refuge Cove had grown to about 200 people - a real community, with everything a remote coastal settlement needed and nothing it didn't. Tindall sold to Norman and Buster Hope in 1946. For roughly four decades the cove enjoyed the particular prosperity of being on the way to somewhere, a place where vessels had to stop because there was nowhere else.

## What Ripple Rock Took

Then somebody blew up a mountain and the traffic went away. Ripple Rock, a twin-peaked underwater hazard in Seymour Narrows that had sunk or damaged more than a hundred vessels, was demolished in 1958 in one of the largest non-nuclear explosions ever deliberately set. Clearing it opened the route close along the Vancouver Island shore, and ships took it. Refuge Cove, which had made its living on boats coming past, suddenly had none coming past. The decline was steep. Within a decade the year-round population had collapsed to a small handful of residents. The store burned in 1968, and the barge that replaced it was, by any ordinary measure, a business decision that made no sense at all.

## Eighteen People and a Whole Earth Catalog

In 1972 the property was bought by eighteen people who called themselves the Refuge Cove Land and Housing Co-op - an assortment of back-to-the-landers with idealism, very little money, and copies of the Whole Earth Catalog. Buying a nearly abandoned settlement in the middle of a sound named for its desolation was exactly the sort of thing that era produced, and unlike a great many similar experiments, this one held. The co-op still owns the place. Refuge Cove's survival is not a story of rescue by outside capital; it's a story of people deciding a place should continue, and then doing the work of continuing it.

## Monday, Wednesday, Friday, One to Three

Summer at Refuge Cove is loud. Hundreds of boats a day tie up for moorage, fuel, groceries, ice, showers, laundry, and espresso, and about twenty families live here through the warm months to serve them. Then September ends, the yachts go south, and the place contracts to roughly a dozen people. The store and fuel dock keep winter hours that read like a koan: Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, one o'clock to three. You arrive by boat or by float plane, or you take a ferry from Vancouver or Victoria to Powell River, drive north to Lund where the highway simply stops, and cover the last thirteen miles on the water. Roughly 150 kilometres northwest of Vancouver, and none of them by car.

## Flight Context

Refuge Cove sits at 50.1247°N, 124.8450°W, in the southwest corner of West Redonda Island at the northern edge of Desolation Sound. Navigate by the island pattern: Cortes Island lies immediately southwest across Lewis Channel, with Squirrel Cove directly opposite the settlement; East and West Redonda fill the view north and east, split by Waddington Channel; the deep fjords of Toba and Bute inlets run into the Coast Mountains beyond. There is no runway and no road - the cove's dock and float plane approach are the only ways in. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500-3,000 ft MSL to read the cove, its floats, and the summer raft of moored boats; 6,000-8,000 ft opens the whole of Desolation Sound with the Coast Range rising behind it. Campbell River (CYBL) is roughly 19 nm west-southwest across Discovery Passage, and Powell River (CYPW) about 22 nm southeast. Summer brings light winds, warm water, and generally excellent visibility; expect low cloud jammed against the mountains, heavy rain, and strong inflow or outflow winds in the inlets from autumn through spring.
