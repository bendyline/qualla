# Alberni Valley

A broad valley at the head of a forty-kilometre inlet on Vancouver Island, shaped by Nuu-chah-nulth history, a tsunami that arrived from Alaska, and the largest flying boats ever to fight fire.

**Location:** 49.2500, -124.8000

**Categories:** Alberni Valley, Valleys of British Columbia, West Coast of Vancouver Island, Nuu-chah-nulth, Natural disasters in Canada

## Introduction

Port Alberni sits forty kilometres from the open Pacific, at the dead end of a long inlet, which ought to make it one of the safest waterfronts on Vancouver Island. On Good Friday in 1964 the geography worked the other way. A magnitude 9.2 earthquake off Alaska sent waves down the coast, and when they entered Alberni Inlet the narrowing walls squeezed them taller and faster. The sea ran up the Somass River at around fifty kilometres an hour. Six waves came through, roughly ninety minutes apart, and neighbourhoods that had never seen saltwater woke up chest-deep in it.

## The Funnel

The largest wave to reach Port Alberni was about three metres; the smallest, around a metre and a half. That does not sound catastrophic until you account for what a funnel does to energy. Roughly sixty homes were swept off their foundations and another three hundred and fifty were damaged. Remarkably, nobody in Port Alberni died — the waves arrived overnight, people ran uphill in the dark, and the town's luck held in a way Alaska's had not. Afterward the community built the Kitsuksis Creek dyke, a low earthwork that most visitors drive past without noticing. It is the physical memory of a night when the ocean came forty kilometres inland to visit.

## The Names Older Than the Inlet's

This valley is Nuu-chah-nulth country, and two of its nations — the Tseshaht and the Hupacasath — have lived at the head of the inlet and along the Somass and Stamp rivers for far longer than the name Alberni has existed. The rivers here have run sockeye and chinook for millennia, and the fishery is not a historical footnote but a working part of life. Anyone learning the valley properly learns these names first, because the newspapers, the hockey team, and the chamber of commerce all borrow a Spanish surname from the 1790s while the people who mapped this landscape in the first place are still here, still fishing, still governing.

## What Stood on Tseshaht Land

On the Tseshaht main reserve, a residential school operated for eighty years. Its roots go back to 1893 and the Presbyterian Church's Alberni Girls Home; as the Alberni Indian Residential School it took in children from across Vancouver Island and from communities up the northwest coast, and it did not close until 31 August 1973, after the West Coast District Council of Indian Chiefs pushed the federal government to end it. In the 1940s and early 1950s, children at this school and at five others in Canada were used as subjects in government nutrition experiments — deliberately kept hungry to measure what supplements would do. The Tseshaht have since confirmed that at least sixty-seven children died while students here. Ground-penetrating radar over just twelve of the site's hundred hectares identified seventeen suspected unmarked graves. When the Peake Hall dormitory came down in February 2009, survivors were invited to help demolish it. A monument to them was installed on the site in 2014. The work of finding the rest of the children continues.

## Water, and More Water

Rain defines the valley, and the map shows where it goes. Great Central Lake stretches across fifty-one square kilometres and drops to 294 metres deep. From its far end, a long walk into Strathcona Provincial Park leads to Della Falls, generally counted as the highest waterfall in Canada — a thin ribbon dropping down a wall in three stages, reachable only by boat and a day's hike, which keeps the crowds sensible. Sproat Lake, named for the nineteenth-century colonial official Gilbert Malcolm Sproat, fills the valley's western side. Above it all stands Mount Arrowsmith, the biggest mountain on southern Vancouver Island and the easiest alpine country to reach from Victoria or Nanaimo. Its slopes hold the Vancouver Island marmot, Canada's rarest mammal, a species that came within a few dozen individuals of disappearing entirely.

## The Last Flying Boat

For half a century, the strangest sight on Sproat Lake was a four-engined flying boat the size of a house sitting at anchor. The Martin Mars water bombers were the largest ever built, and the Hawaii Mars worked British Columbia fires for more than fifty years, scooping lake water on the run and dropping an estimated fifty million gallons over its career. Coulson Aviation bought the operation in 2007 and flew it until 2015. On 11 August 2024 the Hawaii Mars lifted off Sproat Lake for the last time, made a farewell circuit, and traced a route over the forest communities it had spent a lifetime defending before landing for good at the B.C. Aviation Museum near Victoria. The lake is quieter now. People who grew up here still look up when a big engine goes over.

## Flight Context

The Alberni Valley centres on roughly 49.250°N, 124.800°W, at the head of Alberni Inlet in the middle of Vancouver Island. Best viewed from 3,000 to 6,000 feet. The inlet is the unmistakable feature: a narrow, deep, arrow-straight fjord running about 40 kilometres northeast from Barkley Sound on the open Pacific and terminating at Port Alberni's docks and pulp mill, whose plume is visible for miles. Inland of the town the valley opens into a broad bowl holding Sproat Lake to the west and Great Central Lake to the northwest, with the Beaufort Range along the northeast side and Mount Arrowsmith rising to the east toward Qualicum Beach. Alberni Valley Regional (CBS8, elevation 247 feet) sits just north of the city and is home to Coulson Aviation and provincial fire crews; Tofino/Long Beach (CYAZ) lies about 35 nautical miles west on the outer coast, Comox (CYQQ) roughly 35 nautical miles northeast, and Nanaimo (CYCD) about 35 nautical miles east. Expect abundant cloud and rain on the windward side, valley fog on calm mornings, strong inflow winds up the inlet on summer afternoons, and heavy wildfire smoke in some late summers. Terrain rises steeply on all sides of the valley — mind the ridgelines.
