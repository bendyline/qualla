# Baynes Sound

A 40-kilometre sliver of protected water between Denman and Vancouver Island that grows a startling share of British Columbia's shellfish - and carries the name of an admiral famous for refusing to start a war.

**Location:** 49.5333, -124.8333

**Categories:** Coastal Waters, British Columbia, Vancouver Island, Aquaculture, Wildlife, Indigenous History

## Introduction

The man this channel commemorates is remembered mainly for a fight he declined. In 1859, American and British forces were squaring off on San Juan Island over the shooting of a pig, and the colonial governor wanted Royal Marines put ashore. Rear Admiral Robert Lambert Baynes, commanding the Pacific Station from 1857 to 1860, refused. He would not, he said, involve two great nations in a war over a squabble about a pig. The pig remained the conflict's only casualty, and the narrow sheltered channel north of the argument still carries Baynes's name.

## A Thread of Protected Water

Baynes Sound is less a sound than a slot. It runs about 40 kilometres between Denman Island and the east coast of Vancouver Island, a western offshoot of the Strait of Georgia, and at its widest it measures only 3.5 kilometres across - the average is under two. The southern end is marked by Chrome Island, a speck off Boyle Point at Denman's southern tip. The northern boundary is vaguer, somewhere between Tree Island and Comox harbour. What that narrowness buys is shelter. For boats heading into Comox Harbour, the sound is the safe way in, letting vessels sidestep the shallow Comox Bar that lies between Denman Island and the Comox Peninsula. Geography that would be unremarkable elsewhere becomes, here, both a highway and a nursery.

## Thirty-Nine Percent

Fly low over Baynes Sound at a falling tide and the water reads like farmland, because it is. Racks, trays, longlines, and beach leases occupy much of the intertidal zone, and the numbers behind that clutter are outsized: this single channel produces 39 percent of the oysters and 55 percent of the manila clams farmed in British Columbia. The still, nutrient-rich water and the enormous tidal flats do the work. Small communities line the Vancouver Island shore - Royston, Union Bay, Buckley Bay, Mud Bay, Fanny Bay, and Deep Bay, running north to south - and several of them are better known to seafood buyers than to tourists. Fanny Bay in particular is a name that appears on menus far from here, attached to oysters that grew within sight of the highway.

## K'ómoks Water

None of this is new. These are the traditional waters of the K'ómoks First Nation, whose people harvested the same flats for millennia before anyone drew a chart or named a channel for a British admiral, and who remain active in the shellfish industry that grew from those grounds. The sound has also become a place of study: Vancouver Island University's Centre for Shellfish Research operates its Deep Bay Marine Field Station on these shores, working on the biology and sustainability of the aquaculture that supports the region. Between the research station, the nation's operations, and the family farms, a single narrow channel supports an unusual density of people whose livelihoods depend on reading water, weather, and tide correctly.

## The Birds Know Too

Shellfish are not the only crop the sound attracts. Baynes Sound forms part of a designated Important Bird Area, and every spring it becomes a fuelling stop on one of the Pacific's great migrations. Black brant - small, dark, sea-going geese - arrive in continentally significant numbers, resting and feeding here before pushing north to Arctic breeding grounds. Surveys in 1980 and 1981 recorded brant representing roughly four percent of the entire western subspecies. The same qualities that make the sound good for oysters make it good for birds: shallow, sheltered, productive water that never freezes. Crossing between Buckley Bay and Denman Island, the cable ferry MV Baynes Sound Connector threads through all of it - the farms, the flats, the flocks - several times an hour, a two-kilometre commute over one of the most quietly productive stretches of water on the coast.

## Flight Context

Baynes Sound lies at roughly 49.5333°N, 124.8333°W, on the east side of Vancouver Island opposite the Comox Valley. From the air it is easy to identify: a long, narrow, straight-sided channel separating the flat, wooded rectangle of Denman Island from the Vancouver Island shore, with Hornby Island hooked off Denman's southeast corner and the broad Strait of Georgia beyond. Chrome Island's light marks the south end near Boyle Point; the Comox estuary and the Comox Bar mark the north. At low tide the intertidal shellfish leases are unmistakable - geometric grids and lines in the mudflats. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,500-4,000 ft MSL to pick out the farms, the ferry crossing at Buckley Bay, and the shoreline villages; 8,000-10,000 ft frames the whole sound with the Beaufort Range rising behind it to the west and the Coast Mountains across the strait. Comox (CYQQ) is about 11 nm north, Powell River (CYPW) roughly 22 nm northeast across the strait, and Nanaimo (CYCD) about 47 nm southeast. Expect fog on calm autumn mornings, strong southeast winds funnelling up the channel during winter fronts, and excellent visibility on summer afternoons.
