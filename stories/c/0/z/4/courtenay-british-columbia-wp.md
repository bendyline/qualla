# Courtenay, British Columbia

A Comox Valley city built on an estuary Coast Salish people have fished for four thousand years, where an eighty-million-year-old seabed surfaces in the riverbank gravel.

**Location:** 49.6878, -124.9940

**Categories:** Cities in British Columbia, Comox Valley, Populated places on the British Columbia Coast, Paleontology, Coast Salish

## Introduction

A Courtenay man and his daughter were walking the banks of the Puntledge River in 1988 when they found a bone weathering out of the bank. It belonged to an elasmosaur - a long-necked marine reptile that had died roughly eighty million years earlier, when everything now called Vancouver Island lay beneath a warm salt sea. The excavation that followed pulled a nearly complete skeleton out of the riverbank. The skull was too damaged to identify the species, so the animal remains, to this day, nameless. That is Courtenay in a single image: a working city of twenty-eight thousand on the east coast of Vancouver Island, going about its business directly on top of a seabed that keeps surfacing.

## Four Thousand Years at the River Mouth

Long before any of that, the estuary was a place people lived because it fed them. Archaeological evidence points to an active Coast Salish fishing settlement on the shores of the Courtenay River estuary for at least four thousand years - mild winters, deep soil, and a tidal flat thick with salmon and shellfish. The Coast Salish name for the area, kw'umuxws, means plentiful in Li'kwala; anglicized through Komoux, it became Comox. Wooden stakes from ancient fish weirs still stand in the estuary mud. Carbon dating put the oldest at around 750 CE, cut from a hemlock; the youngest date from roughly 1830. The Pentlatch Nation, speakers of a Coast Salish language of that name, held the shores of what is now Comox Bay when Europeans first arrived.

## What the 1860s Took

In 1862 a smallpox epidemic swept Vancouver Island and killed an estimated thirty percent of its First Nations people. The Comox Valley communities had already been under pressure from raiding by the Lekwiltok to the north. What the numbers show is stark enough without commentary: a census of First Nations in the Comox Valley in 1876 counted 88 K'ómoks and 21 Pentlatch - the surviving population of nations who had held this estuary for millennia. European settlers had begun arriving in 1872, and by 1876 the colonial Indian Reserve Commission had confined the K'ómoks and Pentlatch to two reserves, one beside the village of Comox and one at the confluence of the Puntledge and Tsolum rivers. The Pentlatch language has since fallen silent, though the K'ómoks First Nation remains at the estuary today.

## A River Named by Sailors, a Town That Moved

Spanish and British survey ships worked these waters in the 1790s; George Vancouver entered the estuary in 1792 and charted its shoreline. The Hudson's Bay Company steamship Beaver came looking for trading-post sites from 1837 and established one overlooking the estuary. The river's name arrived casually - in 1848 sailors aboard HMS Constance began calling it the Courtenay after their captain, and in 1857 Captain George Richards, surveying the coast with authority to name things officially, simply confirmed what the sailors had already decided. The first village grew on the east bank, around the Courtenay Hotel, a general store, a bank, a barber and livery barns. It flooded. Repeatedly, whenever king tides or spring runoff arrived. So a new townsite was surveyed on the drier west bank and the whole enterprise moved across the water.

## Eighty Million Years Under the Gravel

The elasmosaur was only the beginning. Glacial erosion along the Puntledge and Trent rivers has cut down into an eighty-million-year-old Cretaceous seabed, which is why the Comox Valley is treated as the first stop on the Great Canadian Fossil Trail and why amateurs keep finding things. Excavations in the summers of 1997 and 1998 produced a nearly complete mosasaur - and, on analysis, a species new to science, named Kourisodon puntledgensis for the two extra rows of razor-edged teeth in its jaws and for the river that gave it up. Fossil sea turtles, a vampire squid and several octopods have come out of the same beds. The original elasmosaur bones and a replica are on display at the Courtenay and District Museum and Paleontology Centre.

## What the Valley Does Now

For most of its history Courtenay lived on what could be extracted - coal from Cumberland, timber from the hills, fish from the strait, crops from the prairies on the river flats. Those industries shrank, and the city reoriented toward services: health care, retirees drawn by one of the mildest winters in Canada, and the military. CFB Comox is the valley's largest single employer, with roughly 1,400 jobs. Tourism has become the growth story, much of it pointed uphill at Mount Washington, which is also the last stronghold of the critically endangered Vancouver Island marmot. The 2021 census counted 28,420 people, up from 25,639 five years earlier. Courtenay is growing the way many coastal towns now grow - not because of what it ships out, but because of who wants to live there.

## Flight Context

Courtenay sits at 49.6878 N, 124.994 W on the east coast of Vancouver Island, at the head of the Courtenay River estuary in the Comox Valley. Recommended viewing altitude is 3,000-6,000 ft AGL. The estuary is the unmistakable feature from the air: a broad, braided tidal flat where the Puntledge and Tsolum rivers merge and empty into Comox Harbour and the Strait of Georgia, with the city on both banks and the long runway of CFB Comox to the northeast. Inland, the Beaufort Range climbs sharply, with Mount Washington's ski runs cut into the slopes to the northwest. Denman and Hornby Islands lie offshore to the southeast, with the mainland Coast Mountains beyond the strait. Nearest airports: CYQQ (Comox Valley Airport / CFB Comox), a joint military-civil field immediately northeast of the city; CYBL (Campbell River) about 45 km northwest; CYCD (Nanaimo) about 100 km southeast. The Courtenay Airpark and its adjacent water aerodrome sit right on the river downtown. Expect low cloud and rain from October through March; summer afternoons are usually clear, with inflow winds up the strait.
