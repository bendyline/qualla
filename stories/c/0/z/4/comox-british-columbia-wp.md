# Comox, British Columbia

A town on Vancouver Island's east coast whose tideflats hold eleven centuries of fishing stakes, and whose name means, simply, plentiful.

**Location:** 49.6733, -124.9022

**Categories:** Towns, Vancouver Island, British Columbia, Indigenous Heritage, Military Aviation

## Introduction

The oldest wooden stake recovered from the shoreline at Comox was cut from a hemlock around 750 CE. The youngest dates to roughly 1830. Between those two points, a thousand years of people walked out at low tide to tend the same fishing works, driving fresh stakes where the old ones rotted, generation replacing generation without any interruption a carbon date can detect. Archaeological evidence puts an active Coast Salish fishing settlement here for at least four thousand years. Everything else in this town's history is recent by comparison.

## A Layer of Shell

The Pentlatch harvested shellfish from Comox Bay in quantities hard to picture now. What they left behind accumulated: centuries of discarded shells built a deep stratum along the shoreline of what is now the town, a formation known as the Great Comox Midden. It is not a ruin and not a monument. It is the physical residue of ordinary meals, repeated across so many lifetimes that the ground itself changed composition. Walk the Comox waterfront today and the soil underfoot in places is more shell than earth. Warm dry summers, mild winters, fertile ground and an ocean full of food had made this one of the most reliably comfortable places to live on the coast, and people responded accordingly.

## Plentiful

The name says it outright. Comox derives from kw'umuxws, a word in Kwak'wala, the language the K'omoks came to adopt, meaning plentiful. But the K'omoks did not begin here. By the nineteenth century they had been driven from their own territory further north by the Laich-kwil-tach, a Kwakwaka'wakw group whose raids on neighbouring villages took captives into slavery. Displaced families moved south to what is now Comox, where they found the Pentlatch already established and made common cause with them against a shared threat. Two peoples, pushed together by violence, sharing one exceptionally rich stretch of shoreline. The K'omoks Nation remains here today, and the name of the town, the bay and the valley is theirs.

## Charts, Captains and a Name Taken Back

In 1791 a Spanish expedition under Dionisio Alcala Galiano and Cayetano Valdes y Flores produced a rough chart of the Strait of Georgia and may have called at Comox. George Vancouver arrived the following year and charted the shoreline properly, working the Courtenay River estuary in concert with the Spanish. The Hudson's Bay Company steamship Beaver came prospecting for trading-post sites in 1837 and established one it recorded as Komoux. A Royal Navy captain named Courtenay used the long sandy hook of the Goose Spit for gunnery practice, and in 1848 British sailors informally attached his name to the river; Captain George Richards, surveying the island coast in 1857 with authority to name landmarks, made it official. Then in 1893 the provincial government renamed the village, the bay and the valley from Augusta to Comox, without asking anybody who lived there.

## The Wharf, the Store and the Road

Settlement clustered where the ships could land. The Scottish immigrant James Robb and his son William worked out that the shoreline sheltered by the Goose Spit was the only practical landing between the Courtenay River and the point, and a wharf followed. Joseph Rodello, an Italian immigrant with a sharp commercial instinct, bought the two lots flanking it and put up a store at the wharf's head so his stock would never have to be hauled uphill. It burned to the ground in 1880. He rebuilt, and reopened in 1882. Then came the institutions: St. John the Baptist Catholic Church in 1886, blown down by a storm and rebuilt two years later, and in 1891 the Comox District Free Press, known locally and affectionately as the Yellow Paper. A road from Nanaimo had been promised for nearly sixty years. It finally arrived in 1910, the same year the town's first automobile did.

## The Base and the Glacier

For over fifty years Comox reached the world only by ship. Roads and a railway came during the First World War; an air force base arrived during the Second, and it changed everything. CFB Comox still operates here alongside a civilian airport and the sea cadet training establishment HMCS Quadra, and the Comox Air Force Museum documents the coastal aviation history that grew out of it. Above it all sits the Comox Glacier, visible from much of the town and its unmistakable signature. The 2021 census counted 14,806 residents, a population notably older than the provincial norm, drawn by a mild climate and the valley's easy access to Mount Washington, the Forbidden Plateau and Strathcona Provincial Park. On the BC Day long weekend the town fills for the Filberg Festival and Nautical Days, and the Goose Spit, which once absorbed naval gunfire, fills with kiteboarders.

## Flight Context

Comox sits at 49.6733 N, 124.9022 W on the Comox Peninsula, facing the Strait of Georgia on Vancouver Island's east coast. Best viewing altitude is 2,500 to 4,500 feet for the town and harbour, or 8,000 feet and above to frame the Comox Glacier and the Beaufort Range behind it. Key visual landmarks: the long curved sandbar of the Goose Spit enclosing Comox Harbour, the Courtenay River estuary immediately west separating Comox from Courtenay, Denman Island lying just offshore to the southeast, and the white bulk of the Comox Glacier inland to the southwest. The airfield is Comox Airport / CFB Comox (CYQQ), a joint military and civilian field immediately north of town; Campbell River (CYBL) lies about 25 nm northwest and Nanaimo (CYCD) about 55 nm southeast. Note that CYQQ is an active Royal Canadian Air Force base with associated controlled and restricted airspace. Expect morning stratus off the strait and orographic cloud obscuring the glacier and the Beauforts on moist southwest flow.
