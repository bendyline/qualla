# Comox Valley

A glacier-topped lowland on Vancouver Island where deliberately burned Indigenous meadows became settler farms, a coal town grew one of Canada's largest Chinese communities, and the whole valley now runs on festivals and jet noise.

**Location:** 49.6798, -124.9694

**Categories:** Comox Valley, Mid Vancouver Island, Comox Valley Regional District, Valleys of British Columbia

## Introduction

The open meadows that made the settlers' eyes light up in the spring of 1861 were not an accident of nature. The Comox, the Pentlatch, and the Lekwiltok had been farming this lowland for generations and keeping it clear by burning - a maintained landscape, not a wilderness, and the deep alluvial soil underneath was the whole point. When the first European farmers arrived expecting to fight the forest for every acre, they found ground already prepared and read it as a gift from Providence. The Comox Valley has been misread, in one way or another, ever since: as empty, then as remote, then as merely scenic. It is none of those things.

## A Glacier Watching Farmland

Geographically, the valley is a piece of good luck. Mountains rise to the west and the Comox Glacier hangs above the whole basin, a white wedge visible from almost every field and parking lot below. Eastward the land flattens into beaches along the Strait of Georgia, with Denman and Hornby Islands lying just offshore like breakwaters. Between the ice and the salt water sits that alluvial floor, which is why farms took hold here and why they persist in a province where flat, fertile, low-elevation ground is genuinely scarce. Courtenay, Comox and Cumberland form the valley's three points, with Royston, Union Bay, Fanny Bay, Black Creek and Merville strung between them. The arrangement gives the valley something unusual on this coast: real agriculture, real mountains and real beach, all inside a twenty-minute drive of each other.

## 1862

HMS Discovery anchored in Comox Harbour in 1792, the first documented European arrival. Nearly seventy years passed before anyone came to stay. Farmers landed in the spring of 1861, followed by coal miners and Hudson's Bay Company men. The following year smallpox swept the coast, and the valley's Indigenous population was devastated by it - not a statistic but families, elders who carried the burning practices and the place names, entire village sites emptied inside a single season. The nations who had shaped this valley were still here afterward, and are here now, but the epidemic broke the continuity of a landscape that had been deliberately tended for a very long time. The meadows the settlers admired were, in a real sense, inherited rather than earned.

## The Road That Was Never Built

Getting here was the valley's long-running problem. In 1862 Surveyor General Pemberton secured colonial money in Victoria for a road north from Nanaimo, but a fifteen-foot wagon road proved far too expensive, so a bridle path with a few bridges went in instead. Flooding and falling timber made it impossible to keep open. Until the mid-1890s, in practice, you came by sea. The fix arrived in pieces: Highway 19A hugging the shoreline and stitching the communities together, then Highway 19 running faster and farther inland, bypassing everything. The Esquimalt and Nanaimo Railway still ends at Courtenay, though it carries only freight now - passenger service was suspended in 2011. BC Ferries sails from Little River across to Powell River and Texada Island, which means the old sea route never actually went away.

## Union, Renamed Cumberland

Robert Dunsmuir bought the valley's coal enterprise in 1887 and made it the Union Colliery Company. Output climbed hard - 31,204 tons in 1889, 66,556 by 1892 - and by 1897 six hundred workers were pulling up to a thousand tons a day. The company town called Union was renamed Cumberland in 1894, after the English coalfield, and incorporated in 1898. A little over a kilometre west grew a Chinatown that reached roughly two thousand residents and a hundred businesses, among the largest Chinese communities in Canada by the end of the First World War; a Japanese settlement of some thirty-six homes stood a kilometre beyond that. Its residents left in 1942, forced from the coast to internment camps, and never returned. When Cumberland miners were locked out in 1912, the strike spread across the island and the province sent troops. The last mine closed in 1966.

## The Valley of Festivals

About 76,000 people now live here, making this Canada's 47th largest metropolitan area - which is to say small, but no longer a backwater. The service sector accounts for more than half of all employment. Tourism and construction are the growth industries, fed by the ski resort at Mount Washington and by CFB Comox, whose aircraft are the valley's constant background noise and a serious piece of the local economy. In 2007 Canadian Heritage named the region one of the country's Cultural Capitals, and the valley leaned into the label: the Comox Valley Shellfish Festival, Vancouver Island MusicFest, the Filberg Festival, the North Island Hot Jazz Festival, Comox Nautical Days, Cumberland Wild. Locals call it the Valley of Festivals without much irony. Cumberland, meanwhile, has become a mountain-biking town, its old coal roads repurposed as singletrack.

## Flight Context

The Comox Valley centres on 49.6798 N, 124.9694 W, on the east side of Vancouver Island facing the Strait of Georgia. A run at 3,000 to 5,000 feet AGL from southeast to northwest gives the full read: Denman and Hornby Islands offshore, the estuary and tidal flats at Comox Harbour, the farm grid of the lowland, and the Comox Glacier and Vancouver Island Ranges filling the view to the west and southwest. Mount Washington's ski runs are an obvious cut in the timber northwest of the glacier. Comox Valley Airport / CFB Comox (CYQQ) sits about 4 nm northeast of the valley centre and is the primary field - expect military traffic and active airspace. Courtenay Airpark (CAH3) serves light aircraft and floatplanes right at the estuary. Campbell River (CYBL) is roughly 20 nm northwest and Powell River (CYPW) about 20 nm east-northeast across the strait. Summer visibility is generally excellent; autumn and winter bring low ceilings, valley fog on the flats, and rotor and turbulence off the ranges in strong westerlies.
