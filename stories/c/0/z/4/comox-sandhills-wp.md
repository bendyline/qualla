# Comox Sandhills

An ancient dune field near Cape Lazo, buried under a forest that only the poorest soils could allow, and re-mapped three times because the houses built on it kept changing the ground.

**Location:** 49.6850, -124.8907

**Categories:** Mid Vancouver Island, Nature centres in British Columbia

## Introduction

Soil scientists mapped this ground in 1959 and called it a classic podzol, the Kye-Custer complex, Ky-Cu on the sheet. By 1985 a new report filed the same acreage under a different soil order entirely - brunisolic, the Kuhushan association. Four years after that, a third study split it again, Beddis series on the dry parts and Baynes on the patches that drain badly. Nobody had been wrong. The ground had simply been rebuilt underfoot by driveways, foundations and lawns, until the profiles the earlier surveyors had described no longer existed to describe.

## A Dune Field Wearing a Forest

The Comox Sandhills are an ancient sand dune system lying east-northeast of Comox town centre, on the Comox Peninsula near Cape Lazo. You would not identify them as dunes on sight, because a forest grew over them long ago: Douglas-fir and shore pine mainly, with western hemlock moving into the damper hollows. Other species are present but scarce - western white pine, Sitka spruce, western red cedar, red alder, bigleaf maple, black cottonwood, trembling aspen - and the reason they stay scarce is the sand itself. Exceptionally low fertility keeps the roster short. What looks like ordinary coastal woodland is in fact a plant community disciplined by hunger, growing on a landform whose shape survives beneath the roots.

## The Ground Keeps Moving

Sand is a forgiving thing to build on and an unforgiving thing to build near. Most of Lazo Road east of the Guthrie Road junction runs straight through the old dune field, and housing developments along Kinnikinick Road, Sand Pines Drive and Sierra Pine Road sit squarely on it - the street names themselves a kind of survey record, kinnikinnick and pine being exactly what grows in this soil. The northeastern boundary of the Sandhills runs near Knight Road, roughly parallel to it. Low-lying pockets within the system flood, sometimes badly enough to damage property, because dune country is a landscape of hollows as much as of hills, and hollows collect what the sand will not hold.

## What Is Left Unbuilt

At the northwest corner of the dune area, Lazo Wildlife Park protects a piece of the Sandhills in something closer to its unaltered state. It is a modest defence for a landform of this age, and it makes the rest of the system legible by contrast: walk the park, then drive Lazo Road, and the rise and fall of the dunes reappears in the grades of the pavement and the dips in the front lawns. Songbirds and deer use the dune woodland the way they use any coastal forest, but the ground they move across is a relic landform, older than the trees standing on it and older than any survey that has tried to pin it down. This is not a wilderness story. It is a story about a landscape that was absorbed rather than destroyed - still there, still shaping where water goes and what trees can manage, underneath a subdivision.

## The Snare and the Plentiful Place

The headland just east of the dunes is Cape Lazo, named in 1791 by the Spanish navigator Jose Maria Narvaez, who called it Punta de Lazo de la Vega - lazo meaning snare, vega an open plain. The valley behind it carries a much older name. Comox derives from the Kwak'wala word kw'umuxws, meaning plentiful, and the K'omoks people have taken that abundance from these shores for a very long time. In the tidal flats nearby, archaeologists have found the remains of enormous fish weirs: up to 200,000 wooden stakes, carbon-dated to a span running from roughly 750 CE to 1830. The dunes were already ancient then, and the forest already standing on them. Narvaez saw the headland; the people who set those stakes had been reading this coastline for a thousand years before he sailed past it.

## Flight Context

The Sandhills lie at 49.685 N, 124.8907 W, between Comox town centre and the Cape Lazo headland on the east side of the Comox Peninsula. This is CFB Comox (CYQQ) airspace - the runway is immediately southwest of the dune field - so transit requires coordination and there is no casual low-level sightseeing here; from an approved 1,500-3,000 ft you can read the wooded hummocks against the cleared farmland inland. Landmarks are unmistakable: the blunt bluff of Cape Lazo to the east, the long hook of Goose Spit curving into Comox Harbour to the southwest, the Strait of Georgia beyond, and the Comox Glacier standing white in the ranges behind town. Campbell River (CYBL) is about 50 km northwest and Nanaimo (CYCD) roughly 100 km southeast. Low stratus off the strait is common in the mornings, and the glacier makes an excellent horizon check when the shoreline haze sets in.
