# Lazo Marsh-North East Comox Wildlife Management Area

Farmers drained this marsh for potato fields in the 1930s. The beavers dammed it back, and eighty years later the water they saved is a protected refuge for more than 140 species of birds.

**Location:** 49.6847, -124.8911

**Categories:** Protected areas of British Columbia, Wetlands, Birdwatching sites, Protected areas established in 2001

## Introduction

The beavers won. In the 1930s and 1940s, this low ground east of Comox was drained so it could be planted in potatoes — a reasonable ambition on Vancouver Island, where flat arable land is scarce and wetland was widely regarded as wasted acreage. The ditches went in. The water went out. And then beavers moved back through the drainage and rebuilt it, dam by dam, until the fields would not stay dry. The farming stopped. The marsh returned. For decades afterward nobody thought much of the place at all; people dumped scrap metal and car bodies in the trees, and the rusting remains are still there if you look. The land the beavers refused to give up is now a provincial Wildlife Management Area.

## Two Metres Above the Sea

Lazo Marsh sits about three kilometres east of the town of Comox, on a low peninsula that pushes out into the Strait of Georgia. The whole protected area rises only two to twelve metres above sea level — close enough to the water that salt air moves through the alders, far enough to keep its own freshwater character. It covers 127 hectares of wetland, riparian corridor, upland forest, sand dune, and coastal bluff, and it connects directly to the Comox North East Woods, so that what looks on a map like a modest patch of green is in practice part of a much larger unbroken run of habitat. The climate does the rest of the work: cool summers, mild winters, high humidity, and a temperature range so narrow that the growing season barely pauses. All of this lies within the traditional territory of the K'ómoks people, who have lived on this coast since the retreat of the last ice.

## Radford's Swamp

For a while the place was known as Radford's Swamp, which tells you exactly how it was valued. A swamp was a problem to be solved — drained, filled, farmed, or failing that, used as a place to put things you no longer wanted. Old car parts and scrap steel still lie among the trees and reeds, a rusting archive of an era when nobody would have called this land anything but waste. That reading held until surprisingly recently. In 1994, when development proposals came forward for the area, a public hearing was held and local residents turned out to oppose them. The land was designated Crown Forest instead, and in 2001 the province took the final step. On 6 April of that year, the Lazo Marsh-North East Comox Wildlife Management Area was formally established.

## More Than a Hundred and Forty Kinds of Bird

Count the birds that use this marsh over a full year and the total passes 140 species. Some are permanent residents, some pass through on migration, and some — the trumpeter swans that winter on the Comox Valley's wet ground — arrive in numbers that change the character of the place entirely. Great horned owls hunt the edges. Ducks and geese crowd the open water. Below the surface, cutthroat and rainbow trout share the channels with stickleback and with coho salmon fry, which need exactly this kind of slow, cool, vegetated water to survive their first year. The rarity that birders come for is a small one: the Vancouver Island pygmy owl, Glaucidium gnoma swarthi, a subspecies found nowhere else on Earth. It is about the size of a fist and hunts in daylight, which makes it easier to see and no easier to find.

## The Fight That Does Not End

Protection is not the same as safety. The marsh is under sustained pressure from yellow flag iris, an ornamental European plant that escaped gardens, took to the shallows, and has proven stubbornly resistant to removal. It forms dense mats that crowd out native sedges and rushes and gradually convert open water into monoculture. The people managing the site have learned the same lesson that invasive-species work teaches everywhere: catching an infestation early is cheap, and catching it late may not be possible at all. That work is shared out across an unusually crowded partnership — The Nature Trust of British Columbia, Ducks Unlimited Canada, the Habitat Conservation Trust Foundation, the Canadian Wildlife Service, the Comox Valley and Strathcona regional districts, the Town of Comox, and neighbourhood associations, alongside the K'ómoks First Nation, whose restoration and stewardship projects on this coast now include caring for Lazo Marsh. It takes all of them to hold 127 hectares.

## Flight Context

The Lazo Marsh-North East Comox Wildlife Management Area lies at 49.6847 degrees north, 124.8911 degrees west, on the Lazo peninsula east of Comox on Vancouver Island's east coast. Best viewed from 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL — the marsh reads as a dark, irregular wetland mosaic set between second-growth forest and the low bluffs above the Strait of Georgia, distinct from the surrounding farmland grid of the Comox Valley. The clearest navigational reference is CFB Comox / Comox Valley Airport (CYQQ), roughly 5 km west-northwest, with its 10,001-foot concrete runway 12/30 impossible to miss; Goose Spit curls into Comox Harbour just to the south. Note that CYQQ is an active military airfield with associated controlled airspace — check current charts before manoeuvring low in this area. Other airports within range: Nanaimo (CYCD) to the south, Powell River (CYPW) across the strait, and Campbell River to the northwest. Morning fog is common over the low ground in autumn; the Comox Glacier and the Beaufort Range stand up to the west on clear days.
