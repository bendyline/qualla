# Union Bay Post Office

A wooden post office from 1913 still sorts mail on Baynes Sound — one of only two of its kind left in Canada, and the last working piece of a vanished coal port.

**Location:** 49.5835, -124.8860

**Categories:** Comox Valley Regional District, Buildings and structures in British Columbia, Post office buildings in Canada, Historic buildings and structures in British Columbia, Federal government buildings in British Columbia

## Introduction

Buy a stamp here and you are handed it through a brass wicket that has been in the same frame since before the First World War. The Union Bay Post Office went up in 1913, and it is still a post office — not a museum with a mail slot, an actual working Canada Post outlet with a counter and the outgoing mail for Denman Island, Hornby Island, and Fanny Bay stacked behind the partition. It is also one of only two old wooden post offices left standing in Canada. Everything else that gave this place a reason to exist is gone.

## The Deep Water That Made a Town

Coal came out of Cumberland, a dozen kilometres inland, and it had to reach the ocean. Union Wharf opened in 1889 on Baynes Sound - the largest wharf complex built in the province at the time - because the water here is deep enough for seagoing ships to tie up close to shore, and a rail network linked the collieries to a principal pier 490 metres long, able to load four colliers at once. Then the operation got serious. A coal washer, installed in 1896 and fed by water piped from what is now called Hart Creek - known at the time as Washer Creek, after the very machine it served - cleaned the coal before loading. A hundred beehive coke ovens, completed that same year, burned around the clock, baking coal into coke for the smelters up and down the West Coast. For a few decades, this quiet bay ran hot.

## The Men at the Ovens

The labour that built and ran this port was largely done by workers the colony's newspapers rarely named. South Asian crews built and rebuilt sections of the dock to handle coke gently, since coke shatters if you drop it. Japanese workers stoked the ovens, standing in heat that most people could not tolerate for a full shift. Chinese men who had come to British Columbia to build the railway, and then found the railway finished and the country unwilling to let them leave or thrive, worked as trimmers in the holds of coal ships — shovelling loose coal into the corners of a ship's belly, in the dark, breathing dust. Across the coal belt around Cumberland and Union Bay in this era, workers like these were routinely paid less than white miners for harder work and housed apart from them, under laws written to keep them there. The coke that made fortunes in Trail and San Francisco was made, in large part, by them.

## Built as the Tide Turned

The post office opened in 1913, a wooden federal building for a company town at what looked like the height of its importance. The peak did not hold. Oil displaced coal in ship bunkers and smelters changed their processes, and coke production wound down not long after. The beehive ovens sat unused for decades, curved brick domes slowly disappearing into blackberry, until 1966 to 1968, when the last structures - ovens, wharf, and all - were finally demolished. What was left was a shoreline community with a highway through it, a view across to Denman, and a wooden building where the mail still came in.

## Rescued at the Counter

In the 1990s the building was put up for sale, which in a small community usually means the end. The Union Bay Historical Society bought it instead, and then did the harder thing: restored the interior to its original fittings, brass wicket and all, rather than gutting it into something rentable. Heritage status followed the restoration. The post office anchors what locals call Heritage Row — a short stretch of surviving early buildings along the old company town's main line — and it is unmistakably the jewel of it, a modest clapboard structure that has simply refused to stop doing its job for more than a century. Canada Post still occupies the main floor. The sorting still happens here. There is something quietly stubborn in that: a building that outlasted the industry, the wharf, the ovens, and the fortune, and kept the one function that was never about coal at all.

## Flight Context

The Union Bay Post Office sits at 49.5835°N, 124.886°W on the west shore of Baynes Sound, midway down the eastern coast of Vancouver Island in the Comox Valley Regional District. Fly it low and slow — 1,500 to 2,500 feet is plenty for a building this size, and the whole historic site reads best on a shoreline pass. Navigation landmarks are unambiguous: Denman Island lies directly across the narrow sound to the east with Hornby Island beyond it, the long tidal flats of Baynes Sound show as pale green-brown shallows at low water, and the Beaufort Range rises steeply inland to the west with the Comox Glacier visible to the north in clear conditions. The village strings along Highway 19A, and the old wharf pilings can still be picked out along the waterfront. Comox Airport (CYQQ) is roughly 10 nautical miles north and is the nearest tower-controlled field; Qualicum Beach (CAT4) lies south; Nanaimo (CYCD) and Powell River (CYPW), across the Strait of Georgia, round out the options. Winter brings persistent low cloud and southeast wind funnelling up the sound; summer mornings are typically calm with excellent visibility before the afternoon inflow builds.
