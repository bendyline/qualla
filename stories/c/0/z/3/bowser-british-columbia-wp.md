# Bowser, British Columbia

A small Vancouver Island highway community named for a one-year premier, best remembered for the 1930s hotel dog who carried beer to tables and made change.

**Location:** 49.4361, -124.6770

**Categories:** Villages, Coastal, Local History, Nature Reserves, British Columbia

## Introduction

In the 1930s, the most reliable employee at the hotel in Bowser, British Columbia had four legs. A dog named Mike would take an order, carry a bottle of beer out to a table in his mouth, accept the money, trot it back to the bar, and return with the correct change. Patrons came from a long way down the Island Highway to see whether the stories were true. They were true enough that Ripley's Believe It or Not! picked it up, which in that era was roughly equivalent to going viral. The joke writes itself, of course - a town called Bowser with a dog behind the bar - except the name came first, and from an entirely unrelated direction.

## Named for a Premier Nobody Remembers

William J. Bowser was premier of British Columbia from 1915 to 1916. That is the whole of it: barely a year in the chair, sandwiched between longer and louder careers, at a moment when the province was absorbed by a world war and its own hard economic weather. Political reputations are perishable things, and his did not keep. What survived instead was a place name on the east coast of Vancouver Island - a few hundred houses, a highway junction, a stretch of beach - which has now carried his name far longer than he carried the office. Today Bowser is home to roughly 1,729 people, sitting about 66 kilometres north of Nanaimo within Electoral Area H of the Regional District of Nanaimo. Most people passing through are headed somewhere else.

## Lighthouse Country

Locals do not describe the area by its administrative boundaries. They call it Lighthouse Country, an informal name for a stretch of shoreline and highway that runs from Qualicum Beach in the south to Fanny Bay in the north, reaching west inland to Horne Lake and east across the water to Denman and Hornby Islands. It is a coastline of shallow bays, gravel beaches and long tidal flats, with the mainland mountains rising across the Strait of Georgia on clear days. The Island Highway threads the whole of it, and the communities strung along the road - Bowser among them - have the particular character of places that are neither resort towns nor working ports, but something quieter in between: retirees, oyster leases, beachcombers, and a steady seasonal drift of people looking for the parts of the island the tour buses skip.

## The Forest That Grew Back

Just outside the community lies the Bowser Ecological Reserve, 113 hectares set aside in 1996 to protect a stretch of unusually productive coastal forest. Its purpose is research as much as preservation. More than a century ago, fire swept through this ground, and what has grown since is a controlled record of how a Pacific coastal forest reassembles itself when left alone - which species arrive first, which dominate later, how the canopy closes and what happens underneath it. The reserve is open to anyone who wants to walk it, which makes it an unusually approachable piece of science: a long-running experiment you can stroll through on a weekday afternoon, a short detour off the highway and a world away from it.

## A Name in the Soil

There is one more place the name persists, and it is the least expected. Soil scientists working eastern Vancouver Island needed a label for a particular sandy, well-drained soil type that recurs across the region, and they named the series after the community where it is mapped underfoot: Bowser loamy sand. It runs beneath the whole settlement and turns up on soil maps for much of the island's east coast. A premier, a dog, a soil series, a patch of regrowing forest. For a place most drivers pass in under a minute, Bowser has left an unusual number of marks in unusual places - and if only one of them is remembered a century from now, it will almost certainly be Mike.

## Flight Context

Bowser lies at 49.4361 N, 124.6770 W on the east coast of Vancouver Island, about 36 nm northwest of Nanaimo along the Strait of Georgia. From the air the anchor is the coastline itself: the Island Highway runs parallel to the shore here, with Denman and Hornby Islands lying offshore to the northeast and the long gravel beaches and tidal flats of Lighthouse Country marking the shoreline between Qualicum Beach and Fanny Bay. Horne Lake sits inland to the west. A viewing altitude of 3,000 to 5,000 feet MSL gives a clear read on the shoreline, the island group offshore, and the Coast Mountains rising across the strait on the mainland side. Qualicum Beach Airport (CAT4) is the closest field, roughly 10 nm southeast; Comox (CYQQ) lies northwest and Nanaimo (CYCD) to the south. Terrain inland rises quickly into the Beaufort Range, so keep an eye on cloud on the ridges; the strait side is usually the clearer of the two.
