# Deep Bay, British Columbia

An earthquake dropped the floor out of this sheltered Vancouver Island harbour in 1946, and four years earlier the Canadian government took the logging company that built the community from the Japanese Canadian who founded it.

**Location:** 49.4653, -124.7267

**Categories:** Unincorporated settlements in British Columbia, Ports and harbours of British Columbia, Japanese Canadian history, Earthquakes, Logging history

## Introduction

At 10:15 on the morning of June 23, 1946, the ground under central Vancouver Island let go. The epicentre was up on the Forbidden Plateau northwest of Courtenay, and the shaking was felt from Portland to Prince Rupert. When the Canadian Hydrographic Service went back afterward to resound the harbour at Deep Bay, the charts made no sense. The bottom of the bay had dropped -- in places by nine feet, in places by eighty-four. A sheltered anchorage that fishermen had sounded and trusted for decades had quietly reorganized itself while nobody was looking at it. The surface, of course, gave away nothing at all.

## The Morning the Bottom Fell Out

The 1946 earthquake remains one of the largest recorded in Canada, measured at magnitude 7.0 on the surface-wave scale and later reassessed at 7.5. Chimneys came down across the island. Landslides scarred the hillsides, undersea cables parted in Alberni Inlet and off Powell River, and shorelines all along the Strait of Georgia sagged. On Read Island the ground shifted three metres sideways. Two people died, one of them a man whose dinghy was swamped by the waves the shock threw across the water. That the death toll was so low had more to do with geography than luck: this was still a thinly settled coast of small mills, fishing floats, and farmsteads. Deep Bay's contribution to the record is that subsided seafloor, a reminder that the calm water in a protected harbour sits on top of a coastline still very much under construction.

## The Man Who Built Deep Bay Logging

In the 1920s, a Japanese Canadian businessman named Eikichi Kagetsu bought 885 acres of timberland at Deep Bay and incorporated the Deep Bay Logging Company. He ran it here and at Fanny Bay a few miles north, and he ran it well. By the time the Second World War began, the operation had grown into thousands of acres of prime coastal timber served by its own railway -- some nine and a half miles of track, a locomotive, nineteen flatcars, donkey engines, tractors, and two dozen camp buildings housing a workforce that was largely Japanese Canadian. Kagetsu was, by the measure of the day, one of the wealthiest Japanese Canadians in the country. The community at Deep Bay existed in large part because his company was there, cutting, hauling, and putting logs into Baynes Sound.

## Custodian of Enemy Property

In 1942 the federal cabinet authorized the forced uprooting of Japanese Canadians from the coast. Kagetsu was interned. His workers were scattered to work camps and internment sites far inland, and with the men gone the railway stopped and the camps emptied. His property passed to the Custodian of Enemy Property -- an office whose name did the work of explaining itself -- and was sold without his consent to the H.R. MacMillan Export Company, one of the largest timber concerns on the coast. A lifetime of building was liquidated by a government that had classed its builder as an enemy on the basis of his ancestry. He was not compensated at anything approaching value. The shape of Deep Bay today, its road grid and its cleared ground, is still partly the shape he gave it, though his name was removed from the title deeds within a matter of months.

## A Quiet Harbour, Still Working

Deep Bay sits on Highway 19A between Fanny Bay and Bowser, north of Qualicum Beach, on a low spit that curls out to enclose the harbour. There is a government marina inside it, naturally protected, with fishing boats and pleasure craft on the floats and Denman Island filling the view east across Baynes Sound. The sound is sheltered, shallow, and rich, and shellfish grow in it in commercial quantity; the Deep Bay Marine Field Station studies that water and what lives in it. The Island Rail Corridor still runs through the area, its rails rusting quietly in the salal, though no train has used them in years. It is an unassuming place -- a spit, a marina, a highway, a view. The history under it is anything but.

## Flight Context

Deep Bay lies at 49.465N, 124.727W on the east coast of Vancouver Island, about 20 km northwest of Qualicum Beach. The defining feature from the air is the curved sand-and-gravel spit that hooks north to enclose the harbour, an unmistakable comma of land against the straight shoreline of Baynes Sound. Denman Island lies immediately east across the sound, with Hornby Island beyond it and the mainland Coast Mountains on the far horizon. Highway 19A traces the shoreline; the old Island Rail Corridor runs parallel just inland. Best viewed at 1,500-3,000 feet, which shows the spit, the marina basin, and the shallow bar of Baynes Sound in one pass. Nearest airports: Qualicum Beach Airport (CAT4) about 12nm southeast, Comox Airport (CYQQ) about 15nm northwest, and Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) about 35nm south. Morning fog can sit on the sound in autumn, and outflow winds funnel down the sound in winter.
