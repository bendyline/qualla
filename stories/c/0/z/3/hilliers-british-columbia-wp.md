# Hilliers, British Columbia

A quiet crossroads on Vancouver Island that hosted a fall fair, a wetland worth saving, and one of the province's strangest six-year experiments in communal living.

**Location:** 49.5167, -124.8000

**Categories:** british-columbia, vancouver-island, rural, doukhobor, history, wetland

## Introduction

In the spring of 1947, two hundred people arrived at a 348-acre parcel north of Nanaimo and began passing the soil through sieves by hand. A reporter who came out to look described women stooping to the ground, working the earth inch by inch, and reached for the only comparison available to him: a scene out of the Bible. They called themselves the Elders of the Spiritual Community of Christ. They held no private property, expected the second coming, and planted several thousand strawberry and raspberry canes while they waited. Six years later every one of them was gone, and Hilliers went back to being what it is today - an unincorporated stretch of farmland and forest between Coombs and Qualicum Beach, home to about 1,590 people, easy to drive through without noticing.

## The Fair That Moved Down the Road

Long before the commune, Hilliers had its moment as the social centre of this corner of Vancouver Island. The first annual Fall Fair was held here in 1913 - livestock, preserves, vegetables laid out for judging, the whole rural ritual of showing your neighbours what the season produced.

It lasted a decade in Hilliers. In 1923 the fair relocated to Coombs, a few kilometres east, and it has stayed there ever since, growing into an institution that outlived its founding generation several times over. Coombs got the crowds and, eventually, a roadside market with goats grazing on the roof. Hilliers kept the farmland and lost the parade. It is a small transaction, but it explains why one of these names is on the tourist map and the other is mostly a road sign.

## The Elders Arrive

The Doukhobors came to Canada from Russia in 1899, pacifists who refused military service and rejected church hierarchy, and by the 1940s their communities were concentrated in the BC interior. The group that bought the Hilliers property followed Michael Verigin, called "the Archangel" by his followers, and they were looking for somewhere to build the communal life they believed in without interference.

What they built was substantial. Within a few seasons the farm had a dining hall, residences, barns, a small sawmill, a canning plant, a school, apiaries, orchards, and gardens laid out on land they cleared themselves. A spokesman explained the principle simply enough: private property was the cause of the world's troubles. Everything on the 348 acres was held in common, and the work was shared the same way.

## Six Springs and an Ending

Interference found them anyway. The Doukhobor world of that era was fractured, and a militant faction in the Kootenays was setting fire to the property of Doukhobors who had integrated into Canadian life. In the spring of 1950 Verigin was convicted of inciting others to commit arson and sentenced to two years. A month after sentencing he suffered a stroke, and on 27 July 1951 he died of pneumonia at sixty-nine.

The community did not survive him. Without its leader the Hilliers colony lost cohesion, and by December 1952 the land was listed for sale. By early 1953 the families had returned to the Kootenays. Six growing seasons, a whole village of buildings, thousands of berry plants - and then the gates of it swung shut, which is roughly how most utopias end, though rarely so tidily.

## The Marsh That Stayed

What Hilliers has now is quieter and, in its way, more durable. Hamilton Marsh lies within the community, a wetland in the French Creek watershed that filters and stores water for everything downstream toward the coast. It is open to the public and looked after by the Hamilton Wetlands and Forest Preservation Society, a group that has spent years arguing for its protection against the logging that surrounds it.

Walk in on a spring morning and you get the full Vancouver Island register: standing water dark as tea, frogs starting up and stopping in unison, second-growth Douglas fir closing in overhead, and the particular hush that wetlands impose on anyone who arrives loudly. Errington is south, Coombs east, Qualicum Beach northeast with its long shallow beach. Hilliers sits in the middle of them, keeping its wetland and its odd, half-forgotten story.

## Flight Context

Hilliers lies at 49.517N, 124.800W in the eastern lowlands of Vancouver Island, roughly 5 nm inland from the Strait of Georgia and about 30 nm northwest of Nanaimo. From 3,000 to 5,000 feet the landmark is the pattern rather than any single feature: cleared rectangular farm parcels set into second-growth forest, with Highway 4 running west toward Port Alberni and Highway 19A tracing the coast through Qualicum Beach. Hamilton Marsh shows as a dark wetland break in the timber. Qualicum Beach Airport (CAT4) is the closest field, just a few miles northeast; Nanaimo (CYCD) lies southeast, Port Alberni (CBS8) west over the Beaufort Range, and Comox (CYQQ) northwest up the coast. Morning radiation fog in the lowlands is common in autumn, and the Alberni valley to the west can channel strong afternoon winds through the pass.
