# Fisher Poets Gathering

Every February, commercial fishermen gather in Astoria, Oregon to read poetry, sing songs, and tell tales born on the water -- a celebration that started with a handful of friends in a pub in 1998 and has grown into a nationally recognized literary event.

**Location:** 46.2012, -123.8291

**Categories:** culture, festivals, fishing, poetry, oregon

## Introduction

The hands that read poetry at the Fisher Poets Gathering are the same ones that haul nets, mend gear, and gut salmon. Every last weekend of February, Astoria, Oregon fills with commercial fishermen who happen to write -- or maybe writers who happen to fish. The distinction blurs quickly once the readings begin. What started in 1998 as a handful of far-flung friends meeting in a pub to share verses has grown into an annual gathering of around 70 performing artists and hundreds of audience members, featured on the Today Show, profiled in Smithsonian Magazine, and documented in Jen Winston's film "Fisher Poets." The event has no red carpets or literary pretensions. It has docks, rain, and words shaped by salt water.

## Born in a Bar

The Fisher Poets Gathering traces its origin to 1998, when a small group of friends scattered across the Pacific coast fishing industry decided to meet in Astoria and share their writing. They chose a pub. They read poems about nets, tides, fatigue, and the strange solitude of open water. The audience was mostly each other. But word traveled through the fishing community the way it always does -- from boat to boat, port to port -- and within a few years, the gathering had outgrown its casual beginnings. Astoria, a town built on the Columbia River's commercial fishing heritage, proved the perfect host. The event sprawls across multiple venues now, but it retains the feel of that first evening: informal, earnest, and slightly weathered around the edges.

## Words from the Water

Commercial fishing is among the most dangerous occupations in the United States. The people who do it spend weeks on rolling decks in freezing spray, working twenty-hour shifts during salmon or crab season, knowing that the sea does not negotiate. This proximity to risk and solitude produces a particular kind of attention -- the fisherman's eye for detail, for weather, for the subtle shift in current that means the difference between a full hold and an empty one. The poetry that emerges from this life carries that attention. It is specific in ways that landlocked writing rarely achieves: the weight of a particular net, the color of a particular dawn, the sound of a particular engine failing. The Fisher Poets Gathering gives these voices a stage they would never seek on their own.

## Beyond Borders

For its first sixteen years, the Fisher Poets Gathering was an exclusively American affair. That changed in 2014, when Katrina Porteous traveled from the Northumbrian fishing village of Beadnell, England, to read her poetry about coastal communities on the other side of the Atlantic. Her participation marked the first time an international poet joined the lineup, expanding the gathering's reach while confirming its core insight: fishing communities everywhere share a language that transcends national boundaries. The ocean imposes similar rhythms on the people who work it, whether they haul crab pots off the Oregon coast or tend lobster creels in the North Sea. Porteous's presence underscored that the Fisher Poets Gathering speaks to something universal about life on the water.

## Astoria's Stage

Astoria is not an accidental backdrop for this event. The town sits at the mouth of the Columbia River, where the Pacific meets one of North America's great waterways, and its identity has been tied to fishing since long before Lewis and Clark arrived in 1805. Canneries once lined the waterfront, processing the enormous salmon runs that made the Columbia famous. Scandinavian and Finnish immigrants built the fishing fleet that sustained the town through the 20th century. That industry has diminished but not vanished, and Astoria still smells of the river on certain mornings. The Fisher Poets Gathering draws on this heritage, turning the town into a stage where the culture of commercial fishing is celebrated not through nostalgia but through living art -- new poems written by people still hauling lines.

## Rain, Verse, and Resilience

In 2021, the gathering went virtual for the first time, forced online by circumstances that kept the whole world indoors. Fishermen read their poems into laptop cameras instead of bar microphones. It was, by most accounts, a pale substitute -- the gathering's power comes partly from proximity, from the warmth of a crowded room in a cold coastal February. But the event survived, as fishing communities tend to survive: by adapting. The Fisher Poets Gathering endures because it fills a need that no other literary event addresses. It gives voice to people whose work is physical and whose art is private, creating a space where calloused hands hold carefully folded pages and the audience knows exactly what every poem costs.
