# Octopus Tree

A 250-to-300-year-old Sitka spruce on Cape Meares with no central trunk and a 46-foot circumference, whose massive limbs radiate outward like tentacles -- shaped, according to historians, by the hands of local Tillamook tribes who trained the tree for ceremonial use.

**Location:** 45.4863, -123.9780

**Categories:** Natural landmarks, Oregon Coast, Heritage trees, Indigenous culture

## Introduction

There is no main trunk. That is the first thing visitors notice, and the thing that makes the Octopus Tree impossible to forget. Where a normal Sitka spruce would shoot a single column skyward, this tree sends out multiple enormous limbs from near its base, each one thick enough to be a respectable tree on its own, each one reaching outward and upward in a different direction like the arms of a candelabra -- or, as its most popular name suggests, the tentacles of something reaching out of the earth. Standing 105 feet tall with a circumference of 46 feet, the Octopus Tree grows on Cape Meares in Tillamook County, Oregon, and it has been baffling and delighting visitors for generations.

## Shaped by Human Hands

The most compelling explanation for the tree's bizarre form is also the most human. Historians say local Tillamook tribes used this tree for ceremonies and deliberately trained it into its distinctive shape. The practice of shaping living trees -- bending young branches, weighting limbs, pruning to redirect growth -- was documented among Pacific Northwest coastal peoples, who modified trees for purposes ranging from navigation markers to burial canoes to spiritual sites. If the Tillamook shaped the Octopus Tree two and a half to three centuries ago, they created something that has long outlived the culture that made it. The tree kept growing after the people who tended it were gone, each trained limb thickening decade by decade into something that looks less like cultivation and more like a force of nature. Which is, perhaps, exactly the point of a ceremonial tree: to become something larger than the hands that shaped it.

## A Sitka Spruce Unlike Any Other

Sitka spruce are the largest spruce species in the world and a defining tree of the Pacific Northwest coastal forest. They thrive in the salt air and persistent moisture of the fog belt, growing tall and straight -- typically, emphatically straight. That is what makes the Octopus Tree so striking. At an estimated 250 to 300 years old, it has had centuries to develop its sprawling architecture, and every year the limbs grow thicker, the canopy spreads wider, the silhouette becomes more dramatic. The tree has no primary trunk at all. Instead, its massive lateral branches begin spreading just a few feet above the ground, some extending horizontally for dozens of feet before curving skyward. The effect from below is like standing inside a living cathedral whose columns have decided to wander.

## Cape Meares and the Coastal Pilgrimage

Cape Meares itself is a headland jutting into the Pacific between Tillamook Bay and Netarts Bay, home to the Cape Meares Lighthouse and the Cape Meares National Wildlife Refuge. The Octopus Tree sits along a short trail in Cape Meares State Scenic Viewpoint, reachable by a paved path that makes it one of the most accessible natural oddities on the Oregon coast. The Oregonian has included it in multiple lists of the state's most iconic landmarks and best roadside attractions -- recognition that speaks to its power as a destination. People drive hours to stand beneath it. The Oregon Travel Experience has designated it a state heritage tree, placing it among the most significant individual trees in Oregon. For all the official recognition, though, the tree's real authority is simpler than any plaque. You round a bend in the trail, the forest opens slightly, and there it is -- too strange to be real, too solid to be anything else.

## What Names Reveal

The tree has accumulated names the way it has accumulated limbs: generously and in several directions at once. Octopus Tree is the most popular, and the most evocative -- it captures the reaching, grasping quality of the branches. Candelabra Tree focuses on the upward sweep, the way the limbs curve from horizontal to vertical like the arms of a branched candlestick. Council Tree hints at its ceremonial past, suggesting a place where people gathered. And then there is its fourth name: Monstrosity Tree. That one says more about the namer than the named. What the Tillamook people shaped with intention, what the tree accomplished through centuries of patient growth, some early observer looked at and saw only aberration. The tree does not care about any of its names. It stands on the cape, absorbing fog and rain, growing imperceptibly wider, and waiting for the next person to round the corner and stop in their tracks.

## Flight Context

Located at 45.49N, 123.98W on Cape Meares, a headland on the northern Oregon coast between Tillamook Bay and Netarts Bay. The tree itself is not visible from altitude -- it sits in dense coastal forest -- but Cape Meares is a prominent geographic feature, a rocky headland projecting west into the Pacific. The Cape Meares Lighthouse is a useful visual reference. Best approach from the west over the ocean for dramatic views of the headland and the Three Arch Rocks National Wildlife Refuge just offshore. Nearest airports: Tillamook Airport (KTMK) approximately 8nm south-southeast, Portland-Hillsboro (KHIO) about 55nm east.
