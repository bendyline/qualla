# Hatzic Valley

A glacial trough between Stave Lake and Hatzic Lake where 1,300 people farm ground that floods from above and drinks from three vulnerable aquifers below.

**Location:** 49.2333, -122.2333

**Categories:** Valleys, Rural communities, Water and flooding, History, British Columbia

## Introduction

On Hatzic Prairie the water table sits close enough to the surface that it shapes everything - where you can build, where a septic field is safe, what a farm can do with its runoff. Ice made this valley: a low trough gouged southward from the foot of Stave Lake down to Hatzic Lake, about 12.5 kilometres end to end and roughly 2.5 kilometres across at its widest near Durieu. The Douglas Ranges wall it on the east, with Durieu Ridge closest and Dewdney Peak at the ridge's southern tip. Around 1,300 people live in it. They have spent a century and a half negotiating with water that arrives from the Fraser, from the mountains and from directly underfoot.

## Who Was Here

Long before anything was surveyed, Hatzic Prairie was a summer camping place for the Kwantlen, one of the Stó:lō peoples of the lower Fraser, whose territory runs through Mission and well beyond it in every direction. The first recorded settler of European descent arrived in the 1860s: an Irish squatter named Kelly, a former Cariboo teamster, who was caught illegally importing cattle and deported to the United States for it. By the 1880s the Burton family held some 1,200 acres running up to what is now Durieu Road, and the place was called Burton Prairie. Then the Fraser flooded, Ralph Burton sold up, and the name went with him.

## Names That Kept Moving

One of the new owners, a former army captain named Livingston Thompson, opened a post office on 1 April 1889, made himself postmaster and renamed the land Hatzic Prairie. On 1 March 1910 the post office was renamed again, this time Durieu, in memory of Pierre-Paul Durieu, the French missionary who became the first Bishop of New Westminster and had died in 1899. Provincial cartographers formally recognised the settlement of Durieu on 12 December 1939, and the post office closed three years later, on the last day of 1942 - by which point the name had outlasted the institution that produced it. North of Durieu Road, McConnell Creek carries the name of a logger, Jack McConnell, who worked the area but lived down in Silverdale, and who had been John Connell until a failed Columbia River logging venture persuaded him to try a different spelling.

## The River Decides

Flooding is not an episode in this valley's history; it is the plot. The Fraser came over in 1894 and repeatedly afterward, but the benchmark remains the Great Flood of 1948, which put more than 13,000 acres of neighbouring lowland under water, twelve square miles of it on Hatzic Prairie alone. Studies followed, as studies do. A drainage study in 1992 set out to reduce flood damage and channel instability; a 2005 report on Legace Creek, which drains most of the low ground, recommended gravel removal, recontouring, bank protection and sediment traps at a cost of just over a million dollars - while warning candidly that debris floods can dump enough sediment to overwhelm the work. Regional flood mapping later identified the deepest projected floodwaters as lying north and east of Hatzic Lake, around Dewdney and Deroche. Then, in November 2021, atmospheric rivers closed Highway 7 along the north bank with mudslides that stranded hundreds of people, and the abstraction became a week of helicopters.

## Three Aquifers

Underneath the fields lie three separate bodies of groundwater, and most valley households draw directly from one of them. The Hatzic Prairie aquifer in the south is the shallowest; the Cascade aquifer sits at the north end between Cascade and Davis creeks; the McConnell aquifer lies between them. The first two are classed as highly vulnerable, because the soil above them is permeable enough to let contaminants straight through - a real concern in a valley of farms and septic systems where the water table is already high. The McConnell aquifer is capped with tighter sediments and rated low-vulnerability, but as the geomorphologist Tom Millard pointed out in a 2013 assessment, the same cap that keeps pollutants out may keep recharge out too. Protection and replenishment pull in opposite directions.

## What the Valley Does Now

The timber mills that once worked McConnell Creek have all closed. Electricity reached the area in 1949, the same year the community hall opened on Hartley Road, and that pairing - power and a place to gather - still describes rural life here reasonably well. Land goes to hobby farms and commercial agriculture; timber and aggregate come off the surrounding slopes; Davis Lake Provincial Park and Cascade Falls Regional Park cover the northern end, under the 1,278-metre summit of Mount St. Benedict. At 14100 Stave Lake Road sits a 160-acre property the Salvation Army bought in 1963 and ran as a rehabilitation centre for people recovering from addiction, a place locals called Miracle Valley. It closed in 2010 after financial trouble and was sold; the land is now the Khalsa Centre, a cultural retreat and campground with a blueberry farm. Online maps still print the old name over ground that has moved on.

## Flight Context

Hatzic Valley centres on 49.2333 N, 122.2333 W, running roughly northwest to southeast between the southeast corner of Stave Lake and Hatzic Lake, just east of Mission on the north side of the Fraser River. From 3,000 to 5,000 feet AGL the shape is unmistakable: a flat green floor perhaps 2.5 km wide, walled on the east by Durieu Ridge and closed at the north by the forested bulk of Mount St. Benedict at 1,278 m. Hatzic Lake at the south end and the dark expanse of Stave Lake to the northwest bracket the valley; Highway 7 and the CP line run along the Fraser across its southern mouth. Abbotsford International (CYXX) lies south across the river, Chilliwack (CYCW) east up the valley, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) west - all better options than anything closer, since this is a narrow trough with rising terrain on three sides. Expect cloud on Mount St. Benedict long before the valley floor goes in, and heavy rain and low ceilings through the autumn atmospheric-river season.
