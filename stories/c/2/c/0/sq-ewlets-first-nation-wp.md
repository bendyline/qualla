# Sq'éwlets First Nation

A Sto:lo community on Harrison Bay whose ancestral hill holds 198 burial mounds, and which helped change how archaeology is done on Indigenous ground.

**Location:** 49.2359, -122.0025

**Categories:** Indigenous history, First Nations, British Columbia, Fraser Valley, Archaeology, Rivers and lakes

## Introduction

Nobody who lives here calls them pyramids. On the rise above the place where the Harrison River spreads out into a wide, lake-like bay, there are 198 earthen mounds arranged in fifteen distinct clusters, built up over a very long span of time on a hill the Sq'éwlets know as Qithyll. Outsiders reached for the nearest available comparison and started calling them the Fraser Valley Pyramids, or the Scowlitz Mounds. What they actually are is a graveyard, ancient and enormous, and the people whose ancestors lie in it are still living at the foot of the hill. That last fact is the part that has mattered most, because the Sq'éwlets are not a subject of the archaeological work on Qithyll. They are a partner in it.

## The River People

Stó:lo is the Halq'eméylem word for river, and the Stó:lo are, plainly and precisely, the river people. Their world, S'ólh Téméxw, runs through the Fraser Valley and up into the lower canyon, and it is a landscape they understand as deliberately shaped: in Stó:lo accounts the Xexá:ls, the transformers, moved through this country fixing it, making it right, turning certain people and beings into the stone and terrain that are still here. This is not distant mythology in a book. It is a description of specific, identifiable places you can stand next to. The language that carries those accounts is in serious trouble, with fewer than five fluent speakers of Halq'eméylem remaining, all of them elderly, which puts an urgency behind every recording and every classroom that would be hard to overstate.

## Two Hundred and Thirty-Seven Hectares

The Sq'éwlets First Nation, known until recently as the Scowlitz First Nation, governs three Indian Reserves, and their combined size is worth stating in full because the arithmetic makes an argument that no amount of description can. Scowlitz Indian Reserve No. 1, at the outlet of Harrison Bay beside Harrison Mills, is 69.0 hectares. Williams Indian Reserve No. 2, adjacent to it, is 9.7. Squawkum Creek Indian Reserve No. 3, at the southwest corner of the bay, is 158.0. Together they come to roughly 237 hectares, which is to say about two and a third square kilometres, for a people whose territory once took in the whole of this valley. The Nation is a member government of the Stó:lo Tribal Council, one of eight bands in a council that has stayed outside the British Columbia treaty process.

## Digging With, Not On

For most of the twentieth century, archaeology on Indigenous ground in North America followed a familiar pattern: researchers arrived, excavated, published, and took the material away. Qithyll became one of the places where that pattern broke. The mound complex on Harrison Knob is exactly the kind of site that would once have been stripped for a museum collection, and instead the work there has proceeded as a partnership with the community whose ancestors are buried in it, with the Sq'éwlets setting terms about what is examined, what is disturbed, and what is said publicly. The results have been substantial, and so has the model. What a burial site of this scale tells you, before any specialist opens a report, is simple enough: people lived on this bay in large numbers, in a settled way, for long enough to build a landscape of monuments to their own dead.

## Where the River Widens

The geography explains the settlement. The Harrison is a short, heavy river, effectively the continuation of the Lillooet, draining Harrison Lake southward until it slows below the mouth of the Chehalis and sprawls into Harrison Bay, a backwater so broad it reads as a lake. Then it joins the Fraser. Water like that, warm and slow and shallow at the edges, is a gathering place for fish, and chum salmon are among the species the Harrison carries. That is why people settled the bay, and why the Sts'ailes have their reserve on its north side while the Sq'éwlets hold the western shore. The settler village of Harrison Mills sits at the western foot of Mount Woodside at the bay's outlet, where the Kilby general store went up in 1906 and a hotel doubled the building's size a year later; it is a heritage site and campground now. The store is a hundred and twenty years old. Qithyll is older by an order of magnitude that is hard to hold in the mind at once.

## Flight Context

Sq'éwlets territory centres on 49.2359 N, 122.0025 W, on the western shore of Harrison Bay in the Upper Fraser Valley, between Chehalis to the east and Lake Errock to the west. Best viewed from 3,000 to 6,000 feet, from which the hydrology is unmistakable: Harrison Lake running deep into the Coast Mountains to the north, narrowing into the Harrison River, then flaring open into the pale shallow sheet of Harrison Bay before joining the brown line of the Fraser. Mount Woodside rises immediately east of the bay's outlet, with Harrison Mills and the Highway 7 crossing at its foot; Harrison Knob is the modest wooded rise near the confluence, a burial ground rather than a visitor site and best appreciated from a distance. Nearest airports are Chilliwack (CYCW) about 6 nm southeast, Abbotsford International (CYXX) roughly 19 nm southwest, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) some 28 nm west. Valley fog forms readily over the bay on autumn and winter mornings; clear days give a long view up Harrison Lake into the mountains.
