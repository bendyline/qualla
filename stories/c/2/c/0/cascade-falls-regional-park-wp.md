# Cascade Falls Regional Park

A thirty-metre waterfall fifteen minutes' walk from a parking lot in the Hatzic Valley, reached by a suspension bridge - and named for a creek named for a logger who was not using his real name.

**Location:** 49.2746, -122.2144

**Categories:** 1986 establishments in British Columbia, Waterfalls of British Columbia, Regional parks of Canada, Lower Mainland, Fraser Valley Regional District, Protected areas established in 1986, Landforms of Lower Mainland

## Introduction

Jack McConnell was not born Jack McConnell. He was John Connell, a logger working the timber northeast of Mission, British Columbia, and when his logging business failed he added a few letters to the front of his surname and carried on as somebody slightly different. The creek in that valley was named McConnell Creek after him, which means for a good many years a British Columbia watercourse carried a pseudonym. It is called Cascade Creek now, and the reason is obvious the moment you see it: thirty metres of white water falling through dark conifer, fifteen minutes' walk from a gravel parking lot, in a 22-hectare park most people outside the Fraser Valley have never heard of.

## Thirty Metres, Straight Down

The trail is 1.2 kilometres and it is mostly stairs, which is the honest arrangement for a valley wall. It climbs through second-growth coniferous forest, the understorey deep in fern and moss that stays wet nine months of the year, and delivers you to a suspension bridge strung directly across Cascade Creek in front of the falls. There are viewing platforms on both banks, so you can watch the drop from either side and from the middle, swaying slightly, which is the point of building a suspension bridge rather than a culvert. Below the main falls a second, smaller run of water descends about eighteen metres into a series of quiet pools. The contrast is the whole experience: violence at the top, stillness a short distance down, and the sound changing as you descend.

## Where This Water Is Going

Cascade Creek drains southwest into the southeastern end of Stave Lake, and Stave Lake is not quite what it appears. It was a natural lake before anyone dammed it, but the Stave Falls Dam and Powerhouse, built between 1920 and 1922 by the Stave Falls Power Company - later absorbed into British Columbia Electric Railway - raised and enlarged it, and the Ruskin Dam downstream, finished in 1930, added Hayward Lake below. The main arm of Stave Lake today is roughly three times its pre-dam size, and the whole reservoir covers about 55 square kilometres. Most of the surrounding land belongs to BC Hydro. This was the territory of the Sxa'yaks, a Coast Salish people, before it was a timber lease and long before it was a reservoir; the water now flowing off Cascade Creek runs through a system engineered a century ago to light Vancouver.

## A Park Made by Committee

The park exists because a regional district commissioned studies. In 1984 the Dewdney-Alouette Regional District - a body that no longer exists - looked at the McConnell Creek area and concluded the falls were worth protecting, and on 7 June 1986 the park was officially inaugurated. It started at 9.5 hectares and has since been enlarged to 22. Administration passed to the Fraser Valley Regional District, and what the FVRD provides is deliberately minimal: picnic tables, pit toilets, a parking lot, the stairs and the bridge. The gates open at seven in the morning and close at sunset, every day of the year. As of 2026 the FVRD has been assessing storm damage across its park system and asking visitors to use extreme caution - worth checking before you drive out, because a valley this steep does not shed a bad winter quickly.

## The Fraser Valley Plays Itself

Cascade Falls has also worked as a film set, which is the fate of nearly every accessible piece of scenery within two hours of Vancouver. The final scene of Big Bully was shot here, staging a fight between the characters played by Tom Arnold and Rick Moranis on and around the falls. The Twilight Saga: Breaking Dawn - Part 2 used the forest here for a running sequence. Neither film asked the location to be anywhere in particular; it was simply a photogenic waterfall a manageable drive from a production base, which is how British Columbia has made a great deal of money for forty years. The falls neither gained nor lost anything from the arrangement.

## Above the Bridge

One warning belongs in any honest account of this place. Above the main drop, Cascade Creek runs over smooth bedrock chutes that look, on a hot August afternoon, exactly like a natural waterslide, and that is precisely how people describe them online. Wet granite and moving water above a thirty-metre fall are not a swimming hole. The park's built infrastructure - the stairs, the railings, the bridge, the platforms - all exists to let you get close to the thing without being in it. Stand on the bridge in October when the rain has been going for a week, feel the deck move, watch the creek come off the lip in a solid column, and it is very clear which side of the railing is the good side.

## Flight Context

Cascade Falls sits at 49.2746 N, 122.2144 W, in the Hatzic Valley about 10 nautical miles northeast of Mission, British Columbia, on the eastern flank of the Stave Lake basin. From 2,500 to 5,000 feet AGL the falls themselves are a thin white thread in heavy timber and easy to miss - navigate to the drainage instead. Stave Lake is the unmistakable anchor: a large, irregular, fjord-like reservoir running north into the Coast Mountains, with the Stave Falls and Ruskin dams at its southern end and Hayward Lake below them. Cascade Creek enters at the southeast corner; Davis Lake Provincial Park lies just northeast. To the south the land drops away to the flat Fraser River floodplain around Hatzic Lake and Mission, with Westminster Abbey's tower on the ridge above the river. Terrain rises quickly north of the park, so respect minimum safe altitudes. Abbotsford International (CYXX) is about 16 nautical miles south, Chilliwack (CYCW) about 13 nautical miles southeast, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 20 nautical miles west. Expect low cloud clinging to the valley walls from October through April and frequent morning valley fog; midsummer afternoons give the most reliable ceilings, though the falls run heaviest during spring melt and autumn rain.
