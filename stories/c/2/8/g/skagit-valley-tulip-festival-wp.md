# Skagit Valley Tulip Festival

Five dollars of flower bulbs bought on Orcas Island in the 1890s turned into a North American bulb industry and an April festival that fills a Washington farming valley with half a million visitors.

**Location:** 48.4167, -122.4170

**Categories:** Festivals in Washington (state), Spring festivals in the United States, Mount Vernon, Washington, Tourist attractions in Skagit County, Washington, Flower festivals in the United States, 1984 establishments in Washington (state), Festivals established in 1984

## Introduction

It started with five dollars of bulbs. George Gibbs had come from England and settled on Orcas Island around 1883 to grow apples and hazelnuts, and nine years later he spent five dollars on flower bulbs more or less as an experiment. When he dug them up a couple of seasons later, they had multiplied so enthusiastically that he began to wonder what Puget Sound might be capable of. He wrote to Dutch growers for advice and got very little - the Netherlands bulb trade guarded its methods closely. So he sent them a few of his own bulbs instead. The Dutch, sufficiently impressed, sailed to a small island in Washington to see with their own eyes how tulips could grow somewhere that was not Holland.

## A Government Experiment

In 1899 Gibbs wrote to the United States Department of Agriculture arguing that bulbs could be a commercial crop in the Pacific Northwest, and to his considerable credit the department listened. In 1905 the USDA shipped him 15,000 bulbs imported from Holland to grow under contract. The trial went well enough that in 1908 the government established its own ten-acre test garden near Bellingham, and well enough again that by 1920 Bellingham had a tulip festival of its own to celebrate what the industry had become. It did not last. Hard freezes in 1916, 1925 and 1929 cost growers heavily, and the Great Depression finished the argument; the Bellingham festival was discontinued in 1930. The growers, however, did not quit. They moved south, into the flat river-bottom farmland of Skagit County, and started again.

## Roozen

William Roozen arrived in the United States in 1946 from a Dutch family that had been growing bulbs for six generations. He worked other people's farms first, then started his own in Skagit County in 1950, and in 1955 he bought the Washington Bulb Company outright. That purchase made him the dominant figure among the valley's four flower-growing families and made Washington Bulb the largest grower of tulip, daffodil and iris bulbs in North America - a fair distance from five dollars of bulbs on Orcas Island half a century earlier. In 1985 the operation opened Roozengaarde, a public display garden and gift shop that has become one of the festival's biggest draws in its own right: fifty acres and roughly a million bulbs, planted specifically for people to walk through.

## The Festival Is the Bloom

Growers were opening display gardens to the public long before anyone organised a festival around them. In 1984 the Mount Vernon Chamber of Commerce put on the first Skagit Valley Tulip Festival as a modest two-day affair, meant to add some structure to a month when strangers were going to show up anyway. It has since stretched to fill the whole of April, from the first to the thirtieth, with street fairs, art shows and sporting events attached. The scheduling is honest about its own limitations. Nobody controls when tulips open; a cold spring pushes the colour into late April, a warm one brings it early, and the festival simply runs for the month and lets the fields decide what visitors will see on any given day.

## Half a Million People in a Farm Valley

The numbers are genuinely hard to pin down, which is what happens when the attraction is a public road through private farmland. The festival claims more than a million visitors. Travel + Leisure put attendance at 500,000 back in 2003; one 2008 report said 350,000; in 2023 KING 5 estimated 400,000 to 500,000. Whatever the true figure, the 2024 festival was reckoned to have generated around $83 million within Skagit County, which is real money for an agricultural county of that size. The fragility showed in 2020, when organisers cancelled on 25 March - first scaling down to limited entry, then to car-only tours, then abandoning it entirely under the state's stay-at-home order. Some growers cut their fields early to keep people from coming. It was a strange, deliberate act: destroying the bloom in order to protect everyone from it.

## Flight Context

The Skagit Valley Tulip Festival centres on 48.4167 N, 122.4170 W around Mount Vernon and Burlington, Washington, on the flat delta farmland between Interstate 5 and Padilla Bay. In April the valley is unmistakable from 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL: hard-edged rectangles of saturated red, yellow, pink and white laid across the grid of section roads, with the Skagit River winding through and Mount Baker standing over the whole scene to the northeast on a clear day. Outside the bloom it reads as ordinary delta cropland. Skagit Regional Airport (KBVS) sits about 3 nm north at Burlington and is the closest field; Bellingham International (KBLI) is roughly 23 nm north and Paine Field (KPAE) about 31 nm south. Expect heavy sightseeing traffic and congested roads below during April, plus morning valley fog and low marine stratus off Padilla Bay that often clears by midday.
