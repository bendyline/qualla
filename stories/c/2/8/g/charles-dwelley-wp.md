# Charles Dwelley

The editor who spent forty years making a Skagit County mill-town weekly famous, beat a libel suit with the biggest headline he ever set, and talked a cement plant and a mountain highway into existence.

**Location:** 48.3918, -122.4478

**Categories:** People from Mount Vernon, Washington, Journalists from Washington (state), American newspaper editors, 1908 births, 1993 deaths, 20th-century American journalists, Skagit County, Washington

## Introduction

When the libel case finally ended in his favour, Charles Dwelley set the words WE WON across the front page of The Concrete Herald in 80-point bold — larger type than he had used to announce the end of the Second World War. It was an act of pure editorial temperament, and it was entirely in character. For forty-one years Dwelley ran a weekly newspaper in a cement town of a few hundred people in the upper Skagit Valley, and he ran it as though the whole country were reading. By the 1950s a surprising amount of the country actually was.

## Sent Upriver to Save a Dying Paper

He was twenty-one. Frank Evans, the editor of the Sedro-Woolley Courier Times, had just bought a failing weekly in Concrete, Washington, and in 1929 he sent his young employee up the valley to see whether anything could be done with it. Dwelley and his wife Helen moved into the top floor of the building that housed the Linotype machines, and their son Art was born there in February 1930 — the same year the paper was reincorporated, and the same year the Depression arrived. The 1930s were brutal. In one memorable stretch, too sick to produce an issue, Dwelley invited his readers to pay for the paper he had not published so that he could cover the doctor's bill. They did. He cleared the debts, bought out Evans's share, and kept going: between 1930 and 1943, while Concrete's population slipped slightly, Herald subscriptions climbed 67 percent.

## Dwellisms

Dwelley held two firm convictions about editorials. The first was that an editor who declines to state an opinion has no business writing one. The second was that short beat long, always. Starting in 1949 the results began leaving the valley: his pieces were read aloud on national radio, reprinted in Reader's Digest and The New York Times, and passed around the trade as "Dwellisms," admired for a dry, deadpan turn that was hard to imitate. The Washington State Press Club gave him first place for editorial writing in 1949 and again in 1950. In 1952 the Ladies' Home Journal named him one of the four best editorial writers in the United States, which earned him the nickname "Ladies' man" from colleagues who could not let it go. His 44-page edition marking the paper's fiftieth anniversary took a first-place award from the National Newspaper Association. By 1957 the Herald's circulation was larger than the population of the town it came from.

## The Biggest Headline He Ever Set

In 1955 Dwelley wrote an editorial about how Jacob Koops, the police judge in nearby Lyman, was conducting his office. Koops sued, naming both the paper and Dwelley personally, and the timing could hardly have been worse — a recent Washington Supreme Court decision had sharply narrowed what a newspaper could say about a public official without exposure. The case dragged on for nearly three years, a genuinely dangerous stretch for a small weekly with no legal cushion. The court finally cleared him. Whatever relief he felt went straight into the composing room, and the next issue carried those two words in type he had never had occasion to use before.

## Twenty-One Months Away

On 27 March 1944 Dwelley learned that his younger brother William had been killed in Europe. He was thirty-six and under no obligation to go anywhere. He enlisted in the Navy. Boot camp took him to Farragut, Idaho, and he graduated with honours from the torpedo school at San Diego that December as a Torpedoman Third Class, then shipped to the Seventh Fleet in the Admiralty Islands — where it turned out the Navy had a surplus of torpedomen; from April 1945 he spent the rest of the war in Manila on shore patrol. He came home on a liberty ship in December 1945. Back in Concrete, Helen had tried to keep a thinned-down Herald going for seven months before the paper simply stopped; there was no issue at all between November 1944 and the end of May 1945. All three of the women Dwelley married wrote for the paper, and without them there would have been nothing to come back to.

## Dust and a Highway

The Herald was only half of what he did. For forty years Dwelley fought the dust pouring off the Superior Portland Cement Company's plant — the industry the town was named for and lived on, which made the campaign considerably braver than it sounds. He organized residents, brought in Senator Lowell Peterson, and backed a dust-emission study that found pollution more than ten times over acceptable levels; the effort helped create the Northwest Air Pollution Authority, and five days after the State Air Pollution Control Board first met, the plant shut down for good. From 1948 he campaigned for a road across the mountains, becoming president of the North Cross-State Highway Association in 1959 and living to drive the North Cascades Highway he had argued into being. His editorials also won the upper valley its first bridge across the Skagit; a plaque went up on it in his honour in 2006. He sold the Herald in 1970, retired to La Conner, wrote a column called "The Bridge Tender," and died in 1993. The paper he rescued failed under later owners and was revived in 2009 by a Concrete resident who thought the town should still have one.

## Flight Context

Charles Dwelley's territory runs along 48.392°N, 122.448°W and eastward — the lower Skagit delta around La Conner and Mount Vernon, where he was born and is buried, connected by the Skagit River to Concrete some 50 kilometres upriver, where he made his name. Best viewed from 2,000 to 5,000 feet. From the air the delta is unmistakable: a flat green-and-brown quilt of diked farm fields running out to Skagit and Padilla bays, with the Swinomish Channel as a straight cut of water separating Fidalgo Island from the mainland and La Conner sitting on its east bank. Follow the Skagit inland and the valley narrows fast, the farmland giving way to timber and then to the North Cascades; Concrete sits on the north bank where the Baker River comes in, with Lake Shannon and the Baker Dam immediately upstream and Mount Baker filling the sky to the northwest. Skagit Regional (KBVS) at Burlington is the principal field, with Anacortes (KOTS) to the west, Arlington (KAWO) to the south, and Bellingham International (KBLI) about 25 nautical miles north; Concrete has its own small strip in the upper valley. Expect dense ground fog over the delta on calm autumn mornings, strong easterly outflow winds coming down the Skagit gorge in winter, and rapidly deteriorating conditions eastward as the terrain rises toward the North Cascades Highway corridor.
