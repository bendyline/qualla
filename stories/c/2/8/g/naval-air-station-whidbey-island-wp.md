# Naval Air Station Whidbey Island

A Puget Sound air station surveyed the day after Pearl Harbor, built for flying boats, and now home to the Navy's electronic attack force.

**Location:** 48.3518, -122.6559

**Categories:** Military Aviation, Naval History, Whidbey Island, Washington State, World War II

## Introduction

On 8 December 1941, one day after the attack on Pearl Harbor, three workers walked onto a patch of Whidbey Island farmland and started a topographic survey. What they were laying out would become Ault Field, the main runway complex of Naval Air Station Whidbey Island. The Navy had been looking for a site on Puget Sound for most of the preceding year, and had just changed its mind about what kind of site it needed. Construction began the following March. By August a plane had landed, and the war the survey crew had learned about the day before was well under way.

## Ten Days to Choose a Shoreline

The search started on 17 January 1941, almost eleven months before the United States entered the war, when the Chief of Naval Operations asked the Commandant of the 13th Naval District to find somewhere to re-arm and refuel the patrol planes defending Puget Sound. The candidates fell away quickly. Lake Ozette, Indian Island, Keystone Harbor, Penn Cove and Oak Harbor were each rejected in turn: mountainous terrain here, a bluff shorefront there, inaccessibility, no usable beaches, or an exposed lee shore. Within ten days the commanding officer of Naval Air Station Seattle came back with an answer, recommending a site on Saratoga Passage along the shores of Crescent Harbor and Forbes Point, where seaplanes could get down in instrument conditions. Dredging, filling and utility work were all under way by the end of November. Then the order arrived to find a land plane site as well.

## A Field Named for a Man Who Did Not Come Back

Construction of Ault Field, four miles north of the seaplane site, began on 1 March 1942. The first aircraft touched down on 5 August, an SNJ trainer flown by Lieutenant Newton Wakefield, a former civil engineer and airline pilot who would later serve as the station's operations officer. Almost nobody looked up. The runway was still unfinished and everyone was busy working on it. On 21 September 1942 the first commanding officer, Captain Cyril Thomas Simard, read the orders placing the field in service, and the air station was commissioned. A year later, on 25 September 1943, the land plane field was formally named Ault Field for Commander William B. Ault, who had gone missing in action at the Battle of the Coral Sea the previous year.

## Wildcats, Catalinas and Target Tugs

The wartime station filled up fast. Grumman F4F Wildcats arrived in 1942 and were entirely replaced by F6F Hellcats by the end of 1943; Lockheed PV-1 Venturas came in for training, and in 1944 Douglas SBD Dauntless dive bombers became the dominant type at Ault Field. Down at the Seaplane Base, Consolidated PBY Catalinas and Martin PBM Mariners rode at their moorings through the summer of 1944, alongside a handful of land-based Martin B-26 Marauders assigned to the unglamorous but essential job of towing gunnery targets. Meanwhile an auxiliary strip two and a half miles southeast of Coupeville was surveyed in February 1943 and in use by September. It is still there, still busy, and still has no permanently assigned personnel.

## Six Decades of Changing Missions

Peace nearly closed the place. Operations dropped to reduced status after 1945, and across the country stations were being shuttered for failing the new standards: 6,000-foot runways, approach paths that radar could work with in any weather. Whidbey survived, took on Lockheed P2V Neptunes, and came fully back to life with the Korean War. In 1955 the crews of a disestablished patrol squadron became the nucleus of the first heavy attack squadron on the West Coast, the word heavy signalling nuclear weapons delivery. A search and rescue team started in 1960 with two Sikorsky helicopters on a two-year trial. Seaplane patrol operations by active forces ended on 1 March 1970. And then the station found the identity it has kept ever since: sixteen Grumman A-6 Intruder squadrons made it the West Coast home of all-weather medium attack, and in October 1970 a redesignated squadron became the Navy's first EA-6B Prowler unit and the sole replacement training squadron for Navy and Marine Corps Prowler crews.

## What Flies Here Now

The Prowler's successor arrived on 3 June 2008, when the first Boeing EA-18G Growler was accepted by VAQ-129 "Vikings" at Ault Field, and the electronic attack mission remains Whidbey's signature. The maritime patrol line continued too: P-3C Orions consolidated here in the 1990s as other bases closed, and in October 2016 a patrol squadron here became the first at the station to convert to the P-8 Poseidon, with a P-8A training centre opening that same month. Twenty active duty squadrons and three reserve squadrons operate from the base today, flying the Growler, the MH-60S Seahawk, the P-8 and the C-40 Clipper, supported by more than fifty tenant commands. Two MH-60S Nighthawks stand search and rescue alert around the clock, covering water, inland terrain and mountains across the greater Pacific Northwest, and while the unit exists for military aircrews, missions to reach civilians in trouble are frequently approved and flown.

## Flight Context

Naval Air Station Whidbey Island centres on Ault Field at 48.3518 N, 122.6559 W, about three miles north of Oak Harbor on Whidbey Island in Island County, Washington. Best viewing altitude for the surrounding geography is 4,000 to 6,000 feet, though this is active and heavily used military airspace and transit requires care. Key visual landmarks: the runway complex of Ault Field with the town of Oak Harbor to its south, the Seaplane Base on Crescent Harbor to the southeast, Deception Pass and its bridge at the island's northern tip, Penn Cove cutting deep into the island to the south, and Naval Outlying Landing Field Coupeville roughly nine miles south, used for field carrier landing practice. The station is KNUW. Nearby civil fields include Skagit Regional (KBVS) about 12 nm east, Bellingham International (KBLI) to the northeast, and Paine Field (KPAE) to the southeast; Victoria International (CYYJ) lies across the strait to the northwest. Expect intense pattern activity, low-level jet operations around both fields, marine stratus in summer mornings, and the Olympic and Cascade ranges dominating the horizon west and east.
