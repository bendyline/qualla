# Anacortes Community Forest Lands

A city bought a water company in 1919 and ended up owning a forest - then spent the 1990s deciding, against its own balance sheet, to stop cutting it down.

**Location:** 48.4677, -122.6290

**Categories:** Forests, Washington, Conservation, Hiking, Parks, Puget Sound

## Introduction

Anacortes never meant to own a forest. On August 2, 1919, the city bought the equipment and lands of the Washington Power, Light and Water Company for $135,000, because a town on an island needs to control its own water. What came attached to those reservoirs was several thousand acres of Fidalgo Island timber - lakes, bogs, rock faces, and second-growth conifers wrapped around Little Cranberry, Heart, and Whistle lakes. For seventy years the city treated it the way any owner of standing timber would. Then it stopped, and the argument about why is one of the more interesting municipal fights on Puget Sound.

## Logging the Municipal Woodlot

Until 1989 the forest was cut for revenue, which is unremarkable - this is Washington, and the logic of timber as a public asset ran deep. What made the arrangement collapse was arithmetic rather than sentiment. By the late 1980s the forest was producing little or no net income; the logging was no longer paying for itself in any obvious way. Into that gap stepped a community organization, Friends of the Forest, formed specifically to oppose the cutting. The Parks Department went out and asked residents what they wanted from the land, and the answers came back clearly in favour of non-forestry uses. A separate study confirmed what anyone walking the trails on a Saturday could have guessed: the trail network was the thing people actually valued.

## April 15, 1991

Change followed quickly for a municipal process. The Forest Endowment Fund started in 1990, and on April 15, 1991, the City Council struck logging from the permitted uses of the forest altogether, with an exception only for removing downed or dangerous trees. That is a rare decision for a city to make - a deliberate write-down of an asset in favour of something harder to put on a ledger. It also left an obvious question: a council can reverse a council. What stops a future set of commissioners, facing a bad budget year, from reaching for the timber again?

## A Thousand Dollars an Acre

The answer was to make the protection permanent and to let the public buy it, acre by acre. Contributions to the conservation easement program preserve the forest at a rate of $1,000 per acre, and the easements themselves are held and monitored by Skagit Land Trust - an outside body with a legal interest in seeing the terms kept, which is precisely the point. By mid-2007 more than $1.5 million had been contributed and 1,500 acres placed under easement. It is a slow, unglamorous mechanism, funded largely by people who live nearby and hike here, and it converts affection into something a future council cannot simply vote away.

## Douglas-Fir, Madrone, and Salal

Most of this forest has been logged once or twice, and it shows in the even-aged stands and the old skid routes. A notable grove of low-elevation old growth survives, though, which on Puget Sound is close to a miracle of oversight. The canopy is dominated by the pine family - Douglas-fir, western hemlock, shore pine, grand fir - mixed with bigleaf maple, red alder, and the peeling cinnamon bark of Pacific madrone, a tree that looks tropical and is not. Underneath, salal and vine maple do most of the work of making the understory nearly impassable off-trail. Between the timber lie lakes, bogs, wetlands, low mountains, and rocky cliffs, packed into a surprisingly small area.

## The View from Erie

Mount Erie is the high point of Fidalgo Island at 1,273 feet, and it earns far more altitude than that number suggests, because it rises from sea level with nothing in the way. From the top the San Juan Islands lie scattered north and west, Whidbey Island runs south, and on a clear day Mount Baker and the Cascades stand up to the east. Sugarloaf, 1,044 feet, sits just six-tenths of a mile north with a view of its own. The whole holding now runs to more than 2,800 acres laced with over fifty miles of marked multi-use trails, which means a small island city maintains a trail system that many national forests would recognize as substantial. Not bad for a water utility purchase.

## Flight Context

The Anacortes Community Forest Lands occupy the interior and southern half of Fidalgo Island at roughly 48.4677°N, 122.6290°W, immediately west and south of the city of Anacortes in Skagit County, Washington. The landmark is Mount Erie - a dark, abrupt forested dome with a summit road and antennas, standing 1,273 ft above the surrounding lowland, with Sugarloaf just north of it and Campbell Lake to the south. Heart Lake, Whistle Lake, and Little Cranberry Lake sit in the timber between the summits. Deception Pass and its bridge lie a few miles south; the San Juan Islands and the Anacortes ferry terminal are west; the Skagit flats and refinery stacks mark the east. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,000-3,500 ft MSL to read the lakes, the summits, and the trail network, or 6,500-8,000 ft to set Fidalgo Island against the San Juans with Mount Baker on the horizon. NAS Whidbey Island (KNUW) is roughly 7 nm south with Class C airspace and heavy military traffic - check before transiting; Skagit Regional (KBVS) is about 8 nm east and Bellingham International (KBLI) about 22 nm north. Anacortes Airport (74S) lies on the island's north side. Expect strong updrafts and rotor near Mount Erie in westerly wind, common winter low cloud, and excellent summer visibility.
