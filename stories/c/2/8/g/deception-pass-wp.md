# Deception Pass

A strait named after the mistake it caused, where eight-knot tides boil under a 1935 steel bridge and more than three million visitors a year come to watch the water.

**Location:** 48.4072, -122.6225

**Categories:** Straits, State Parks, Bridges, Maritime History, Washington

## Introduction

George Vancouver named this strait after his own error. In May 1792 he had sent Joseph Whidbey east in small boats to probe the waters behind the long island that would take Whidbey's name. Whidbey worked up Saratoga Passage into the shallows of Skagit Bay, found no way through, and came back. The conclusion was obvious and wrong: the land was a peninsula joined to the mainland. Only in June, working north along the outer coast, did Whidbey find the gap - on 7 June 1792 - and what he found was, in the expedition's own words, a very narrow and intricate channel abounding with rocks above and beneath the surface of the water. Vancouver called it Deception, and the name has stuck for more than two centuries: a permanent monument to a navigator admitting he had been fooled by a coastline.

## Older Names, Earlier Ships

The pass had names long before that. Coast Salish peoples have lived around these waters for thousands of years, and the strait carries Indigenous names still in use - sčudᶻ in Lushootseed, Xwchsónges in the Samish dialect of Straits Salish - names attached to a place that was a fishing ground and a travel route, not a puzzle to be solved. Nor was Vancouver first among Europeans. Two years earlier, in 1790, members of Manuel Quimper's expedition aboard the Princesa Real had already seen the gap and marked it on Spanish charts as Boca de Flon. That name did not survive the century. Vancouver's did, which says less about who arrived first than about whose empire did the printing.

## Eight Knots

Everything that happens here comes down to a bottleneck. Puget Sound has to breathe through this slot, and on ebb and flood the current runs at roughly eight knots - faster than most small craft can make headway against. The water does not flow so much as convulse: standing waves, whirlpools wide enough to see from the bridge walkway, boiling eddies that rise and collapse and rise again. Boats gather on both sides, engines idling, waiting for slack. Kayakers do the opposite, timing the biggest tidal exchanges to surf the standing waves through class 2 and class 3 conditions. Divers come too, though the window is narrow - a handful of days a year when the tide allows a drift dive from the cove, under the bridge and back again. The reward for that risk is one of the richest walls in the Pacific Northwest, the rock plastered in colourful invertebrates, with lingcod and greenlings working the current.

## Ben Ure Island

Just east of the bridge sits a small island that carries a much darker history. In the late 1880s, United States law had shut Chinese labourers out of the country, and a smuggling trade grew up along this coast to move people across the closed border. Ben Ure ran such an operation from the island now named for him, working with a partner known as Pirate Kelly and evading the Customs Department for years. Local tradition holds that Ure's wife, a Native woman whose name has not come down to us, camped on nearby Strawberry Island and signalled with a fire from its summit when the coast was clear. The part that should not be softened is what Ure did with his passengers. He bound them into burlap sacks for the crossing, so that if a revenue cutter appeared he could put them over the side. The tide did the rest. Currents carried the drowned north and west toward San Juan Island, where a number washed up in the inlet still called Dead Man's Bay. They were people who had paid for passage to a new life, and they were treated as cargo to be jettisoned.

## Quarry, Then Bridge

Between 1910 and 1914 a rock quarry operated on the Fidalgo side of the pass, worked by inmates on an honour programme from the state penitentiary at Walla Walla - around forty men at its height, some serving sentences for murder, crushing stone into gravel under guard and loading it onto barges bound for the Seattle waterfront. The camp was dismantled in 1924; its tunnel survived until 2006, when it was sealed after visitors died on the cliffs around it. What replaced all of it is the structure everyone now photographs. The Deception Pass Bridge opened on 31 July 1935: a 976-foot main span arching from Whidbey Island to Pass Island, with a second, shorter span over Canoe Pass carrying the road on to Fidalgo, the whole structure hanging over that churning water with nothing but air between. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1982.

## Three Million a Year

Deception Pass State Park wraps 3,854 acres around the strait and draws more than three million visitors annually, making it the busiest state park in Washington. Most of them do the same thing: walk out onto the bridge sidewalk, look straight down, and stay longer than they meant to. The place has an outsized hold on the regional imagination - Seattle bands have filmed videos here and named songs after it - but the pull is simpler than any of that. It is one of the few places where you can stand still on solid steel and watch an entire inland sea change direction beneath your feet.

## Flight Context

Deception Pass cuts through at 48.4072 N, 122.6225 W, separating Whidbey Island to the south from Fidalgo Island to the north and connecting Skagit Bay with Rosario Strait and the Strait of Juan de Fuca. From the air it is one of the most legible features in the northern Sound: a narrow slot barely wider than the bridge that spans it, with visible current lines, standing waves and whirlpool patterns streaming from the gap on a running tide. The twin steel spans - the main 976-foot bridge from Whidbey to Pass Island, and the shorter Canoe Pass crossing beyond it - carry State Route 20 and mark the spot unmistakably. Naval Air Station Whidbey Island (KNUW) lies about 6 nm south and its Class C airspace and military traffic dominate the area, so check NOTAMs and stay in contact; Skagit Regional (KBVS) is roughly 12 nm east and Anacortes (74S) sits to the northwest. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 3,500 feet MSL frames the pass, both islands and the park's forested headlands. Fog forms readily in the strait, and westerly wind funnelling through the gap can make the approach turbulent at low level.
