# Kiket Island

A Skagit County island once slated for a 1,100-megawatt nuclear plant, now co-owned by Washington State Parks and the Swinomish Indian Tribal Community.

**Location:** 48.4192, -122.5672

**Categories:** washington, puget-sound, island, state-park, conservation, tribal-comanagement

## Introduction

Kiket Island came within about three years of holding a nuclear power station. In 1969 Seattle City Light and the Snohomish County PUD put forward a $250 million plan for an 1,100-megawatt plant on this small wooded island in Skagit Bay, less than four miles northwest of La Conner. By 1972 the proposal was dead on environmental grounds. The utilities held the land for another eight years, sold it in 1980, and the island went on being what it had always been: a low hump of fir and madrona tethered to Fidalgo Island by a sand spit, with a Lushootseed name that goes back a great deal further than any of this - kʷuʔkʷuʔtali, the place of cattail mat.

## Changing Canoes

Kiket is a tied island, joined to the larger Fidalgo Island by a tombolo - a bar of sand and gravel built up by waves converging behind an obstacle until the gap closes. An access road runs over it now. The Lushootseed name for that spit is sdᶻalgʷiɬ, which means changing canoes, and it is hard to read that name without picturing the practical business it describes: the crossing point, the place where you get out of one boat and into another.

The geography explains why. Kiket, with Hope Island to the south and little Skagit Island a few hundred feet to the southwest, sits at the seam between Skagit Bay and Similk Bay. Two bodies of water, two sets of conditions, one narrow neck of land between them - exactly the kind of place a name like that attaches itself to and stays.

## The Reactor That Wasn't

The late 1960s were the high-water mark of American nuclear optimism, and Puget Sound was full of proposals. Kiket looked, on paper, like a straightforward site: saltwater on every side, almost nobody living on it, and a position within reach of the load centres growing to the south.

What it also had was an ecosystem, and by 1972 that argument had won. The plan was dropped over environmental concerns before a shovel went in the ground. The comparison that is impossible not to make sits a little way down the coast at Satsop, where Washington's public power system did build, and then did not finish, and spent decades paying for it. Kiket's reactor stayed on paper. The island kept its trees.

## The Best-Studied Shoreline

Here is the strange legacy of a plant that was never built. To make the case for the reactor - and then to argue against it - scientists spent the last decades of the twentieth century working over this shoreline in detail, surveying what lived in the eelgrass and the intertidal and the forest above it. The result is that the coast around Kiket Island has been called one of the best-studied stretches of shoreline in Washington.

That body of knowledge did not evaporate when the project died. It became the baseline - the record of what was here before, against which anyone can measure what is here now. Further wildlife surveys were run in the winter of 2008 and 2009 as the island moved toward protection, adding another layer to a dataset that started as an industrial due-diligence exercise and ended up as conservation science.

## Two Governments, One Island

On 23 June 2010, the Washington State Parks and Recreation Commission and the Swinomish Indian Tribal Community signed a joint ownership agreement covering Kiket Island and tiny Flagstaff Island, which hangs off it by a second tombolo. The purchase price was $14 million, assembled by the two partners with The Trust for Public Land from grants and donations across a wide range of sources.

The result is the Kukutali Preserve, part of Deception Pass State Park and co-managed by a state agency and a tribal government - an arrangement rare enough to be worth noticing. The preserve is open to the public: a parking area off Snee-Oosh Road, restrooms there and at the west end of the island, and a walk out across the spit into second-growth forest with saltwater on both sides. It is a quiet place to spend an afternoon, which is exactly the point, and it is only quiet because two different plans for it both fell through and a third one worked.

## Flight Context

Kiket Island lies at 48.419N, 122.567W at the southwestern edge of Fidalgo Island, between Skagit Bay and Similk Bay in Skagit County. From 2,000 to 3,500 feet the tombolo is the feature to look for - a thin pale ribbon of sand connecting Kiket to the Fidalgo shore, with Flagstaff Island hanging off its west end and Hope and Skagit Islands lying just south. Deception Pass and its bridge are 5 nm northwest; the Skagit River delta spreads east toward Mount Vernon. Nearest fields are Skagit Regional (KBVS) at Burlington about 10 nm east, Anacortes (74S) to the north, and NAS Whidbey Island (KNUW) to the west across Skagit Bay - expect military traffic and active airspace in that direction. Morning fog off the delta is common in autumn, and strong outflow through Deception Pass can produce rough air on the lee side of the ridges.
