# Swinomish Indian Tribal Community

On Fidalgo Island, a Coast Salish nation rebuilt the first traditional clam garden in the United States in two hundred years - and won a $394 million judgment against a railroad.

**Location:** 48.4014, -122.5272

**Categories:** Coast Salish governments, Native American tribes in Washington (state), Geography of Skagit County, Washington, Federally recognized tribes in the United States, Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast

## Introduction

In 2022, on the shore of Kiket Island, the Swinomish built a clam garden - a low rock terrace laid across the intertidal zone at exactly the right elevation, which flattens the beach behind it, warms it, and lets clams settle and grow in numbers the raw shoreline cannot support. It was the first traditional clam garden built in the United States in two hundred years. The technique is not folklore recovered from a book; it is engineering that Coast Salish people ran along this coast for millennia, and it works, producing roughly four times the clams of an unterraced beach. That project tells you most of what you need to know about the Swinomish Indian Tribal Community: an old technology, revived deliberately, as a response to a warming sea.

## Seven Rivers, One Reservation

The Swinomish Tribe is the legal successor to signatories of the 1855 Treaty of Point Elliott, and it is a community assembled from several. Its people descend from Swinomish, Lower Skagit, Kikiallus and Samish bands whose territories ran through the Skagit and Samish river valleys and out across Fidalgo, Whidbey, Camano and the San Juan islands; Suquamish and Upper Skagit families live on the reservation too. The reservation itself occupies the southeastern side of Fidalgo Island in Skagit County, roughly 15 square miles across the Swinomish Channel from the town of La Conner, sixty-five miles north of Seattle. The 2020 census counted 3,228 residents, tribal and non-tribal. The tribe reports 1,439 enrolled members.

## The Boundary That Was Moved

In 1873 President Ulysses S. Grant issued an executive order redrawing the reservation's western boundary, removing March Point and the ground around it. The Supreme Court has since held repeatedly that only Congress, never a president, can shrink a reservation, and the Swinomish have never stopped asserting the line drawn on the original treaty maps. In 1971 the Indian Claims Commission declined to restore it. The following July the same commission awarded the tribe $29,000 for ceded land, having found that the United States had originally paid an "unconscionably low" amount - a finding and a remedy that sit oddly beside each other. March Point today carries two oil refineries. In 2008 the tribe produced a documentary, March Point, following three Swinomish teenagers as they investigated what those refineries meant for the people living beside them.

## One Hundred Cars at a Time

A railway has crossed this reservation since 1889, when the Seattle and Northern Railroad laid track over it without asking. A 1991 easement set the terms plainly: one eastbound and one westbound train per day, twenty-five cars or fewer. In September 2012 the tribe learned from news reports that unit trains of a hundred cars and more had begun crossing, and by April 2015 BNSF was running six such trains a week, hauling crude oil past the tribe's own hotel, casino, gas station and dispensary. The tribe sued in April 2015. The federal court in Seattle found a breach; the Ninth Circuit rejected BNSF's appeal in 2020; a 2022 ruling found the trespass intentional and a 2023 trial found it willful and knowing. On 6 June 2024 the court ordered BNSF to disgorge $394,517,169 - net trespass profits of $362,267,169 plus $32,250,000 for the use value of those profits.

## What the Warming River Took

The harder fight is not in court. Swinomish fish harvests have fallen by more than eighty percent in two decades, and the reason is temperature: the Skagit River has grown too warm for the salmon that have fed this community since before the treaty. In February 2024 the tribe announced plans to sue the Environmental Protection Agency over its failure to bring those stream temperatures down. The Swinomish have treated the connection between ecology and health as a formal matter, arguing that Indigenous health indicators are themselves a metric of climate change - ocean acidification thins the shells of the marine animals people here eat, and the effect shows up in bodies before it shows up in policy. The tribe helps co-manage the watershed through the Skagit River System Cooperative, formed in 1976, and through the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission, created after the 1974 Boldt Decision affirmed treaty fishing rights.

## Cedar Hats and a Shared Park

Government here runs through an eleven-member Senate elected to staggered five-year terms, under a constitution adopted on 27 January 1936, working through nineteen committees and ten boards and commissions. Its economic arm has made the tribe one of Skagit County's five largest employers, with over 350 people in tribal government and roughly 600 more across the casino and other enterprises. But the places that read most clearly are the public ones. At Swadabs Park on the Swinomish Channel, three pavilions shaped like woven cedar hats stand above a native plant garden, built for the 2011 Canoe Journey when the paddle came to Swinomish. And Kukutali Preserve - 83 acres across three islands, with more than two miles of natural shoreline - is the first tribal state park in United States history to be co-owned and jointly managed by a Native nation and a state.

## Flight Context

The Swinomish Reservation lies at 48.4014 N, 122.5272 W, on the southeastern side of Fidalgo Island in Skagit County, Washington, about 65 miles north of Seattle. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,500-5,000 ft AGL. The orienting feature is the Swinomish Channel, the narrow dredged waterway separating Fidalgo Island from the mainland, crossed at La Conner by the distinctive orange arch of the Rainbow Bridge. North of the channel, March Point pushes into Padilla Bay carrying two oil refineries whose tanks and flare stacks are visible for miles. East and south, the Skagit River delta spreads into an enormous flat of diked farmland - in April it turns to blocks of solid red and yellow when the tulip fields bloom. Mount Erie rises on Fidalgo Island to the northwest, with Deception Pass beyond it and the San Juan Islands out to the west. Nearest airports: KBVS (Skagit Regional, Burlington) about 15 km east, KNUW (NAS Whidbey Island) about 15 km southwest - expect military traffic and restricted airspace - and KPAE (Paine Field) about 50 km south. Winter brings persistent low stratus over the delta; summer afternoons are usually clear with northwesterly flow.
