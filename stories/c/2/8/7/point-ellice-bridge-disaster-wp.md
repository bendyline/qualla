# Point Ellice Bridge disaster

On a holiday afternoon in 1896, an overloaded streetcar took the Point Ellice Bridge into Victoria's Upper Harbour, killing 55 people — the worst transit disaster in British Columbia's history.

## Introduction

The fare register said 98. When the wreck was examined, that was the number of tickets the conductor of streetcar No. 16 had managed to punch, and investigators knew he had not finished his round — there were 143 people aboard. That gap between the money collected and the bodies counted is the closest thing this disaster has to a single piece of evidence, and it tells you everything about the afternoon of 26 May 1896: a car packed past any sensible limit, on a holiday, on a bridge nobody had bothered to inspect properly, in a city where nothing of the sort had ever gone wrong before.

## Two O'Clock on the Queen's Birthday

Victoria was celebrating Queen Victoria's birthday, and the entertainment of the day was a mock naval battle out at Esquimalt. Half the city wanted to see it. Streetcars ran west along Bay Street and over the Point Ellice Bridge to get there, and No. 16 went onto the span just before two in the afternoon with people standing in the aisles and packed onto the platforms. Two horse-drawn vehicles and a man on a bicycle were on the bridge at the same time. The deck failed beneath the streetcar and everything went into the Upper Harbour together. One detail decided who lived, and it was as arbitrary as where you happened to sit down: only the passengers on the left-hand side of the car got clear of it.

## Fifty-Five

Fifty-five people died — killed by the impact or drowned in the harbour — and twelve of them were children. It remains the worst public transit disaster in British Columbia's history, the worst streetcar accident in North America, and the largest single-day loss of life Victoria has ever suffered. These were not commuters. They were families on a day off, dressed for a holiday, going to watch ships fire blanks at each other in the harbour. Some households lost several members at once. In a city of roughly twenty thousand at the time, there was hardly a neighbourhood that did not know someone in the water.

## The Lawn at 304 Bay Street

The rescue was entirely improvised, and it began within seconds. People along the shoreline at Point Ellice heard the collapse and went straight into the harbour. The daughters of Mr. Justice Drake, from the Tyrwhitt-Drake house, are credited with pulling seven people out of the water themselves. The Grant residence at 304 Bay Street became the centre of everything: Mrs. Grant turned the drawing room into an infirmary while the dead were carried up and laid out on the lawn, and neighbours arrived with blankets, brandy, and whatever else might revive someone. Work went on all afternoon. The Daily Colonist, writing the next morning, reached for the language of its era — it marvelled that "class distinctions and all were forgotten," and that "delicate ladies whom one might expect to shrink from scenes of horror" had worked at resuscitating the victims. Strip away the period condescension and what is left is accurate enough: ordinary people spent an afternoon pulling their neighbours out of a harbour, and a good many of them succeeded.

## Weldless Iron, Welded

The coroner's jury reported on 12 June and did not distribute blame gently. The Consolidated Electric Railway Company was held responsible for permitting its streetcar to be loaded far beyond what the bridge was designed to carry. The City of Victoria was found guilty of contributory negligence: the bridge had not been properly maintained, and council had failed to restrict traffic across it to safe limits despite having the power to do so. Then came the detail that still makes engineers wince. The specifications for the bridge had called for weldless iron. The ironwork actually installed was almost entirely welded — a cheaper substitution, and a fundamentally weaker one, in a structure whose whole job was to resist exactly the kind of load that broke it.

## What the Bridge Is Now

The disaster destroyed the company. Consolidated Electric Railway went into receivership and emerged reorganized on 15 April 1897 as the British Columbia Electric Railway, which would run transit and power across the south coast for the next sixty years. A bridge still carries Bay Street across the same water, joining Victoria to Victoria West at the point where the Upper Harbour ends and the Selkirk Water begins. The name on it belongs to Edward Ellice, who joined the North West Company in 1805, pushed hardest for its merger with the Hudson's Bay Company, went into British politics, and served as the HBC's deputy governor from 1858 to 1863 — a man who almost certainly never saw the crossing that carries his name, and who has been outlived in local memory by fifty-five people whose names are much harder to find.

## Flight Context
