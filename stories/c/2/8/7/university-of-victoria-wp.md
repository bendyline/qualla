# University of Victoria

A college that met in a coal baron's castle, died once, came back twice, and now runs the world's largest transgender archive and a cable on the floor of the Pacific.

**Location:** 48.4633, -123.3117

**Categories:** universities, archives-and-libraries, research-institutions, victoria-bc, british-columbia

## Introduction

For twenty-five years, higher education in Victoria happened inside a castle. From 1921 to 1946, Victoria College held its classes in Craigdarroch Castle, the sandstone pile a coal millionaire had built on a hill above the city, and students walked to lectures past stained glass and carved oak that had been intended for an entirely different purpose. That is the least ordinary chapter in an institutional history full of odd turns. The University of Victoria counts its founding to 1903 and its independence to 1963, and the sixty years between those dates include an outright shutdown, two separate resurrections, and three different landlords.

## Killed Once, Revived Twice

Victoria College opened in 1903 as an affiliate of McGill University, a continent away in Montreal, offering first- and second-year arts and science courses. It was administered by the Victoria School Board and operated as an adjunct to Victoria High School, sharing its building and even its principal -- E.B. Paul until 1908, then S.J. Willis. In 1915 the newly opened University of British Columbia obliged the college to suspend higher education in Victoria altogether. It stayed dead for five years. Local pressure brought it back in 1920, this time affiliated with UBC, and there it remained for four more decades, moving into Craigdarroch Castle in 1921 and then to the Lansdowne site in 1946. The last stage ran from 1945 to 1963 under principals J.M. Ewing and W.H. Hickman, ending with the transformation from two-year college into an independent university.

## The Institution's Debts, Worn on the Sleeve

Universities encode their genealogies in cloth, and UVic's regalia is unusually candid about whose children they are. The Bachelor of Arts hood is solid red, a nod to the McGill affiliation, and the martlets in the coat of arms come from the same source. The BSc hood is gold and the BEd hood blue -- the colours of the University of British Columbia, which sheltered the college for forty-three years. Blue and gold were kept as the university's own. Two mottoes sit on the arms, one at the top in Hebrew characters reading Let there be Light, and one at the bottom in Latin: A Multitude of the Wise is the Health of the World. It is a coat of arms that reads less like a claim of ancient lineage and more like a thank-you note.

## The Chilly Climate Report

On 11 May 1992, the Department of Political Science struck a committee to make the department more supportive to women, formed in response to concerns raised by both graduate and undergraduate students. Its members were five female undergraduates and Dr. Somer Brodribb, an untenured professor in the department. Within the department it came to be known informally as the Climate Committee, borrowing a phrase -- chilly climate -- from the American Project on the Status and Education of Women. On 23 March 1993 the committee delivered a preliminary report examining the experiences of faculty and students alike, setting out what it found troubling in the treatment of women in the department and recommending changes. The report became one of the most widely argued-over documents in Canadian university politics of the decade, and the argument itself, about who gets to describe a workplace, long outlived the committee.

## What the Library Keeps

UVic Libraries holds over four million physical and electronic resources, close to 200,000 journals, and 985 databases across the McPherson Library and the Diana M. Priestly Law Library. The distinctive material is in Special Collections. The Transgender Archives here constitute the largest trans archive in the world, preserving the records of activists, community organizers, and researchers whose histories were, for most of the twentieth century, deliberately not collected by anyone. Alongside them sit the Victoria Women's Movement Archives, collections in anarchist studies, medieval manuscript fragments, and a complete run of the textbooks used in British Columbia's public schools since the province entered confederation in 1871 -- a shelf-by-shelf record of what the province decided children ought to know. The art holdings run to 6,000 works begun in 1953 under Principal Hickman, plus 12,000 objects from the bequest of the English sculptor Katharine Emma Maltwood and her husband John.

## A Cable on the Seafloor, a House With a Name

The research reaches well past the ring of buildings in Oak Bay and Saanich. Ocean Networks Canada, hosted and owned by the university, runs cabled observatories on the ocean floor, the largest of which is NEPTUNE -- an instrumented loop off the west coast of Vancouver Island that has let scientists anywhere in the world watch and operate deep-water experiments in real time since December 2009. On campus, the Bob Wright Centre houses the Canadian Centre for Climate Modelling and Analysis along with an astronomy dome. The oldest building, Clearihue, dates only to 1962, which tells you how quickly this campus was built. The newest arrivals carry different names: Čeqʷəŋín ʔéʔləŋ, Cheko'nien House, completed in 2023 as residence and dining hall, named to acknowledge the Songhees and Esquimalt nations whose territory the university occupies.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.46 N, 123.31 W, straddling the boundary of Oak Bay and Saanich on the Saanich Peninsula, northeast of downtown Victoria. The campus is one of the easiest academic institutions in Canada to identify from the air: its buildings are arranged inside a near-perfect circular ring road roughly a kilometre across, set in a block of Garry oak woodland and playing fields. Cadboro Bay and Cordova Bay lie immediately east and north on the Haro Strait side; downtown Victoria and the Inner Harbour are about 4 nm southwest. Mount Baker is often visible on the eastern horizon across Haro Strait. Best viewed at 2,000-3,500 ft AGL. Victoria International (CYYJ) sits about 10 nm north-northwest and its control zone covers much of the peninsula, so obtain clearance before transiting; Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is 4 nm southwest with heavy floatplane traffic. The area lies in the Olympic rain shadow and enjoys some of the best VFR weather in Canada.
