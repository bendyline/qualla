# Sail and Life Training Society

A Victoria charity that builds its own tall ships - one of them assembled in public at Expo 86 - and puts 1,700 teenagers a year on their decks.

**Location:** 48.4294, -123.3730

**Categories:** Tall ships of Canada, Sail training associations, Organizations based in Victoria, British Columbia, 1974 establishments in British Columbia

## Introduction

At Expo 86 in Vancouver, among the pavilions and monorails, one exhibit was a wooden hull taking shape. Visitors could stand and watch shipwrights plank a brigantine-hulled vessel modelled on the Swift of 1778, and when the fair ended the exhibit did not go into storage. She was launched. Pacific Swift has been sailing ever since, and most of the people aboard her on any given week are between thirteen and twenty-three years old and have never handled a line in their lives.

## Two Ships, Both Homemade

The Sail and Life Training Society builds what it sails. Both of its tall ships came out of a shipyard the society ran at the former Coast Guard base on Victoria's Upper Harbour - an unusual thing for a charity to attempt once, let alone twice. Pacific Swift measures 111 feet sparred and 81 overall, carries 510 square metres of sail on a topsail schooner rig, and weighs in at 71.45 gross tons, with 37 berths. Her predecessor in the yard was the brigantine Spirit of Chemainus, built in 1985. The society's larger vessel, Pacific Grace, was launched in October 1999 and commissioned in May 2001: 138 feet 7 inches sparred, 115 overall, a gaff schooner of 94 gross tons carrying 740 square metres of canvas, modelled on the Grand Banks fishing schooner Robertson II.

## Thirteen to Twenty-Three

About 1,700 young people sail with SALTS each year, and the organization is blunt about its ambition for them: it describes the experience as life-changing. In spring and autumn, school and community groups come aboard for four- and five-day trips that start and finish at Ship Point Wharf in Victoria's Inner Harbour and work their way through the Gulf Islands. The summer programme is longer and far more serious - ten-day voyages that circumnavigate Vancouver Island, run the central British Columbia coast past the Great Bear Rainforest, or reach Haida Gwaii. Day sails are open to anyone of any age. What the ship asks of a teenager is simple and unfamiliar: be somewhere on time, in the dark, in the rain, because four other people are depending on you to be there.

## Pitcairn and Back

Pacific Swift has made four offshore voyages, and the port list reads like a nineteenth-century logbook rather than a youth programme's itinerary: Expo 88 in Australia, Expo 92 in Seville, the West Indies, and the far Pacific outposts of Easter Island and Pitcairn Island. Young people joined for selected legs. Those voyages are now on hold. New Canadian regulations for offshore vessels set requirements the society's current ships do not meet, which is a quiet way of saying that a wooden schooner built to an eighteenth-century model cannot simply be updated to satisfy a modern offshore standard.

## The Ship That Wasn't Built

The answer was to build again. A 116-foot pilot schooner named Leonora was custom-designed to meet the offshore rules and carry the deep-water programme forward. Drawings were done. Then, in September 2022, construction was cancelled - the regulatory hurdles and inflation together had put the vessel out of reach. For an organization whose entire identity rests on the proposition that you can still build a serious wooden ship in a Victoria shipyard and take children to Pitcairn in her, that cancellation is a real loss. The coastal programme continues at full strength; the horizon has simply moved closer for now.

## Paid For in Boats

SALTS is a registered Canadian charity, and a large share of its programme costs is funded in an unusual currency: donated boats. Someone's sloop or trawler, no longer wanted, becomes a bursary. The society arranges third-party appraisal for the tax receipt and absorbs the moorage, transport and broker's fees that would otherwise make such a gift more trouble than it is worth. The administrative offices sit on Herald Street in downtown Victoria with a shop space nearby in Rock Bay - a modest shoreside footprint for an operation whose real premises are two wooden ships and several hundred miles of coastline.

## Flight Context

SALTS is based at 48.4294 N, 123.373 W on Victoria's harbour, with its ships loading at Ship Point Wharf in the Inner Harbour and its former shipyard up in the Upper Harbour above the Johnson Street Bridge. Victoria Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) operates on this same water with dense scheduled floatplane traffic, so overflight needs coordination and altitude discipline; from 2,000-3,000 ft the harbour's three basins and the bridge separating them are easy to pick out, and a two-masted schooner under sail is visible a long way off. The summer training routes run northeast through the Gulf Islands and north up the island's east coast. Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 25 km north on the Saanich Peninsula. Expect morning fog off the Strait of Juan de Fuca, strong afternoon westerlies through the harbour approaches, and rapid deterioration in winter southeasters.
