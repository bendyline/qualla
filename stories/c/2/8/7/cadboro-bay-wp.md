# Cadboro Bay

A gentle Victoria beach with a concrete sea monster in the playground, an 8,000-year-old Songhees village beneath it, and one of the ugliest episodes of 1862 on its shoreline.

**Location:** 48.4577, -123.2930

**Categories:** British Columbia, Victoria, Indigenous History, Beaches, Parks, Coastal History

## Introduction

In May 1862 two Royal Navy gunboats, HMS Grappler and HMS Forward, anchored off a sheltered beach on the eastern edge of Victoria. Ashore, several hundred Haida people had made camp after being driven out of the city, many of them already ill with smallpox. Victoria's police commissioner, Augustus Pemberton, arrived with a police force and the two warships and ordered them gone. About three hundred people were put into their canoes and sent north toward Haida Gwaii, one gunboat escorting them past Nanaimo to make sure they kept going. The beach is Cadboro Bay. Today it has a playground.

## Sungayka

Long before any of that, this bay was Sungayka - snow patches, in the language of the Songhees Nation - and it was a village site for something like eight thousand years. That is not a round number thrown out for effect; it is a span of continuous habitation that predates most of what anyone considers ancient. The Songhees were relocated to Victoria's Inner Harbour in the mid-1800s as the colonial town expanded, but the ground itself keeps the record. The Royal Victoria Yacht Club on the Oak Bay side of the bay sits atop a large Songhees archaeological site, and the land stretching from Gyro Park to Telegraph Bay falls within one of the Douglas Treaties, the fourteen agreements James Douglas made with Vancouver Island nations in the 1850s whose meaning has been argued over ever since.

## Seventy-One Tons and Six Guns

The English name arrived by water. The Cadboro was a Hudson's Bay Company schooner - a small vessel of seventy-one tons, one deck, two masts, and six guns - and she was the first European ship to enter this bay. That was enough to fix the label permanently. It is a modest origin for a place name, and characteristic of this coast, where an enormous number of headlands, channels, and bays carry the names of ships or the officers who sailed them rather than anything descriptive of the place itself. The Songhees name described the land. The English name described a boat that passed by.

## What Happened in 1862

The smallpox epidemic that swept the Pacific Northwest in 1862 began in Victoria, and the colonial response made it catastrophic. Thousands of Indigenous people camped in and around the city were forcibly evicted as the disease spread, a policy that did nothing to contain smallpox and a great deal to broadcast it along the coast. Those who fled to Cadboro Bay were not strangers passing through; they were people who had come to Victoria to trade and work, and who were expelled when they became inconvenient and frightening. Sending sick people home in open canoes did not save Victoria and it devastated the communities they returned to. The epidemic killed a large share of the Indigenous population of what is now British Columbia. The events of that May on this beach are a small piece of it, and a legible one.

## Octopus, Salmon, Tugboat, Sea Monster

Cadboro-Gyro Park now occupies the land behind the beach, and it is beloved by a couple of generations of Victoria children for four large concrete climbing sculptures: an octopus, a salmon, a tugboat, and Cadborosaurus. That last one is local. Caddy, as the alleged sea serpent is known, takes its name from this very bay, one of the rare cases of a cryptid being named for a Victoria neighbourhood rather than the other way round. The park began in 1953 when the Gyro Club bought 4.37 acres from the Goward estate and handed it to Saanich the following year; expropriation of adjacent land in 1961 brought it to its present 6.022 hectares, and the play area was reworked in 2014, with the octopus and salmon relocated. There is more here than concrete. The bay lies within the Victoria Harbour Migratory Bird Sanctuary and provides habitat for species listed under the Species at Risk Act, and the park holds the last population of yellow sand verbena anywhere in Saanich.

## Up the Hill

The name now also covers the neighbourhood between the bay and the University of Victoria, bounded by the Uplands to the south, Ten Mile Point east, and Queenswood north, with Cadboro Bay Village at its centre. Its most consequential twentieth-century resident was Frank V. Hobbs, who came here after his brother Edwin - who had bought land in the bay for a dairy farm - died, and who then threw himself into municipal politics and the Victoria School Board, pushing hard for more high schools across greater Victoria. The elementary school that opened here in 1951 carries his name. UVic sits just up the slope, close enough that its sailing club keeps boats on the beach, which means that on most fine afternoons the water off Sungayka is full of students learning to read the wind.

## Flight Context

Cadboro Bay lies at 48.4577°N, 123.2930°W, on the eastern shore of the Saanich Peninsula's southern tip in Greater Victoria. From the air it reads as a neat crescent of pale sand notched into an otherwise rocky coast, with Ten Mile Point projecting east of it and the Uplands' large wooded lots to the south. The University of Victoria's campus sits on the plateau immediately inland - a distinctive ring road enclosing playing fields. Beyond the bay, Haro Strait opens east toward San Juan Island, and Mount Baker stands out on a clear day. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500-2,500 ft MSL to pick out the beach, Gyro Park, and the Royal Victoria Yacht Club floats; 5,000-6,000 ft frames the whole of Greater Victoria with the Olympic Mountains to the south and the Gulf Islands to the north. Victoria International (CYYJ) is roughly 12 nm north-northwest and Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) about 4 nm southwest. Expect summer afternoon sea breezes, morning fog in late summer and autumn, and generally excellent visibility - this is one of the driest stretches of the coast.
