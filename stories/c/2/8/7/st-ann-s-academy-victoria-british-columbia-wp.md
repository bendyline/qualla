# St. Ann's Academy (Victoria, British Columbia)

The oldest part of this Victoria landmark is not part of the school at all - it is a cathedral that was picked up and moved.

**Location:** 48.4190, -123.3636

**Categories:** National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Schools in Victoria, British Columbia, Tourist attractions in Victoria, British Columbia, Historic buildings and structures in British Columbia, Religious history

## Introduction

The oldest thing at St. Ann's Academy was never meant to be there. The small wooden chapel standing beside the great grey academy building began life in 1858 as St. Andrew's Cathedral, the seat of Victoria's Catholic bishop, designed by a member of the Clerics of Saint Viator who was then a brother and would later be a priest - Joseph Michaud. In 1886 it was moved to this site and pressed into service as the school chapel. A cathedral demoted to a chapel and relocated across town is an odd artefact for any city to keep. Victoria kept it, and today it is the oldest surviving part of a complex that has been a convent, a girls' school, an abandoned ruin, and a government office block, in that order.

## Four Months from Lachine

The Sisters of Saint Anne were a Quebec congregation, founded at Vaudreuil in 1850 by Esther Blondin - in religion, Marie Anne Blondin - to educate rural children whose parents could not read. The order's motherhouse did not move to Lachine until 1864. In 1858, at the request of Bishop Modeste Demers, a party of the Sisters set sail on 8 April for a colony most of Quebec could not have placed on a map, and reached Victoria on 5 June. They opened a school almost immediately. The stone academy that anchors the site came later, in 1871, and it was never only a school: the same building served as the order's Provincial House, its convent, and its novitiate, which meant that the women teaching in the classrooms also entered religious life, lived, and in many cases died within the same walls. Wings went up in 1886 and 1909. The style is Quebec Colonial, transplanted whole - mansard-roofed and severe, and completely unlike anything else in Victoria.

## Who Was Sent Here

St. Ann's was a boarding and day school for girls from grade one to twelve, with boys admitted in the youngest grades until the practice ended in the 1950s. Some of its students were the daughters of Victoria's Catholic families. Others did not choose to come and neither did their parents: First Nations orphans and girls were sent to the academy from other missions run by the Sisters of St. Ann. The same congregation founded and taught at residential schools across British Columbia - Kamloops, Kuper Island, St. Mary's, Lower Post among them - and ran its own residential school at Duncan from 1864 to 1876. Those institutions removed Indigenous children from their families as a matter of policy, and the children transferred into this building in Victoria arrived out of that system. The academy's own records tell us the transfers happened. They tell us almost nothing about the children themselves, which is its own kind of evidence.

## Gutted, Vandalised, and Argued Over

The Sisters closed the academy and sold the property to the provincial government in 1973. The public service used it as office space for a few years and then gave up; the building needed repairs on a scale nobody wanted to fund. What followed was years of civic argument over what Victoria should do with a decaying convent on some of its most valuable ground, while the building sat empty and its stained glass was smashed. The organ - a Casavant Freres instrument, opus 517, built for the chapel in 1914 - and the rooms around it waited out the debate. Eventually the Provincial Capital Commission took stewardship and the province committed sixteen million dollars.

## A Facade with a New Building Inside It

The restoration was radical. The interior was gutted and rebuilt from basement to attic, seismically upgraded and fitted out as modern offices, while the exterior facade was retained and repaired so that the street sees the same building it always saw. Most of the floor space was leased to the provincial Ministry of Advanced Education - a use the Sisters would likely have recognised. Three spaces were spared the office treatment: the chapel, the parlours and the infirmary, restored to their 1920s decor and run as an interpretive centre. The auditorium at the far end was upgraded too, and now hosts lectures and concerts. The doors reopened in 1997. The chapel had been deconsecrated when the Sisters sold, and it and the adjacent Novitiate Garden are now booked for weddings - which is either a graceful second life or a very strange one, depending on your view. St. Ann's Academy was designated a National Historic Site of Canada in 1989.

## The Sisters Who Were Moved Twice

Two cemeteries once lay on these grounds, holding many of the first Sisters to come west. In 1908 a plot was opened in the northeast corner of Ross Bay Cemetery for future burials, and when the academy closed the earliest Sisters were exhumed and reinterred there with the others - the founding generation relocated, like their chapel, by a city that had other plans for the land. A garden niche holding a statue of Mary now marks where the graves were. One of those women, Sister Mary Osithe Labossiere, left more than a grave: she painted the Immaculate Conception that still hangs in the Sisters' waiting room, and she designed two more schools for the order, Little Flower Academy in Vancouver and St. Ann's Boys' School at Quamichan.

## Flight Context

St. Ann's Academy stands at 48.4190 N, 123.3636 W on Humboldt Street in downtown Victoria, a short block inland from the Inner Harbour. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,200-2,500 ft AGL. From above, the academy reads as a long, pale, mansard-roofed rectangle with wings, set in an unusually large green block for downtown Victoria, with its formal gardens and the small dark-roofed chapel beside it. Orient on the Inner Harbour: the copper domes of the Parliament Buildings and the ivy-covered mass of the Empress Hotel are two blocks west, the Royal British Columbia Museum immediately adjacent, and Beacon Hill Park's open meadows lie a short distance south toward the Strait of Juan de Fuca. The Olympic Mountains fill the southern horizon in clear weather. Nearest airports: CYWH (Victoria Harbour water aerodrome) roughly 1 km northwest, with constant floatplane traffic at low level over the harbour - stay well clear; CYYJ (Victoria International) about 22 km north; KCLM (William R. Fairchild International, Port Angeles) across the strait. Victoria is the sunniest corner of coastal British Columbia, but summer and autumn mornings often start under strait fog.
