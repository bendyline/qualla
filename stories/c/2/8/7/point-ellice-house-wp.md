# Point Ellice House

One of Victoria's oldest houses, occupied by a single family for 108 years and sold to the province with its contents intact - built for the daughter of a Hudson's Bay chief factor, later home to the man who drew British Columbia's reserve boundaries, now stewarded by Metis Nation British Columbia.

**Location:** 48.4361, -123.3771

**Categories:** Historic Houses, Museums, Victoria, Colonial History, Indigenous Heritage, British Columbia

## Introduction

When the last of the O'Reillys sold Point Ellice House in 1975, they did not sell it empty. The province bought the house, the land, and the accumulated household ephemera of a hundred and eight years in one transaction - the letters, the diaries, the furniture, the things nobody thought to throw out. Most historic houses are reconstructions, furnished later with period pieces bought at auction. This one is simply the same family's belongings in the same rooms, which makes it a rarity in Canada and an oddly intimate place to walk through. You are not looking at how people like this lived. You are looking at how these people lived, on the strength of what they happened to keep.

## One Hundred Dollars, Two Acres

The house began as a wedding-adjacent gift. John Work, a Hudson's Bay Company chief factor and by the late 1850s the largest private landowner on Vancouver Island, sold his daughter Catherine and her husband Charles Wentworth Wallace a two-acre parcel out of his estate for one hundred dollars. Catherine's mother was Josette Legace, a woman of the Spokane, and the couple's children grew up inside the mixed world that the fur trade had made on this coast. The house went up between 1861 and 1862, overlooking Selkirk Water on the Gorge Waterway, designed by the architects Wright and Sanders in the Italianate villa style then fashionable - low roof, wide eaves, generous verandah. John Work died in December 1861, before it was finished. Charles Wallace, who had money in a great many Victoria ventures, was bankrupt by 1866, and the family gave up the house to settle the debts. They had lived in it four years.

## The Commissioner's House

Peter O'Reilly moved in that December with his wife Caroline - sister of Joseph Trutch, the most powerful man in colonial British Columbia - and their infant son Frank. Their daughter Charlotte was born within weeks, Mary Augusta followed in 1869, and Jack in 1873. The family would stay for over a century. O'Reilly had come from Ireland in 1859 and worked his way through the colonial civil service as magistrate, gold commissioner and sheriff, and in 1880 he took the post that defined him: Indian Reserve Commissioner, which he held for eighteen years. The job was to travel the province and draw the boundaries of Indigenous reserves, and the lines O'Reilly drew are, in large part, the lines that exist today. He was criticized in his own time, and has been criticized by historians since, for avoiding the meetings with First Nations leaders that the work required. The salaries of those offices paid for the house he kept improving - an entire new wing in the late nineteenth century - and the family's letters and diaries record the renovations alongside the endless business of the kitchen and flower gardens.

## Fifty-Five at the Bridge

On 26 May 1896 the bridge that shares the house's name gave way. It was Queen Victoria's birthday, and a streetcar packed with 143 people bound for the celebrations was crossing the Upper Harbour when the span dropped out from under it. Fifty-five died. The count started at forty-seven and kept climbing as the wreckage came out of the water. A coroner's jury in June found the railway company at fault for overloading the car and the city council at fault for failing to maintain the bridge and limit the traffic on it; the specifications had called for weldless iron and welded iron had been used. The company went into receivership and re-emerged the following spring as the British Columbia Electric Railway. None of that mattered to the families. Fifty-five people who had put on good clothes for a public holiday went into the water at the crossing beside the O'Reillys' property, and the city spent the rest of the summer counting them.

## Whose House It Is Now

Point Ellice House was designated a National Historic Site in 1966, a Provincial Historic Site in 1975, and it also sits on the City of Victoria's heritage registry - three separate layers of protection that have never quite translated into a settled way of paying for it. After the province bought the property it ran the site directly, then began looking in 2002 for a non-profit to take it on. The Capital Mental Health Association agreed in 2004. The Point Ellice House Preservation Society followed in 2009, the Vancouver Island Local History Society in 2019, and that society gave up in March 2023, citing the absence of sustainable provincial funding. The Forager Foundation held the site on an interim basis. Then, in December 2023, the province announced that Metis Nation British Columbia would take over management from January 2024. A house built on land sold cheap by a Hudson's Bay factor to his daughter, and lived in for a century by the man who drew the province's reserve lines, is now in the care of an Indigenous nation. Whatever else that is, it is not an accident of administration.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.436 N, 123.377 W in the Burnside-Gorge neighbourhood of Victoria, British Columbia, on the north shore of Selkirk Water where the Gorge Waterway narrows into Victoria's Upper Harbour. From the air the property reads as a pocket of mature trees and lawn on the waterfront, immediately beside the Bay Street bridge crossing - the same crossing point as the 1896 Point Ellice Bridge - and about 2.5 km northwest of Victoria's Inner Harbour and the Parliament Buildings. The Gorge Waterway is the navigational feature: a narrow, winding tidal channel running northwest from the harbour, unmistakable against the surrounding street grid. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,000-2,000 ft MSL for the house and waterway, higher for the whole harbour system. Victoria Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) is roughly 2 km southeast with busy floatplane traffic, Victoria International (CYYJ) about 20 km north, and Port Angeles (KCLM) 38 km south across the Strait of Juan de Fuca. Expect controlled airspace over the harbour, active marine traffic below, and frequent morning stratus that clears to good afternoon visibility.
