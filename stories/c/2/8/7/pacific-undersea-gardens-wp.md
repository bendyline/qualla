# Pacific Undersea Gardens

For nearly fifty years a 150-foot barge moored in Victoria's Inner Harbour let visitors walk fifteen feet below the surface to watch wolf eels, salmon and a giant Pacific octopus named Armstrong through plate glass.

**Location:** 48.4215, -123.3710

**Categories:** Tourist attractions in Victoria, British Columbia, Aquaria in Canada, Culture of Victoria, British Columbia, Lost attractions

## Introduction

Armstrong was a giant Pacific octopus, and for twenty minutes at a stretch he was the most watched resident of Victoria's Inner Harbour. Visitors filed down a staircase inside a moored barge, sat in a small theatre fifteen feet below the surface, and waited for a diver in a full-face communication mask to coax him out into view — eight arms unfolding across the glass, skin shifting colour, a creature the size of a small car engine doing whatever it felt like doing. For nearly half a century the Pacific Undersea Gardens made the case that the water people were walking past every day was worth stopping for. Then it closed, and the harbour closed over the space where it had been.

## Fifteen Feet Down

The Gardens were not a building. They were a 150-foot vessel, towed from the Oak Bay Marina around to the Inner Harbour and moored in the heart of downtown, with the aquarium built into the hull below the waterline. You bought a ticket at street level, walked down a set of stairs, and found yourself looking through a ring of thick viewing windows into the water of the harbour itself. That was the trick of the place, and it was a good one. Nothing had been trucked in from a warmer ocean. Everything on the other side of the glass — the kelp, the sea stars, the drifting silt — belonged to coastal British Columbia and was living in something close to its own conditions. On the upper level a shallow tidal pond held sea stars and anemones that visitors were invited to touch gently, which for a great many children was the first time they had ever put a hand on a wild animal.

## The Undersea Theatre

At the far end of the vessel was the Undersea Theatre, a seated room facing a wall of glass. A guide welcomed the audience and narrated while a diver worked on the other side, speaking back through a full-face mask wired for communication — a small piece of technology that turned an aquarium into a conversation. The diver would bring animals toward the window and hand them past: crabs, sea urchins, sea stars, anemones, a cabezon or a lingcod hanging in the current with the sullen patience those fish are known for. The billing, though, went to two acts. The wolf eels, with their heavy grey heads and expressions that read as permanently disappointed, looked far more dangerous than they are. And there was Armstrong. The show ran twenty minutes, and the whole visit came to about forty-five minutes to an hour.

## Three Windows on One Coast

The exhibits were arranged as a kind of tour through the province's underwater habitats. In one, fish moved through a standing kelp forest — rockfish, cabezon, lingcod, greenlings, sculpins, perch and halibut — over a floor crowded with sea stars, anemones and crabs. In another, a school of roughly a thousand Pacific salmon circled the ruins of a sunken ship, and the aquarium was run so that the fish completed their actual life cycle inside it, spawning and producing young where visitors could watch. A third was a ghost garden of white and crimson anemones, and it was here that the wolf eel lurked in its den and the giant Pacific octopus — the largest octopus species in the world — moved across the reef with that unnerving liquid deliberation, pouring itself through spaces that should not have accommodated it.

## The Annual Haul

Once a year the Gardens turned their attention outward. Staff and volunteers organized a clean-up of the Inner Harbour, pulling in the Pacific Navy Fleet Diving Unit, Coast Guard Auxiliary 35 Unit, and local recreational divers. The planning took months. The dive itself was usually finished inside two hours, and what came up was a running inventory of everything downtown Victoria had managed to drop in the water: suitcases, pants, bicycles, tires, boat batteries, cell phones, shoes, and on at least one occasion a toilet. It was a modest operation with an outsized point to make — that the water under the seaplane docks and the whale-watching boats was a habitat, and that it was accumulating the city's mistakes.

## Charles White's Idea, and What Came After

The concept belonged to Charles White, who designed the Undersea Gardens in 1963. He was a biologist and photographer who had studied salmon for the Oregon Fish Commission and worked as a charter fishing guide on Vancouver Island, and he wrote a widely read book called How to Catch Salmon: Basic Fundamentals. His love of fish, as one account put it plainly, led him to conceive the whole thing. The first Undersea Gardens opened at the Oak Bay Marina in 1964 and moved to the Inner Harbour in 1969; by 1976 the format was running in four Pacific coast cities, Victoria and Newport, Oregon among them. The Victoria operation, latterly owned by the Oak Bay Marine Group, closed on October 17, 2013, just short of fifty years — undone not by lack of interest but by the age of the vessel and the cost of bringing it up to code. Its berth was used from 2017 until 2020 by V2V, a three-hour walk-on passenger ferry to downtown Vancouver, before that service shut down. The site has gone quiet since, and most people walking past have no idea there was once a theatre underneath it.

## Flight Context

The Pacific Undersea Gardens was moored at 48.42N, 123.37W in Victoria's Inner Harbour, British Columbia, on the causeway side below the Fairmont Empress and across from the Parliament Buildings. The vessel is gone, but the site is easy to identify from the air: the innermost basin of the harbour, immediately south of the Johnson Street Bridge and ringed by the stone causeway walk. Look for floatplanes on step in the outer basin and the copper domes of the Parliament Buildings on the south shore. Nearest airports: Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is the harbour itself — this is active controlled water with continuous scheduled floatplane and helicopter traffic, so overflight below 2,000 feet is not appropriate; Victoria International (CYYJ) lies about 13nm north and Victoria Harbour Heliport is on the north shore. Best viewing is a clear morning with slack water, when the harbour surface is flat enough to see the moorage layout.
