# Patrick Arena

A wooden rink in suburban Oak Bay where the last non-NHL team ever to win the Stanley Cup did it in 1925, and where the Detroit Red Wings can trace their entire roster back to.

**Location:** 48.4327, -123.3190

**Categories:** sports-history, ice-hockey, victoria, british-columbia, lost-buildings

## Introduction

The last team from outside the National Hockey League ever to win the Stanley Cup did it in a wood-framed barn on a suburban corner in Oak Bay, British Columbia. In March 1925 the Victoria Cougars beat the Montreal Canadiens three games to one in a best-of-five final, and three of those games - the first, the third, and the clinching fourth - were played inside the Patrick Arena at Cadboro Bay Road and what was then Empress Street. No club outside the NHL has managed it since, and none ever will; the Cup became the NHL's trophy not long afterward. The building that hosted it burned to the ground four years later and has never been rebuilt. There is a cairn across the street and a residential block where the ice used to be.

## Six Hundred Skaters on Christmas Day

Frank and Lester Patrick built the arena in 1911 for $110,000, a startling sum on Vancouver Island at the time, and they built it for one reason: they had a hockey team and nowhere to play. The Pacific Coast Hockey Association was brand new, and the Patricks were its architects, its owners, and in Lester's case its coach. What they put up in Oak Bay was a wooden structure faced in brick, seating four thousand, with an artificial ice plant underneath - a genuine novelty in Canada, where rinks still mostly depended on weather. It opened to the public on Christmas Day 1911, and more than six hundred people laced up and went around. The first professional game came a week later, on 2 January 1912: the New Westminster Royals against the hometown Victoria Senators, in what was in all likelihood the first professional hockey game ever played on artificial ice in Canada.

## A Team With Four Names

The club changed identities the way small-market franchises do. Senators in 1912; Aristocrats from 1913. In 1916 the Canadian military commandeered the arena outright and the team, with no rink, decamped south of the border to become the Spokane Canaries. When the soldiers left in 1918 a new team formed and took the Aristocrats name back. In 1923 they became the Victoria Cougars, and when the Pacific Coast Hockey Association folded in 1924 they carried the name into the Western Canada Hockey League. Through all of it the Patricks kept experimenting. The brothers are credited with introducing more than twenty rules and innovations that the NHL still uses, which means a great deal of modern hockey was worked out inside this particular building.

## March 1925, and the End of an Era

The 1925 final matched the Western Canada Hockey League champion Cougars against the NHL champion Canadiens, and Victoria won it three games to one. It was the second Stanley Cup for British Columbia, a decade after the Vancouver Millionaires, and it was the last one for a non-NHL club anywhere. The following season the league renamed itself the Western Hockey League and the Cougars won it again, then travelled east to the brand-new Montreal Forum and were comprehensively beaten by the Maroons, outscored ten goals to three across a series they lost three games to one. Then the league simply stopped existing. A new NHL franchise in Detroit bought the Victoria players wholesale and put them on the ice as the Detroit Cougars, a name that lasted until 1930, then Falcons, and from October 1932 the Red Wings. One of the most decorated franchises in hockey began as a roster assembled in Oak Bay.

## Pre-Dawn, 11 November 1929

The fire came in the small hours of Armistice Day, 1929, and took the building completely. The Victoria Cubs of the Pacific Coast Hockey League, who had started play the year before, were left without a home in the middle of a scheduled season, and they played 1929-30 as a road team - all eighteen of their home dates farmed out to Vancouver and Seattle, a full year of away games. In 1930 they moved to Tacoma and became the Tigers. Greater Victoria had no professional hockey at all for the next nineteen years, until 1949 brought a new Victoria Cougars, a new Pacific Coast Hockey League, and the new Victoria Memorial Arena. The old site is houses now. In 2001 a cairn went up on Cadboro Bay Road in front of Oak Bay High School, directly across from where the ice was, to mark what happened there in 1925.

## Flight Context

The Patrick Arena site sits at 48.433 degrees north, 123.319 degrees west, at the northeast corner of Cadboro Bay Road and Epworth Street in Oak Bay, an inner suburb roughly four kilometres east of downtown Victoria, British Columbia. Nothing of the arena survives; from 2,000 to 3,000 feet you are looking at tree-lined residential streets with Oak Bay High School's grounds on the south side of Cadboro Bay Road. Navigate by the coastline: Oak Bay's marina and the Trial Islands lie southeast, Cadboro Bay and Ten Mile Point north, and the Olympic Mountains stand across the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the south on a clear day. Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 18 kilometres northwest, Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) 5 kilometres southwest with heavy floatplane traffic, and Port Angeles (KCLM) lies across the strait in Washington. The area falls within Victoria's terminal airspace. Expect winter low ceilings and rain; summer afternoons are often clear with a steady onshore breeze.
