# British Columbia Parliament Buildings

A 25-year-old immigrant entered the design competition under a false name, won it, and gave Victoria the domed legislature that still defines its harbour.

**Location:** 48.4194, -123.3703

**Categories:** Government buildings completed in 1898, Buildings and structures in Victoria, British Columbia, Legislative buildings in Canada, Politics of British Columbia, Francis Rattenbury buildings, Historic buildings and structures in British Columbia

## Introduction

The winning drawings were signed "A B.C. Architect." The man behind the pseudonym was Francis Rattenbury, twenty-five years old, recently off the boat from England, with no significant building to his name and no standing whatsoever in the province whose legislature he proposed to design. He made the second round and signed that set "For Queen and Province," which suggests he had figured out his audience. Then he won. The buildings he produced open onto Belleville Street facing Victoria's Inner Harbour, five hundred feet of grey andesite with a central dome and a pavilion at each end, and they have been the visual definition of British Columbia ever since. Rattenbury's own ending was a great deal uglier than his architecture.

## The Birdcages

What came before was a joke, and everyone involved knew it. The Legislature of the Colony of Vancouver Island met at Bachelor's Hall in Fort Victoria from 1856 to 1860, then moved into a two-storey wooden building that stood alongside the Land Office, the Colonial Office, the Supreme Court and the Treasury. Locals called the cluster the Birdcages because of their shape. They were notoriously drafty and leaked in the rain, which in Victoria is a sustained condition rather than an occasional event, and by the 1890s a province eager to look like a serious jurisdiction had had enough of governing itself from inside them. The Parliament Buildings Construction Act passed in 1893 and the competition followed. The old Birdcages finally burned in 1957.

## Nine Hundred Thousand Dollars

The budget was five hundred thousand dollars. The final bill came to nine hundred and twenty-three thousand, which is the sort of overrun that ends careers and in this case launched one. The buildings opened in 1898, and the response was enthusiasm rather than scandal: white marble inside, rigorous Baroque symmetry outside, rusticated stone in the Renaissance manner then in fashion, all of it far grander than a province of fewer than two hundred thousand people had any practical need for. That was the point. Commissions followed for Rattenbury across British Columbia, including the Empress Hotel across the harbour, the Crystal Garden, the Vancouver Court House that is now the Vancouver Art Gallery, and the Legislative Library built between 1913 and 1915, whose cornerstone was laid by Prince Arthur, Duke of Connaught. He later returned to England, and in March 1935, at sixty-seven, he was beaten to death in his Bournemouth home. His wife's teenage lover was convicted; she took her own life days after the verdict. The case filled British newspapers for months and has been dramatized ever since.

## Two Islands' Worth of Stone

The building is literally made of British Columbia. Its andesite came from Haddington Island in the Alert Bay Volcanic Belt off the north end of Vancouver Island; its granite came from Nelson Island at the mouth of Jervis Inlet on the Sunshine Coast. A gold-covered statue of Captain George Vancouver stands on top of the central dome. On the front lawn, Queen Victoria surveys the traffic, and the Legislature Cenotaph commemorates the province's dead from the First and Second World Wars, Korea, and Afghanistan. In front of the Queen stands the thirty-metre Provincial Christmas Tree, a giant sequoia which the granite marker in front of it confidently identifies as a coastal redwood. It is not. Nobody has fixed the marker.

## Fourteen Men and Twelve Allegories

Provincial librarian E. O. S. Scholefield designed the sculpture program, and Charles Marega executed it with his assistant Bernard Carrier. Marega carved fourteen figures for the library exterior: Chief Maquinna of the Nuu-chah-nulth alongside Captain James Cook, George Vancouver, Sir James Douglas, Sir Matthew Baillie Begbie, Dr. John McLoughlin, John Sebastian Helmcken, Sir Francis Drake, Sir Alexander Mackenzie, Simon Fraser, David Thompson, Lord Lytton, Sir Anthony Musgrave, and Colonel R. C. Moody. It is a roll call of explorers, administrators and traders, with one Indigenous leader admitted to the company. Carrier produced twelve female figures, all allegorical rather than historical, three set around each of the building's four domes. The arrangement tells you a good deal about whose history the province thought it was carving in 1915.

## Behind the False Walls

In 1932 the province commissioned George Southwell to paint the rotunda with scenes from British Columbia between 1792 and 1843. He finished three years later. The murals became steadily harder to defend. One, titled Labour, shows bare-breasted Indigenous women hauling timber while a white man supervises. Another, Justice, shows an Indigenous chief standing before a judge, generally read as a depiction of Indigenous people brought to heel under colonial law, although Southwell's daughter maintained her father meant that judge as a defender of Indigenous rights. A 2001 report recommended moving the paintings to a museum where they could be presented with context, but they are painted directly onto the rotunda walls and removal was costed at $280,000. In April 2007 the legislature voted to take them down, with only three of seventy-one members opposed. The murals were fully restored and then sealed behind false walls, where they remain: not destroyed, not displayed, and not resolved.

## Flight Context

The Parliament Buildings stand at 48.419N, 123.370W, on the south side of Victoria's Inner Harbour on southern Vancouver Island. From the air the giveaway is the green copper central dome with its gilded statue, at the head of a long symmetrical grey stone facade fronting the water, with the equally distinctive green roofline of the Empress Hotel diagonally across the harbour to the northeast. The Inner Harbour itself is a busy floatplane and ferry basin; expect heavy seaplane traffic. Look south across the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the Olympic Mountains, roughly 35 kilometres away. Victoria International Airport (CYYJ) is about 22 kilometres north near Sidney, and Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is directly below the buildings, which makes low overflight here a bad idea without coordination. Best viewed at 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, with the building floodlit and outlined in thousands of bulbs after dark. Marine fog can fill the harbour in the morning while the hills behind stay clear.
