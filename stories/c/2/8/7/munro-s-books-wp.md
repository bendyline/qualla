# Munro's Books

A 1909 bank on Government Street, a 24-foot coffered ceiling, a future Nobel laureate behind the counter in 1963, and an owner who handed the whole thing to his staff.

**Location:** 48.4253, -123.3681

**Categories:** bookstores, heritage-architecture, literary-landmarks, victoria-bc, british-columbia

## Introduction

Look up first. Twenty-four feet above the fiction tables in Munro's Books, a coffered ceiling runs the length of the room, built in 1909 to impress people who had come to deposit money. Thomas Hooper designed the building for the Royal Bank of Canada in the neo-classical manner of the era, which is to say it was meant to look like it would be there forever and to make you slightly quieter when you walked in. The bank left. The proportions stayed. Since 1984 the room has held books instead of tellers, and the effect of that ceiling over shelves rather than counters is exactly what the columnist Allan Fotheringham was reaching for when he called this the most magnificent bookstore in Canada, possibly in North America.

## 1963, Mostly Paperbacks

The store did not begin in a bank. It began in 1963, small and stocked largely with paperbacks, opened by Jim Munro and his then-wife Alice -- a young couple in Victoria trying to make a living from books at a moment when the mass-market paperback was the disruptive new format. Alice Munro was writing short stories in whatever hours the shop and the children left over. Fifty years later, in 2013, she won the Nobel Prize in Literature for those stories. By then she had been unconnected to the bookstore for decades, which never stopped the phone from ringing. Fans and journalists called Munro's for years asking to speak with her, as though a shop could be a forwarding address for a life.

## Textile Against Stone

The other artist in the family left her work on the walls. Carole Sabiston, a textile artist and Jim Munro's second wife, made the large tapestries that hang in the store. They are a deliberate counterweight to the architecture: Hooper's building is all hard geometry, plaster coffers and classical restraint, and Sabiston's pieces are colour, layered fabric, and movement. A bookstore in a bank could easily feel like a museum of itself. The tapestries keep it from doing that. They hang high enough to read as part of the room's structure and soft enough to break its severity, and regular customers navigate by them without particularly noticing that they do.

## Fiftieth Year, and the Call From Stockholm

September 2013 brought the store's fiftieth anniversary. The following month brought the Nobel announcement for its co-founder. Any bookseller would take one of those in a year. Getting both in the same autumn gave Munro's an anniversary no publicist could have scripted. There is a symmetry in it that is almost too neat: a store opened in 1963 to sell other people's paperbacks, reaching its half-century in the same weeks that the woman who helped open it became the first Canadian woman to win the literature Nobel.

## The Handover

In 2014, after fifty-one years, Jim Munro retired -- and did not sell the business to a chain, a developer, or anyone at all in the conventional sense. He handed it to four longtime employees: Carol Mentha, Sarah Frye, Ian Cochran, and Jessica Walker. He described the staff as his extended family and behaved accordingly. The same year, he was named to the Order of Canada. He died at home in November 2016. Since his retirement the store has had no connection to the Munro family, and it continues to trade under their name on Government Street -- a large independent bookstore in an era that has not been kind to large independent bookstores, still occupying a banking hall, still owned by the people who work in it. The ceiling above it was commissioned by an institution that assumed it would outlast everything around it. Forty years on, the books are still there.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.43 N, 123.37 W on Government Street in downtown Victoria, British Columbia. The store is a storefront within a heritage block and is not identifiable from the air on its own; navigate to it by the Inner Harbour, one of the most legible urban waterfronts on the Pacific coast -- the green copper domes of the BC Parliament Buildings on the south side, the ivy-covered Empress Hotel on the east, and Government Street running north from the causeway. Ogden Point breakwater and cruise terminal lie to the southwest, Beacon Hill Park to the south, and the Olympic Mountains stand across the Strait of Juan de Fuca. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) sits directly below in the harbour and handles dense scheduled floatplane traffic -- treat the harbour as busy, low-level airspace. Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 12 nm north. The city lies in the Olympic rain shadow, giving unusually good visibility for the coast, though summer morning fog off the strait is frequent.
