# Beacon Hill Park

Victoria's 75-hectare waterfront park, named for two masts that once marked a sunken reef, holding camas meadows the Lekwungen tended for centuries, a 38-metre pole raised by Mungo Martin, and Mile 0 of the Trans-Canada Highway.

**Location:** 48.4120, -123.3640

**Categories:** Parks in Victoria, British Columbia, Urban parks, First Nations history, Totem poles

## Introduction

Two masts once stood on the slope above the water, one higher than the other. A ship coming in toward Victoria's harbour would watch them until they lined up, and at that instant the navigator knew exactly where Brotchie Ledge lay — a submerged reef that had already taken ships and would take more. The beacons went up in 1846. They are long gone, but the hill kept their name, and so did the 75 hectares of meadow, woodland, pond and shoreline that grew up around it. Beacon Hill Park is where Victoria goes to do almost everything, and almost none of it has anything to do with navigation any more.

## Warmed by the Sun

The hill at the park's southern end has an older name. To the Lekwungen people — the Songhees — it is míqən, pronounced roughly MEE-qan and sometimes written Meegan, and it means warmed by the sun. The name is a description of function. This was a place to rest and to play games, and above all it was farmland: the slopes carried an enormous crop of camas and great camas, whose bulbs were a staple food, dug and pit-cooked and traded. Lekwungen women managed those fields, clearing competing growth and harvesting selectively so the beds would produce again. At the base of the hill stood a settlement occupied on and off from roughly a thousand to three hundred years ago. As the park was developed for settler recreation the camas crop was largely destroyed, and the effort to restore it and to reassert Lekwungen authority over the species is being led today, as it was then, by Lekwungen women.

## From Beacon to Park

Victoria in the 1840s was a Hudson's Bay Company post with a harbour entrance guarded by a reef the sailors called Buoy Rock. Two beacons on the hill solved the problem with pure geometry — align them from the deck and you had your position. The colonial government set the surrounding land aside, and in 1882 the City of Victoria formally made it a municipal park under the name it still carries. What followed was a century of Victorian and Edwardian park-making: gravel paths, ornamental ponds, imported plantings, a bandshell, cricket pitches and lawn bowling greens laid out on ground that had been meadow. The result is a hybrid, and the hybridity is visible. Walk from the manicured beds near Douglas Street toward the Dallas Road bluffs and you pass out of an English garden and into something much closer to the Garry oak landscape that was here first.

## The Tallest Pole in the World

In 1956 a pole rose in the park that stood 38.9 metres — nearly 128 feet — and was at the time the tallest in the world. It was carved by a team led by Mungo Martin, the Kwakwaka'wakw chief and master carver, working with his son David and with Henry Hunt. Martin's role in that decade is hard to overstate: he carved, restored and taught at a moment when the Canadian government's decades-long ban on the potlatch had done severe damage to the transmission of these art forms, and much of what survived into the present passed through his hands and his students'. The pole has needed repair several times in the weather of a coastal park, and between 2000 and 2001 it was lowered entirely for a major restoration before going back up. It still dominates the skyline of the park's northern section, visible from well out on the strait.

## A Sister's Tribute, a Bandshell, and Mile Zero

Smaller things reward attention here. Between Goodacre and Fountain Lake, a bridge made of pebbles crosses the connecting stream — built in 1945 by Alice Carr as a memorial to her sister Emily, the painter whose forests and coastal skies did more than anyone's to give British Columbia a visual identity. The Cameron Bandshell, known locally as The Stage, hosts concerts from June through September, and on a warm evening the sound carries out over the lawns. At the park's southwest corner sits Mile 0 of the Trans-Canada Highway, the beginning of a road that runs to Newfoundland, marked by a statue of Terry Fox. Across the intersection are the old Beacon Lodge and the Beacon Drive-In, where the ice cream line runs down the sidewalk on any decent summer day.

## Everything at Once

For a park of only 190 acres, Beacon Hill attempts an improbable amount. There are two playgrounds and a waterpark, a petting zoo in summer, tennis and pickleball courts, baseball diamonds, cricket, lawn bowling, an off-leash dog area at the south end, and enough ponds to lose track of. There are also quiet corners: woodland trails, viewpoints over the Strait of Juan de Fuca with the Olympic Mountains standing across the water, and stands of tall trees where great blue herons nest in noisy, ungainly colonies each spring. Tourists come for the totem pole and Mile 0. Locals come because it is simply where you go — to walk the dog, watch the herons, eat an ice cream, or sit on míqən in the afternoon and confirm that the name was accurate all along.

## Flight Context

Beacon Hill Park occupies the waterfront south of downtown Victoria, British Columbia, at 48.41N, 123.36W, fronting the Strait of Juan de Fuca along Dallas Road. From the air it reads as a large green rectangle between the dense street grid of James Bay and the open water, with ornamental ponds glinting in its northern half and the bare rounded summit of Beacon Hill at the south end. The Mungo Martin pole and the Mile 0 marker sit near the park's southwest corner. Use Victoria's Inner Harbour and the copper-domed Parliament Buildings, less than 1nm northwest, as your primary fix, with Clover Point jutting into the strait just east. The Olympic Mountains fill the horizon 20nm south. Nearest airports: Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) 1nm northwest with continuous floatplane traffic, Victoria International (CYYJ) about 13nm north, and Port Angeles (KCLM) 17nm south across the strait. Stay above 2,000 feet over the city; the harbour aerodrome circuit is busy and the heron colony nests in the park's tall conifers from spring into summer.
