# Royal British Columbia Museum

Founded in 1886 to stop British Columbia's treasures leaving the province, and now working out what it means to send some of them home.

**Location:** 48.4194, -123.3678

**Categories:** british-columbia, victoria, museum, first-nations, history, natural-history

## Introduction

The museum exists because a group of Victorians in 1886 were angry about theft. Not burglary - export. European and American collectors were carrying BC's natural specimens and, above all, its First Nations objects out of the province by the crateload. A petition went to the provincial government demanding it stop, signed by a judge, a future premier, a former premier, and a Hudson's Bay Company doctor. Their argument turned on a single phrase: the loss of these things was frequently irreparable. The government agreed. On 25 October 1886 the Provincial Museum of Natural History and Anthropology opened in a room fifteen feet by twenty. A hundred and forty years later it holds roughly seven million objects, and is working through what its founding argument means in reverse.

## A Room Fifteen by Twenty

The first home was in the Birdcages, the wooden colonial buildings that served as British Columbia's legislature before the current stone one went up. The first curator was John Fannin, a naturalist who solved the problem of an empty museum by donating his own large collection of preserved birds and animals. In 1891 he published a checklist of BC birds, and the institution has been publishing ever since.

Expansion came in stages, always sideways into whatever space was going: the east wing of the new Legislative Buildings in 1896, an excavated basement under the east annex in 1921. A 1913 revision of the mandate made natural history, anthropology, and public education official duties rather than enthusiasms. The building visitors know came later: a centennial project championed by Premier W. A. C. Bennett, opened 16 August 1968 at a final cost of $9.5 million. The Royal came in 1987, approved by Elizabeth II and conferred by Prince Philip during a royal tour.

## Old Town, and a Mammoth

What people remember is Old Town. Built between 1969 and 1972 on the third floor, it is a full-scale reconstruction of Victoria between the 1870s and the 1920s - twenty building fronts, a cobblestone street, a silent movie theatre, a hotel, a railway station, old automobiles, a Chinatown. You walk into it rather than look at it, and the lighting stays permanently at dusk. It is the sort of exhibit people still describe decades after the school trip that took them there.

The natural history gallery below has its own icon in the woolly mammoth, standing full-size among displays that run from the Fraser delta back through deep time. There is a tide pool with live crabs and sea stars, a mock Victorian submarine housing a 360-litre aquarium, and a more recent section on climate change. And then there is John Lennon's psychedelically painted Rolls-Royce, bought by the Vancouver businessman Jim Pattison and given to the museum - the least British Columbian object in the collection, and one of the most photographed.

## The Longhouse on the Third Floor

The First Peoples Gallery holds the longhouse of Chief Kwakwabalasami - Jonathan Hunt - a Kwakwaka'wakw chief from Tsaxis, Fort Rupert. The house and its carvings were made by his son Henry Hunt and his grandsons Tony and Richard Hunt, three generations of one family, working in Victoria. Nearby are ceremonial masks carved by Chief Nakap'ankam, Mungo Martin, and argillite pieces by Bill Reid.

The gallery is also the museum's hardest room. Indigenous scholars have criticised how it represents First Nations people, particularly its use of Edward Curtis photographs and film - early twentieth-century images, often staged, that shaped how a continent pictured Indigenous life. A museum founded to keep objects in British Columbia has had to reckon with the fact that keeping them was never the same thing as having the right to them.

## Going Home

That reckoning is now visible in the record. In 2010 the museum's Nisga'a holdings were returned to the Nisga'a, where they are held at the Nisga'a Museum in northwestern BC. In early 2023 a totem pole went north to the Nuxalk Nation, which had sold it to the museum in 1915 - a transaction that looks very different from either end of the intervening century.

The symmetry is exact. The founders argued that objects leaving British Columbia were irreparably lost to the people of British Columbia. Nations making repatriation claims make the same argument from a smaller and older circle: that these things belong where they were made, to the descendants of the people who made them. The museum still holds the largest collection of First Nations archaeological material in the province, over 192,000 items, alongside some 14,000 ethnological objects. What happens to them is a live question.

## Seven Million Things

The public galleries are the visible fraction. Behind them sit collections divided into eight natural-history disciplines - entomology, botany, palaeontology, ichthyology, invertebrate zoology, herpetology, mammalogy, ornithology - and numbers that stop being intuitive quickly: 55,000 fossils spanning 600 million years, over 200,000 plant specimens, 19,335 bird study skins, 2,713 clutches of eggs. Since 2003 the provincial archives have been part of the same institution.

The conservation department, established in 1966, was the first lab of its kind in western Canada. Its conservators worked with the Haida through the 1970s and 1980s on the poles at Ninstints, and since 1999 with the Champagne and Aishihik First Nations on Kwäday Dän Ts'ìnchi, the ancestor found in a melting glacier - work that is as much about protocol and consent as about chemistry. Outside, one of the oldest native plant gardens in western Canada has grown since 1967, some 400 BC species rooted beside the Inner Harbour.

## Flight Context

The Royal BC Museum stands at 48.419N, 123.368W on the south side of Victoria's Inner Harbour, wedged between the Empress Hotel and the BC Legislative Buildings. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet on a harbour approach the precinct is unmistakable: the green copper dome of the Legislature, the ivy-covered Empress, and the museum's boxy 1968 modernist block immediately east, with Thunderbird Park and its poles on the corner. The Inner Harbour itself is busy with floatplanes and ferries. Victoria Inner Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) lands right at the museum's doorstep; Victoria International (CYYJ) is 12 nm north. Note the control zone and heavy seaplane traffic on the harbour approach. The Olympic Mountains stand across the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the south, and summer marine fog can close the harbour early and clear by mid-morning.
