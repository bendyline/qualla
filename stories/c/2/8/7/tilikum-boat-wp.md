# Tilikum (boat)

A hundred-year-old Nuu-chah-nulth cedar dugout, bought for eighty dollars and rigged with three masts, sailed from Vancouver Island to the Thames — and killed one of the men aboard her.

**Location:** 48.4259, -123.3690

**Categories:** Canoes, Schooners, Individual sailing vessels, Sailing ships of Canada, Nuu-chah-nulth, Ships preserved in museums, Museum ships in Canada, Museum ships in British Columbia, Ships built in British Columbia, Three-masted ships

## Introduction

On 25 October 1901, a wave took the binnacle, the compass, and the mate. Louis Begent went over the side somewhere between Fiji and Australia and was not recovered. John Voss was left alone aboard a converted dugout canoe, 1,200 miles from Sydney, with no compass and no one to stand a watch. He got there. That is the kind of voyage this was — an improbable three-year passage from Vancouver Island to London in a boat that had been a red cedar log before either man was born.

## The Log, and the Woman Who Sold It

Tilikum was built in the early nineteenth century by Nuu-chah-nulth craftsmen, carved from a single enormous red cedar and shaped for the open water off Vancouver Island's west coast — a design refined over many generations by people who knew that ocean better than anyone alive. By April 1901 she was old, and Voss bought her for $80 in silver from a Nuu-chah-nulth woman whose name has not come down to us. In his own account Voss called her by a slur in common settler use at the time, a word that flattened every Indigenous person on the coast into one dismissive syllable; it tells you nothing about her and a great deal about him. The transfer was reportedly sealed with a bottle of rye whiskey. The canoe's new name, Tilikum, means "friend" in Chinook Jargon, the trade language of this coast. Whatever the boat later accomplished, she was already a masterpiece before anyone bolted a mast to her.

## Rebuilt at Spotlight Cove

Voss and his partner Norman Luxton had read about Joshua Slocum, who had just circled the world alone in the 37-foot sloop Spray and turned the story into a bestseller. They wanted the same thing, only stranger and therefore better copy. At a yard in Spotlight Cove on Galiano Island, the canoe was reinforced, decked over, and rigged with 230 square feet of sail across three small masts — a dugout turned schooner, a hybrid with no obvious precedent. She sailed to Oak Bay, and on 20 May 1901 the two men pointed her at the Cook Islands and left.

## Coral

Five months and roughly 10,000 miles of Pacific later, Tilikum struck a reef. Luxton was thrown out of the boat and dragged across coral, which opened cuts over most of his body — the kind of injury that heals slowly and badly in the tropics. The boat limped into harbour at Penrhyn Island in the Cooks on 2 September 1901. Luxton kept going as far as Suva, Fiji, and on 17 October 1901 he left the voyage for good. Eight days later came the wave that took Louis Begent. Between Suva and the Thames, ten more men crewed for Voss at one stage or another; none of them stayed for the whole thing, and it is not hard to understand why.

## Dropped in Melbourne

The most humiliating damage of the entire voyage happened on land. Tilikum reached Melbourne on 13 March 1902 and was put on display in full rig on Collins Street, a dugout canoe with three masts standing in a city street, drawing crowds. When she was moved to the Exhibition Buildings and hoisted onto a wagon, the hook broke. The hull fell and split in five separate places. Voss repaired her himself with thin steel ribs, then sailed her on Lake Wendouree at Ballarat, where local yachtsmen presented him with a new suit of sails, before hauling her to Geelong and putting her back into salt water. In September 1904 she came up the Thames and into London.

## Two Books, and a Long Way Home

Voss lectured across Britain and was nominated as a Fellow of the Royal Geographical Society, though he never completed the process. In 1913 he published The Venturesome Voyages of Captain Voss. Luxton wrote his own version of the journey, and it sat unpublished until his daughter Eleanor edited the manuscript and brought it out after his death as Tilikum: Luxton's Pacific Crossing in 1971 — the other side of a story that for sixty years had only been told by one man. The boat fared worse than either. She passed through several owners in London and lost her bowsprit and masts, until the Furness Shipping Line carried her back to Victoria in 1929. Restoration began in 1936 under the Thermopylae Club. She entered the Maritime Museum in 1965 and stayed until 2015, then spent time on display at the Ogden Point cruise ship terminal. At present she is not on public display — a hundred-and-twenty-year-old cedar hull, resting, between exhibitions.

## Flight Context

Tilikum's story is anchored to Victoria, British Columbia, near 48.426°N, 123.369°W, where the Maritime Museum of BC holds her and where she was returned in 1929. Fly the Inner Harbour at 1,000 to 2,000 feet and the relevant geography lays out in one view: Ogden Point and its long breakwater on the southwest, where the canoe was last publicly displayed; the Inner Harbour with the BC Legislature's copper domes and the Empress Hotel; and Oak Bay on the eastern shore of the peninsula, the harbour from which she departed on 20 May 1901. Galiano Island — Spotlight Cove, where she was rebuilt as a schooner — lies about 25 nautical miles northeast among the Gulf Islands, easily picked out as a long narrow island along Trincomali Channel. The Strait of Juan de Fuca opens south toward the Olympic Mountains and the Pacific route Tilikum took. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is beneath the harbour itself with constant floatplane traffic; Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 13 nautical miles north; Vancouver International (CYVR) is the nearest major hub. Summer mornings are fog-prone, and westerly winds funnel hard down the strait most afternoons.
