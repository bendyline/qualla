# Sealand of the Pacific

For twenty-three years an aquarium at Oak Bay Marina kept killer whales in netting hung in the open sea, a few hundred metres from the waters they came from. It closed after the death of a young trainer, and almost nothing of it remains.

**Location:** 48.4249, -123.3020

**Categories:** Aquaria in Canada, Greater Victoria, 1969 establishments in British Columbia, 1992 disestablishments in British Columbia

## Introduction

The pool was not a pool. At Oak Bay Marina, on the sheltered water just east of Victoria, Sealand of the Pacific kept its killer whales in netting suspended in the ocean itself - a double-net enclosure open to the tide and the cold and the sound of the strait. From a distance it looked almost gentle. Real sea water, real current, and the animals' own coastline visible in every direction. It was still a cage, and it sat within earshot of the home waters of the whales inside it. Between 1969 and 1992 that arrangement produced one of the most consequential and most painful chapters in the history of marine mammal captivity, and it ended with the death of a twenty-year-old woman.

## Haida and the Mates

Sealand opened in 1969 with a single orca, Haida, taken from the wild the year before. The aquarium then decided he needed a mate. On 1 March 1970, four members of a pod of Bigg's killer whales were caught off Pedder Bay near Victoria; two of them, Chimo and Nootka, went to Sealand. Chimo was strikingly white, and was first assumed to be albino - in fact she had Chediak-Higashi syndrome, which left her badly vulnerable to illness. She died in 1972, little more than two years after capture. Nootka was sold on and passed through several aquariums before dying in California. A replacement, Nootka II, lasted nine months. A third, Nootka III, was also short-lived. Haida was then alone for five years, having mourned each of them, and showing what his keepers recorded as signs of depression.

## Miracle

In 1977 word reached Vancouver that a Campbell River man was hand-feeding a sick killer whale calf in the wild. Researchers flew up to Menzies Bay to check the story and found it was true: the calf was alive, alone, and carrying a bullet wound. Sealand took her in for emergency care and named her Miracle. Given her age, her exposure to people and the fact that nobody knew where her family pod was, Fisheries and Oceans Canada judged her unreleasable, and in 1978 she was handed to Sealand as a last resort after two other institutions could not take her. She became the aquarium's most loved animal. She and Haida were penned separately but called back and forth to one another constantly. She died in January 1982, drowned in the nets that held her - the same nets she had learned to damage, because damaged nets brought divers into the water to play with her.

## The Release That Never Happened

By the early 1980s the anti-captivity movement was pressing hard on aquariums, and Sealand agreed to let Haida go. He died in October 1982, a few days before his scheduled release, with no evidence of foul play. The release had never been purely an act of mercy: it was part of a plan to make room for new whales. When word got out that Sealand intended to capture more, protesters gathered at the site where the capture was expected. The aquarium went to Iceland instead, and acquired three orcas taken from North Atlantic waters - Tilikum, Nootka IV and Haida II. The three never got on. Tilikum, the male and the newcomer, was routinely bullied by the two females and chased into the medical pen, which meant that the animals in that enclosure were under continuous social stress.

## 20 February 1991

Keltie Byrne was twenty years old, a marine biology student and a part-time trainer, and by all accounts exactly where she wanted to be. After a show on 20 February 1991 she fell into the whale pool. The three orcas pulled her under repeatedly and she drowned, despite everything the other trainers could do to reach her. The subsequent accounts pointed to the animals' poor relations with each other, their unfamiliarity with people in the water, and the pregnancy of at least one of the females. Sealand closed in November 1992 and sold all three whales to SeaWorld: Tilikum and Nootka IV to Orlando, Haida II and her son Kyuquot - born on 14 December 1991 - to San Antonio. Nootka IV died in 1994, Haida II in 2001, Tilikum in January 2017. Kyuquot is still in captivity. At Oak Bay today there is a marina, and boats, and no sign that any of it happened.

## Flight Context

The former Sealand of the Pacific site is at 48.4249 N, 123.3020 W, at Oak Bay Marina on the east side of the Saanich Peninsula's southern tip, facing Haro Strait. From 1,000 to 2,500 feet AGL the marina is unmistakable - a breakwater and finger docks tucked between rocky headlands, with the Chatham and Discovery Islands just offshore to the northeast and the Olympic Mountains across Juan de Fuca Strait to the south. Nothing of the aquarium survives; the interest is in the setting, which is exactly the whales' own water. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Airport (CYWH) lies about 3 to 4 nm west and Victoria International (CYYJ) roughly 14 nm north-northwest. This is congested Class C and control-zone airspace with heavy floatplane traffic and the international boundary close by to the east - check restrictions carefully. Visibility is generally excellent in summer; expect fog and low stratus off Haro Strait in autumn and early winter.
