# Congregation Emanu-El (Victoria, British Columbia)

Built in 1863 by gold-rush immigrants, Victoria's Congregation Emanu-El is the oldest synagogue building still in use as a synagogue in Canada and on the west coast of North America.

**Location:** 48.4273, -123.3620

**Categories:** Religious Sites, Historic Buildings, Jewish Heritage, Architecture, British Columbia

## Introduction

When the cornerstone went into the wall at Blanshard and Pandora in 1863, the builders sealed a time capsule beneath it, and the choice of contents says everything about the town Victoria was that year. In went the congregation's constitution and the list of donors to the building fund, which you would expect. In went a handful of coins and a copy of the British Colonist, the local paper - still publishing today as the Victoria Times Colonist. And in went the complete membership rolls of the Germania Sing Verein and the French Benevolent Society of Victoria: a German choral club and a French mutual-aid society, tucked under a synagogue by a Jewish congregation, in a British colony on the Pacific. The building above that stone is still in use, and no other synagogue in Canada can say the same.

## Gold, and the People It Carried

Gold was found on the Fraser River in 1858, and Victoria, until then a quiet Hudson's Bay Company post, became overnight the staging ground for everyone heading to the diggings. A flood of prospectors passed through, a great many of them arriving by ship from San Francisco, and among them were Jewish merchants and tradespeople who had already made one migration and now made another. Enough of them stayed to form a community quickly. By 1859 the congregation existed in some organised form, because that is the year its cemetery was dedicated - a detail that tells you something about how these communities took root. Before a building to pray in, they secured ground to bury their dead in. Four years later, with the boomtown building frenzy still running, they put up a synagogue.

## A Cornerstone, Twice Laid

The ceremony was, by the standards of a raw colonial town, an event. The newly organised Victoria chapter of the Freemasons laid the cornerstone, and Emanu-El is said to have been the first building in the town to receive that honour. A second cornerstone was set the same day by a member of the congregation's own Building Committee, so the honours were shared between the fraternal order and the congregation itself. It is worth pausing on what that meant in 1863: a young frontier settlement turning out in numbers, its Masons and its German singers and its French benevolent society all lending their names, to see a Jewish house of worship go up. Victoria in the gold years was not a tolerant utopia, but on this street corner on this day, the town's immigrant societies showed up for one another.

## Brick, Rose Window, Dome

The architects were Wright & Sanders, and what they produced is Romanesque Revival done confidently in brick: two storeys, a rose window set into the facade above the entrance, and a corbelled gable stepping up along the roofline. Inside, the ceiling lifts into a dome. The sanctuary fills nearly the entire building, arranged in the older manner with the seating - originally reserved for men - surrounding the bimah rather than facing a stage, so that the reading of the Torah happens in the middle of the room and not in front of it. The ark projects from the east wall, toward Jerusalem. Above, a U-shaped gallery runs around three sides; it was built as the women's section and now serves as overflow seating, which is its own quiet record of how the congregation has changed in a century and a half.

## Still in Use

That last phrase carries the weight of the whole story. Plenty of nineteenth-century synagogues survive in North America as museums, offices, churches or apartments. Emanu-El is the oldest synagogue building on the west coast of North America and the oldest in Canada still functioning as a synagogue - a Conservative congregation affiliated with the United Synagogue of Conservative Judaism, holding services in the same room its founders built. The building is a National Historic Site of Canada and a heritage property under provincial legislation, but neither designation is what keeps it alive. In the 1980s the congregation renovated, replacing an ageing wooden structure alongside with a new wing, the Al & Sylvia Fisher Building, for education and cultural programmes. The old sanctuary was left to do what it has done since 1863.

## Flight Context

Congregation Emanu-El stands at 48.4273 N, 123.3620 W, on Blanshard Street at Pandora Avenue in downtown Victoria, British Columbia, a few blocks inland from the Inner Harbour. From the air the orientation points are the Inner Harbour itself with the Parliament Buildings and the Empress Hotel on its south and east shores; the synagogue sits in the dense older grid northeast of that, a small brick building easily lost among taller neighbours - this is a street-level landmark rather than an aerial one. Victoria Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is right there in the Inner Harbour, roughly half a nautical mile southwest, with floatplane traffic on the water; Victoria International (CYYJ) lies about 13 nm north near Sidney. Downtown Victoria sits beneath the CYWH harbour airspace, so altitudes are constrained and any sightseeing pass belongs well clear of the seaplane operating area. A general viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet MSL takes in the harbour, the downtown grid and the Olympic Mountains across the Strait of Juan de Fuca. Morning fog off the strait is common in late summer and autumn; afternoons usually clear.
