# Galloping Goose Regional Trail

Fifty-five kilometres of abandoned railway turned into Victoria's busiest commuter route, named after a rattling gas-powered railcar and ending at a locked gate in front of a gold-rush ghost town nobody is allowed to visit.

**Location:** 48.4284, -123.3720

**Categories:** Rail trails in British Columbia, Historic trails and roads in British Columbia, Transport in the Capital Regional District, Bike paths in British Columbia

## Introduction

The trail stops before it arrives. Maps still show the Galloping Goose running all the way to Leechtown, a gold-rush ghost town in the hills north of Sooke, but since 2007 that ground has belonged to the Greater Victoria water supply, and a warning sign and locked gate now stand across the last stretch. You can ride fifty-odd kilometres out from downtown Victoria, watch the city give way to suburb and then to second-growth forest and river canyon, and then read a notice telling you that the destination is closed on account of everyone's drinking water. There are worse reasons to be turned back. It is, in its way, a very Vancouver Island ending.

## The Goose Itself

The name belongs to a machine, not a bird. From 1922 to 1931 a gas-powered passenger railcar, No. 15813, ran this line out of Victoria, and something about the way it moved - the noise, the gait, the general lack of dignity - earned it the nickname. Passenger service on the line did not last, and the Canadian National Railway eventually gave up the corridor altogether. What a railway leaves behind is unusually valuable: a continuous strip of gently graded, publicly held land running straight through country that has since been built over. In 1987 the Capital Regional District took the old right-of-way and made a trail of it. The name of a rattling 1920s railcar has outlasted the railcar, the railway, and the era by a wide margin.

## Gold Will Speak for Itself

Leechtown exists because of six weeks in 1864. The Vancouver Island Exploring Expedition, under Robert Brown, was working the interior of the island when John Foley found gold on a tributary of the Sooke River that July. Brown's summary was admirably brief: the gold will speak for itself. It spoke loudly. By mid-August 227 mining licences had been issued; by November some 1,200 people were on the ground, and at its peak Leechtown and neighbouring Boulder City together held roughly four thousand. The place assembled itself accordingly - six general stores, three hotels and thirty saloons by the end of that first year. Then it came apart just as fast. The rush was finished by 1865, and by 1874 the diggings were largely deserted. Cairns mark the site today, including a monument unveiled in 2014 at Kapoor Regional Park for the rush's 150th anniversary.

## A Corridor That Actually Works

Most rail trails are recreation. The Goose is infrastructure. It runs 55 kilometres through Sooke, Metchosin, Colwood, Langford, View Royal, Saanich, Victoria and unincorporated East Sooke, and the thirteen kilometres between the Johnson Street Bridge and Wale Road in Colwood are paved - about a quarter of the total length, and the quarter that carries people to work. On a weekday morning it fills with commuters on bicycles, while walkers, runners, rollerbladers, skateboarders and, in places, riders on horseback use the rest of it. Through Vic West and Burnside-Gorge it functions as an urban street that happens to have no cars. It also plugs into a much bigger network: part of the Trans-Canada Trail, intersecting the Lochside Regional Trail, and carrying the Vancouver Island Trail between Esquimalt's Harbour Road and the Veterans Memorial Parkway in Langford.

## Two Bridges, 1996

A trail is only as continuous as its worst gap, and for its first decade the Goose had two bad ones. Both closed in 1996. The Selkirk Trestle was rebuilt across the Selkirk Water, giving walkers and cyclists a long timber deck over the Gorge with the city on one side and the inlet opening on the other - a crossing people take deliberately, not merely because it is on the way. The Switch Bridge went up over the Trans-Canada Highway, lifting the route clear of the one piece of traffic that could otherwise have severed it. Between them, those two structures turned a pleasant series of disconnected segments into a single working corridor. Everything the Goose does now - the commuting, the long weekend rides out to the Sooke Potholes, the link into the national trail network - depends on two crossings built in the same year.

## Flight Context

The Galloping Goose Regional Trail's urban end is at 48.4284 N, 123.3720 W beside the Johnson Street Bridge in downtown Victoria, from which it runs west and northwest 55 kilometres to the gated approach to Leechtown north of Sooke. Best traced from 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL: look for the unnaturally straight, tree-lined ribbon of a former rail grade crossing the suburban street grid of View Royal, Colwood and Langford, then bending west into forest along the Sooke Hills. The Selkirk Trestle shows clearly as a long span across the Gorge Waterway, and the Switch Bridge as a crossing over the Trans-Canada Highway near Uptown. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Airport (CYWH) is about 1 nm from the downtown terminus and Victoria International (CYYJ) roughly 15 nm north-northeast; this is controlled, busy airspace over Victoria Harbour with heavy floatplane and helicopter traffic. The western half runs through rising terrain with the Sooke Hills ahead; expect low cloud and rain there in winter and good summer visibility.
