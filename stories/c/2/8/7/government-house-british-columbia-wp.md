# Government House (British Columbia)

British Columbia has burned down its vice-regal residence twice; the third one stands on 36 acres of Victoria hillside, half of it Garry oak meadow.

**Location:** 48.4185, -123.3420

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Victoria, British Columbia, Historic buildings and structures in British Columbia, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Government Houses in Canada, Estate gardens in Canada, Government of British Columbia

## Introduction

On 15 April 1957, Victoria watched Government House burn - for the second time in fifty-eight years. The first fire took Cary Castle in May 1899. The second took the mansion that Francis Rattenbury and Samuel Maclure had raised on its ashes, a building that had hosted a reigning monarch eighteen years earlier. There is a certain grim comedy in a province losing the same institution to fire twice, and no obvious moral to draw from it, but it explains why the house standing today on Rockland Avenue is made of steel and granite and reads, to a visitor, as slightly less castle than its predecessors. It is the official residence of the lieutenant governor of British Columbia, and it has been described - informally, but it has stuck - as the ceremonial home of all British Columbians.

## Cary Castle

It began as a private conceit. George Hunter Cary built a castellated house on the hill in 1860, and five years later the British Crown bought it as an official residence for the Governor of Vancouver Island, with Arthur Kennedy the first to occupy it. Then the map changed underneath the building. When the colonies of British Columbia and Vancouver Island merged in 1866, the mainland's Government House in New Westminster was abandoned to other uses and Cary Castle became the residence of the united colony's governor; from 1871, with Confederation, it housed the provincial lieutenant governor instead. A private folly had become, within eleven years, the constitutional address of a Canadian province.

## Rattenbury, Maclure, and a King

After the 1899 fire the province turned to the two architects who were then defining how British Columbia would look to itself: Francis Rattenbury, who gave Victoria its Parliament Buildings and the Empress, and Samuel Maclure, the quieter Arts and Crafts master. Their Government House was completed in 1903. Its great moment came in 1939, when King George VI, accompanied by Queen Elizabeth, arrived as part of that year’s cross-Canada tour - the first time the house was used by the reigning monarch of Canada. Eighteen years later the building was gone. Almost the only piece to survive was the Tudor Revival porte cochere, which had been modelled on Rattenbury's own Hatley Castle.

## The Third House

Construction began soon after the 1957 fire and finished on 19 May 1959, the work of John Laing and Sons. The result is a T-shaped, four-level building with a steel frame hidden inside a Modern Tudor Revival envelope - walls of rusticated British Columbia granite in blue, grey and pink, trimmed with Haddington Island stone, under a roof so steeply pitched it stands two storeys high on its own, broken by chalet gables and a scatter of dormers. The salvaged porte cochere was set back in place over the main entrance, so that visitors now walk into the third Government House through the front door of the second. Lieutenant Governor Frank Mackenzie Ross bought the furnishings in Britain and then donated them to the Crown when he left office.

## The Room That Faces the Olympics

The ballroom occupies the entire south wing and is the largest room in the house, rising twelve metres past an encircling balcony to a cathedral ceiling, from which three Swiss cut-crystal chandeliers hang above matching wall sconces. Along both sides run the Millennium Windows, completed in 2006, and on one wall hangs Reflections at Government House, a textile piece by Carol Sabiston that answers the view out the great south bow window: down over Ross Bay and the roofs of Fairfield, across the Strait of Juan de Fuca, to the Olympic Mountains standing up in Washington State. Above that window, carved into the gable outside, are the Royal Arms of British Columbia. Elsewhere the house keeps its architects close - a Maclure room with a copper-foil ceiling and handmade furniture, a Rattenbury room furnished with the man's own table and chairs, on loan from the Royal British Columbia Museum.

## Thirty-Six Acres, Half of It Wild

What makes the estate unusual is not the house. The 14.6-hectare grounds are open to the public and largely maintained by volunteers of the Friends of Government House Gardens Society, and they are organised as a sequence of set pieces: a British Columbia native plant garden, a cottage garden, herb and cut-flower beds, a rock garden, an orchard of apple, plum and quince, and separate iris, lily, rhododendron and rose gardens, one of them a formal Victorian rose garden. Beyond them lies 8.9 hectares of Garry oak ecosystem - open, mossy, crooked-limbed oak meadow, the landscape that covered this corner of Vancouver Island before the city did. Government House was designated a National Historic Site in 2002. On New Year's Day the lieutenant governor's levee fills the place, and the rest of the year the neighbourhood simply walks its dogs through the grounds of a royal residence.

## Flight Context

Government House stands at 48.4185 N, 123.342 W, at 1401 Rockland Avenue in the Rockland neighbourhood of Victoria, roughly 1.5 km east of the Legislative Buildings. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500-2,500 ft AGL - low enough to pick out the estate's distinctive signature: a large rectangle of mature trees and open meadow set into an otherwise dense grid of streets, with the steep-roofed, T-shaped house at its centre. Useful landmarks: the Inner Harbour and the copper-domed Parliament Buildings to the west, the long dark rectangle of Ross Bay Cemetery and the Ross Bay waterfront directly south, and Beacon Hill Park southwest. Across the Strait of Juan de Fuca the Olympic Mountains give an unmistakable southern horizon in clear weather. Nearest airports: CYWH (Victoria Harbour water aerodrome) about 3 km west, with frequent low floatplane traffic on the Vancouver and Seattle routes; CYYJ (Victoria International) about 20 km north; KCLM (William R. Fairchild International, Port Angeles) across the strait. Victoria is the driest and sunniest part of coastal British Columbia, but morning fog off the strait is common in late summer and early autumn.
