# Thunderbird Park (Victoria, British Columbia)

A small green square beside the Royal BC Museum where Chief Mungo Martin held the first legal public potlatch in Canada in almost seventy years, and where a carving program trained a generation of Northwest Coast artists.

**Location:** 48.4200, -123.3670

**Categories:** First Nations culture in Canada, Northwest Coast art, Parks in Victoria, British Columbia, Indigenous history, Museums

## Introduction

In 1953, Chief Mungo Martin finished building a Kwakwa̱ka̱ʼwakw big house on a patch of grass in downtown Victoria and opened it the correct way: with a potlatch. That sentence contains a small revolution. The potlatch, the ceremony at the centre of Northwest Coast social and legal life, had been outlawed under Canada's Indian Act since the 1880s, and the prohibition was only dropped in 1951. Martin's opening ceremony at Wawadit'la was the first public potlatch held legally in Canada in nearly seventy years. He carved a pole for it with his family. The house he built stands in Thunderbird Park still, a short walk from the provincial legislature that passed a good deal of the law that had made the whole thing a crime.

## Poles in the Rain

The park began in 1940 with an argument about decay. Totem poles are cedar, cedar is mortal, and by the late 1930s many poles standing in coastal villages were falling apart, in communities that the same government policies had done a great deal to hollow out. The province's answer was to bring poles to Victoria and stand them on a lot beside the museum. The site opened as Thunderbird Park in 1941, named for the Thunderbird that appears at the top of so many of these carvings. The collection is mostly Gitxsan, Haida, and Kwakwa̱ka̱ʼwakw work, which is to say it gathers in one small square the distinct artistic traditions of nations separated by hundreds of kilometres of coast. It was a rescue, and it was also a removal, and both of those things stayed true at once.

## Martin Takes Over

Moving the poles indoors in spirit did not stop them rotting. By 1951 many of the transplanted poles were in poor condition, and in 1952 the Royal BC Museum started a restoration program with a crucial decision behind it: rather than hiring conservators to patch old wood, it hired a Kwakwa̱ka̱ʼwakw chief to carve new poles, properly. Mungo Martin became head carver. He was in his sixties, trained in the old way, and he held knowledge that the potlatch ban and the residential school system had come close to severing. What the museum got was not a repair shop. It was a working carving studio run by a hereditary chief, in public, in the middle of the provincial capital, at exactly the moment it was again legal to do so.

## The Carvers Who Came Through

Martin died in 1962, and the program passed to Henry Hunt, who had worked alongside him. The list of artists who carved at Thunderbird Park over the following decades reads like a syllabus of modern Northwest Coast art. Henry Hunt's sons Richard and Tony Hunt both worked there. So did Tim Paul, Lawrence Bell, David Gladstone, and David Martin. So did Bill Reid, whose Haida work would eventually stand in airports, on Canadian currency, and outside embassies. Whatever the province intended when it started the program, its lasting product was not a set of poles. It was a chain of apprenticeships, running from one of the last carvers trained before the ban to artists whose careers defined the second half of the twentieth century.

## Every One a New One

By 1992 every original pole in the park had been replaced by a newly carved version, with some of the originals moved into the museum's collection for preservation. To a European sensibility trained on cathedrals and marble, that sounds like loss. Within the traditions the poles come from, it is closer to the point. A pole is raised, it stands, it weathers, it eventually returns to the ground, and the knowledge of how to make another one is the thing that must not be lost. Thunderbird Park has spent eighty years quietly demonstrating which of those two kinds of permanence it chose.

## Strange Neighbours

The park shares its ground with two relics of the colony that made the poles necessary to rescue. St. Anne's Schoolhouse, a small log building from 1844, stands a few steps from Helmcken House, built in 1852 by Dr. John Helmcken, Fort Victoria's physician and later a figure in British Columbia's entry into Confederation. Around them runs the Royal BC Museum Cultural Precinct, which places a big house, a schoolhouse, a pioneer doctor's home, and a forest of poles within about a hundred metres of each other. The juxtaposition is jarring by design. Walk it slowly and Wawadit'la is the building that tells you most: not a monument to something that ended, but a house built in 1953 by a man who had waited most of his life for permission, and who threw a party the moment he got it.

## Flight Context

Thunderbird Park lies at 48.420N, 123.367W, in downtown Victoria immediately beside the Royal British Columbia Museum and just south of the Inner Harbour. From the air, locate the Inner Harbour's distinctive rectangular basin: the domed BC Legislature building sits on its south side, the Empress Hotel on its east, and the museum complex with its small wooded park is directly behind them, with Beacon Hill Park's larger green expanse a few hundred metres south toward the Juan de Fuca shoreline. Victoria Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) operates on the water a few hundred metres north with frequent scheduled floatplane traffic, so the airspace here is busy and controlled; Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 12nm north and Port Angeles (KCLM) roughly 20nm south across the strait. Stay well above harbour floatplane operations, 2,000 feet or higher, and check Victoria Harbour procedures before transiting. Morning fog is common over the harbour basin in spring and autumn.
