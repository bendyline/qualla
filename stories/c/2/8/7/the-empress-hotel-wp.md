# The Empress (hotel)

Victoria's grand railway hotel, built on a filled-in tidal bay, finished by a second architect after the first was dismissed six weeks before opening.

**Location:** 48.4218, -123.3680

**Categories:** Canadian Pacific Railway hotels, Hotels in Victoria, British Columbia, Hotel buildings completed in 1908, Fairmont Hotels & Resorts, Châteauesque architecture in Canada, Francis Rattenbury buildings, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Hotels established in 1908, Hotels on the National Historic Sites of Canada register, 1908 establishments in British Columbia

## Introduction

On 5 December 1907, with the building essentially finished and its opening six weeks away, Francis Rattenbury was relieved as architect of the Empress Hotel. He had designed it. He had already given Victoria its Parliament Buildings across the way, winning that commission at twenty-five. William Sutherland Maxwell took over for the finish, and on 20 January 1908 the doors opened on what would become the emblem of the city. Rattenbury's name is still attached to it, as it should be. The hotel is also a reminder that the people who make monuments do not always get to stand in the lobby on opening night.

## A Hotel Where the Tide Used to Be

The site itself is an act of will. James Bay was a shallow tidal inlet on the south side of Victoria's Inner Harbour until a stone causeway begun in 1901 allowed the water behind it to be filled. From 1903 the Canadian Pacific Railway moved to acquire the new ground, and Canadian Pacific Hotels built here between 1904 and 1908, adding another link to the company's chain of Châteauesque grand railway hotels. The name honoured Queen Victoria, who was also Empress of India. The form is unmistakable from any angle: steep pitched roofs, gables, polygonal turrets, an asymmetrical plan, projecting pavilions with oriel windows, and an arcaded central loggia. It sits at 721 Government Street, bounded by Humboldt, Belleville and Douglas, facing the harbour across the road.

## Growing Into Itself

The Empress that opened in 1908 was not the Empress you see now. A first expansion ran from 1910 to 1912 and a second was completed in 1928, building the hotel out to its present bulk of 464 guest rooms and suites. The clientele changed as well. The hotel was conceived for business travellers and visitors arriving by Canadian Pacific steamship, but when the company wound down its passenger services to Victoria, the Empress was remarketed as a resort - and Victoria itself emerged as a tourist destination through the mid-to-late 1920s, which was fortunate timing. A small detail says a great deal about how confident the place was: until 1960, there was no sign above the front entrance. Everyone already knew.

## Bodyguards, Kings, and Tea

Guests have supplied the hotel's folklore. In the 1930s Shirley Temple arrived with her parents amid rumours that the family had left California because of kidnapping threats - a story that gained credibility from the two very large bodyguards who took the room across the corridor and kept their door open at all hours. On 30 May 1939, King George VI and Queen Elizabeth came for a luncheon during the royal tour of Canada. Between those set pieces runs the steadier ritual: afternoon tea in the Lobby Lounge, served for generations, the single thing most visitors to Victoria can name about the building without ever having stayed in it.

## The Year It Nearly Came Down

In 1965 somebody proposed demolishing the Empress and replacing it with a modern, functional high-rise hotel. The proposal was serious, and the reaction was immediate. "Without this splendid relic of the Edwardian era, literally tens of thousands of tourists will never return," one local newspaper wrote. "This is the Mecca, this is the heart and soul of the city." The building survived. What followed instead was renovation: refurbished guest rooms, a health club, an indoor swimming pool, a new guest reception. In January 1981 - the fifteenth, precisely - Parks Canada designated the building a National Historic Site of Canada for its contribution to the development of the Châteauesque style in this country, which put a rather firmer floor under any future conversations about wrecking balls.

## Ivy Off, Brick Back

The last thirty years have been a run of transactions and repairs. A conservatory link to the Victoria Conference Centre next door opened in 1989. The hotel was renamed Fairmont in 1999, and when locals took the change badly the company left the original exterior signage untouched as a compromise - a small piece of corporate diplomacy that worked. On 31 October 2000 the property sold to the Legacy Hotels REIT for CA$120 million, with Fairmont staying on to manage it. On 27 June 2014 it sold again, to Nat and Flora Bosa of Vancouver, who put more than $60 million into a restoration running from 2015 to 2017, marked with a ceremony on 28 June 2017. Guest rooms, spa, lobby and dining rooms were all redone, the restaurants redesigned by the San Francisco firm Puccini Group. The most visible change was subtraction: the ivy that had covered much of the brickwork came off, and the brick underneath was repointed and allowed to be brick again.

## Flight Context

The Fairmont Empress stands at 48.4218°N, 123.368°W on the south side of Victoria's Inner Harbour. From 1,500-2,500 ft it is the easiest building in the city to identify: a dark, steep-roofed Châteauesque mass with turrets, set back behind a green lawn on the harbour causeway, with the domed British Columbia Parliament Buildings immediately southwest across Belleville Street and the Royal British Columbia Museum just east. The Inner Harbour's narrow dogleg channel runs northwest to the Upper Harbour; the open Strait of Juan de Fuca and the Olympic Mountains lie south. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) operates in the harbour directly in front of the hotel with frequent scheduled floatplane traffic to Vancouver and Seattle, so keep clear and observe published harbour procedures; Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 22 km north-northwest in North Saanich. Marine stratus is common on summer mornings and usually clears by midday.
