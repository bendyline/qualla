# Fort Victoria (British Columbia)

A Hudson's Bay Company stockade built in 1843 on Lekwungen ground called Camosun, which outgrew the fur trade, became a colonial capital, and was torn down within twenty-one years.

**Location:** 48.4258, -123.3690

**Categories:** Commercial buildings completed in 1843, History of Victoria, British Columbia, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, British North America, Forts in British Columbia, Hudson's Bay Company forts, 1843 establishments in Oregon Country

## Introduction

Before it was a fort, the place was Camosun - a name carried down from the Lekwungen word Camossung, a girl whom the Transformer Hayls turned to stone so that she would watch over the resources of what is now the Gorge waterway. Her name comes from the camas, the blue-flowered lily whose bulbs were cultivated and harvested across these meadows for generations. In 1843 the Hudson's Bay Company drove a palisade into that ground and called it something else.

## Why Not the Columbia

The company's Pacific headquarters had been Fort Vancouver, on the lower Columbia River, and by the early 1840s the arrangement was failing on three counts. The site was difficult to defend. Ships kept struggling to cross the bar at the Columbia's mouth. And it sat a long way south of the richest fur country, in New Caledonia. American settlers were arriving in numbers, and the border was still an open question, so in 1843 the company sent Chief Factor James Douglas north to build a new post on Vancouver Island and made him its superintendent. The Oregon Treaty of 1846 settled the argument three years later: Britain gave up its claims south of the 49th parallel, the United States dropped theirs north of it, and Vancouver Island stayed British. Douglas had built in the right place.

## Forty Pickets for a Blanket

The fort went up with local First Nations labour, paid at the rate of one Hudson's Bay blanket for every forty pickets cut - a bargain struck in the company's favour and a reminder of who set the terms of exchange. The post was briefly known as Fort Albert. On 10 June 1843, by resolution of the HBC's Council of the Northern Department, it was officially renamed Victoria for the young queen. The Songhees soon established a village across the harbour from the palisade, close enough to trade and to watch; it was later moved to the north shore of Esquimalt Harbour. Two communities now shared one small inlet, on very unequal footing.

## Two Days of Musket Balls

In 1844 a dispute brought warriors to the fort under Tzouhalem, a Quamichan chief. For two days they pressed the palisade with threats and musket fire, and the fort held - not because the company had more men, but because Chief Factor Roderick Finlayson eventually demonstrated what the fort's nine-pounder cannon could do. Nobody who watched that demonstration needed it repeated. It is a small episode in the record and an enormous one in what it foretold: a trading post that could not be stormed, backed by artillery the surrounding nations had no answer to, sitting permanently on ground that had been Lekwungen since long before anyone imagined a fort there.

## Furs, Then Lumber, Then Salmon

In 1849 the Colony of Vancouver Island was established, and the HBC received exclusive proprietary rights over the island on two conditions: found a settlement within five years, and spend ninety percent of land-sale revenue on roads, schools and the like. A town was laid out and made the colonial capital, and London sent Richard Blanshard to govern it. Meanwhile the business the fort had been built for was quietly dying - animal populations were thinning from overtrapping - while a new one arrived from the south. The California gold rush created ravenous demand and almost nowhere on the west coast to buy anything. By 1850 several sawmills were running at Victoria. The company was soon shipping salted salmon to Hawaii and provisioning Royal Navy ships bound for the Crimean War.

## Twenty-One Years

Douglas became the colony's second governor in 1851 and remained the dominant figure in Victoria's development until he retired in 1864; he governed the mainland colony as well from 1858 until Frederick Seymour replaced him in 1862. When a small legislature formed in 1860, Douglas rejected any suggestion of responsible government - the town had outgrown the fort, but not yet its factor. The gold rush filled Victoria with people and money, and the palisade became an obstruction in the middle of a commercial city trading with California, Washington Territory and Britain. In November 1864 it was demolished. Sixty years later, on 4 June 1924, the ground where it had stood was designated a National Historic Site of Canada, which is how a place gets commemorated for something it no longer contains.

## Flight Context

The fort stood at 48.4258 N, 123.369 W, on what is now Victoria's Inner Harbour waterfront. This is busy, controlled airspace: Victoria Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) operates directly below, with heavy scheduled floatplane traffic in and out of the harbour, so plan any overflight above the published circuit and talk to the tower. From 2,000-3,000 ft the geography that decided the site is obvious - a deep, sheltered, easily defended notch in the coastline, with Esquimalt Harbour a short distance west and the Strait of Juan de Fuca opening south toward the Olympic Mountains. Visual anchors are the Parliament Buildings and the Empress on the Inner Harbour, the Ogden Point breakwater, and Songhees Point on the north shore. Victoria International (CYYJ) lies about 25 km north on the Saanich Peninsula; Port Angeles (KCLM) is across the strait. Morning fog off the strait is routine, and winter southeasters bring low ceilings over the harbour.
