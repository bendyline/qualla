# Art Gallery of Greater Victoria

British Columbia's largest art collection lives in an 1889 mansion named after Ipswich, shows five percent of what it owns, and keeps a Meiji-era Shinto shrine in the back garden.

**Location:** 48.4219, -123.3480

**Categories:** Museums, Art, British Columbia, Architecture, Asian Art, Historic Houses

## Introduction

In 2004, restorers went at the exterior of the Spencer Mansion with scrapers and patience, working down through fifteen layers of paint to find out what colour the house had been when it was new. The answer was dark red, dark green, and pale salmon - a Victorian scheme that had been buried since the 1970s, when the building was painted plain cream to match the brick of a modern gallery wing bolted onto its side. That small excavation says most of what there is to say about the Art Gallery of Greater Victoria: an old house, a modern museum, and a long negotiation between the two.

## It Started in a Car Dealership

The institution began in 1946 as the Little Centre, founded by former members of the Vancouver Island branch of the Federation of Canadian Artists together with others from the local arts community. Its first home was a former car dealership in downtown Victoria, which it occupied for five years - an unglamorous space for an organization with no collection and considerable ambition. In 1951 Sarah Spencer donated her family's mansion in the Rockland neighbourhood, the Little Centre moved uphill, and it reopened under a new name: the Art Gallery of Greater Victoria. The transition was not immediately complete. The building went on housing its caretakers as a residence through the 1960s, which meant that for a decade people were living upstairs from the art.

## Gyppeswyk

The house was built in 1889 to a design by William Ridgeway Wilson, for the Green family, who called it Gyppeswyk - the Old English name for Ipswich. It sat on a far larger property than it does now, running from Fort Street to Rockland Avenue and taking in a garden, two tennis courts, stables, and a coach house, with a belvedere on the roof commanding a view of the ocean. It also had one brief brush with the machinery of the province. When Cary Castle, the original Government House, burned down in 1903, Gyppeswyk served for a time as the residence of the Lieutenant Governor of British Columbia. Inside, a good deal has been altered over the years, but the original fireplace survives, along with ornate tilework and woodwork in the southeast corner of the second floor.

## What Expansion Costs

Between 1955 and 1978 the museum built seven galleries in a modernist wing east of the mansion, collectively the Exhibition Galleries. The first two, fireproof and opened in 1958 and 1959, were named the Centennial Galleries for the hundredth anniversary of the Colony of British Columbia. Growth came at a price paid by the old house. Construction in the 1950s largely destroyed the mansion's eastern facade and parts of the north, removed its porte-cochère, and drove a glass-enclosed staircase up the north side - alterations severe enough that the Spencer Mansion cannot now qualify for heritage designation. Later work tried to give something back. The rooftop Palladian window was rebuilt in 2004 and the chimney was seismically retrofitted. A further expansion adding 11,000 square feet of exhibition space, a cafe, and a lecture hall won municipal approval in 2015, but rising construction costs pushed the start date back from 2019 and it has not yet been built.

## Twenty Thousand Works, Five Percent on the Wall

The gallery holds roughly 20,000 works, the largest permanent collection of any art museum in British Columbia, organized into departments covering Canadian art, Indigenous Canadian art, contemporary art, decorative art, and Asian art. The building can display about five per cent of it at a time. That ratio is the central frustration of the institution and the reason the expansion keeps being proposed. What is on view rotates through Canadian and Indigenous Canadian work alongside artists from across the Pacific Rim, supplemented by travelling exhibitions the gallery both hosts and organizes. The other ninety-five per cent waits in storage, which is the ordinary condition of most museum collections and no less strange for being ordinary.

## The Shrine in the Garden

The Asian holdings are the surprise. Some 8,000 works - about forty per cent of everything the gallery owns - come from Asia, making this the second-largest collection of Asian art in Canada, behind only the Royal Ontario Museum in Toronto. The Chinese material in particular is thought to have climbed sharply in value through the first decades of this century as interest in the field grew. Outside, behind the mansion, the museum keeps an Asian garden of moss, stone, and winding paths, and standing in it is a Shinto shrine built in Japan during the Meiji era and brought here in the 1980s. It is understood to be the only authentic shrine of its kind in North America, which makes the back garden of a Victorian house in Victoria a genuinely unlikely place to find one.

## Flight Context

The Art Gallery of Greater Victoria stands at 48.4219°N, 123.3480°W in the Rockland neighbourhood, just east of downtown Victoria on Vancouver Island. From the air, orient on Victoria's Inner Harbour with the Parliament buildings and the Empress Hotel on its south shore, then look inland and east about a kilometre to the leafy Rockland district; Government House and its grounds are the largest green block in the immediate area and the gallery sits a short distance west of them. Beacon Hill Park and the Dallas Road waterfront define the southern edge of the city. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,000-3,000 ft MSL for neighbourhood detail, or 6,000-8,000 ft to frame Victoria against the Strait of Juan de Fuca with Washington's Olympic Mountains filling the horizon to the south. Victoria International (CYYJ) lies roughly 12 nm north and Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is about 1.5 nm west. Note that Victoria's airspace is busy with floatplane and helicopter traffic over the harbour. Summer visibility is usually excellent; morning fog is common along the strait in late summer and autumn.
