# Johnson Street Bridge

Four bridges have crossed this one narrow gap in Victoria's harbour since 1855, including a Strauss bascule that the city painted blue, loved, argued about for a decade, and finally replaced in 2018.

**Location:** 48.4281, -123.3719

**Categories:** Bascule bridges in Canada, Buildings and structures in Victoria, British Columbia, Engineering, Road bridges in British Columbia, Railway bridges in British Columbia

## Introduction

The plans for Victoria's most photographed piece of municipal hardware were drawn in Chicago by a company belonging to Joseph Strauss, who thirteen years later would be chief engineer of the Golden Gate Bridge. The Strauss Bascule Bridge Company held the patents on the lifting mechanism, and when the City of Victoria decided in 1920 that it needed a proper drawbridge across the narrows of its harbour, Strauss's firm supplied the design for two 45-metre leaves and the machinery to swing them. The result opened in January 1924, ran for ninety-four years, got painted a distinctive light blue, and became the thing Victorians meant when they said the Blue Bridge.

## Four Bridges, One Gap

The narrows between Victoria's Inner and Upper Harbours is a short crossing with a long history of failed attempts. The first bridge, a low wagon crossing finished in 1855, had the fundamental flaw of sitting in the way of boats; after seven years of complaints the city took it down and ran a ferry instead. A second went up in 1888 for trains and pedestrians, a swing span opened by hand, and held on until 1923. Then came the Strauss bascule. And in 2018, a fourth. Every one existed to solve the same stubborn problem: a working harbour needs its water open, and a city needs its halves connected.

## Old Blue

City engineer F. M. Preston directed the 1924 bridge's design, and the numbers give a sense of it. A hundred tons of steel from Walkerville, Ontario, made up the superstructure; Victoria's own engineering department poured a substructure consuming 9,144 cubic metres of concrete. Hollow concrete counterweights of 780 long tons balanced the leaves, moved by struts and pinions driven by 75-horsepower electric motors. The final bill came to $918,000, some 27 percent over the $720,000 estimate, which was probably the least surprising detail of the entire project. Streetcar rails laid down the middle of the road span were never once used and came up a few years later. The blue arrived in 1979, after severe corrosion forced major repairs: the shade barely fades, because the oxides of its pigment are the same colour as the pigment itself, and it matched the lamp standards the city already used.

## A Bridge That Behaved Like Weather

Old Blue had moods. Its original wooden deck absorbed rain, and a soaked deck weighs more than a dry one, which threw the carefully balanced leaves out of trim and strained the lifting gear; in 1966 the wood came out and a steel grid went in. That solved one problem and created another, since steel expands. During abnormally high temperatures in 1995 the decking grew enough that the bridge would no longer open or close properly, and crews cut roughly an inch out of it. By 2010 some 30,000 trips crossed daily at peak, 23,000 by vehicle and the rest on foot or by bicycle, while the Via Rail Dayliner rumbled over the separate rail span twice a day toward Duncan, Nanaimo, and Courtenay.

## Ten Years of Argument

In April 2009, engineers told Victoria City Council what nobody wanted to hear: the 85-year-old bridge was seismically vulnerable and needed a major retrofit or full replacement within two or three years. What followed was one of the most exhaustively litigated municipal decisions in the city's history. Council approved replacement in principle, chose a rolling bascule design, and ran into a counter-petition that forced a referendum by January 2010. The province declined to help pay; Ottawa put in $21 million. Consultants produced duelling cost estimates, roughly $103 million to refurbish and $89 million to replace, both confirmed reasonable by a peer reviewer. After surveys, open houses, and public bridge tours, council voted in August 2010 to replace, and on 20 November the borrowing referendum passed, 61 percent to 39.

## The Rail Span Goes Quiet

The trains left before the bridge did. On 31 March 2011, a routine inspection turned up corrosion on key structural members of the rail span and it closed to rail traffic; that same month, Victoria-to-Courtenay passenger service was suspended indefinitely because the track beyond was deteriorating. The rail span and its approaches came out in February 2012. Freight up Store Street had ended decades earlier, so the E&N line had already dwindled to a small passenger station beside the bridge. Not carrying rail across the new structure was practical and consequential both: any future commuter rail from the western communities now stops short of downtown, in Victoria West.

## The Fourth Bridge

Construction started in May 2013 on the north side of the old bridge and took nearly six years, a schedule stretched by a steel fabrication programme that began overseas in March 2014, halted over form deficiencies, restarted in March 2015, and delivered its first shipment only in August 2017. Crews sank cofferdams and sixteen caissons into the harbour floor. The bridge opened to the public on 31 March 2018; Old Blue's road span closed two days earlier and came out that spring. What stands now is Canada's largest single-leaf bascule bridge, carrying three lanes with a walkway cantilevered off the south side and a multi-use path off the north, both separated from cars. It is lit with blue LEDs, a deliberate nod to the bridge it replaced, and it is one of only two road drawbridges on Vancouver Island. The other is the 17th Street Bridge in Courtenay.

## Flight Context

The Johnson Street Bridge crosses at 48.428N, 123.372W, at the narrows between Victoria's Inner Harbour and Upper Harbour, linking Johnson Street downtown with Esquimalt Road in Victoria West. From the air it is easy to pick out: a single white bascule leaf with a large counterweight housing at the west end, set at the pinch point of a harbour shaped like an hourglass, with the Empress Hotel and the BC Legislature buildings a few hundred metres southeast around the Inner Harbour basin. Victoria Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) operates directly on the water here with heavy scheduled floatplane traffic, so the airspace is busy and controlled; Victoria International Airport (CYYJ) is about 12nm north and Port Angeles (KCLM) roughly 20nm south across Juan de Fuca Strait. Any overflight should be well above harbour floatplane operations, 2,000 feet or higher, with a check of Victoria Harbour procedures first. Morning fog is common in the harbour basin.
