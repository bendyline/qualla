# Christ Church Cathedral (Victoria, British Columbia)

Anglican cathedral of the Diocese of British Columbia - a third building on ground that has seen a fire, a famous schism, a stonemason's robin, and a central tower that was designed but never built.

**Location:** 48.4222, -123.3590

**Categories:** Cathedrals and Churches, Gothic Revival Architecture, Victoria, Colonial History, British Columbia

## Introduction

Work stopped for a robin. Sometime in the late 1920s, with the third Christ Church Cathedral rising in stone above Victoria, a robin built her nest on the scaffolding beside the top of one of the main nave pillars. The crew left that section alone until the nesting season finished. When it was over, R. W. Marsh - the man responsible for the hundreds of cast stone elements going into the pillars and arches - suggested that the bird deserved something permanent. The architect's assistant, Reginald Dove, modelled her in clay, nest and all. A casting was made and set on the capital of the pillar, where it remains. A cathedral is normally a record of bishops and donors. This one also keeps a record of a bird who got there first.

## A Congregation of Two Hundred

Anglican worship in Victoria began in a mess room. The Hudson's Bay Company had hired Robert John Staines, a Cambridge graduate, to teach the children of Fort Victoria and sweetened the offer with a stipend to take Holy Orders and serve as chaplain; he arrived in 1849 with his wife Emma, unimpressed by the rustic post, and the community was soon equally unimpressed by him. He was discharged in 1854. His replacement, Edward Cridge, reached Victoria with his wife Mary on 1 April 1855, having crossed the Isthmus of Panama by the newly built railway. The census that year counted 232 non-Native residents. For them, the Colonial Surveyor J. D. Pemberton designed and oversaw a church, ready for use on 31 August 1856. Then, in 1858, the Fraser Canyon gold rush hit. Hundreds of makeshift buildings went up almost overnight, and a fort town became a city in a few years.

## Two Churches, One Cathedral

In 1859 Letters Patent created the Diocese of British Columbia, endowed by the Baroness Burdett-Coutts, and George Hills was consecrated its first bishop in Westminster Abbey on 24 February. He arrived in Victoria on 6 January 1860, having engaged the clipper barque Athelstan to bring out a prefabricated church - a building shipped flat from England, erected on Douglas Street about half a mile away, and consecrated as St John the Divine. That left Hills with an awkward choice. St John's could hold six hundred; Christ Church had been built for four hundred, though it had been enlarged twice since. He picked Christ Church for its location, consecrated it as his cathedral on 7 December 1865, and made Cridge its first dean. On the night of 30 September 1869 it burned to the ground. The communion vessels, the organ and the vestry books came out. Everything else did not, and the parish borrowed a vacant Presbyterian church while a new wooden cathedral went up in a little over six months.

## The Dean Who Would Not Bow

The trouble started at evensong. On 5 December 1872, at services marking the consecration of that new building, a guest preacher delivered what Dean Cridge heard as an endorsement of ritualism - the ornate liturgical style that a low churchman like Cridge regarded as a betrayal. Instead of announcing the next hymn, he stood up and contradicted the sermon in front of the congregation, which canon law flatly forbade. Bishop Hills tried censure first. Cridge would not yield. Victoria's newspapers and citizens took sides with enthusiasm. The ecclesiastical trial, held in that same borrowed Presbyterian church and open to the public, found Cridge guilty on sixteen of eighteen charges in September 1874 and revoked his licence; he ignored the verdict and demanded a secular hearing. It went to the Supreme Court of British Columbia, where the case fell to Chief Justice Matthew Baillie Begbie - a friend of both men, a member of the cathedral parish, and a member of its building committee. He urged them to settle. They did not. Begbie granted the injunction, then quietly moved his own membership to St John the Divine. Days later a substantial part of the congregation, including Sir James Douglas and Dr J. S. Helmcken, left with Cridge to found the Church of Our Lord.

## Thirty-Eight Years, and Still No Spire

The wooden cathedral eventually proved too small, and in 1891 the diocese held an international design competition. J. C. M. Keith of Victoria won it in January 1893 with a thirteenth-century Gothic design crowned by a spire. Then the money ran out, and the project sat untouched until 1920. Bishop Charles Schofield, elected in 1916, had already rebuilt one cathedral - Fredericton's, struck by lightning in 1911 while he was dean there - and had the persistence to restart this one. A $250,000 campaign was authorized in 1919, and Keith, still available, revised his drawings to add two west towers and a central lantern tower. Ground was broken in May 1926. Winston Churchill, passing through in September 1929, was handed a stone by the site superintendent and laid it on the north tower. Funds stretched to the nave, the narthex and the bottoms of two towers, with brick vaulting instead of stone, flattened by two feet to save money, and a temporary wooden wall closing the east end. It was consecrated on 28 September 1929. An anonymous £5,000 gift raised the northwest tower to its bell chamber in 1933, and in 1936 two donors paid for a peal of eight change-ringing bells from Mears and Stainbank of Whitechapel, built to the same design as the peal at Westminster Abbey. The central lantern tower was never built. Neither was the spire.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.422 N, 123.359 W in downtown Victoria, British Columbia, on Burdett Avenue above the Inner Harbour. From the air the cathedral reads as a long, pale Gothic mass with two squat, obviously unfinished west towers - the absence of a spire or central tower is the identifying feature, since no other building in the area has that silhouette. It stands just southeast of the Inner Harbour, close to the Royal BC Museum and the domed Parliament Buildings, which together make the easiest visual fix in the city. The Strait of Juan de Fuca and the Olympic Mountains of Washington fill the southern view across about 30 km of water. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,200-2,500 ft MSL for the downtown core. Victoria Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) is roughly 1 km northwest with active floatplane traffic and its own procedures; Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 22 km north; Port Angeles (KCLM) lies 35 km south across the strait. Expect controlled airspace and heavy seasonal harbour traffic, plus morning stratus that burns off to exceptional summer visibility.
