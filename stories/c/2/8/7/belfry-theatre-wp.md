# Belfry Theatre

A Victoria theatre company that grew out of a graduate student staging plays in an unheated Baptist chapel, and has since produced more than 300 shows in a church built before British Columbia had paved roads.

**Location:** 48.4301, -123.3450

**Categories:** theatre, victoria, british-columbia, arts-and-culture, historic-buildings

## Introduction

In 1974 a graduate student at the University of Victoria named Blair Shakel talked his way into an unheated chapel in Fernwood and started putting on plays. Fernwood at that point was not a neighbourhood anyone was moving to. The chapel belonged to Emmanuel Baptist Church, which had worshipped in that building since the 1890s and was on its way out of it. What Shakel had was a room with good bones, bad heating, and no obvious future - which, in the theatre, is close to ideal. Half a century later the Belfry Theatre has staged more than three hundred plays in that building, better than two-thirds of them Canadian, and Fernwood has become the kind of neighbourhood that people write real-estate articles about. The order of those two developments is not a coincidence.

## A Lot at Fernwood and Gladstone, $550

The congregation came first, forming in 1874. They bought the corner lot at Fernwood and Gladstone for five hundred and fifty dollars and dedicated the Spring Ridge Chapel there on 6 February 1887. Under the supervision of the Reverend Peter H. McEwen the building grew into a full church, finished in 1892, designed by Thomas Hooper - an architect whose fingerprints are all over late-Victorian British Columbia, in banks, blocks, and churches from Victoria to the interior. It served as Emmanuel Baptist Church of Fernwood until 1971, when the congregation held its final service there and relocated to Saanich. What Hooper gave the congregation, without meaning to, was a volume that works beautifully for an audience: high, wooden, acoustically alive, sized for a couple of hundred people who have all come to look in the same direction.

## Puttin' on the Ritz

The company as a company dates from 1976, and its first production arrived the following year: Don Shipley's Puttin' on the Ritz, in 1977. From that beginning the Belfry has mounted more than 312 plays. More than 233 of them were Canadian and at least 47 were premieres - work that had never been staged anywhere before it was staged in a converted Baptist church on Vancouver Island. The rhythm of the year is steady and has been for decades: four mainstage productions, one in September and October, one in November and December, one in February and March, one in April and May. The gaps are where the developmental work happens, in commissions and workshops and readings that most audiences never see and every new play depends on.

## Arguing About the Play Afterward

A theatre this size survives by being useful to the people who live around it, and the Belfry has built an unusual amount of scaffolding for that. B4Play sends audiences in already talking; Afterplay and Talkback Thursdays keep them there when the curtain comes down, arguing with the actors and each other. The Belfry 101 programme, later extended into 201 and 301, puts high school students backstage on real productions - not watching, working - and hands them discounted season tickets on the way out. Then there is Upstage, the company's own magazine, alongside the Heart of the Matter resources and a feature called the Belfry Librarian for people who leave a show wanting to read everything behind it. None of this sells tickets directly. All of it explains why the tickets keep selling.

## Doors to New Worlds

In the 2008-09 season the Belfry added SPARK, a festival built to give emerging and unconventional Canadian theatre a place to be seen, surrounded by workshops and free community events. It fits the house philosophy. Michael Shamata, who ran the company from December 2007 until 2026, described the ambition plainly: it was his hope that every play they produced would open a door to a new world. In September 2026 the artistic directorship passed to Nigel Shawn Williams, a director, actor, dramaturg, and teacher with more than thirty-five years in Canadian theatre. He inherits a nineteenth-century church, a fiercely local audience, and a mandate that has not changed much since a cold night in Fernwood in 1974.

## Flight Context

The Belfry Theatre stands at 48.430 degrees north, 123.345 degrees west, at Fernwood Road and Gladstone Avenue in the Fernwood neighbourhood of Victoria, British Columbia, about a kilometre and a half northeast of the provincial Legislature. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet the church is a small pitched-roof landmark in a grid of heritage houses and mature street trees, with Victoria's Inner Harbour and the Legislature dome to the southwest and Royal Jubilee Hospital just to the east. The clearest navigation anchor is the harbour itself and the long green rectangle of Beacon Hill Park on the southern waterfront. Victoria International (CYYJ) lies about 22 kilometres north on the Saanich Peninsula, Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is 2 kilometres southwest and handles constant floatplane traffic to Vancouver and Seattle, and Port Angeles (KCLM) sits across the Strait of Juan de Fuca in Washington. Downtown Victoria is under controlled airspace with active harbour seaplane operations; expect winter rain and low ceilings, and clear stable air most summer afternoons.
