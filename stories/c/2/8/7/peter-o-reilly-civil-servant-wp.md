# Peter O'Reilly (civil servant)

From a verandah on the Gorge Waterway, an Irish immigrant spent eighteen years drawing the lines that became British Columbia's Indian reserve system - and his lawn became a rescue station on the worst day in Victoria's history.

**Location:** 48.4114, -123.3399

**Categories:** Historical Figures, British Columbia, Victoria, Colonial History, Indigenous History, Historic Houses

## Introduction

On the afternoon of May 26, 1896, survivors came crawling up out of the water onto Peter O'Reilly's lawn. The Point Ellice Bridge had just given way beneath a streetcar built for sixty and loaded with 143 people heading to celebrate Queen Victoria's birthday; fifty-five of them died, twelve of them children, in what remains the worst streetcar accident in North American history. The O'Reilly family's shoreline was the nearest dry ground. The house is still there, gardens and croquet lawn intact - a comfortable Italianate villa whose owner had spent the previous sixteen years deciding, on behalf of the Crown, how much land Indigenous nations across British Columbia would be allowed to keep.

## An Irishman on the Make

Peter O'Reilly was born in Ince, England, on 27 March 1827, raised in Ireland by his Irish father, and came to Canada in 1859, arriving in a colony that was being remade by gold. He rose quickly through colonial appointments, and in 1863 he married Caroline Agnes Trutch - a marriage that placed him inside the small circle running the place. Her brothers were John Trutch and Sir Joseph W. Trutch, the latter of whom became British Columbia's first lieutenant-governor and one of the most consequential architects of its land policy. In December 1867 Peter and Caroline moved into Point Ellice House on the Gorge Waterway, and they raised four children there: Frank, Charlotte Kathleen, Mary Augusta, and Arthur John, known as Jack.

## Gold Commissioner at Rock Creek

Before the reserves, there was the goldfield. O'Reilly served as the second Gold Commissioner of the Rock Creek Mining District, taking over from W.G. Cox around the time of the Governor's visit during the Rock Creek War - a confrontation between American miners and colonial authority in the southern Interior that the colony resolved without shooting, largely by showing up in person and insisting on British law. The job of a gold commissioner in that era was part magistrate, part surveyor, part bailiff: register claims, settle disputes, collect fees, and keep a boomtown from becoming ungovernable. It was the kind of work that taught a man to draw a boundary and make it stick.

## Eighteen Years of Drawing Lines

In 1880 O'Reilly was appointed Indian Reserve Commissioner, and he held the post for eighteen years until retiring in 1898. In that time he allotted most of the Indian reserves in British Columbia - a single official, dividing the space of an entire province. What he laid down is, in substance, the reserve geography of British Columbia today. He was criticized then and has been criticized since for how he did it: for largely shirking the work, and in particular for avoiding meetings with the First Nations leaders whose territories he was apportioning. The reserves he marked out at Metlakatla in 1882 were disputed immediately and vigorously by the people who lived there, and with good reason. The consequence for nation after nation was small allotments carved from vast traditional territories, decided quickly, often without the presence or consent of the people affected - a set of lines that still shapes where families live, where they can fish, and what they must go to court to argue about more than a century later.

## The House on the Gorge

Point Ellice House was not built for the O'Reillys. It went up between 1861 and 1862 for Catherine and Charles Wentworth Wallace, designed by the architects Wright & Sanders in the Italianate villa manner then fashionable - a single storey plus attic, shallow-pitched gabled roofs, wide bracketed eaves, verandahs and porches, medieval-style chimneys, and tall windows, arranged in a rambling asymmetrical plan of narrow halls and enclosed rooms. The O'Reillys made it theirs for decades, and the garden became as much a character-defining feature as the building: croquet and tennis lawns, pathways, rose beds, mature trees and shrubs, and a sequoia that Peter O'Reilly planted himself. A carriage accident in 1885 left him immobile for months and he walked with a cane for the rest of his life. He died on 3 September 1905.

## What Survives, and What It Holds

Point Ellice House was designated a National Historic Site in 1966 and a Provincial Historic Site in 1975, and it sits on Victoria's heritage registry as well. It survives as a house museum with an unusually intact interior - original floors, wall coverings, ceilings, doors, mouldings, and hardware - and with its historic garden and carriage shed, the whole arrangement still readable from both land and water, as it was designed to be. Visitors come for the gardens and the tea. They are also standing in the household from which a great deal of British Columbia's land question was administered, a few steps from the water where fifty-five people drowned on a public holiday. A small site, holding more than one large story.

## Flight Context

Point Ellice House, Peter O'Reilly's home and the site associated with him, stands at 48.4114°N, 123.3399°W on the north shore of Selkirk Water where Victoria's Upper Harbour narrows into the Gorge Waterway. Navigate by the harbour: the Inner Harbour with the Parliament buildings and the Empress Hotel lies about a mile southeast, the Johnson Street Bridge marks the entrance to the Upper Harbour, and the Bay Street Bridge - successor to the Point Ellice Bridge that collapsed in 1896 - crosses immediately beside the property. Upstream the Gorge Waterway threads northwest toward Portage Inlet and Craigflower. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500-2,500 ft MSL to read the waterway, the bridges, and the wooded grounds of the house; 5,000 ft frames Greater Victoria against the Strait of Juan de Fuca and the Olympic Mountains. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is less than 2 nm southeast with heavy floatplane traffic on approach; Victoria International (CYYJ) lies roughly 13 nm north. Expect good visibility year-round, occasional marine fog in late summer mornings, and low cloud with rain during winter frontal passages.
