# AbeBooks

Two couples in Victoria launched a website in 1996 listing the stock of exactly four bookstores; twelve years later Amazon bought it, and a single ornithology book on it sold for $191,000.

**Location:** 48.4335, -123.3798

**Categories:** business-history, books-and-publishing, technology, victoria-bc, british-columbia

## Introduction

When the Advanced Book Exchange went live in 1996, you could search the entire inventory of four bookstores. Four. The founders -- Rick and Vivian Pura and Keith and Cathy Waters, two couples working out of Victoria, British Columbia -- had incorporated the company the year before on a premise that sounds obvious now and was faintly absurd then: that the world's used and out-of-print books were not actually lost, merely scattered across thousands of shops with no way to see into each other's shelves. Give every dusty storefront a shared catalogue and the scarcity largely evaporates. The name got shortened to AbeBooks somewhere along the way. The four bookstores became sellers in more than fifty countries.

## A Decade of Being Bought and Buying

For its first decade AbeBooks grew along two tracks at once. It struck reseller agreements that pushed its booksellers' stock onto eBay, Half.com, Barnes & Noble.com, BibliOZ.com and Amazon.com, an arrangement that lasted until 2005, when all of those were dissolved except the Amazon channel. Meanwhile it went shopping. Germany's JustBooks GmbH came in 2001, opening the German, French and British markets. The Spanish marketplace IberLibro followed in 2004, the same year the company started listing new books alongside used. In 2005 it bought BookFinder.com, the American price-comparison service, and in 2006 it took both the inventory-management firm Fillz and a forty percent stake in LibraryThing, the cataloguing site where obsessive readers shelve their own libraries in public. The founders themselves had been bought out in 2002 by the German media group Hubert Burda Media. In June 2008 the British Columbia Technology Industry Association gave AbeBooks its Impact Award for Leadership in Social Responsibility. Six months later, in December, Amazon bought the company.

## What a First Edition Hobbit Is Worth

In February 2015 AbeBooks recorded the highest sale in its history to that point: a rare illustrated ornithology volume, sold on behalf of one of its dealers for $191,000. Beneath that sat a tier of six-figure-adjacent trophies -- John Milton's Areopagitica, the 1644 argument against licensing the press, and a first edition of Tolkien's The Hobbit, each at $65,000. The company publishes these numbers periodically, and they function as advertising in the way a jeweller's window does. The real character of the site lives at the other end of the scale. Prices are fixed, there are no auctions, and the floor is one US dollar. A given title might appear in hundreds of identical listings from hundreds of shops, or exactly once, signed, in a condition described by someone who has handled it. Both are on the same search results page.

## Not Only Books

The inventory drifted well past the bound codex a long time ago. Alongside the books sit periodicals and journals, prints and posters, vintage photographs, maps, sheet music, and the category booksellers call ephemera -- postcards, letters, playbills, receipts, the paper that was never meant to survive and therefore rarely did. That last category is where the marketplace becomes something closer to an archive with a checkout button. A letter written in 1912 and thrown in a drawer is not a book by any definition, but it is an object that a used-book dealer in a small town is likely to own, unlikely to be able to price, and now able to put in front of someone in Osaka or Helsinki who has been looking for exactly it.

## The Sellers' Side of the Ledger

For the bookshops, AbeBooks is a landlord as much as a shopfront. Sellers pay a monthly subscription scaled to inventory size -- from $25 to $500 -- and a percentage of each sale on top. The terms have moved over the years, not usually in the sellers' favour. In April 2006 credit-card processing through AbeBooks became mandatory, with a 5.5 percent charge attached to a service that had previously been optional. In 2008 the commission began applying to postage as well as the book price. Then, in 2018, the company announced it would stop fully supporting sellers in several countries, among them South Korea, Hungary, and the Czech Republic. The response was unusual for an industry of scattered small businesses: several hundred sellers elsewhere in the world pulled their inventories from the site in protest on behalf of colleagues they had never met. It was a boycott by people whose entire trade depends on a single search box.

## Run From a Small City on the Edge

The company is still headquartered in Victoria, with a European office in Munich, and it operates localized storefronts for North America, France, Germany, Italy, the United Kingdom and Spain, each differentiated by country domain, language and currency. It has been named one of British Columbia's Top Employers every year since 2008. There is a pleasing symmetry in the address. Victoria is a small provincial capital at the far southwestern corner of Canada, a city of ferry terminals and secondhand shops, home to Munro's Books, one of the most admired independent bookstores in the country. From here, a database quietly keeps track of where the world's unwanted books are sitting, and waits for someone to want one.

## Flight Context

AbeBooks is headquartered near 48.43 N, 123.38 W in Victoria, British Columbia, on the southern tip of Vancouver Island. This is an office, not a visual landmark -- navigate by the city itself: Victoria's Inner Harbour with the Parliament Buildings and the Empress Hotel on its edge, the Ogden Point breakwater to the southwest, and the long green rectangle of Beacon Hill Park above the Strait of Juan de Fuca. The Olympic Mountains of Washington State stand across the strait to the south. Best viewed at 1,500-3,000 ft AGL. Victoria International (CYYJ) lies about 12 nm north; Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is in the harbour itself and is one of the busiest floatplane terminals in Canada -- expect heavy seaplane traffic at low altitude over the water. Vancouver International (CYVR) is roughly 35 nm northeast across the Strait of Georgia. Victoria sits in a rain shadow and enjoys some of the mildest, clearest flying weather in Canada, though morning fog off the strait is common.
