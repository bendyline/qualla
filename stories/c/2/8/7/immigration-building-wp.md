# Immigration Building

When the wreckers stripped this Victoria building in 1978, they found poems written in Chinese under the paint — left by people who had been locked inside without being told why.

**Location:** 48.4210, -123.3847

**Categories:** Historic sites in British Columbia, History of immigration to Canada, History of Chinese Canadians, Victoria, British Columbia, Demolished buildings and structures

## Introduction

The poems were found under the paint. In 1978, as crews prepared to demolish the old Immigration Building at Ontario Street and Dallas Road in Victoria, layers of overpainting came away from the interior walls and revealed writing underneath — messages and verses in Chinese characters, put there by people who had been held in those rooms. They had been detained in a country they were trying to enter, often for weeks, sometimes for months. Many spoke no English. Many were never given an explanation they could understand. What they left behind on the plaster is one of the few first-person records of what this building actually was, written by the only people in a position to describe it.

## Ninety-Six, Thirty-Six, Twenty-Four, Forty-Eight, Sixteen

The building that opened on 13 November 1909 was two storeys of red brick containing medical facilities, immigration offices, and wards divided by race. Its capacity was set out in the plans with a specificity that tells you everything about the thinking behind it: room, in the language of the period documents, for ninety-six Hindus, thirty-six women, twenty-four Chinese, forty-eight Japanese, and sixteen others — roughly two hundred and twenty people, sorted before they arrived. The basement held a kitchen. The first floor had a reception room and a large dining room. The second storey was partitioned by strong concrete walls into cells, and bars were fitted across the windows. Officials of the day reassured white residents of Victoria that arrivals would be kept apart from one another at every stage. The architecture was the policy, rendered in brick.

## The Walk From Rithet's Wharf

Ships carrying migrants from Hong Kong tied up at Rithet's Wharf, and from there, as the University of Victoria geographer David Chuenyan Lai documented, the new arrivals were marched to the Immigration Building to be examined. Under the Chinese Immigration Act of 1885, they were required to pay a head tax simply for the right to enter — a charge levied on no other group of newcomers to Canada. A person could be detained for any of several reasons. If they could not pay the tax, they were held and then sent back. If an officer judged them physically defective — the phrase belongs to the era, not to this account — they were held. If a ship arrived with more passengers than the building could process, they were held. If their paperwork was found to contain a false statement, they were held. In 1923 the Chinese Exclusion Act shut Chinese immigration down almost entirely, and the building carried on processing arrivals from elsewhere in Asia.

## A Building Some MPs Thought Useless

Major arrivals by sea into Victoria had begun in 1862, and for decades the city handled them out of whatever premises were available, including a former barracks. Everyone agreed the arrangement was inadequate. What Parliament could not agree on was whether to fix it. Some members opposed funding the new building for a revealing reason: they wanted Asian immigration stopped altogether, and since relatively few white immigrants arrived at Victoria by sea, they considered a permanent immigration facility there a waste of money. The building went up anyway, and the argument that preceded it is preserved in the record — a reminder that the exclusionary policies of the period were not quiet assumptions but open parliamentary positions, debated and voted on.

## Closed, Abandoned, Demolished

The Immigration Building processed and detained people until 1957, and closed in 1958. It then stood empty for twenty years — a large brick structure with barred windows on a prominent Victoria corner, gradually deteriorating while the city around it changed. Demolition came in 1978. Had it been emptied a decade later, in a different climate of thought about what deserves preserving, it might well be standing today, and the writing on its walls with it.

## Three Plaques on a Low Wall

The reckoning came slowly. Between November 2013 and January 2014, the British Columbia government held public consultations in preparation for a formal apology for historical wrongs against Chinese Canadians, delivered in May 2014. One recommendation from that process was an inventory of historic sites, and when the province invited nominations in January 2015, seventy-seven places were identified — the Immigration Building among them. On 27 January 2016 the site was designated a Provincially Recognized Heritage Site under the Heritage Conservation Act, and on 2 June 2017 it was entered in the Canadian Register of Historic Places. There is a townhouse development on the ground now, ringed by a low wall. Set into that wall are three identical plaques, one facing each of the three surrounding streets, explaining what stood here. It is a modest memorial for a building that shaped so many lives, but it is deliberate: you cannot walk past this corner from any direction without being told.

## Flight Context

The site of the former Immigration Building is at 48.4210 degrees north, 123.3847 degrees west — 50 Dallas Road, at the corner of Ontario Street in Victoria's James Bay neighbourhood, on the southern tip of Vancouver Island. Best viewed from 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL on an east-west track along the Dallas Road waterfront. The landmarks that frame it are unmistakable: the Inner Harbour and the copper dome of the BC Parliament Buildings a few hundred metres north, the Ogden Point breakwater and cruise ship berths immediately west, and the open Strait of Juan de Fuca to the south with the Olympic Mountains beyond. Victoria Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) sits directly north in the Inner Harbour and handles constant floatplane traffic; Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 25 km north on the Saanich Peninsula. Note the harbour's controlled airspace and heavy seaplane movement. Summer mornings often bring low marine stratus along the Dallas Road shoreline that clears by midday.
