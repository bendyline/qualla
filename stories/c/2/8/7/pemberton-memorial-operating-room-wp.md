# Pemberton Memorial Operating Room

An octagonal tiled room in Victoria where Joseph Lister's antiseptic method arrived in British Columbia, built from a bequest meant for something else.

**Location:** 48.4328, -123.3276

**Categories:** National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Buildings and structures in Victoria, British Columbia, Hospital buildings completed in 1896, History of medicine, Pemberton family

## Introduction

Teresa Pemberton laid the cornerstone on 15 May 1896 and was handed a silver trowel for the occasion. It was not an ordinary trowel. The handle had been carved from an oak timber taken out of the Beaver, the first steamship to work the Pacific Northwest. The blade was silver from the Bluebell mine, the base was made from matte out of the Silver King, and the ferrule was gold from Le Roi. Somebody had assembled a small anthology of British Columbia and put it in a widow's hand. The cornerstone she set that day, and the time capsule buried beneath it, have never been found again. What stands above them is the Pemberton Memorial Operating Room, the first operating room of the Royal Jubilee Hospital and the building that made Victoria the surgical centre of the province.

## A Bequest That Would Not Stretch

Joseph Despard Pemberton, the colonial surveyor who decades earlier had built the Fisgard lighthouse at the mouth of Esquimalt Harbour, died of a heart attack in 1893 while out riding. On 9 January 1894 his son F. B. Pemberton told the Royal Jubilee's board of directors that his father had left the hospital two thousand dollars, with the condition that it go toward developing new property. The board decided on a maternity ward. Teresa Jane Pemberton, his widow, added fifteen hundred dollars of her own. Thirty-five hundred dollars was not going to build a maternity ward, and after eighteen months of argument the board changed course. On 7 October 1895 they settled on an operating room instead, the option Dr. John Chapman Davie Jr. had wanted all along.

## Lister's Method Reaches the Coast

Davie had been agitating for a proper hospital in Victoria since the mid-1880s, and the Royal Jubilee opened in May 1890 to his preferred plan, a single-level pavilion of interconnected buildings. Money was tight from the start. The hospital's debt climbed through the early 1890s until the city, grateful for how the institution handled a smallpox epidemic in 1893, passed a bylaw to pay off the mortgage as a show of appreciation. Davie had spent a sabbatical in Europe in the 1880s studying the antiseptic techniques Joseph Lister had developed, and he intended to build a room designed around them. John Teague's design, submitted as Plan 3, was chosen on 20 March 1896. Thomas Catterall won the construction tender at $3,614, which was $114 more than the fund held. Teresa Pemberton wrote another cheque.

## Everything in It Could Be Washed

The room is octagonal, set on a stone foundation and connected by a short passage to a sterilizing room at its north end. Brick walls were covered in painted plaster; a ceramic tile mosaic was laid over the concrete floor. Large windows brought in daylight, and the original kerosene lamps were replaced with electrical fixtures in 1897 when the hospital joined the grid. Every one of those choices was a decision about germs. Surfaces had to be scrubbed and disinfected after each procedure, so there were no crevices, no fabric, nothing porous. The room held the patient and the surgical team and nobody else, no gallery of onlooking students, and a fifteen-metre corridor linked it back to the hospital. Once it was built, the board discovered it still needed five hundred dollars to equip it. The Women's Auxiliary and Teresa Pemberton covered that too.

## Lister Comes to Look

On 13 September 1897, Joseph Lister himself visited the room. The record says he was favourably impressed, which from the man who invented the discipline is a considerable endorsement for a colonial hospital on the far edge of the empire. He made one recommendation: add a room where patients could be watched as they came out of anaesthesia. The hospital built two of them the following year at a cost of a thousand dollars, and they were the first post-operative recovery rooms in Canada. A second sterilizing room was added in 1918. The Pemberton Memorial Operating Room was in use every day from 1896 until 1925, when the hospital's east wing expansion opened six new theatres and this one became, in turn, the radiography department, the cancer agency, and finally medical records.

## Saved by a Recession

In 1981 the Victoria Medical Society asked city council to protect the building under bylaw 173. The hospital's own board of directors objected, since heritage status would complicate its expansion plans, and the building looked likely to come down. What saved it was a provincial budget. In 1983 British Columbia cut health care capital spending, the demolition was delayed, and the room simply survived long enough for opinion to change. A 1991 excavation of its contents recovered artefacts from its working years. Victoria designated it a municipal heritage site in 1995 and Canada recognized it as a National Historic Site on 28 November 2005, listing it on the Canadian Register of Historic Places in 2009. Teresa Pemberton had also funded the Pemberton Chapel in 1909 to remember her husband, and the chapel and the operating room are now joined by a walkway through a garden courtyard. The silver trowel is in the Royal British Columbia Museum.

## Flight Context

The Pemberton Memorial Operating Room stands at 48.433N, 123.328W, on the Royal Jubilee Hospital campus in the Oaklands area of Victoria, about three kilometres northeast of the Inner Harbour. From the air the hospital complex is the obvious landmark, a dense cluster of large institutional buildings amid otherwise low-rise residential streets; the operating room itself is a small octagonal structure at ground level and is easy to miss from altitude. Orient on Victoria's Inner Harbour and the Parliament Buildings to the southwest, and on the open water of Haro Strait and the San Juan Islands to the east. Victoria International Airport (CYYJ) is about 20 kilometres north near Sidney, and Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is a few kilometres southwest with heavy floatplane traffic. Best viewed at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL. Note that hospital helipad operations are active in this area.
