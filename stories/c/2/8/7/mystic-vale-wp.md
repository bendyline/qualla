# Mystic Vale

Eleven acres of old ravine forest on the edge of a university campus, bought for $2.7 million to stop a subdivision, and carrying a name borrowed from a story that was never quite the settlers' to tell.

**Location:** 48.4606, -123.3030

**Categories:** Parks, British Columbia, Victoria, Forests, Conservation, First Nations

## Introduction

In 1904 the Victoria author D.W. Higgins published his version of a local legend. A huge maple once stood in this ravine beside a spring of pure water, he wrote, and the tree was a god that guarded the spirit of the spring. Look into the water at full moon and a woman would see the face of the man who loved her; a man would see the woman who would marry him. "Cut down the tree," Higgins warned, "and the spring will be seen no more." It is a lovely piece of writing and a fairly typical one for its era - a settler collecting an Indigenous belief, smoothing it into fable, and publishing it as local colour. Mavis Henry has put the correction plainly: the mythology attached to this place "goes beyond fable and represents real use and real belief systems at work." Mystic Vale was not a story. It was a sacred site, in active use, belonging to the Straits Coast Salish peoples whose homeland this remains.

## Eleven Acres, $2.7 Million

By the early 1990s the ravine was privately held and residential development was closing in. The University of Victoria had wanted the land for three decades and finally moved during its thirtieth-anniversary year, buying Mystic Vale in 1993 for $2.7 million with help from the provincial government and the Municipality of Saanich. The purchase happened because a lot of people pushed for it at once. The Friends of Mystic Vale organized public support; the Mystic Vale Action Committee, which included former UVic chancellor Dr. Bill Gibson, worked the institutional angles; Saanich mayor Murray Coell and his council backed it; and Tom Perry, then the provincial minister responsible for advanced education, found the provincial share. It cost a great deal of money for 4.7 hectares. Nobody who walks it now argues with the price.

## Under the Canopy

The ravine holds the kind of forest that southern Vancouver Island has mostly converted into neighbourhoods. Douglas-fir and grand fir dominate the canopy, with scattered western red cedar among them. Bigleaf maple, black cottonwood, and willow fill the wetter ground near the creek, Garry oak appears along the forest edge, and arbutus - Canada's only native broadleaf evergreen tree, with its peeling cinnamon bark - grows here too. The oldest trees anyone has verified are roughly 100 to 150 years old, but foresters suspect some of the largest specimens are between 350 and 500. Below them grows an understory of more than 75 native plant and animal species, including oceanspray, snowberry, Indian plum, and sword fern. A few are genuinely uncommon around Victoria: rattlesnake plantain, stink currant, and vanilla-leaf are hard to find anywhere else in the region.

## Who Else Uses the Ravine

Thousands of students and neighbours walk these trails every year, and a surprising amount of wildlife tolerates them. Black-tailed deer browse the slopes. Raccoons, eastern cottontail rabbits, and bats are regular residents, and river otter tracks show up along the creek bed. In 2005 someone saw a cougar, which in a ravine bordered on all sides by suburb and campus counts as an event. Overhead the bird list is better than the acreage suggests: bald eagles, Cooper's hawks, great horned owls, and five species of woodpecker, which is a good indicator of standing dead wood - the snags that most managed landscapes remove and that a working forest needs.

## Ivy, Daphne, Holly

Protection turned out to be the beginning of the work rather than the end of it. A narrow ravine surrounded by development absorbs everything the development sheds - stormwater surging down the creek and scouring the banks, and garden escapees rooting in the understory. English ivy, daphne, and holly all spread aggressively into coastal forest and will smother native ground cover given a season or two. The university's response has been unglamorous and continuous: student EcoAction groups and sustainability projects pulling invasives by hand, and constructed detention ponds upstream to slow the runoff before it eats the stream banks. Facilities Management runs long-term plans for streamside remediation and public education, and the vale doubles as an outdoor laboratory for teaching and research.

## What the Name Is For

There is a tidy irony in an institution spending $2.7 million to preserve a place whose sacredness it inherited secondhand, through a settler author's retelling, under a name that author's era popularized. The university has been explicit that the land sits within Straits Coast Salish territory and has committed to protecting both Mystic Vale and the adjacent Haro Woods for the long term. Higgins wrote that the spring would vanish if the tree came down. Read as ecology rather than enchantment, that is simply accurate: remove the canopy and the hydrology goes with it. He heard something true and turned it into a charming story. The ravine is still here, which suggests somebody eventually took the warning literally.

## Flight Context

Mystic Vale lies at 48.461 N, 123.303 W in Saanich, British Columbia, on the southeast edge of the University of Victoria campus just outside the ring road. At 4.7 hectares it is small but visually distinct from the air - a dark, narrow wedge of mature conifer forest cutting through an otherwise uniform suburban and campus landscape. A viewing altitude of 1,500-3,000 feet MSL is appropriate; the UVic ring road shows as a clear circular pattern, with the vale trailing off its southeast quadrant toward Hobbs Creek and the Cadboro Bay area. Cadboro Bay and the Haro Strait lie to the east, downtown Victoria about 5 km southwest. Victoria International (CYYJ) is roughly 8 nm north at Patricia Bay and Victoria Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) about 4 nm southwest - both busy, both generating traffic across this area, so check airspace before descending. Conditions are typically mild with a wet season from November through March; the canopy is dense enough that the ravine floor stays dark even in summer.
