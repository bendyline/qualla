# Ross Bay Cemetery

Victoria's seaside Victorian cemetery holds more than 27,000 people, a long row of British Columbia premiers, and the graves of the Anishinaabe-Metis woman whose land it was.

**Location:** 48.4114, -123.3400

**Categories:** Cemeteries, Local History, Indigenous Culture, Victorian Heritage, British Columbia

## Introduction

The cemetery is named after the woman who owned the land, and her story is better than any of the monuments standing on it. Isabella Mainville Ross was Anishinaabe and French Metis, and in a colony where property law was written by and for British men, she became the first registered independent woman landowner in British Columbia. Her son Alexander was buried in the ground that had been hers in 1876, under a plain wooden marker she chose. That marker is the only known original from the cemetery's earliest years still held by the Old Cemeteries Society, and it is now the pattern from which the society's heritage markers are made. Isabella was buried across the path from her son in 1885. In 1994, more than a century later, the society set a heritage marker at her grave, styled after the one she had chosen for Alexander.

## A Cemetery Facing the Open Water

Ross Bay Cemetery opened in late 1872 at what is now 1516 Fairfield Road, and the first person laid to rest here was Mary Letitia Pearse, born Pemberton, the wife of Benjamin Pearse. The ground covers 27.5 acres and its southern boundary fronts directly onto Ross Bay and the open Pacific - which turned out to be a problem. Waves worked at the shoreline year after year until, in 1911, the city had to build a sea wall to stop the cemetery from eroding into the sea. The place looked nothing like it does now. Early photographs show largely barren ground, exposed to the weather. It was only in the 1930s that the city began planting trees in quantity, and the mature, shaded park that visitors walk through today is the result of that century-old decision.

## Where the Premiers Went

Walk the paths and the density of provincial history becomes almost comic. Victoria is the capital of British Columbia, and until improved ferry service and air travel in the second quarter of the twentieth century made leaving the island easy, senior politicians simply lived here permanently - so this is where they were buried. Robert Beaven, Amor De Cosmos, John Robson, George Anthony Walkem, Alexander and Theodore Davie, Harlan Brewster, Sir Richard McBride, Edward Gawler Prior, Byron Johnson, James Dunsmuir: premier after premier, under the elaborate mausoleums and tall pillars the colonial elite favoured. Sir James Douglas is here, the Hudson's Bay executive who became the first governor of British Columbia. So is Sir Matthew Begbie, the province's first chief justice, and Roderick Finlayson, remembered as the father of Victoria.

## The Other Twenty-Seven Thousand

More than 27,000 people lie here, and the ones without titles are the more interesting company. Emily Carr, who painted the forests and the Indigenous villages of this coast and won little recognition until late in life, is buried in the city she never really left. Billy Barker, the frontiersman and prospector whose name is on Barkerville, is here too, and so is Nellie Cashman, a nurse and gold prospector who spent her life on mining frontiers. Hannah Maynard, whose experimental multiple-exposure photographs still look startlingly modern, rests among them, as does the painter Sophie Pemberton. Set into the grounds are 135 Commonwealth war graves - 133 from the First World War, two from the Second.

## Full, and Then Not

For most of the twentieth century Ross Bay was considered closed: no room left, nothing to sell. Then in the late 1990s a survey turned up roughly 270 plots that had never been used. The city held a lottery, sold seven of them in April 2004 and another 65 in February 2007, and put the proceeds straight back into restoring the cemetery's crumbling monuments and paths. It is a neat arrangement - the recently dead paying to repair the resting places of the long dead. Ross Bay has also acquired a stranger afterlife in popular culture. A 1980 book, since thoroughly discredited, claimed the cemetery as the site of satanic rituals and helped ignite a decade of moral panic; more cheerfully, the grounds later lent their name to a local heavy metal scene that took the cemetery as its emblem. Both reputations belong to the living. The 27,000 are here for the view of the water.

## Flight Context

Ross Bay Cemetery occupies the shoreline at 48.4114 N, 123.3400 W in Victoria's Fairfield neighbourhood, on Vancouver Island's southern tip. The visual anchor from the air is Ross Bay itself - a shallow scallop of coastline on the Strait of Juan de Fuca about 1.5 nm southeast of the Inner Harbour - with the cemetery's dark canopy of mature trees forming a distinct rectangular block right against the sea wall, an obvious green break in the surrounding residential grid. Dallas Road and its clifftop walkway run along the shore, with the Olympic Mountains filling the horizon across the strait to the south. Victoria Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) lies just northwest in the Inner Harbour with active floatplane traffic; Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 14 nm north near Sidney. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet MSL takes in the cemetery, the Dallas Road waterfront and Clover Point, while remaining clear of the harbour seaplane operating area. Southerly wind off the strait can make the shoreline turbulent; the clearest light on the monuments is late afternoon.
