# Maritime Museum of British Columbia

A museum founded by naval officers in 1955 that holds 35,000 artefacts, three historic small boats, and — since losing its courthouse home — no permanent building of its own.

**Location:** 48.4260, -123.3690

**Categories:** Maritime museums in British Columbia, Museums in Victoria, British Columbia

## Introduction

One of this museum's holdings is not in a building at all. Dorothy, a fantail cutter built in Victoria in 1897, came through a full refit and went back in the water, and she sails — a boat older than powered flight by six years, still working to windward in the same harbour where she was launched. That tells you something about the Maritime Museum of British Columbia's idea of preservation. It also sets up the institution's central irony: a museum whose collection includes a seaworthy nineteenth-century yacht has itself been without a permanent home for over a decade.

## Started by Men Who Had Been at Sea

It began in 1955, at Signal Hill in Esquimalt, opened by naval officers who understood that the west coast's maritime record was scattered across attics, shipyard offices, and the memories of people who were getting old. In 1957 the Maritime Museum of British Columbia Society was registered as a non-profit with a mandate deliberately drawn wider than the navy — Pacific coastal heritage in general, merchant and fishing and Indigenous and recreational alike. Sixteen years after the Society's registration, a corresponding Foundation was set up to build long-term financial support. The naval thread eventually found its own home: in 1981 a separate group founded the CFB Esquimalt Naval and Military Museum, which continues at Naden on the base today.

## The Courthouse That Had to Empty

For half a century the museum lived at 28 Bastion Square, in the provincially owned courthouse of 1889 — a handsome brick pile in the oldest part of downtown Victoria, where a former courtroom made a superb exhibit hall. Then the engineering caught up with it. The building needed seismic upgrading and other structural work, and in October 2014 the museum's venue closed. Packing 35,000 objects took more than a year. By July 2015 the Society had completed its move to a society office in Nootka Court on Humboldt Street, with the collection itself going into climate-controlled storage in the north end of the city. In August 2021 the museum opened an interim public space at 744 Douglas Street — exhibits, a research room, a gift shop, and offices, all in a storefront rather than a landmark.

## Thirty-Five Thousand Objects, Most of Them Unseen

The numbers are the real exhibit. Some 35,000 artefacts, including 800 ship models. A reference library of 6,000 volumes, 200 of them historically significant in their own right. Ships' plans for 1,800 vessels. Roughly 36,000 photographs. Logbooks, naval records, charts, an art collection, and — quietly among the most valuable material in the province — the business archives of local ship-owning and shipbuilding firms, the paperwork that shows how a coast actually ran. The bulk of it sits in controlled storage, which sounds like a loss until you consider the alternative. Salt air, in the end, is the enemy of everything a maritime museum exists to save. The reference room is open to researchers, and staff and volunteers will help you find what you are after.

## Three Hulls

Three small vessels anchor the collection, and each represents a different flavour of nerve. Tilikum is a 38-foot Nuu-chah-nulth cedar dugout, already old when Captain John Voss rigged her with sails and set off westward from Vancouver Island in 1901, eventually reaching London. Trekka is a 20.5-foot sailboat that her Victoria builder sailed around the world beginning in 1954 — at the time, the smallest yacht ever to circumnavigate the globe. And Dorothy, the 1897 fantail cutter, is the oldest of them and the only one still doing what she was built for. Standing among the three, you notice how small they all are. The Pacific is the largest thing on the planet, and people crossed it in boats you could park in a driveway.

## Flight Context

The Maritime Museum of British Columbia sits in downtown Victoria at approximately 48.426°N, 123.369°W, a short walk from the Inner Harbour on Vancouver Island's southeastern tip. Fly the harbour approach at 1,000 to 2,000 feet for the best orientation: the Inner Harbour is unmistakable from the air, with the copper domes of the BC Legislature on the south shore, the green-roofed Empress Hotel on the east, and floatplanes working the water almost continuously in daylight. Bastion Square and the 1889 courthouse — the museum's former home — sit just inland of the Wharf Street waterfront, and the Douglas Street corridor runs northeast from the harbour through the core. Ogden Point's breakwater juts into the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the southwest, with the Olympic Mountains beyond. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is directly below and is one of the busiest water aerodromes in Canada — expect heavy floatplane traffic and treat the harbour airspace with care. Victoria International (CYYJ) lies about 13 nautical miles north on the Saanich Peninsula, and Vancouver International (CYVR) is the nearest major hub across the strait. Marine fog is common summer mornings; winter afternoons after a front often bring exceptional clarity.
