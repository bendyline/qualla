# Emily Carr House

The Victoria villa where Emily Carr grew up, restored by scraping back a century of paint until the original patterns reappeared.

**Location:** 48.4138, -123.3700

**Categories:** Historic Houses, Museums, Victoria, British Columbia, Art History

## Introduction

To restore this house, someone had to take it apart with a scraper. Layer after layer of newer paint and wallpaper came off the interior walls, decade by decade in reverse, until the original patterns and colours surfaced underneath, exactly as they had been when a small girl named Emily Carr lived here. Outside, restorers worked from photographs taken during her lifetime to return the exterior to its first design and colour. The wood finishes she would later describe in her books had to be recreated from scratch. The result is a National Historic Site in downtown Victoria that is less a museum than a reconstruction of a specific set of childhood memories.

## A Villa on Carr Street

Richard Carr built the house in 1863 for his affluent family, on a large property he owned, at an address that was then simply 44 Carr Street. He hired Wright & Sanders, prominent local architects whose other work includes Fisgard Lighthouse, itself now a National Historic Site. They gave him an Italianate villa, the fashionable form of the moment, and it survives as an unusually well-preserved example of the style. The neighbourhood it stood in was the centre of nineteenth-century Victoria, full of merchants, businessmen and politicians. The Dunsmuirs lived nearby; Helmcken House went up in the same district. This was where colonial Victoria's money built its houses, and the Carrs were comfortably part of it.

## The Child Who Noticed

Emily Carr was born in 1871, eight years after the house was finished, and spent most of her childhood inside it. She left eventually for artistic training in San Francisco, then London, then Paris, and came back to Vancouver Island, where she made the paintings and wrote the books she is known for. What is striking is how little distance she ever put between herself and this address. Much of her life was lived within walking distance of the family home. Its rooms, its garden, its smells and light turn up in all of her books, which means the house is not merely where she came from. It is source material, referenced and re-referenced across decades of writing by a woman who knew exactly what she was drawing on.

## The Estate Divided

Richard Carr died in 1888, and ownership of the family farm passed to her elder sister Edith. Edith subdivided the estate into lots and sold them off, with the Carr sisters keeping five for themselves. It was the ordinary fate of large family properties in a city that was filling in around them, and it is the reason the house now sits in an urban block rather than at the centre of acreage. The address changed too: what was 44 Carr Street is now 207 Government Street, six blocks from the Inner Harbour and the Parliament Buildings. The street grid absorbed the farm. The house stayed.

## Undoing a Century

Staying is not the same as surviving intact. Over the intervening decades the building accumulated additions and modernisations, the accretions any working house collects. Two different architects led the extensive restoration that stripped them away, working toward a single reference point: the house as it stood in Emily Carr's time. Period photographs governed the exterior. The interior demanded the slower archaeology of paint removal, stripping down through successive redecorations until the original schemes appeared. It is painstaking, unglamorous work, and it is the reason the rooms feel specific rather than generically Victorian. This is not a period house. It is this house, at this date.

## What Came Back

Almost none of the original contents survived the century, and most of what visitors see is replica or period substitute. The piano in the parlour, for instance, belonged not to the Carrs but to their neighbours. A handful of genuine pieces did find their way home: the Carr family Bible, recovered and restored, and some of the original hardware from the doors and windows. Small things, and worth more than their size suggests. The house today is owned by the Province of British Columbia and run as a cultural centre, with a site manager, a Victorian-style garden, seasonal tours by appointment through the summer and rotating exhibits and events the rest of the year. Its programming follows the threads of Carr's own life and preoccupations: art and environmentalism, respect for First Nations communities, feminism, and the mentorship of emerging artists and writers, along with workshops in heritage preservation and gardening.

## Flight Context

Emily Carr House stands at 48.4138 N, 123.37 W at 207 Government Street in the James Bay district of Victoria, British Columbia, six blocks south of the Inner Harbour. Best viewing altitude is 1,000 to 2,000 feet over the city, though the building itself is a modest two-storey house and is best identified by its surroundings rather than from the air. Key visual landmarks: the enclosed basin of Victoria's Inner Harbour with the Parliament Buildings and the Empress Hotel on its south and east sides, the Ogden Point breakwater to the southwest, Beacon Hill Park immediately southeast, and the Strait of Juan de Fuca opening south toward the Olympic Mountains. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is directly adjacent with heavy scheduled floatplane traffic, and Victoria International (CYYJ) lies about 13 nm north. This is congested, controlled airspace close to the international border. Expect morning fog off the strait and frequent low cloud in winter.
