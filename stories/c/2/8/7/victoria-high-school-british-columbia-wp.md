# Victoria High School (British Columbia)

The oldest public high school west of Winnipeg, where a 1914 building hides a suspended running track, an attic rifle range signed by generations of graduates, and a four-storey memorial flag that spent decades lost in the basement.

**Location:** 48.4290, -123.3470

**Categories:** High schools in Victoria, British Columbia, Educational institutions established in 1876, Heritage buildings in British Columbia, War memorials

## Introduction

The flag was four storeys tall, and for roughly sixty years nobody knew where it had gone. It had been commissioned after the Second World War and covered in maple leaves — blue ones for each student who had died, red for each member of staff — and then it disappeared into the workings of a very large old building. In 2005 somebody found it in the basement of Victoria High School. It now comes out for Remembrance Day, unrolled from the fourth floor down the face of the building, which is exactly the sort of thing that happens at a school old enough and strange enough to lose a flag that size in the first place.

## A Log Cabin on the School Reserve

Vic High opened on August 7, 1876, in a two-room log cabin on the school reserve between Yates and Fort Streets — the same cabin that had served since 1853 as the first public school in British Columbia. That makes it the oldest public high school west of Winnipeg and north of San Francisco. In 1882 it moved into a wing of a brick building and spent the next twenty-one years wedged between the primary girls' school in the east wing and the primary boys' school in the west, eighty high school students pressed in the middle. Principal Edward Paul described the arrangement, with some restraint, as one of the most inadequate school buildings in the province. A third Victoria High School opened in 1902 — designed by Francis Mawson Rattenbury, the architect of the Parliament Buildings and the Empress Hotel — and Victoria's booming population outgrew that one too. It came down in 1953.

## The Building That Stuck

The fourth Vic High opened on May 1, 1914, on three and a half acres donated by the City of Victoria on Grant Street. C. Elwood Watkins, architect to the Victoria Board of Education, designed it; it cost slightly over $460,000; S. J. Willis was its first principal there. The foundations are blocks of granite four feet square, which is the kind of detail that explains why the building is still standing. It has two ornate facades and enormous windows looking over downtown and the Fernwood neighbourhood, and it is laid out in a U with a large auditorium filling the middle. Later additions sealed the gaps between auditorium and wings, creating courtyards that nobody can now get into. One of the two gymnasiums dates from 1914 and carries a wooden running track suspended twenty feet above the floor. There is a rifle range in the attic, where graduating students have long signed their names, and three full basement levels beneath everything. The building has been on the City of Victoria's Heritage Registry since 1982.

## Two Memorials and a Lost Flag

Two war memorials stand in the main entrance, one for each world war, and a school this old sent a great many people to both. The names on those plaques were teenagers not long before they were casualties — the same students who had run laps on the suspended track and signed the attic wall on their way out. The great flag commissioned after 1945 made that arithmetic visible in a way a plaque cannot: a leaf for each life, sized so the whole school could take in the total at once. Losing it for six decades was an accident of storage, not of memory. Hanging it down four floors of granite and glass each November is a considerably more eloquent gesture than a moment of silence.

## Fairey Tech

In 1943 the school opened technical shops as part of the wartime training effort, and the buildings west of the playfield were put up by trainee soldiers with help from Vic High students — carpentry, bricklaying and metalwork taught by doing the work on the premises. In September 1949 new industrial arts facilities came into use on the south end of the wartime structure: electrical, automotive, sheet metal, welding and woodwork shops, plus classrooms and drafting rooms. The complex was named for Colonel F. T. Fairey, former deputy minister of education and then provincial director of industrial and technical education. Fairey Tech became the centre of trades education for Victoria and well beyond it, running day classes for students and evening classes for adults. The 1950s brought a much larger auto shop and an electronics wing, and over the years the roster expanded to industrial design, art metal and jewellery, and even dance. The old facility closed in 2011 and was replaced by a 57,000-square-foot addition on the school's north side.

## Who Walked Out the Front Door

In 1903 Victoria College began life as an annex to the high school, spending 1914-15 on the top floor of the new Grant Street building before suspending operations when the University of British Columbia opened in 1915. It reopened in 1920 at Craigdarroch Castle and eventually became the University of Victoria. The alumni list is improbable for one school in a mid-sized island city: the painters Emily Carr and Jack Shadbolt, the Kwakwaka’wakw artist Richard Hunt, the architect Samuel Maclure, the journalist and suffragist Lily Laverock, the medical missionary Victoria Chung, and two premiers of British Columbia in Simon Fraser Tolmie and Byron Ingemar Johnson. Beginning in August 2020 the 1914 building went through a $77.1 million seismic upgrade and expansion, with students decanted to a renovated S. J. Willis — named, fittingly, for the man who ran the place when it was new. They came back to Grant Street on April 8, 2024.

## Flight Context

Victoria High School stands at 48.43N, 123.35W in the Fernwood neighbourhood of Victoria, British Columbia, on Grant Street between Fernwood and Camosun. From the air the 1914 building is a large U-shaped masonry block with a filled centre, distinctly bigger and more formal than the surrounding streets of small wooden houses, set against an open playfield on its south side. Use downtown Victoria and the Inner Harbour about 1.5nm southwest as your fix, with Beacon Hill Park on the waterfront beyond, and the green expanse of Royal Athletic Park just west of the school. Nearest airports: Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) 1.5nm southwest with continuous scheduled floatplane traffic, Victoria International (CYYJ) about 12nm north, and Victoria Harbour Heliport on the harbour's north shore. Stay at or above 2,000 feet over the city core. Winter afternoons give the best contrast on the granite facades, which face roughly north and south.
