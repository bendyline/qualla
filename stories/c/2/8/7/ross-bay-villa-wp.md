# Ross Bay Villa

One of roughly ten Victoria houses surviving from before 1870, this 1865 Gothic Revival cottage was hours from demolition in 1998 and has since been rebuilt down to its wallpaper, using the auction list from 1879.

**Location:** 48.4119, -123.3417

## Introduction

In 1869, the retired governor Sir James Douglas wrote to his daughter about a place he had passed on the Fairfield road: a pretty cottage, half-buried in roses and honeysuckle. The house was four years old then, sitting alone five kilometres outside Victoria's town limits at the end of a dirt trail, surrounded by bush, farmland, and swamp. A hundred and twenty-nine years later, a developer applied to knock it down and put townhouses on the lot. That the cottage is still standing -- and that roses and honeysuckle are once again being trained up its walls, deliberately, because of Douglas's sentence -- is the result of one of the more obsessive restoration projects on the Canadian west coast.

## Five Kilometres Out of Town

The 1.9-acre parcel belonged to Charles Buxton, an English philanthropist and Member of Parliament who never saw it. The one-storey cottage that went up on it in 1865 was rural in every sense: the nearest neighbours were Isabella Ross, the first woman to own property in the colony, and Robert Burnaby, and the route to them was a track through the Fairfield swamps. The design is almost certainly the work of John Wright, at the time the only full-time professional architect practising in Victoria and later regarded as the founding figure of British Columbia's architectural establishment. Wright specialised in Gothic Revival, and the details are all his idiom -- a porch on turned columns, a trefoil in the gable, finials on four peaks, chamfered window surrounds. Nobody called it a villa until 1882 or 1883. Before that it was simply the house on the Fairfield road.

## Small From the Outside

The cottage looks modest from the street and is not. Roughly 1,650 square feet sit on a single floor, and the main rooms carry twelve-foot ceilings that make the interior feel like a house twice its footprint; only the original rear kitchen annex is genuinely cramped. The building is full of the small confident moves of a good architect working in wood: a split-flue chimney, an angled front bay under a hipped roof, an unusual double-ridged roof, six-over-six windows, a recessed porch with sidelights and transom around the original front door, all of it once finished in faux oak graining. Inside there is a cast-iron fireplace with a marbleized mantle and a plaster ceiling medallion. It is a colonial cottage pretending, quite successfully, to be something grander.

## The Families Who Lived Here

Francis James Roscoe and his wife Anna Letitia Le Breton raised five children in these rooms. Roscoe, a grandson of the Liverpool Unitarian reformer and MP William Roscoe, had come to Victoria in 1862 and partnered with Arthur Fellows in a hardware business outfitting miners headed for the Cariboo goldfields. He was elected Liberal MP for Victoria in 1874, served one term, and did not stand again; his seat went to Sir John A. Macdonald. In December 1878, reportedly in the grip of a long depression, Roscoe took his own life. He was forty-eight. His widow sold the household contents at auction and took the children home to England. George Winter, coachman to the colonial governor and several lieutenant-governors, rented and then bought the house; his family lived here from 1892 to 1911. Harvey and Margaret Combe rented it earlier, and Harvey Combe is credited with founding the Victoria Golf Club in 1893, Canada's oldest. The stonemason John Mortimer lived here too, and his mark is on monuments across the cemetery opposite.

## Rebuilt From an Auction List

The acreage was cut into fourteen lots in 1912, with the house awkwardly straddling two of them. By the late 1980s it needed everything, and a 1988 purchase for redevelopment simply left it rotting. Heritage registration in 1992 did not stop a 1998 demolition proposal; what stopped it was advocates persuading The Land Conservancy of British Columbia to buy the place outright. On 2 January 2000 volunteers walked in with shovels. They replaced every decaying foundation post, put on a new roof, wired and plumbed the building, and gave it under-floor heating it had never had. Then the fine work began: samples of every wallpaper in the house were analyzed, more than a hundred patterns identified, and the dining-room and drawing-room originals reprinted on vintage machines. Paint colours were matched on all the woodwork. The 1879 auction catalogue of Mrs. Roscoe's belongings became the shopping list for refurnishing. The Ross Bay Villa Society, incorporated in 2013, bought the property in October 2015, the year the house turned 150.

## Flight Context

Ross Bay Villa stands at 48.412N, 123.342W on Fairfield Road in Victoria, roughly 300 metres inland from the shoreline of Ross Bay. The navigational landmark is the cemetery: Ross Bay Cemetery's large green rectangle sits directly across the road from the house, hard against the water, and is unmistakable from the air. Dallas Road and the open Strait of Juan de Fuca run along the south edge, with the Olympic Mountains beyond; Beacon Hill Park lies about 1 km west and the Inner Harbour about 2.5 km northwest. The villa itself is a single small pale roof in a dense residential street -- use the cemetery to find it. Best viewed at 1,000-1,500 feet. Nearest airports: Victoria Inner Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) about 2nm northwest, Victoria International (CYYJ) about 14nm north, and Port Angeles (KCLM) about 22nm south. Winter southeasterlies come straight in off the strait along this shoreline.
