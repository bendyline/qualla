# Abkhazi Garden

An exiled Georgian prince and a woman who survived a Japanese internment camp met again after twenty-four years, married, and spent the rest of their lives building one acre of garden in Victoria.

**Location:** 48.4162, -123.3266

**Categories:** Gardens, British Columbia, Victoria, Heritage Sites, Human Stories

## Introduction

They had last seen each other in 1933. By the time they met again - New York, November 1946 - Nicholas Abkhazi had fought with the French army and spent much of the war in a German prison camp, and Peggy Pemberton-Carter had spent 1943 to 1945 interned by the Japanese in Shanghai. Neither had any particular reason to expect the other to still be alive. They married almost immediately, moved to Victoria, and bought an acre of rocky, awkward ground on Fairfield Road that nobody else wanted to build on. Then they started planting. The result is known locally as the garden that love built, which sounds like tourist-brochure sentiment until you learn what the two of them had come through to start it.

## Paris, 1922

Nicholas Abkhazi was the last surviving son of an old Abkhazian line from the Black Sea coast, living in France in exile after the Bolshevik Revolution drove his family out. Peggy Pemberton-Carter, born Marjorie Mable Jane Carter, was passing through. They met in Paris in 1922 and then spent the next two decades mostly apart - letters, occasional visits, a friendship conducted across continents and interrupted first by distance and then by a war that swallowed both of them separately. Her internment in Shanghai and his captivity in Germany ran on parallel tracks, neither knowing much about the other. When the war ended, they were middle-aged, displaced, and had not been in the same room for thirteen years. They decided anyway.

## Building on Bedrock

The property they chose in Victoria was not promising. Glacier-scoured rock breaks through the soil across much of the acre, leaving outcrops too solid to dig and pockets of ground too shallow to plant conventionally. Garry oaks, the gnarled and slow-growing signature tree of this corner of the island, already stood on the site. Rather than fight the geology, the Abkhazis designed around it. They set paths of crushed gravel and stone to follow the contours, planted into the pockets the rock allowed, and let three pools collect in the low ground - pools that became a reliable summer address for a pair of wild mallards and their ducklings. The garden's structure is essentially the site's own structure, revealed rather than imposed.

## Rhododendrons the Size of Trees

Seventy-five years of patient cultivation produces effects that cannot be bought. The rhododendrons the Abkhazis planted have grown into genuine trees, tall enough to walk beneath, and in spring they throw a canopy of colour over the whole acre - different varieties flowering in sequence in different shades. Beneath them, in the deep shade those rhododendrons now cast, grows the second garden: ferns, small flowering perennials, and the shade-tolerant understory plants that only work under mature cover. A visitor walks through what is essentially a layered forest built by hand on bare rock by two people with nothing but time and a very long view. At the centre sits a tea house and gift shop, which is where most people end up sitting down to think about it.

## The Fight to Keep an Acre

Small urban gardens are fragile in a way that parks are not, because the land underneath them is worth a great deal to somebody. The Land Conservancy of British Columbia holds Abkhazi Garden, and the property has spent years inside a complicated argument about zoning, heritage designation, and money. When the City of Victoria moved to impose heritage protection, the Conservancy resisted - it was restructuring at the time and needed to sell some assets to stay solvent. It eventually sold a heritage-designated parking area on Foul Bay Road and was out of creditor protection by 2017. In March 2022 a new proposal took a different route, offering to shift the property's development rights to another site nearby in exchange for formal recognition of the garden. Admission today is a suggested ten-dollar donation. One acre, two lifetimes, and an ongoing negotiation about whether that is worth more than the lot it stands on.

## Flight Context

Abkhazi Garden is at 48.416 N, 123.327 W in the Fairfield neighbourhood of Victoria, British Columbia, roughly 2 km east of the Inner Harbour. At one acre it is far too small to identify from altitude; treat it as a ground destination rather than an aerial landmark and use the surrounding geography to place it. A viewing altitude of 1,500-2,500 feet MSL over Victoria shows the useful references: the Inner Harbour and Legislature to the west, Beacon Hill Park as a large green rectangle on the Dallas Road waterfront to the southwest, and Oak Bay and Foul Bay opening east. The garden sits in the residential grid between Beacon Hill and Oak Bay. Victoria Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is about 2 nm west with constant floatplane traffic, and Victoria International (CYYJ) lies 12 nm north at Patricia Bay - expect controlled airspace and heavy circuit activity around both. Victoria's climate is the mildest in Canada, and the garden's rhododendron display peaks in spring.
