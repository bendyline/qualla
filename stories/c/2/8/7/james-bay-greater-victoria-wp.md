# James Bay, Greater Victoria

Victoria's oldest residential quarter, built on a Lekwungen village site, a filled-in tidal bay, a farm, and the ambitions of a twenty-five-year-old architect.

**Location:** 48.4190, -123.3820

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in Victoria, British Columbia

## Introduction

There is no James Bay in James Bay. The shallow tidal inlet that gave this Victoria neighbourhood its name was filled in more than a century ago, and the grand hotel that stands on the fill has been there so long that visitors assume the ground was always dry. Everything about the place works this way - a layer over a layer over a layer. Fort Victoria's farmland became a suburb; a suburb became a government district; the government district acquired apartment towers; the towers provoked a fight. James Bay is the oldest residential neighbourhood on the west coast of North America north of San Francisco, and it has been revised more times than most cities have existed.

## The People Who Were Here

Before any of it, this was the ground of the Swenghwung, part of the Lekwungen people of the Coast Salish, whose descendants today are the Songhees First Nation. Even after the land was recorded as sold to the Hudson's Bay Company, the physical evidence of that older occupation stayed in place: remains of fortifications at Holland Point, burial grounds at Laurel Point. These are not abstractions on a heritage plaque. They are the places where people defended themselves and buried their dead, on a peninsula that a fur-trading company subsequently platted into building lots. Fort Victoria went up in 1843, and the farmland behind it passed through a sequence of names - Ogden's Fields Farms, then Dutnall's Farm, then Beckley Farm - before it became streets.

## Birdcages, and a Twenty-Five-Year-Old

In 1859 Governor James Douglas, for whom the bay itself was named, decided the colony needed administrative offices. What went up was a two-storey wooden legislative building surrounded by a Land Office, a Colonial Office, a Supreme Court and a Treasury, and Victoria promptly nicknamed the whole fanciful cluster the Birdcages. The legislature met in them from 1860 to 1898. Their replacement arrived by competition, and the competition was won by Francis Mawson Rattenbury, a recent English immigrant of twenty-five who submitted his drawings under the pseudonym "A B.C. Architect" and later "For Queen and Province." Construction ran from 1893 to 1897; the Parliament Buildings opened for business in 1898 and still house the Legislative Assembly of British Columbia. The old Birdcages burned in 1957.

## Every Size of House

Between the Birdcages and the Parliament Buildings, James Bay filled in with homes at every scale. The Victorian Italianate house where Emily Carr grew up, built in 1863, still stands on Government Street - which was Carr Street then - among blocks of modest wooden houses from the later decades of the century. The family home of James and Amelia Douglas stood where the Royal British Columbia Museum is now; behind it sits Helmcken House, built for John Sebastian Helmcken, the colony's first doctor, speaker of the Assembly, and the governor's son-in-law. The grand end of the market produced the Queen Anne-style Pendray residence of 1897 on Belleville Street, built for William and Amelia Pendray on a Cariboo Gold Rush fortune later converted into a soap factory, and Pinehurst on Battery Street, from 1890. Armadale, built in 1877 on 28 acres, was the largest estate of all; demolished in 1944, part of its grounds is now Macdonald Park.

## Filling the Bay

The tidal flats were the problem and the opportunity. Starting in the summer of 1901 a stone causeway replaced the old wooden bridge across them, and the enclosed water behind it could then be filled. It began as a transportation and sanitation project. From 1903 the Canadian Pacific Railway negotiated to acquire the reclaimed land and the properties around it, and in 1908 the Empress Hotel opened on ground that had been under salt water within living memory. The waterfront elsewhere was working, not decorative: Sehl's furniture factory and Pendray's soap works at Laurel Point, Bapco Paint later, Laing's shipyard in the 1860s where Fisherman's Wharf now floats, a flour mill, a chemical plant, warehouses. Commercial wharves appeared around 1883 at Ogden Point and Shoal Point. The Canadian Coast Guard built its present base in 1975.

## Towers and the Fight They Started

South Park Elementary School went up in 1914, and with it the building boom largely stopped for half a century. When development returned in the 1960s it arrived with wrecking equipment, clearing older houses for apartment blocks - Orchard House on Michigan Street reaching twenty storeys, still the tallest building in Greater Victoria. The prospect of losing more of the nineteenth-century fabric produced a grassroots heritage preservation movement, and the neighbourhood that stands today is the negotiated result. One last local peculiarity: surrounded by water on three sides, James Bay runs 3 to 5 degrees Celsius cooler than inland Victoria in summer and a few degrees warmer in winter, which means it gets noticeably less snow than the rest of the city. Victoria's Mediterranean climate delivers mild rainy winters and cool dry summers; James Bay gets a slightly gentler version of both.

## Flight Context

James Bay occupies the peninsula south of Victoria's Inner Harbour at 48.419°N, 123.382°W. From 1,500-2,500 ft the layout is textbook: the Inner Harbour cuts in from the northwest, the domed Parliament Buildings and the broad Chateauesque block of the Empress face each other across the causeway, and Dallas Road runs the exposed southern shore with Beacon Hill Park's open green immediately inland. Ogden Point's breakwater and cruise terminal mark the southwest corner; Clover, Finlayson, Holland and Victoria Points step along the ocean side. The Olympic Mountains stand across the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the south. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) sits directly in the harbour and is busy with scheduled floatplane traffic to Vancouver and Seattle, so expect low seaplane movements and check the harbour procedures; Victoria International (CYYJ) lies about 22 km north-northwest in North Saanich. Summer mornings often bring marine stratus off the strait that burns back by midday.
