# Craigdarroch Castle

A coal baron built Victoria's most extravagant house and died seventeen months before it was finished; within thirty years it had been raffled off, mortgaged into ruin, and handed to a bank.

**Location:** 48.4226, -123.3438

**Categories:** Castles in Canada, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Historic house museums in British Columbia, History of Victoria, British Columbia, Scottish baronial architecture

## Introduction

Robert Dunsmuir never slept a night in the house that defines him. He died on 12 April 1889, seventeen months before the last workmen finished the Scottish baronial pile he had commissioned above Victoria -- a tower, thirty-seven rooms, and enough imported hardwood to floor a small town, all of it paid for with coal money and none of it enjoyed by the man who ordered it. His sons Alexander and James took over the job of completing their father's monument. James, who evidently found one castle insufficient, later commissioned a second one at Colwood. Craigdarroch was finished in 1890 and stood on twenty-eight acres of Garry oak meadow. It was the largest, most expensive private statement anyone on Vancouver Island had made, and the family's grip on it lasted barely a generation.

## Shipped In and Bolted Together

For all its Old World posturing, Craigdarroch was a thoroughly industrial building, assembled from parts ordered across a continent. Stairs, doors, window frames, and 2,128 wall panels were manufactured by the A.H. Andrews Company in Chicago and shipped west. The window shutters came from the Willer Blind Company in Milwaukee. The stained and leaded glass -- still one of the finest Victorian collections in Canada -- is attributed to the Pacific Art Glass Works of San Francisco, and the encaustic tile floor was made by Minton, Hollins & Co. in England. The woods inside read like a manifest from the whole Pacific trade: white oak in the main hall and staircase, Spanish mahogany in the library, western red cedar in the porte-cochere, cherry in the breakfast room, Hawaiian koa on the drawing room floor, American ash on the back stairs, and parquet inlaid with ebony, rosewood, walnut, maple, holly, and teak.

## Thirty-Seven Rooms and Eighty-Seven Steps

The finished house runs to roughly 25,000 square feet, with thirty-seven rooms, seventeen fireplaces, and a tower reached by eighty-seven steps. What it cost is genuinely unknown -- estimates range from $185,000 to $500,000 including land and grounds, a spread so wide it suggests nobody was keeping careful books. The architects were Warren Heywood Williams and Arthur Lorenzo Smith of Portland, Oregon, but their original drawings have not survived, and exactly one interior photograph is known from the years the Dunsmuirs actually lived there. Historians reconstructing the house have had to read the building itself. The name is Gaelic, meaning roughly rocky, oak place -- a fair description of the Rockland neighbourhood's Garry oak meadows, though only about 1.75 acres of the original estate remain with the building today.

## The House That Was Raffled

When Robert's widow Joan died, the estate went to a land speculator named Griffith Hughes for $38,000, and the twenty-eight acres were carved into building lots. The market was slow. To move the lots, Hughes hit on a promotion of magnificent audacity: the castle itself would be the prize in a raffle, open to anyone who bought a parcel. Solomon Cameron won it. Rather than live quietly in a mansion he had acquired for the price of a residential lot, Cameron mortgaged the place to fund other speculative ventures, which failed. In 1919 the house passed to one of his creditors, the Bank of Montreal. In thirty years the grandest residence on the island had gone from coal baron to widow to speculator to raffle winner to bank -- a compressed history of British Columbia's boom economy told through a single address.

## Five Layers of Paint

The building spent most of the twentieth century being useful rather than grand. It served as a military hospital, then as Victoria College, then as offices for the school board -- the drawing room alone worked as a recreation space for patients, a classroom, and an office. In 1935, when the college moved in, the hand-painted drawing room ceiling was simply rolled over in white. It stayed buried until conservators worked between 1995 and 2007 to strip five layers of paint and recover the stencilled and hand-painted designs underneath, using a surviving fragment in the southeast corner as the template for the rest. It is believed to be the only decoratively painted ceiling in the house. The drawing room's woman-with-a-swan window, a replica after Frederic Leighton's 1862 painting Odalisque, stands in for an original broken in 1927. The castle has been a museum since 1979.

## Flight Context

Craigdarroch Castle stands at 48.423N, 123.344W in Victoria's Rockland neighbourhood, about 1.5 km east of the Inner Harbour. From the air it is easy to pick out: a dark stone mass with a distinctive square tower and steep slate roofline, sitting higher than everything around it on a wooded knoll among large heritage lots and Garry oaks. The Inner Harbour, the Parliament Buildings, and the Empress Hotel lie west; Beacon Hill Park is a short distance southwest; the Juan de Fuca Strait and the Olympic Mountains fill the southern horizon. Best viewed at 1,000-2,000 feet, which keeps the tower profile legible against the neighbourhood canopy. Nearest airports: Victoria Inner Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) about 1.5nm west, Victoria International (CYYJ) about 13nm north, and Port Angeles (KCLM) about 25nm south across the strait. Victoria is one of the driest spots on the coast, but morning fog off the strait is common in autumn.
