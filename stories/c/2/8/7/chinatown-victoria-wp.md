# Chinatown, Victoria

Canada's oldest Chinatown, built by gold-rush migrants from Guangdong, shrunk by decades of exclusion law, and now a National Historic Site holding the narrowest street in the country.

**Location:** 48.4294, -123.3680

**Categories:** Chinatowns in Canada, Chinese-Canadian culture in British Columbia, Ethnic enclaves in British Columbia, Historic districts in Canada, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Neighbourhoods in Victoria, British Columbia

## Introduction

At one point there were fourteen opium factories operating within a few blocks of Fisgard Street, and every one of them was legal. They sat in wooden buildings tucked behind the brick storefronts, run by merchants with names still known here — Tai Soong, Kwong Lee, Shon Yuen — and they made Victoria one of the opium capitals of the world. Ottawa and the city both took their cut in licence fees and taxes right up until 1908, when the trade was suddenly criminalized and the same neighbourhood that had been paying for it was blamed for it. That double standard is a useful way into Canada's oldest Chinatown: almost nothing about its reputation was written by the people who lived here.

## 1858, and the Boats Kept Coming

Gold was found in the Fraser Canyon in 1858, and the rush that followed emptied a good part of California northward. Roughly a third of the miners who came up were Chinese, and within a year ships were sailing directly from China as the news travelled. Gold was only part of it. Famine, drought, and war in the home provinces pushed people across the Pacific as hard as opportunity pulled them, and the overwhelming majority came from Guangdong in the south. Victoria, on the Crown Colony of Vancouver Island, was the gateway — the place where you landed before heading for the Fraser, and later Barkerville and the other goldfields. When the Canadian Pacific Railway began recruiting, more came. What started as a scatter of rough wooden huts at the north end of town became, within a generation, a dense working neighbourhood with schools, churches, temples, theatres, and a hospital.

## Six Blocks, 3,158 People

Chinatown peaked in 1911. It filled about six city blocks — two of Herald Street, two of Fisgard, two of what was then Cormorant Street — and housed 3,158 residents, making it the largest Chinese settlement in Canada from the late nineteenth century through the first decade of the twentieth. The Royal British Columbia Museum describes it as a maze of alleyways and interior courtyards, a neighbourhood that faced inward because the city outside was not welcoming. The street grid itself has since been rewritten over top of it: the block of Cormorant between Store and Government is now Pandora Avenue, and the stretch between Government and Douglas was absorbed into Centennial Square. You can stand in a public plaza in downtown Victoria and be standing in a place that had a different name and a different population within living memory.

## Three Feet Wide

Fan Tan Alley runs 240 feet from Fisgard Street through to Pandora, between three and six feet wide, pinching to under 90 centimetres at its narrowest — the narrowest street in Canada, and the narrowest commercial street in North America. It began as a private walkway, not a street at all, and it was the spine of the interior world that the alleys and courtyards created: gambling houses, the opium works out of sight behind the shopfronts, and the ordinary business of people living close together. Outsiders wrote about it as sinister. What it actually was, for most of the people using it, was a shortcut home. Today it holds record shops, a barber, studios, and a slow single-file crowd of visitors turning sideways to let each other pass.

## The Law Arrives

In 1885 Canada began charging a head tax of $50 on every Chinese person entering the country. It rose to $100 in 1900 and to $500 in 1903 — roughly two years' wages for the labourers it targeted. About 81,000 people paid it, and the money went to the government. Then in 1923 Parliament passed the Chinese Immigration Act, which simply stated that no person of Chinese origin or descent would be permitted to enter Canada. It took effect on 1 July, Dominion Day, and Chinese Canadians marked that date for decades afterward as Humiliation Day. The act stood until 1947. Victoria's Chinatown shrank steadily and dramatically from the 1920s through the 1970s, losing both area and population, and it is worth being plain about why: a community that cannot bring in wives, children, or new arrivals for a quarter of a century does not decline by accident.

## The Gate and What Sits Behind It

Revitalization came in the 1980s, and its emblem is the Gate of Harmonious Interest at Fisgard and Government — a red and gold paifang whose stone lions were carved in Suzhou, one of Victoria's sister cities. The district was designated a National Historic Site of Canada in 1995 and has been discussed as a candidate for World Heritage listing. Coffee shops, galleries, workshops, and a small condominium development in Dragon Alley have filled in around the older Chinese businesses and the historic school building. But the most significant thing here is still not on the tourist route. The Tam Kung Temple, the oldest Chinese temple of its kind in Canada, sits out of public view above street level, as it has since long before anyone thought to build a gate.

## Flight Context

Victoria's Chinatown centres on 48.429°N, 123.368°W, at the north end of downtown Victoria on Vancouver Island, focused on the 500 and 600 blocks of Fisgard Street. Best viewed from 1,000 to 2,500 feet, with the caveat that this is dense controlled airspace over a provincial capital — expect to be talking to someone. From the air the district is a compact grid of low brick buildings four blocks north of the Inner Harbour, bounded roughly by Store Street on the west and Douglas on the east; look for the green copper dome of the Parliament Buildings and the dark bulk of the Empress Hotel on the Inner Harbour's south side to orient, then count north past Centennial Square. The harbour itself is the dominant landmark, a hooked inlet cutting east off Juan de Fuca into the heart of the city. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is a few blocks southwest and one of the busiest floatplane facilities in Canada — traffic is constant and low. Victoria International (CYYJ) lies about 12 nautical miles north on the Saanich Peninsula, and William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) at Port Angeles sits roughly 20 nautical miles south across the strait. Expect summer marine fog burning off by mid-morning, excellent winter visibility behind cold fronts, and the Olympic Mountains filling the southern horizon.
