# De Courcy Island

A 460-acre Gulf Island reachable only by boat or floatplane, best known as the colony where a self-declared prophet called Brother XII took money from thousands of followers and vanished with it in gold.

**Location:** 49.1006, -123.7414

**Categories:** Islands of the Gulf Islands, Regional District of Nanaimo, Cults and communes, Provincial parks of British Columbia

## Introduction

Somewhere on or near a 460-acre island southeast of Nanaimo, according to a story that has refused to die for the better part of a century, there are glass jars of gold coins buried in the ground. The man who put them there called himself Brother XII. He had collected the money from thousands of people across North America who believed he was speaking for a higher order, converted it quietly into coin, sealed it into mason jars, and then — when the lawsuits came — disappeared, leaving the colony wrecked behind him. Treasure hunters still come to De Courcy Island. What they are actually digging for is other people's savings.

## Reachable Only on the Tide

De Courcy lies between Pylades and Stuart Channels, about sixteen kilometres southeast of Nanaimo and thirty-eight west of Vancouver, and it lends its name to a small chain running north to south: Mudge, Link, De Courcy, Ruxton and Pylades. The group runs parallel to Vancouver Island's east coast, tucked inside the larger bulk of Gabriola and Valdes, which take the weather off the Strait of Georgia and leave these channels comparatively calm. Getting in is the interesting part. From Nanaimo you pass through Dodd Narrows; from Vancouver, through Gabriola Passage. Both are tidal gates with ferocious currents, and both are run on slack water by anyone with sense. There is no ferry and no bridge — boat or floatplane only, with Gulf Island Seaplanes serving the island daily. The name honours Vice Admiral Michael de Courcy, captain of HMS Pylades, which is also why the channel and the southernmost island in the chain are called what they are.

## The Twelfth Brother

Edward Arthur Wilson was born in Birmingham, England, on July 25, 1878, to parents in the Catholic Apostolic Church. He went to sea and worked as a ship's captain, abandoned his family in 1912, and in his travels took up theosophy — eventually announcing that he was the channel for the Twelfth Brother of the Great White Lodge, a member of a hidden spiritual hierarchy guiding humanity. He wrote manifestos. People read them. In the spring of 1927 he arrived in British Columbia and set up the Aquarian Foundation at Cedar-by-the-Sea, seven miles south of Nanaimo, and within a year more than eight thousand supporters across North America were sending him money. Some sent everything they had. He bought land, then expanded onto De Courcy and Valdes islands, using roughly $25,000 from a wealthy follower named Mary Connally.

## What the Colony Was Actually Like

The people who came to the islands were not fools in any way that matters. They were seekers in a decade full of them, and many were educated, capable and idealistic — which is precisely the profile that makes a movement work. What they found on De Courcy was labour. Accounts from former members describe long days of clearing and building under discipline that grew harsher over time, much of it administered by Wilson's companion Mabel Skottowe, who went by Madame Zee. Meanwhile the Foundation's money was going somewhere. By 1929 followers had worked out that Wilson was embezzling it. He had been converting donations into gold coins and sealing them into glass jars, a form of wealth that does not appear on a bank statement and can be carried away in a boat.

## The Lawsuit and the Vanishing

In 1932 Mary Connally sued to get her donation back. The proceedings that followed have acquired a distinctly odd reputation — accounts describe attorneys fainting and the judge growling like a dog — but whatever happened in that courtroom, the outcome on the islands was decisive. Wilson and Madame Zee left British Columbia that year, and on the way out much of the compound was destroyed: buildings wrecked, machinery smashed, the physical evidence of five years reduced to debris. The gold went with them. A death certificate for Edward Arthur Wilson exists, dated November 7, 1934, and historians have long suspected he arranged it himself; he was reportedly recognized alive two years later. Either way, the followers got nothing back. The jars people still hunt for on these shores are the fragments the story insists he could not carry.

## Pirates Cove

The island today is quiet in the way these small Gulf Islands are quiet — a scatter of private properties, no services, no road off. Its public face is Pirates Cove Marine Provincial Park, a sheltered anchorage on the northeast side with trails, campsites and stern-tie rings set into the rock. Boaters come in on the tide, tuck into the cove, and walk the point in the evening. The name was applied to the cove for its own reasons, and it long predates any of this, but it has become one of the better accidental jokes on the British Columbia coast. There really was a man here who took a fortune off trusting people and sailed away with it in glass jars.

## Flight Context

De Courcy Island lies at 49.10N, 123.74W in the northern Gulf Islands, British Columbia, between Pylades Channel to the east and Stuart Channel to the west, about 9nm southeast of Nanaimo. From the air, identify the north-south chain running inside the larger bulk of Valdes and Gabriola Islands: Mudge, Link, De Courcy, Ruxton, then Pylades. De Courcy is the long narrow one in the middle, roughly 460 acres, with the notch of Pirates Cove cut into its northeast shore. Dodd Narrows — a sharp constriction between Mudge Island and Vancouver Island — is unmistakable from above, often showing standing waves and tide rips on a big exchange, as does Gabriola Passage to the east. Nearest airports: Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) about 5nm west, Nanaimo Harbour Water Aerodrome (CAC8) 9nm northwest, and Vancouver International (CYVR) 22nm east across the strait. Floatplane traffic serves the island daily; overfly at 1,500 to 3,000 feet and watch for aircraft in the water circuit at Pirates Cove.
