# Matia Island

A Civil War veteran came to Matia in 1892 to flip it for a profit and stayed twenty-nine years; today 143 of its 145 acres are federal wilderness where you may not leave the trail.

**Location:** 48.7468, -122.8377

**Categories:** islands, wilderness-areas, wildlife-refuges, san-juan-islands, pacific-northwest

## Introduction

Elvin H. Smith came to Matia Island in April 1892 to make money. He and a Bellingham lawyer had heard the federal government might open the island for homesteading, so the lawyer put up the cash to buy out a pair of squatters and Smith went out to perfect the claim, hold it briefly, and sell it on at a profit. That was the plan. He was still there twenty-nine years later, known up and down the San Juans as the Hermit of Matia, and he left only because the sea took him. Today the island he meant to sell is 145 acres of national wildlife refuge, almost all of it designated wilderness, where visitors are legally required to stay on a single loop trail.

## Isla de Mata

The Spanish got here first, at least with paper. In 1792 the explorer Francisco de Eliza charted the island and named it Isla de Mata, a phrase that translators have handled in more than one way. It can be read as island of no protection -- a fair warning, given what the Strait of Georgia does to small boats -- and it can also be read as referring to lush plant growth, to thicket and bush. Both readings describe the place accurately, which is either a happy accident or evidence that Eliza chose well. The name eroded over two centuries of English-speaking use into Matia, and the Spanish original survives now only in charts and etymologies.

## The Speculator Who Stayed

Smith was born in Wisconsin around 1835 and fought through the Civil War in the Union Army, rising from private to brevet captain. The Army's bureaucracy never formally recognized the battlefield commission, and the grievance stayed with him. So did an unhappy love affair. He went west carrying both, worked a stretch as a newspaperman, and then spent years as a travelling passenger agent for the Northern Pacific Railroad -- a job of timetables, hotels, and strangers -- until he quit the railroad business in 1890 and settled in Bellingham. Two years later he rowed out to Matia on a land deal. His cabin stood on a bay in the island's southeast corner, opposite Rolfe Cove, and he provisioned himself by boat. On 23 February 1921, a man in his mid-eighties set out from Orcas Island in a rowboat loaded with supplies, and a storm came up. Neither the boat nor Smith was seen again.

## Wilderness, Two Acres Excepted

Matia's legal life began in 1937, when it was set aside as a refuge and breeding ground for migratory birds and other wildlife; in 1940 it became part of what is now the San Juan Islands National Wildlife Refuge. Then came the boats. In the prosperous years after the Second World War, private pleasure craft multiplied across Puget Sound and the San Juans, and uncontrolled landings on Matia became a real problem. Rather than fight it, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the Washington State Parks and Recreation Commission signed a cooperative agreement in June 1959, carving out two acres around Rolfe Cove as a state marine park and campground and leaving the rest alone. In 1976, everything outside those two acres was designated Wilderness under the Wilderness Act of 1964. The compromise has held for well over half a century: a small door, firmly framed.

## Stay on the Trail

A 1.2-mile loop runs through the wilderness portion, and it is the only way in. Every area above the high tide line apart from that trail and the campground is closed, and visitors may not step off the path. What the trail delivers is worth the constraint -- old-growth island forest of a kind that survives in only a handful of places in this region, dense and mossy and dark in a landscape where almost everything else was logged. Pets are prohibited. So is collecting wood, and so is fire of any kind; liquid-fuel camp stoves are the sole exception. The rules read as fussy until you consider what 145 acres of undisturbed nesting habitat is worth in an archipelago this heavily visited.

## Five Developed Acres

The human footprint is deliberately tiny: five acres of the park and 680 feet of waterfront. Boaters find a 116-foot pier, a 45-foot ramp, a moorage float, and two buoys, all first come, first served, with fees charged year-round and a three-night limit on continuous moorage. Ashore there are six primitive campsites, a picnic site, and a composting toilet. There is no drinking water at all, and everything you bring must leave with you. Around the edges run 20,000 feet of rocky, cold saltwater shoreline on the Strait of Georgia, worked over twice daily by a tide that swings fourteen feet. The climate is the mild, moderated Puget Sound pattern -- rarely above 80 degrees Fahrenheit, rarely below 30, with about 29 inches of rain a year, roughly four-fifths of what falls on Seattle. People come for camping, picnicking, fishing, diving, clamming, and a short walk in an old forest. That is the entire offering, and it is enough.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.75 N, 122.84 W in the northern San Juan Islands of Washington State, on the edge of the Strait of Georgia. Matia is a small, densely forested island lying about two miles northeast of the tip of Orcas Island and immediately west of Sucia Island, whose distinctive multi-fingered shape makes the best navigational reference in this group. Rolfe Cove notches the island's northwest end, and the pier there is the only visible structure. The Canadian border runs just to the north. Best viewed at 1,500-3,000 ft AGL; low passes should be avoided, as the island is a national wildlife refuge with nesting seabirds and marine mammal haul-outs. Orcas Island Airport (KORS) is about 4 nm southwest; Bellingham International (KBLI) roughly 12 nm east; Friday Harbor (KFHR) about 12 nm south-southwest. Expect strong westerlies through the strait, tidal rips visible as distinct current lines around the island, and summer morning fog that burns off by midday.
