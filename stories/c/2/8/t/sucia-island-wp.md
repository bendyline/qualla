# Sucia Island

A hand-shaped island north of Orcas whose Spanish name means foul ground, where folded sandstone holds marine fossils and Washington's only dinosaur bone, and whose hidden coves sheltered smugglers for a century before the state made it a marine park.

**Location:** 48.7619, -122.9133

**Categories:** San Juan Islands, State parks of Washington (state), Fossil sites, Maritime history

## Introduction

In 1791 the Spanish captain Francisco de Eliza charted this island and wrote down a word that means dirty. Isla Sucia. He was not describing the scenery, which is superb. In nautical Spanish sucia means foul — a shore not to be approached, littered with reefs and rocks that lie just under the surface waiting for a keel. Two centuries later the warning still holds, and the same geology that makes the approaches treacherous is what makes the island extraordinary: folded and tilted sandstone, eroded into fins and coves and hollowed shelves, with fossils lying exposed in the rock where anyone can find them.

## A Hand Laid on the Water

Sucia sits two and a half miles north of Orcas Island in Washington's San Juans, and is the largest of an archipelago of ten — Little Sucia, Ewing, Justice, Herndon, the Cluster Islands islets and several unnamed rocks. The group runs about two and a half miles long and just under half a mile wide, totalling 680 acres, of which the main island accounts for 558. From above it looks unmistakably like a hand, fingers of rock spread northwest with water between them. Those fingers are the product of crustal folding: layers of sedimentary rock bent, tipped on edge, and then worn back by the sea, which is why the shorelines are so broken and why the southeast arm in particular yields good marine fossils. According to the 2000 census, four people lived here permanently.

## Suciasaurus

In April 2012 research associates from the Burke Museum, Christian Sidor and Brandon Peecook, found a fossilized bone lying in the rock on Sucia's shore: a partial left femur, 16.7 inches long, from a theropod — the bipedal, mostly carnivorous group that includes Tyrannosaurus and, eventually, birds. It dates to the Late Cretaceous, roughly 80 million years old, and it was the first dinosaur fossil ever recorded in Washington State. The blank it filled has a geological explanation rather than a biological one. Washington was underwater 80 million years ago, and the rocks that now make up Sucia Island were then somewhere off present-day Baja California or northern California, carried north over tens of millions of years by the same tectonic machinery that folded them. The single bone was not enough to identify a species. The state adopted it anyway: in 2023 Washington named Suciasaurus rex its official state dinosaur, on the strength of one thigh bone found on a beach.

## Coves Made for Not Being Seen

Geology that punishes navigators rewards anyone who wants to disappear. Sucia's isolated bays served Lummi hunters during the seal-hunting era, and later made the island a fixture of the borderland smuggling economy. Through the nineteenth century, United States exclusion laws barred Chinese labourers from entering the country legally, and a trade grew up moving them across the strait by boat. The people in those hulls had no legal standing and no protection from the men transporting them; Sucia's hidden coves were simply somewhere to wait out a revenue cutter. The same coves handled illegally imported wool and opium. During Prohibition in the 1920s and 1930s the island became a rum-runners' waypoint on the run down from British Columbia, and in recent decades it has turned up in drug-trafficking cases. The cargo keeps changing. The geography does not.

## The Wiggins Family

The first known Euro-American settler here was Charles Henry Wiggins, who came to Sucia in the 1880s with his wife Mary Luzier, a Cowlitz woman. They had been living on Waldron Island. They left it after government agents seized eight of their children and took them to the Chemawa Indian School in Salem, Oregon — one family's share of a policy that removed Indigenous children from their homes across the continent. Whatever privacy Sucia offered, the Wigginses took it. They had five more children on the island and built a working farm with fruit trees, cattle and sheep. Wiggins Head, at the southeast end, still carries the family's name, which is a quiet and inadequate memorial to what it cost them to end up there.

## Seven Buoys and a Shallow Bay

In 1960 the Puget Sound Interclub Association bought the Sucia group and later donated it to Washington State, which protected it as a marine park. The anchorages are the point. Shallow Bay on the west side is sheltered from everything but a westerly, with seven mooring buoys, room for forty or fifty small boats, beaches on three sides and an average depth that lives up to its name — about twelve feet. Echo Bay directly opposite, across the island's narrow isthmus, is the largest anchorage and the most exposed, taking southeasterlies full on. Fossil Bay opens southeast and has two docks, which makes it the favourite for boats that would rather tie up than anchor. Fox Cove lies just west, framed by Ev Henry Finger and Little Sucia. Ewing Cove, tucked between Sucia's northeast end and Ewing Island, sits at the far end of a 2.1-mile trail from the island's centre — the reward for walking rather than motoring.

## Flight Context

Sucia Island lies at 48.76N, 122.91W in the northern San Juan Islands, Washington, about 2.5nm north of Orcas Island and roughly 5nm south of the Canadian border. From the air the island is one of the most distinctive shapes in the archipelago: a hooked, hand-like landmass with long rock fingers reaching northwest and deep bays cut between them — Shallow Bay on the west, Echo Bay on the east, Fossil Bay and Fox Cove on the south. Little Sucia sits off the southwest tip and Ewing Island off the northeast. Use Orcas Island's Mount Constitution (2,398 ft) 6nm south as your primary fix, with Patos and Matia Islands just to the northwest and northeast. Nearest airports: Orcas Island Airport (KORS) about 5nm south, Bellingham International (KBLI) 17nm east, and Friday Harbor (KFHR) 12nm southwest, plus heavy floatplane traffic on the Seattle and Vancouver runs. Best viewed from 1,500 to 3,000 feet at low tide, when the reefs that earned the island its name show clearly as pale shelves under the water.
