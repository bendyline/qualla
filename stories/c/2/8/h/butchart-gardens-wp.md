# Butchart Gardens

Canada's most famous flower garden is a worked-out limestone quarry that a cement manufacturer's wife spent twelve years filling with soil, and the industrial village that made it possible has largely vanished.

**Location:** 48.5653, -123.4694

**Categories:** Gardens in Canada, Greater Victoria, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Quarries in Canada, Japanese gardens in Canada, Immigrant history

## Introduction

In 1909 the limestone ran out. Robert Pim Butchart's quarry at Tod Inlet had fed his cement works for years, and when the deposit was exhausted what remained was a pit of bare grey rock at the base of the Saanich Peninsula, the kind of scar that industrial extraction usually just leaves behind. His wife Jennie decided to fill it with flowers. Soil was hauled in, terraces were shaped out of the quarry walls, and over twelve years the hole became the Sunken Garden, finished in 1921. More than a million people a year now walk down into it, most of them with no idea they are standing on the floor of a limestone quarry.

## Why the Butcharts Came West

Robert Butchart, born in Owen Sound, Ontario, in 1856, had been manufacturing Portland cement since 1888. Cement requires limestone in quantity, and the deposits on Vancouver Island were better than anything he could reach in Ontario, so in 1904 he and Jennie, eleven years his junior, built a house beside the new quarry at Tod Inlet and named it Benvenuto, Italian for welcome. Robert collected ornamental birds with the same enthusiasm his wife brought to plants: a parrot in the house, ducks on the Star Pond, peacocks strolling the front lawn, elaborate birdhouses built around the property, and trained pigeons kept where the Begonia Bower now stands. They were a couple who accumulated living things.

## Beyond the Garden Wall

The gardens exist because a factory did, and the factory ran on workers whose names appear in very few of the postcards. By 1911 the cement plant at Tod Inlet employed 368 people, among them Chinese, Sikh, Indigenous, and European labourers. Forty Sikh men arrived in 1906, believed to have come from the Jullundur district of Punjab. The Chinese and Sikh workers were paid less than their white counterparts and were given no housing, so they built their own settlements near the works, with separate bunkhouses and kitchens. They lived, in every practical sense, outside the wall. When the cement plant closed in 1921, some of the Chinese Canadian men who had worked there went on to work in the gardens. Tod Inlet itself carries an older name: the W̱SÁNEĆ people know this place as SṈIDȻEȽ, and their connection to it long predates the limestone.

## Kishida's Year in Victoria

In 1907, a sixty-five-year-old garden designer named Isaburo Kishida arrived from Yokohama at his son's request, to build a tea garden at Esquimalt Gorge Park. Victoria loved it. Suddenly the city's wealthier households wanted Japanese gardens of their own, and several prominent citizens commissioned Kishida before he returned to Japan in 1912. Jennie Butchart was one of them. The Japanese garden at Benvenuto, with its moss, maples, and water, was the work of a man who spent only five years on this coast and left a disproportionate mark on it. It remains the quietest part of the property and, at dusk, the best.

## Everything Becomes a Garden

Once the pattern was established, the Butcharts kept converting. The tennis courts went in 1926, replaced by an Italian garden. The kitchen vegetable plot went in 1929, replaced by a rose garden designed by Butler Sturtevant of Seattle. Samuel Maclure, the architect who consulted on the property, brought a sensibility shaped by the Renaissance and the English Arts and Crafts movement, which is why the place reads as a series of outdoor rooms rather than a single landscape. The Butcharts opened all of it to visitors and hosted weekly symphony concerts, first for friends and then for crowds. The Rose Garden today holds 2,500 plants across 280 varieties, and 50 full-time gardeners work the property and its 26 greenhouses.

## A Twenty-First Birthday Present

In 1939 the Butcharts gave the gardens outright to their grandson Ian Ross on his twenty-first birthday. He ran and promoted them for the next fifty-eight years, until his death in 1997, and his son Christopher took over. It was Christopher who introduced the summer Saturday fireworks in 1977, set to show tunes. Ann-Lee Ross gave Ian the bronze Circle of Doves for their fiftieth anniversary in 1991, and it was installed by the begonia bower two years later. Other bronzes accumulated along the way, including a wild boar bought on a Mediterranean trip in 1973, a Florentine cast of a 1620 Pietro Tacca original, whose snout is polished bright by visitors rubbing it for luck exactly as they do in Florence. The Rose Carousel opened in 2009, its thirty hand-carved basswood animals chosen by Robin Clarke, Jennie Butchart's great-granddaughter. The gardens are now a National Historic Site of Canada, and they are still owned by the family that started them, which is the rarest thing about them.

## Flight Context

The Butchart Gardens sit at 48.565N, 123.469W, at the head of Tod Inlet near Brentwood Bay on the Saanich Peninsula, about 23 km north of downtown Victoria. From the air, look for the narrow dogleg of Tod Inlet branching west off Saanich Inlet; the gardens appear as an intensely manicured patch of colour and glasshouses set among darker second-growth forest and farmland, with the old quarry bowl visible as a distinct depression. Brentwood Bay and the Mill Bay ferry route lie just west. Victoria International Airport (CYYJ) is roughly 6nm east across the peninsula, Victoria Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) about 13nm south, and Vancouver International (CYVR) some 35nm northeast. Best viewed at 1,500 to 3,000 feet. Note that CYYJ's control zone covers this area, so coordinate before overflying, and be aware that on summer Saturday evenings the gardens fire a fireworks display.
