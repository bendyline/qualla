# Victoria International Airport

A 1939 grass strip on the Saanich Peninsula that trained Royal Air Force pilots, hosted flying boats on Patricia Bay, and now shelters a maritime helicopter squadron in a heliport named after a castle in Sussex.

**Location:** 48.6472, -123.4260

**Categories:** Certified airports in British Columbia, Aviation history, Saanich Peninsula, Canadian Forces bases in British Columbia, Transport in the Capital Regional District

## Introduction

The helicopter facility at Victoria International Airport is called Arundel Castle. There is no castle on the Saanich Peninsula and never has been. The name belongs to 443 Maritime Helicopter Squadron, which flew Supermarine Spitfires from West Sussex during the Second World War, took the county's most conspicuous landmark as its own, and carried the name across an ocean and seventy years to a $155-million heliport opened here in 2015. Aviation is full of these small transplanted loyalties. This one sits on an airfield the locals still refuse to call by its official name -- ask anyone in Sidney about the Pat Bay Airport and they will know exactly where you mean.

## Grass, Then a War

It began in 1939 as a grass strip beside Patricia Bay, used for military training almost from the first season. In 1940 and 1941 it operated as Royal Air Force Station Patricia Bay, giving basic flying instruction to RAF pilots before sending them back across the Atlantic to the war -- young men learning to fly over the Gulf Islands with the understanding that they would soon be doing it over the Channel. Around 1942 the field became an RCAF aerodrome with three runways. The bay next door turned out to be the real asset: in an era when flying boats mattered enormously, having a sheltered stretch of protected salt water immediately adjacent to a hard-surface aerodrome was close to ideal. Trans-Canada Airlines began scheduled service in 1943. The Department of Transport took the field over in 1948, calling it Victoria (Patricia Bay) Airport, and the international name did not arrive until 1959.

## The Squadron That Came Back

The last RCAF unit left in 1952, and for thirty-seven years the airfield was purely civil. Then, in July 1989, the military returned: 443 Squadron and its CH-124 Sea King anti-submarine helicopters were moved across the country from CFB Shearwater in Nova Scotia so that Canada's Pacific fleet would have shipborne helicopters on the right coast. The unit was redesignated 443 Maritime Helicopter Squadron in 1995. Its work today is anti-submarine warfare and logistics support for the Royal Canadian Navy -- detachments that deploy aboard frigates and come home to a peninsula full of blueberry farms. Arundel Castle, their 2015 home, is the physical expression of a lineage that runs from Spitfires over southern England to helicopters over the North Pacific, which is a long way for a name to travel.

## A Business With Runways

In 1997 Transport Canada handed operational control to the Victoria Airport Authority, part of a sweeping national devolution that turned federal airfields into locally run enterprises. The authority immediately began rebuilding: a new airside hold room and arrivals rotunda in 2002, a new departures area by 2005. Passengers pay for it directly through an airport improvement fee, $25 per departing traveller as of April 2024, folded invisibly into the ticket price. The route map reflects the geography -- most flights go to British Columbia, Alberta, or western Washington, with seasonal WestJet service to Mexican resorts in the darkest months. American connections have proved fragile. United ended its long-running daily San Francisco flight in January 2019, and Delta dropped Victoria-Seattle that September, closing two doors to the south in the same year.

## Still a Flying Field

For all the terminal expansion, CYYJ has kept the character of a working aerodrome rather than a pure airline facility. Viking Air builds and supports de Havilland aircraft here -- the Twin Otters and water bombers that operate in places without runways. Vancouver Island Helicopters runs its operation from the field. The Victoria Flying Club teaches on small aircraft, multi-engine IFR, and floats; a charter operator trains floatplane pilots on a Cessna 180; the Royal Canadian Air Cadets fly here too. A water aerodrome sits immediately adjacent for seaplanes, and the British Columbia Aviation Museum is on the property. The long-range master plan calls for closing Runway 03-21 in the 2030s to free ground for terminal and apron growth, which is how airports age: the flying surfaces slowly surrender to the buildings.

## Flight Context

Victoria International Airport (CYYJ) sits at 48.647N, 123.426W on the Saanich Peninsula, about 22 km north of downtown Victoria and immediately west of the town of Sidney. The defining visual is Patricia Bay itself, the sheltered inlet off Saanich Inlet on the airport's western boundary, with the main runway 09-27 running parallel to the shoreline and the crossing 03-21 to its east. Highway 17 skirts the eastern edge and runs south to Victoria; the BC Ferries terminal at Swartz Bay is about 4 km north. The Gulf Islands lie east across Haro Strait, with the San Juan Islands beyond and the Olympic Mountains filling the southern horizon. Expect heavy mixed traffic: airline jets, floatplanes on the adjacent water aerodrome, military maritime helicopters from the Arundel Castle facility, and flight training circuits. Nearby airports: Victoria Inner Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) 12nm south, Friday Harbor Airport (KFHR), Washington, about 14nm east across the international boundary, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 35nm northeast. Morning fog on the peninsula is common in autumn.
