# Sidney Museum

A community museum on the Saanich Peninsula holding 6,000 objects, a reconstructed 1899 general store, wreckage from the steamer Iroquois, and seventy-seven years of the local newspaper.

**Location:** 48.6487, -123.3990

**Categories:** Museums, Local History, Saanich Peninsula, Maritime History, Indigenous Heritage, British Columbia

## Introduction

On 10 April 1911 the steamer Iroquois pulled away from the Sidney wharf carrying more cargo than she should have, badly secured. A storm was up. When the load shifted she went over, and at least twenty-one people died within sight of the town they had just left. The Iroquois had spent eleven years running between Sidney and Nanaimo with stops through the southern Gulf Islands - the kind of workaday vessel nobody writes about until something goes wrong. Her captain was charged with manslaughter and acquitted. Her propeller was raised in 1980 and now stands in a Sidney park. And pieces of her sit in a display case a few blocks away, in a museum built on the principle that the ordinary business of a small place is worth keeping.

## Six Thousand Ordinary Things

The Sidney Museum and Archives is a non-profit run by the Society of Saanich Peninsula Museums, and its permanent collection runs past six thousand items. What distinguishes it is less the size than the emphasis. The displays are organized around how things were used and what a day actually consisted of, rather than around dates and dignitaries. Critchley's Store, which served Sidney from 1899 into the 1930s, has been rebuilt inside the museum, shelves and all. There is a vintage kitchen, a mid-century schoolroom, an exhibit on what the world wars did to the families here. Rotating temporary shows fill the remaining space. It is the sort of institution that any town of a few thousand people might have, and mostly does not, because someone has to decide the receipts and the kettle and the shop counter are worth saving before they are thrown out.

## The Railway That Lost

The Saanich Peninsula was mostly forest with a few cleared patches when the Victoria and Sidney Railway opened in 1894, and the roads were bad enough that a railway made obvious sense. The line ran from Topaz Avenue in Victoria up to downtown Sidney, serving the farms and communities along the way, and for two decades it was how the peninsula moved. Then it was outcompeted from two directions at once. BC Electric opened an interurban service in 1913 with far more frequent departures, and the Canadian Northern built a rival line out to Patricia Bay. After the Great Northern withdrew in 1917 the V&S held on briefly and shut down for good in 1919. A section of track survived under Canadian National until 1935. The museum keeps artifacts from it - which is now the main way most people encounter a railway that once defined where this town was.

## Mill, Cannery, Farm, Newspaper

Sidney was a working town before it was a retirement and ferry town, and the collection reflects that: material from the Sidney Mill, from the Saanich Cannery, from the businesses that hired most of the people who lived here. The archives operate as a department of the museum, open by appointment, and their holdings are where the real depth sits. They have The Review, Sidney's local newspaper, from 1912 to 1989 - seventy-seven years of who married whom, what the council argued about, whose boat came in. There is an extensive photograph collection from the Sidney Experimental Farm, municipal records, oral history transcripts, and files on the area's heritage properties. The collection concentrates on the years between 1910 and 1980, though the edges of it reach back to 1850 and forward to now.

## Whose Peninsula

The museum's stated subject is the human history of Sidney and North Saanich, and it begins that history with the First Nations of the peninsula rather than with the settler families who arrived later. The land is W̱SÁNEĆ - ÁLEṈENEȻ in SENĆOŦEN, the language of the Central Coast Salish nations whose descendants live here as the Tsartlip, Pauquachin, Tsawout, Tseycum and Malahat First Nations. SENĆOŦEN has been through a hard century and a serious revival, taught now from preschool upward at the ȽÁU,WELṈEW̱ Tribal School. A town museum cannot carry all of that. What it can do is refuse to start the clock in 1894, and put the peninsula's older history in the first room rather than the last one - which, for a small institution working on donations and volunteer time, is a meaningful choice.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.649 N, 123.399 W in the town of Sidney, on the east side of the Saanich Peninsula, Vancouver Island, British Columbia. The museum sits in the small downtown grid a block off the waterfront; from the air the town is identified by its pier and marina on Sidney Channel, with Sidney Island, James Island and the southern Gulf Islands scattered eastward across shallow, pale water. Swartz Bay and its large ferry terminal lie about 5 km north at the peninsula's tip, and the Saanich Inlet cuts in on the western side. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500-3,000 ft MSL for the town and island channels. Victoria International (CYYJ) is immediately southwest, roughly 4 km away, so expect its controlled airspace and active circuit traffic directly overhead; Victoria Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) is about 25 km south, and Friday Harbor (KFHR) lies roughly 25 km east across the international border in Washington - the boundary runs down Haro Strait, so plan accordingly. Conditions are typically good: the peninsula sits in the rain shadow of the Vancouver Island Ranges, with morning stratus off the strait the most common restriction.
