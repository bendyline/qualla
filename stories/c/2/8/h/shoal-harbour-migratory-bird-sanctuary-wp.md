# Shoal Harbour Migratory Bird Sanctuary

A 1931 federal bird sanctuary on the Saanich Peninsula that also happens to be a working harbour full of marinas — 146 hectares of mudflat and eelgrass sustaining more than forty species through the winter.

**Location:** 48.6722, -123.4111

**Categories:** Migratory Bird Sanctuaries of Canada, 1931 establishments in British Columbia, Important Bird Areas of British Columbia, Salish Sea, Vancouver Island

## Introduction

It is a federal bird sanctuary with boat slips in it. When Ottawa drew the boundaries of Shoal Harbour in April 1931, the lines went around water that people were already using — and nearly a century later the sanctuary still contains marinas, moorings, and a developed shoreline, with pleasure craft manoeuvring past rafts of wintering ducks. Purists find this maddening. The birds, for their part, keep arriving: more than forty species use these sheltered bays and mudflats, and a great many of them do so because the water here does not freeze.

## One of the First

Shoal Harbour is one of the earliest sanctuaries established under Canada's Migratory Birds Convention Act, created on 10 April 1931 and now one of ninety-two such sanctuaries nationwide. It covers roughly 146 hectares along the east side of the Saanich Peninsula, spanning the municipalities of Sidney and North Saanich and taking in Tsehum Harbour along with the smaller, quieter Roberts Bay to the south. The point of a migratory bird sanctuary is narrow and practical: give birds somewhere safe to be. Environment and Climate Change Canada administers the network alongside the Species at Risk Act, and the winter function matters more than the summer one — when lakes and ponds across the interior lock up under ice, an ice-free coastal bay stops being a nice place to feed and starts being the only place to feed.

## Salt Water People

This shoreline is W̱SÁNEĆ territory. The name means "raised up" in the SENĆOŦEN language, and W̱SÁNEĆ people lived across what are now the San Juan Islands and along the eastern and northern shores of the Saanich Peninsula, orienting their year around the sea — they describe themselves as salt water people, and the phrase is a description of livelihood as much as identity. Colonization scattered that territory across multiple reserves and an international border, but four communities continue as W̱SÁNEĆ nations today. The W̱SÁNEĆ Land Trust Society works on conservation here that treats environment and culture as the same project, including stewardship of the sanctuary's shoreline. The mudflats were feeding people long before they were feeding federally protected ducks.

## Why the Mud Matters

What makes these flats so productive is not obvious from a dock. Beds of eelgrass and sheets of sea lettuce cover the intertidal zone, converting sunlight into food at a rate that sustains an enormous quantity of small life — and, in turn, the birds that eat it. Roberts Bay, enclosed and comparatively free of marina development, is where the concentration shows: roughly half the migratory birds using the whole sanctuary can be seen in that one bay, mostly dabbling and diving ducks. Among them is the bufflehead, a small, sharply patterned diving duck that winters on this coast and nests inland in tree cavities. Its nesting habitat has been eroded by tree clearing, but the species itself has done well — a Priority Species in the 2013 Bird Conservation Region Strategy, now assessed as secure, with an estimated 1.3 million mature birds as of 2020.

## One Bird Falling, One Arriving Backwards

Not every species on these flats is doing well. The horned grebe, a short-distance migrant that breeds on ponds and lakes across western and northern Canada, has declined sharply since 1970. COSEWIC designated the Magdalen Islands population as endangered in 2009 and the western population as of special concern; the Species at Risk Act listing followed in 2011, and the IUCN uplisted the species from least concern to vulnerable in 2015. Somewhere between 239,000 and 583,000 were estimated in 2012. Then there is the oddity of the bunch. Heermann's gull does not breed in Canada at all — it nests in the Gulf of California and southern California and then flies north after the breeding season, a pattern biologists call reverse migration. It shows up here as a seasonal visitor, having travelled the wrong way up the map, and the IUCN lists it as near threatened.

## November 2022

The contradiction built into the sanctuary came to a head in late 2022, when a vessel partially sank in Roberts Bay — the very corner where the bird concentrations are highest. A containment boom went in on 29 November. When Coast Guard crews observed small amounts of non-recoverable sheen inside the boom, the decision was made to tow the vessel out entirely. Nothing catastrophic happened, which is rather the point: a boat sinking in a bird sanctuary is a routine harbour event with an entirely non-routine setting, and the question of how to manage water that is simultaneously a marina district and protected habitat is still not settled. Local groups have pushed for years for a joint management plan covering Tsehum Harbour and Roberts Bay together. So far, the ducks are still coming.

## Flight Context

The Shoal Harbour Migratory Bird Sanctuary centres on 48.672°N, 123.411°W, on the east side of the Saanich Peninsula at the north end of Vancouver Island's southern tip. Best viewed from 1,000 to 2,500 feet — but note that this sits immediately alongside Victoria International (CYYJ), which is barely 2 nautical miles southwest, so this is busy terminal airspace and a radio call is mandatory. From the air the sanctuary reads as a pair of indentations on an otherwise straight shoreline: Tsehum Harbour to the north, crowded with marina docks and masts, and the smaller enclosed Roberts Bay to the south, noticeably emptier. At low tide the intertidal flats show as broad pale-brown aprons with darker eelgrass patches. The town of Sidney sits just south, the BC Ferries terminal at Swartz Bay is a few kilometres north, and across Haro Strait to the east lie the San Juan Islands. Friday Harbor (KFHR) is about 12 nautical miles east and Anacortes (KOTS) roughly 25 nautical miles northeast, both in Washington. Expect heavy floatplane and ferry activity, morning fog in late summer and autumn, and best bird viewing from November through March when wintering flocks are concentrated on the flats.
