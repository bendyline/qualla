# Saanich Inlet

A 226-metre-deep fjord outside Victoria whose bottom water has been oxygen-free for roughly seven thousand years - which is precisely why oceanographers have wired it for live video.

**Location:** 48.6000, -123.5000

**Categories:** Fjords, British Columbia, Vancouver Island, Oceanography, Indigenous History, Industrial History

## Introduction

Nothing lives on the floor of Saanich Inlet, and that is the most valuable thing about it. For most of the year the deep water here holds no oxygen at all, and hydrogen sulphide - the rotten-egg gas of stagnant water - shows up near the bottom. No worms burrow through the mud, no crabs churn it, so the sediment settles undisturbed in fine layers, season after season, year after year. The result is a seafloor that reads like tree rings. A dead basin, twenty minutes from downtown Victoria, keeping one of the most legible records of environmental history on the coast.

## The Sill That Made It

The mechanism is simple geology. Saanich Inlet runs about 25 kilometres between the Saanich Peninsula and the Malahat highlands, covering 67 square kilometres, and it drops to 226 metres deep. But its entrance is guarded by a shallow sill only about 70 metres down, and for roughly seven thousand years that lip has choked off circulation with the open sea. Water heavy with organic matter sinks into the basin, decays, consumes the oxygen, and cannot be flushed. The exception comes in late summer and early autumn, when denser oxygenated water from Haro Strait and Satellite Channel finally spills over the sill and slides into the deep. It is the fjord's annual breath - irregular, incomplete, and closely watched.

## A Fishery Older Than the Name

The inlet has been a fishery for the Malahat and Saanich First Nations for many centuries, and both are W̱SÁNEĆ peoples whose relationship to this water long predates any chart of it. Numerous reserves sit along its shoreline today, a geography of small allotments against an enormous traditional territory. The one significant river feeding the inlet is the Goldstream, which empties into the head of Finlayson Arm and still carries an autumn salmon run - a thin, persistent thread of the abundance that made this a place worth living beside in the first place. Since European arrival the inlet has supported commercial and recreational fisheries as well, and its clear, deep, sheltered water has made it a favourite of scuba divers.

## Bamberton

Industry came to the west shore in 1912, when the Associated Cement Company built a plant on the Malahat side and a company town grew around it. Bamberton had a port of its own, serving the cement works, and it stayed in company hands until the plant shut down in 1980. Limestone, a deep-water shoreline, and a straight run to markets down the coast made the site obvious; what made it eventually untenable was everything that makes heavy industry untenable on a scenic inlet within sight of a capital city. The name survives, the quarry scars survive, and the debate over what should happen to the land has outlived the cement.

## Bays, and One Piece of Local Confusion

For a body of water this size, Saanich Inlet has a remarkable number of named corners. Brentwood Bay sits sheltered on the east side, with a small ferry running across to Mill Bay - a crossing that lets drivers avoid the steep, twisting Malahat Drive, which climbs the inlet's western wall above Finlayson Arm. Tod Inlet branches off Brentwood's southwest corner, giving water access to Gowlland Tod Provincial Park, the rural community of Willis Point, and a private dock belonging to Butchart Gardens. Patricia Bay, very deep, hosts the Institute of Ocean Sciences. Finlayson Arm, named for Roderick Finlayson, who lived from 1818 to 1892, reaches south toward Goldstream; Squally Reach connects it to the main inlet, with McKenzie Bight on the eastern side and Coles Bay and Deep Cove further north. And then there is the local trap for visitors: Brentwood College School is not in Brentwood Bay at all. It is across the water, in Mill Bay.

## Wired for the Dark

Because the inlet behaves like a natural laboratory - deep, accessible, chemically extreme, and almost perfectly stratified - researchers have been studying it for decades, and in February 2006 they stopped visiting and simply moved in. The VENUS project, the Victoria Experimental Network Under the Sea run through the University of Victoria, placed a multi-sensor station on the seafloor at 97 metres, cabled to shore and streaming continuously. Scientists and the public alike can pull live data and video from a place where the water holds no oxygen. Watching the annual spill of oxygenated water arrive over the sill, in real time, from a laptop, is a strange and wonderful thing - and the questions it answers about low-oxygen water are becoming less academic as oxygen-poor zones expand in oceans worldwide.

## Flight Context

Saanich Inlet centres on roughly 48.6000°N, 123.5000°W, separating the Saanich Peninsula from the Malahat highlands just northwest of Victoria. It is easy to identify from the air: a deep, steep-sided fjord opening north into Satellite Channel toward Salt Spring Island and narrowing south through Squally Reach into the dead-end of Finlayson Arm. The forested Malahat ridge with its highway switchbacks defines the west wall; the flatter, farmed Saanich Peninsula lies east, with Butchart Gardens' distinctive quarry gardens visible at Tod Inlet and the Brentwood Bay to Mill Bay ferry crossing the middle. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,500-4,000 ft MSL to read the bays and the ferry track; 7,000-8,000 ft frames the whole inlet with the Gulf Islands north, the Olympic Mountains south, and Mount Baker east. Victoria International (CYYJ) sits about 4 nm east at Patricia Bay - expect substantial traffic and floatplane activity, and check the airspace before crossing the peninsula; Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is roughly 16 nm southeast and Duncan Airport (CAM3) about 15 nm northwest. Morning fog forms readily in the inlet in autumn; the Malahat generates turbulence and downdrafts in strong westerlies.
