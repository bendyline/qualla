# Malahat

A sacred mountain with the Trans-Canada Highway laid over it - and the single stretch of road whose closure can cut southern Vancouver Island in half.

**Location:** 48.5333, -123.5500

**Categories:** Small Towns, British Columbia, Roads and Transportation, Indigenous History, Industrial History, Scenic Viewpoints

## Introduction

When the Malahat closes, Vancouver Island effectively splits in two. There is no parallel route over the mountain, so a single collision on this stretch of the Trans-Canada Highway can strand commuters, freight, and ambulances for hours, and the alternatives - a small ferry across Saanich Inlet from Brentwood Bay to Mill Bay, or the long way around on the Pacific Marine Circle Route - absorb only a fraction of the traffic. Engineers call this a single point of failure. Islanders just call it the Malahat, and they check the cameras before they leave the house.

## The Mountain Came First

Long before any road existed, this mountain mattered. It takes its name from the Malahat First Nation, whose ancestors used its caves in spiritual practice, and it is regarded as one of the most sacred sites on southern Vancouver Island. That is not a footnote to the highway; it is the older and larger fact, and the highway is the thing that arrived later and laid itself across it. The summit reaches about 356 metres - roughly 1,168 feet - which is not a great elevation by the standards of the island's interior ranges but is enough, on a clear morning, to put Saanich Inlet, the Gulf Islands, and the Olympic Mountains in a single view.

## Cattle Trail to Trans-Canada

The first link between Greater Victoria and the Cowichan District ran inland, cut as a cattle trail in 1861 and upgraded to wagon-road standards in 1884. Malahat Drive as it exists today opened in 1911, surfaced with gravel, hugging the western side of Saanich Inlet as it climbs. It has been widened and rebuilt many times since, and the result is a road of inconsistent character: sections of two, three, and four lanes traded back and forth along roughly 25 kilometres, with the stretch through the tight canyon of Goldstream Provincial Park still down to two. Drivers feel every one of those transitions, which is part of why the road has the reputation it does.

## Holding the Centre Line

The Malahat's crash record is dominated by one failure mode: vehicles crossing the centre line into oncoming traffic. The consequences are severe, and the closures that follow ripple outward across the whole island. The provincial Ministry of Transportation's response has been unglamorous and effective - pouring concrete median barrier, section by section, to make head-on collisions physically impossible. With the stretch from Finlayson Arm Road up to the summit completed, roughly 65 per cent of Malahat Drive now runs divided. The speed limit is posted uniformly along the route except for a short reduced section at Tunnel Hill, where the geometry tightens and the road demands more attention than most drivers expect to give it.

## The Town That Poured Vancouver

At the northern end of the area sits Bamberton, where the Associated Cement Company built a plant in 1912 and ran it until 1980. For most of the twentieth century, cement from this shoreline went into the major bridges and buildings of Vancouver, the Deas Island Tunnel, hydroelectric projects, mines, airports across British Columbia, and the aluminium smelter at Kitimat. A company town grew up around the works and then, as company towns do, dispersed when the plant shut. The steep, rugged terrain here has long discouraged development, but new subdivisions are now going in around the old cement works and near Elkington Forest. Most residents still live in isolated homes scattered off the highway, which is why "Malahat" functions in practice as a Canada Post delivery district as much as a community.

## A Spiral in the Arbutus

The recognizable centre of the place is modest: the Malahat gas station, which doubles as the post office, along with the Malahat Chalet and the Moon Water Lodge. Malahat Fire Rescue covers the area, and municipal-type services come from Area A of the Cowichan Valley Regional District. Then, in July 2021, something distinctly less modest opened here. The Malahat SkyWalk runs a 600-metre elevated walkway out through arbutus and Douglas fir to a spiral tower ten storeys high, its top platform roughly 250 metres above sea level, offering a full circle of islands, inlets, forest, and mountains spanning two countries. It has become one of the most visited attractions on southern Vancouver Island - which means a great many people now stop on the Malahat instead of simply enduring it.

## Flight Context

Malahat lies at 48.5333°N, 123.5500°W in the Cowichan Valley on southern Vancouver Island. From the air the geography is unmistakable: Saanich Inlet cuts a long, deep finger of water south from Satellite Channel, and the Trans-Canada Highway climbs the ridgeline along its western shore, visible as a pale line switching back through dense forest. The Malahat SkyWalk tower sits near the highway on the ridge and is a useful visual fix. Brentwood Bay and the Saanich Peninsula fill the eastern shore of the inlet; Mill Bay and Cowichan Bay open to the north. Recommended viewing altitude is 3,000-5,000 ft MSL to trace the highway and inlet together, or 8,000-10,000 ft to take in the Gulf Islands, Haro Strait, and the Olympic Mountains in one frame. Victoria International (CYYJ) is roughly 15 nm southeast, Duncan Airport about 12 nm north, and Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) approximately 35 nm north. Terrain-induced turbulence and rapid cloud formation over the ridge are common; winter brings snow and fog to the summit when the valleys below are clear.
