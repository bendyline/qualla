# NRC Herzberg Astronomy and Astrophysics Research Centre

Canada's national astronomy centre sits on a Saanich hilltop around a telescope built to be the largest in the world, which missed that title by six months and has been working ever since.

**Location:** 48.5200, -123.4180

**Categories:** astronomy, observatories, science, british-columbia, victoria

## Introduction

John Stanley Plaskett designed his telescope in 1910 to be the largest in the world, and for most of a decade it was on track to be exactly that. Then the First World War slowed the work, and the 100-inch Hooker reflector at Mount Wilson beat him to first light. When the great mirror on Little Saanich Mountain opened its eye on 6 May 1918, it was second largest on Earth by roughly six months. It stayed second until 1935. More than a century later the Plaskett Telescope is still working on that hilltop north of Victoria, and the grounds around it have grown into the NRC Herzberg Astronomy and Astrophysics Research Centre - the institution through which a mid-sized country holds its share of the world's great observatories.

## A Hill Chosen for Its Darkness

The Dominion Astrophysical Observatory went up on Little Saanich Mountain because the site was dark, high enough to sit above coastal fog, and close enough to Victoria that astronomers could get home. The 1.83-metre Plaskett reflector is the centrepiece; a 1.2-metre instrument works alongside it. When the 1.88-metre telescope at the David Dunlap Observatory near Toronto came on line in 1935, the Plaskett finally slipped to third, and afterward to a ranking that nobody bothers to track. That did not matter much. What made it useful was its spectrograph, and what makes it useful now is that it is a well-understood machine on a stable mount with a very long baseline of observations behind it. Old telescopes in good hands stay productive far longer than the people who build new ones tend to expect.

## The Name on the Building

The centre is named for Gerhard Herzberg, who won the 1971 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work on the electronic structure and geometry of molecules, and particularly on free radicals - the short-lived, highly reactive fragments that turn out to be everywhere in interstellar space. Herzberg did most of that work at the National Research Council in Ottawa, and it was Ottawa where the institute bearing his name was formed in 1975. It came west in 1995, when the headquarters moved to Victoria and settled onto the Dominion Astrophysical Observatory site. A restructuring in 2012 produced the current name. Luc Simard has served as director general since at least 2021. The centre is Canada's institutional link to facilities it could never build alone: the Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope, Gemini, the James Clerk Maxwell Telescope, ALMA in the Atacama, the Square Kilometre Array, and the Thirty Meter Telescope.

## The Number Published Every Day

In 1947 a physicist named Arthur Covington pointed a war-surplus radar dish at the Sun and started measuring, carefully and repeatedly, how much radio energy it was putting out at a wavelength of 10.7 centimetres. He chose that wavelength because it was the one his salvaged equipment worked at. It turned out to be an unusually good proxy for the Sun's ionizing output, which is what drives radio blackouts, satellite drag, and the behaviour of the upper atmosphere. Covington was Canada's first radio astronomer, and the series he began has never stopped. It is measured now at the Dominion Radio Astrophysical Observatory near Penticton and published daily, a single number that space-weather forecasters around the world read every morning. In 2003 the NRC named a building in Penticton after him.

## Fellowships and Deep Surveys

The centre honours both men with fellowships, and the split between them describes the whole organisation. The Plaskett Fellowship brings a recent doctoral graduate to the Dominion Astrophysical Observatory in Victoria to do independent work, usually in observational astrophysics though theorists have held it too. The Covington Fellowship sends its recipient inland to Penticton, where the expertise is radio astronomy and the design of the instruments that make radio astronomy possible. Light in one valley, radio waves in another. Researchers here work on the Next Generation Virgo Cluster Survey, mapping a swarm of galaxies fifty million light years off, and on the Pan-Andromeda Archaeological Survey, which reads the debris of shredded dwarf galaxies in the halo of the nearest large spiral to trace how a galaxy assembles itself. The centre also designs and builds instruments, which is the quieter half of astronomy and often the part that decides what anyone can see at all.

## Flight Context

NRC Herzberg and the Dominion Astrophysical Observatory sit at 48.52 degrees north, 123.418 degrees west, atop Little Saanich Mountain - locally Observatory Hill - in Saanich, about 15 kilometres north of downtown Victoria, British Columbia. A viewing altitude of 2,500 to 4,000 feet shows the white domes standing clear of the surrounding second-growth forest, an obvious landmark on an otherwise green ridge. Navigate by the Saanich Peninsula's shape: Saanich Inlet cuts in on the west side, Haro Strait lies east, and the Malahat ridge rises across the inlet. Victoria International (CYYJ) is only about 8 kilometres north-northeast, so this hilltop sits close under that airport's traffic pattern and control zone - check charts and do not plan low manoeuvring here. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is 14 kilometres south. Summer nights on the peninsula are often exceptionally clear; winter brings low cloud that frequently sits below the summit, leaving the domes above an undercast.
