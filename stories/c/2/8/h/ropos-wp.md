# ROPOS

A Canadian research submersible that gets rebuilt into a different machine depending on how deep it is going, and can lift two tons off the sea floor.

**Location:** 48.6495, -123.4451

**Categories:** Robotic submarines, Remotely operated underwater vehicles, Oceanography, Science and technology in British Columbia

## Introduction

ROPOS is not one vehicle. Between expeditions the crew takes it apart and reassembles it into whichever of three machines the next job requires: a compact configuration rated to 1,000 metres, a larger one for 4,000 metres, and, rarely, a bigger one still for 5,000. Depth costs mass, because pressure housings get thicker and cable gets heavier, and so the submersible that goes over the side in one month is physically not the submersible that went over the side in the last. The name stands for Remotely Operated Platform for Ocean Science, which is bureaucratic to the point of being self-effacing. What it actually is is Canada's principal set of eyes and hands on the deep Pacific floor, flown from a ship on the surface through a fibre-optic umbilical, capable of picking up two tons of equipment and carrying it home.

## Ottawa Builds a Submarine Program

International Submarine Engineering built ROPOS in Vancouver, and the Canadian federal government bought it. The same program ran the crewed submersible Pisces IV, which eventually moved to Hawaii under the Hawai'i Undersea Research Laboratory and there found the two-man Japanese midget submarine sunk outside Pearl Harbor in the hours before the air attack. Then the funding dried up, as funding does. By that point, though, something had happened that outlasted the budget: a community of scientists had formed around the vehicle, and long-term experiments were already sitting on the sea floor waiting to be revisited. Those experiments needed a submersible. The Canadian Scientific Submersible Facility was created, leased ROPOS from the government for several years, and eventually bought it outright. In 2005 the vehicle was rebuilt extensively.

## Electricity Down, Hydraulics Everywhere

Everything ROPOS does comes down a cable. The umbilical carries electrical power from the surface ship and an optical fibre for telemetry and data, which is how a pilot sitting in a container on deck sees what the cameras see with almost no delay. Some of that electricity runs the lights and cameras directly. Most of it goes into an electric motor whose only job is to drive a hydraulic pump, and the hydraulics then power the thrusters and the manipulators. Engineers call this an electro-hydraulic system, and the reason for the indirection is force. Hydraulic actuators can close a gripper on a rock sample at crushing pressure two kilometres down, which electric motors of comparable size cannot.

## Plug and Play at Two Kilometres

Science changes faster than submersibles do, so ROPOS was designed to accept instruments it was never built for. A hot fluid sampler weighing roughly 200 pounds bolts on and asks only for 120 volts AC and a data line. A set of spare hydraulic functions is available for whatever a research team wants to hang on the frame, an approach shared with the Woods Hole vehicles Jason and Jason 2 on the American side. The effect is that the machine ages well. A hull and frame designed decades ago are still doing current science because the parts that go obsolete fastest are the parts that unbolt.

## Two Tons Off the Bottom

The most unusual thing ROPOS does has nothing to do with looking at anything. It has a thru-frame lift capability of 4,000 pounds: a load is attached to the vehicle itself and hoisted by the mid-depth launch and recovery system, an enhanced shipboard crane modified to handle the ROV and its cargo together. That single specification is what lets a cabled ocean observatory exist. Instrument platforms the size of a small car have to be lowered precisely onto the sea floor, connected, and later brought back up for servicing. Without a submersible that can carry them, an observatory is a one-way trip.

## Where It Works

About 260 kilometres southwest of Vancouver Island, the Endeavour Segment of the Juan de Fuca Ridge is venting superheated, mineral-laden water through chimneys of hydrothermal sulfide onto black basalt. It is one of the best-studied vent fields on the planet, and it is wired. Ocean Networks Canada's NEPTUNE observatory runs 840 kilometres of fibre-optic cable out from Vancouver Island across the boundary between the North American and Juan de Fuca plates, feeding five node sites with power and internet, the first high-power and high-bandwidth cabled underwater observatory in the world. ROPOS is the vehicle that installs and maintains it. In August 2016 the ROV made four dives at Endeavour and two more at Middle Valley, and the video it brought back is the kind of record that only exists because somebody kept funding a machine after the program that built it ended.

## Flight Context

ROPOS is based on the Saanich Peninsula at roughly 48.650N, 123.445W, at the Institute of Ocean Sciences on Patricia Bay in North Saanich, British Columbia, about 25 kilometres north of Victoria. From the air the landmark is Patricia Bay itself, a broad inlet on the west side of the peninsula opening into Saanich Inlet, with the Institute's waterfront research campus and dock on its shore. Victoria International Airport (CYYJ) sits immediately adjacent to the east, and Victoria Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is 25 kilometres south. The BC Ferries terminal at Swartz Bay is on the peninsula's northern tip. Look west across Saanich Inlet to the Malahat ridge, and east across Haro Strait to the San Juan Islands and Mount Baker. Best viewed at 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, but note this is directly inside the Victoria International control zone, so contact ATC. The vehicle itself works far offshore, most often over the Juan de Fuca Ridge roughly 260 kilometres southwest of Vancouver Island in open Pacific water.
