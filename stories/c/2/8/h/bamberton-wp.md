# Bamberton

A cement company built a town on Saanich Inlet with a dance floor to rival the Empress Hotel's — and its sister plant across the water is now Butchart Gardens.

**Location:** 48.5858, -123.5220

**Categories:** Ghost towns in British Columbia, Company towns in Canada, Populated places in the Cowichan Valley Regional District, Industrial history, Saanich Inlet

## Introduction

The dance floor at Bamberton was as large as the one in the Empress Hotel. That detail says more about this place than any production figure. Bamberton was a cement town — isolated, company-owned, built for a single industrial purpose on the west shore of Saanich Inlet — and the company that built it understood that men will not stay somewhere remote unless there is something to do on a Saturday night. So the recreation hall got a floor that matched Victoria's grandest hotel. For sixty-eight years, people lived and worked here, and then the plant closed, the mill came down, and the site entered a long second life as one of the most argued-over pieces of ground on Vancouver Island.

## Mr. Bamber of Gravesend

The name belongs to H.K.G. Bamber, a cement manufacturer from Gravesend in England who owned the land and served as managing director of British Portland Cement Manufacturers Ltd. The operation itself began somewhere else entirely. In 1904 the Vancouver Portland Cement Company set up across the inlet at Tod Inlet, working the limestone there — and if that location sounds unexpectedly lovely for heavy industry, it is because the worked-out quarry there later became Butchart Gardens. Demand during British Columbia's building boom outran what Tod Inlet could supply, so the company opened a second plant across the water. Bamberton began production in 1912 under the name Associated Cement Company, with both operations run by B.C. Cement Company Ltd. of Victoria.

## Three Thousand Barrels a Day

The timing was terrible. The outbreak of the First World War killed the real estate boom that had justified the second plant, and both works sat idle for seven years. Bamberton restarted in 1921 and then ran hard. By 1927 the plant was turning out 3,000 barrels of cement a day and burning 40,000 tons of Vancouver Island coal a year to do it — which meant the operation was not just a cement works but a steady customer for the island's collieries, one industry feeding another along the same coastline. The self-contained settlement housed 180 employees at its peak, with everything a company town required. Over the last two decades of operation the arithmetic reversed: production fell, costs climbed, and a protracted strike took its toll. The plant closed in 1980. In October 1982, its owner, Genstar Cement Company, advertised the abandoned and overgrown site for sale. Demolition of the mill began within weeks.

## Whose Inlet

Saanich Inlet is not a blank industrial backdrop. The Malahat, Tseycum, Tsartlip, Tsawout, and Pauquachin peoples have lived along this water for a very long time, and the inlet is central to their culture as both a spiritual place and a source of food. From the late eighteenth century into the mid-nineteenth, these communities suffered devastating population loss from introduced disease and armed conflict following the arrival of European settlers. In 1852 the Saanich nations signed the Douglas Treaties, transferring land to the Colony of Vancouver Island. The treaties stated that the signatories would keep their ceremonial and subsistence practices on the land — but when the McKenna-McBride Commission fixed the boundaries of present-day reserves in 1913, non-Native settlement pressed steadily into the places where those practices had always happened. Bamberton has been privately owned throughout its industrial history. The neighbouring nations have never regarded that ownership as the whole story, and they have said so consistently.

## Sold, Cleaned, Sold Again

What to do with a derelict cement works on a beautiful inlet has occupied four decades. Through the late 1980s and 1990s, the Trust for Sustainable Development proposed a twenty-year plan that would have brought 12,000 residents to the site; it drew criticism over the effect on Saanich Inlet, and over traffic and water supply, and its funders eventually declined to proceed. Bamberton Properties LLP, an offshoot of Three Point Properties, bought the land in 2005, spent as much as $35 million on cleanup, and worked five years with the Cowichan Valley Regional District on a plan the district would approve. The CVRD concluded that Mill Bay did not need more residentially zoned land, and Bamberton Properties was sold to the Malahat First Nation in 2015. Just over a month later, the Nation and Steelhead LNG announced a benefits agreement supporting a proposed liquefied natural gas facility on the site — a floating plant processing up to six million tonnes a year, fed by a 128-kilometre pipeline connecting to the existing system at Sumas, Washington. The neighbouring Tsartlip, Tsawout, Tseycum, and Pauquachin nations opposed it as a bloc, arguing that tanker traffic through the inlet threatened the water, the food, and the ancestral connection to the place. Steelhead LNG cancelled the project in December 2017, citing the economics of the cross-border pipeline route and a collapse in global LNG prices, and turned its attention to a separate proposal on Vancouver Island's west coast. What is finally built at Bamberton itself, if anything is, remains unsettled.

## Flight Context

Bamberton sits at 48.5858 degrees north, 123.5220 degrees west, on the west shore of Saanich Inlet just south of Mill Bay, roughly 45 km north of Victoria on Vancouver Island. Best viewed from 2,500 to 4,000 feet on a north-south track up the inlet — the site is unmistakable from the air as a pale scar of exposed quarry rock and levelled industrial terraces on an otherwise forested shoreline, with a long dock line at the water's edge. Bamberton Provincial Park lies immediately adjacent. Across the inlet to the east, the Saanich Peninsula carries the grounds of Butchart Gardens at Tod Inlet, the site of the sister cement works. The Malahat highway climbs the ridge behind. Nearest airports: Victoria International (CYYJ) about 40 km south on the Saanich Peninsula, Nanaimo (CYCD) to the north, and Victoria Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH). Saanich Inlet is a deep, steep-sided fjord that traps low cloud and morning fog well into the day in autumn and winter; the Malahat ridge to the west can obscure quickly in weather.
