# Dominion Astrophysical Observatory

A two-ton glass mirror left Belgium a week before the First World War, was reground twice, and was hauled up a Saanich hilltop by horse and wagon to become the second-largest telescope on Earth.

**Location:** 48.5198, -123.4183

## Introduction

The mirror shipped out of Antwerp a week before the First World War began. It was 73 inches of glass, a foot thick, weighing about 4,340 pounds, cast by Saint-Gobain at their Charleroi works, and if it had missed that sailing it would have spent four years on the wrong side of a war. It crossed to Pittsburgh instead, where the John A. Brashear Company began grinding it into a telescope mirror. Then somebody found a scratch that nobody could explain, and the whole surface had to be redone. A flaw in the second grinding forced a third attempt. Two years were lost. When the finished mirror finally reached Victoria by train, the last leg up Little Saanich Mountain was done by horse and wagon.

## Six Months Behind

John S. Plaskett proposed and designed this telescope in 1910, backed by the International Union for Cooperation in Solar Research, and there is good reason to think he expected it to be the largest in the world. The delays with the mirror cost him that. The Plaskett telescope saw first light on 6 May 1918, roughly six months after the 100-inch Hooker telescope at Mount Wilson — although the Hooker's own opening was dragged into 1918 by wartime disruption, so the race was closer than the dates suggest. What Plaskett did unambiguously surpass was the 72-inch metal mirror of the Leviathan of Parsonstown, built in Ireland in 1845 and dismantled by the 1910s. He also left the field well behind him: in 1918, the next largest instruments anywhere were 60-inch reflectors at Harvard College Observatory and Mount Wilson, and most observatories were still working with nineteenth-century refractors. His telescope stayed the second-largest on Earth until the 74-inch at the David Dunlap Observatory — also Canadian — arrived in 1935.

## A Building Designed to Hold Still

The Dominion architect Edgar Lewis Horwood designed the observatory, and the McAlpine-Robertson Company of Vancouver built it for $75,000. The dome came from Warner & Swasey. Both the building and the dome are double-walled, which is not an aesthetic decision — a big mirror is exquisitely sensitive to temperature, and warm air rising off sun-heated walls will smear a star into mush. The whole structure is essentially a thermos designed to keep the night air inside matching the night air outside. Plaskett ran the place until 1935. Under him and after him, the observatory produced a long run of discoveries about the structure and motion of the Milky Way, and it remained one of the world's principal astrophysical research centres into the 1960s. It is now a national historic site of Canada.

## Still in the Rotation

This is not a retired instrument. A spectrograph sits at the Cassegrain focus and an imaging CCD at the Newtonian, and the telescope is in regular use. In 1962 the observatory added a 48-inch optical telescope — ordered in 1957 from Grubb Parsons of Newcastle-upon-Tyne — whose Coudé focus feeds a spectrograph the size of a room. In 1995 the site became headquarters of what is now the NRC Herzberg Astronomy and Astrophysics Research Centre (renamed from the Herzberg Institute of Astrophysics in 2012), which operates Canadian optical and radio telescopes and partners internationally: the Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope, the Gemini observatories in Hawaii and Chile, and ALMA on the Chajnantor plateau. The century-old dome on Observatory Hill is a working node in that network, and the telescopes are open to visitors year-round.

## The Public Bought It Back

The Centre of the Universe, the observatory's public interpretive centre, opened in 2002 across the parking lot from the big dome. In August 2013 the federal government closed it, citing finances. What happened next is the good part. Members of the public formed a working group, joined forces with the Royal Astronomical Society of Canada's Victoria Centre and Science Venture at the University of Victoria, and reopened the centre in 2014. They incorporated as the Friends of the Dominion Astrophysical Observatory in June 2015 and signed a licence to occupy with the National Research Council in May 2016. The Friends now run the programming — free star parties on roughly twenty Saturday nights a year, school visits, tours, online outreach — with support from the Victoria Foundation, BC's Community Gaming Grants, and Pacific Economic Development Canada. Public astronomy here dates back to the observatory's founding and has been interrupted only twice: by the Second World War, and by the COVID pandemic.

## A Planetarium Painted Like the Sun

Inside the centre are the artefacts that make the whole improbable story tangible: the original mirror cast in 1914, and the 1:10 scale model of the telescope dome built in 1915. There is also a 25-seat planetarium, 4.47 metres across and painted on the outside to look like the sun, running Nightshade software that can put the sky, stellar motion, and planetary orbits overhead — or play a full-dome film. Telescope tours start from here and walk you across the lot to the real thing. Standing under the Plaskett, it helps to remember that every piece of it came up this hill on a wagon.

## Flight Context

The Dominion Astrophysical Observatory crowns Observatory Hill — Little Saanich Mountain — at 48.5198°N, 123.4183°W, on the Saanich Peninsula north of Victoria, British Columbia. Fly it at 2,000 to 3,500 feet: the white dome on a wooded summit is visible from a long way out and makes an excellent visual fix. Orientation is easy on this peninsula. Saanich Inlet cuts in on the west with the Malahat ridge beyond, the Saanich Peninsula narrows northward toward Sidney and the Swartz Bay ferry terminal, and the Gulf Islands scatter across the water to the northeast with Mount Baker often looming on the mainland horizon. Victoria International Airport (CYYJ) sits roughly 5 nautical miles north-northeast — close enough that its airspace matters, so check the terminal procedures before manoeuvring near the hill. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) lies about 10 nautical miles south, and Vancouver International (CYVR) is the nearest major hub. This is one of the drier, sunnier corners of coastal BC thanks to the Olympic rain shadow, and clear winter nights here are genuinely good — which is precisely why the telescope went up on this particular hill.
