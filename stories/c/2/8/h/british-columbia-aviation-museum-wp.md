# British Columbia Aviation Museum

A volunteer-run museum on the edge of Victoria International Airport that took delivery of the last flying Martin Mars - a 200-foot flying boat that landed and never took off again.

**Location:** 48.6410, -123.4190

**Categories:** Aerospace museums in British Columbia, Saanich Peninsula

## Introduction

On 11 August 2024, a flying boat with a two-hundred-foot wingspan lifted off Sproat Lake on Vancouver Island, flew southeast down the island, and settled onto the water at Patricia Bay. The Hawaii Mars had been in the air, on and off, since the 1940s. This was the last time. She was hauled out, moved onto the grounds of the British Columbia Aviation Museum in Sidney, and opened to the public on 28 September. People came out to watch that final flight the way they turn out for an eclipse, because there was no prospect of a second one.

## Seven Aircraft, One Enormous Idea

The Martin JRM Mars was the largest Allied flying boat to enter production during the Second World War, and only seven were completed. A wingspan of 200 feet and a length of 117 feet 3 inches gave them cargo capacity nobody else could match, and the US Navy put them on the San Francisco to Honolulu run until 1956. What saved them from the scrapper was fire. From 1959 the survivors were converted into water bombers, each fitted with a 7,200-US-gallon plywood tank - roughly 27,000 litres - and retractable scoops. Skimming a lake, a Mars could take on 27 tonnes of water in 22 seconds and carry it to a burning hillside. For decades that was the job, worked out of Sproat Lake.

## Where the Second One Went

There were two left at the end: Hawaii Mars and Philippine Mars. They did not retire together. Philippine Mars made the last flight any Martin Mars will ever make on 10 February 2025, landing at Lake Pleasant in Arizona before being taken apart, trucked to Tucson, and reassembled for public display at the Pima Air & Space Museum by September 2025. So the type ends in two places: a desert in Arizona, and a patch of ground beside a runway on the Saanich Peninsula. It is worth standing under one of those wings to understand the numbers. Two hundred feet is not a statistic you can hold in your head until the shadow of it is over you.

## A Lancaster Out of Storage

The Mars is the headline, but the museum has been collecting since 1985. In September 2018 it acquired Avro Lancaster FM104, one of only seventeen Lancasters left anywhere in the world, brought west from storage in Toronto. Volunteers are returning her to her post-war search-and-rescue configuration - not the bomber most people picture, but what the type became afterwards: an aircraft sent out to find people who were lost. Restoration on a four-engined heavy bomber is measured in years and in the patience of people who show up on a Tuesday to work on a single hatch.

## Cold War Deliveries

The collection has grown quickly in recent years, and it has grown in a distinctly Canadian direction. January 2021 brought a Grumman S2F Tracker out of storage with the Canadian Military Education Centre, the twin-engined submarine hunter the Royal Canadian Navy operated through the Cold War. July 2023 added a CF-104, Canada's version of the Starfighter, an aircraft with a reputation as sharp as its wings. That September two Piasecki H-21 helicopters arrived - the tandem-rotor machine that everybody called the Flying Banana, for reasons obvious the moment you see one in profile.

## Working Museum, Live Airfield

The museum sits at 1910 Norseman Road on the east side of Victoria International Airport, which means the exhibits have company: real aircraft are landing a few hundred metres away throughout the day. Inside, the display space spreads across two hangars, alongside a classroom, a gift shop and, most importantly, a restoration workshop where volunteers are permanently mid-project on something. Uniforms, medals and models fill the cases. The aircraft outside and in the hangars are the reason people come. It is a museum in the oldest and best sense - a place kept going by people who could not stand to see these machines disappear, working on them one rivet at a time.

## Flight Context

The British Columbia Aviation Museum is at 48.641°N, 123.419°W, on the east side of Victoria International Airport (CYYJ) in Sidney, on the Saanich Peninsula. It is the rare museum that doubles as an airport landmark: from the circuit the Hawaii Mars is unmistakable, a white flying boat with a 200-foot wingspan parked on open ground east of the runways, visible well before the field itself resolves. Patricia Bay lies immediately east, the Saanich Inlet west, and the Gulf Islands scatter across the water to the northeast. Swartz Bay's ferry terminal sits at the peninsula's north tip. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is about 22 km south in downtown Victoria, and Vancouver International (CYVR) lies across the Strait of Georgia to the northeast. CYYJ is a controlled field with regular airline and seaplane traffic - get the ATIS and a clearance well out. Summer mornings can bring marine stratus off the strait.
