# Malahat First Nation

A Coast Salish nation of roughly 350 members on the western shore of Saanich Inlet who bought a large piece of their own territory back in 2015 and built a 650-metre treetop walkway over it.

**Location:** 48.6145, -123.5205

**Categories:** Coast Salish governments, Southern Vancouver Island, First Nations history, Indigenous-owned enterprise

## Introduction

In 2015 the Malahat Nation did something that reads, stated plainly, as absurd: they bought their homeland. Not all of it, but a substantial piece — the old industrial lands at Bamberton and the ground around Oliphant Lake — financed through a loan from the First Nations Finance Authority. The purchase roughly tripled the territory the Nation holds outright. Past Chief Michael Harry explained the logic without much decoration: buying the land was a great deal more efficient than spending years in court arguing about who rightfully owned it. It is a transaction that tells you almost everything about what the last two centuries have been like on this shoreline.

## On the Western Shore of the Inlet

Malahat Nation — MÁLEXEȽ — is a Coast Salish community of the W̱SÁNEĆ, the Saanich People, with roughly 350 members and two reserves on the western shore of Saanich Inlet, north of Victoria on Vancouver Island. Their country is mountain, forest, river and waterfall running down to a deep, sheltered arm of the sea. Two ancestral languages belong to the Nation, Hul̓q̓umín̓um̓ and SENĆOŦEN, and both are in revitalization: they are spoken most fluently by elders, which makes every year of teaching urgent rather than merely worthwhile. Malahat belongs to the Naut'sa mawt Tribal Council, and was the ninth First Nation in Canada certified by the First Nations Financial Management Board — a technical distinction that in practice unlocks the ability to borrow, and therefore to act. George Harry, who had served four years on council, was elected Chief on June 10, 2019.

## Treaty, Slowly

The legal ground under all of this is contested and unfinished. The Douglas Treaties of 1850 and 1854 covered parts of southern Vancouver Island in agreements whose terms and interpretation have been argued over ever since. The modern process moves at a geological pace: Malahat signed an Incremental Treaty Agreement in 2013, and the Te'mexw Treaty Association — Malahat together with Snaw-naw-as, Songhees, T'Sou-ke and Scia'new — reached an Agreement-in-Principle in 2015. By 2022 the Te'mexw negotiation had advanced to stage five of six. Six stages sounds finite until you notice how long the first five took. Land bought on the open market, by contrast, is settled the day the papers clear, which is precisely the calculation the Nation made at Bamberton.

## The Goldstream Spill

Salmon are not a symbol here; they are a food supply, a legal interest and a measure of whether anything is working. In 2011 a tanker crash on the Malahat Highway sent gasoline and diesel into the Goldstream River. Thousands of fish died. Crews worked to contain the spill and to collect and dispose of contaminated fish, and the lighter fuel fractions were expected to break down fairly quickly, but a river does not un-happen an event like that on anyone's schedule. In 2018 plans came together to restore chinook — the largest Pacific salmon and, in several stocks, an endangered one — on the west coast of Vancouver Island. The project was first framed as a partnership between the Malahat Nation and Steelhead LNG to be built on Malahat land, and later moved to Huu-ay-aht First Nations territory nearby. Salmon recovery, like treaty, tends to outlast the arrangements made to pursue it.

## What the Schools Took, and What Is Being Asked Back

Five residential schools operated on Vancouver Island, the first opening in 1890 and the last closing in 1983. Figures reported in 2021 place the number of children who died at those schools at a minimum of 202 — a floor, not a total. The record also shows sustained resistance across the whole span of their existence, including buildings that had to be rebuilt after being burned. In 2021, as ground searches at former school sites drew national attention, the Malahat Nation organized a march joining neighbouring nations and non-Indigenous allies, both to protest what was done and to honour the children who did not come home. The Nation has since commissioned totem poles to mark the legacy of those who died and those who survived. A parallel reckoning is underway in the province's museums: British Columbia has committed two million dollars toward repatriation through the Royal BC Museum, covering both cultural belongings and ancestral remains. For the communities involved, the return of remains is not a matter of collections management. It is the chance to complete ceremonies that were interrupted when those ancestors were dug up and carried away.

## Six Hundred and Fifty Metres Above the Forest Floor

The most visible thing the Nation has built on its reacquired land opened in July 2021, in the middle of a pandemic that hit Indigenous communities hard. The Malahat SkyWalk cost roughly $17 million and consists of a 650-metre elevated walkway threading through the forest canopy, ending at a 32-metre spiral tower whose ramp climbs gently enough that a wheelchair can make the summit. From the top, some 250 metres above Saanich Inlet, the view takes in Finlayson Arm, the Gulf Islands and — on a clear day — the mountains of Washington State. It is a tourist attraction, and it is also a demonstration: a Nation-owned enterprise, on ground the Nation purchased, generating employment and revenue on its own terms. Most visitors climb the tower for the view. The more interesting thing to look at is the ground underneath it.

## Flight Context

Malahat Nation territory lies along the western shore of Saanich Inlet on southern Vancouver Island, centred near 48.61N, 123.52W, roughly 15nm northwest of Victoria. From the air the defining feature is Saanich Inlet itself — a long, deep, steep-sided fjord-like arm running north from Finlayson Arm, with the Malahat ridge rising abruptly on its west side and the Saanich Peninsula's farmland on the east. Look for the Trans-Canada Highway climbing the Malahat grade along the ridge, the former Bamberton cement works on the shoreline below, and the SkyWalk tower standing clear of the canopy near the highway. Nearest airports: Victoria International (CYYJ) about 12nm southeast, Duncan Airport (CAM3) about 10nm north, and heavy floatplane traffic into Victoria Inner Harbour (CYWH) to the south. Stay above 2,500 feet along the ridge; the Malahat generates sharp local turbulence and rotor on westerly days, and low cloud frequently caps the summit while the inlet below stays clear.
