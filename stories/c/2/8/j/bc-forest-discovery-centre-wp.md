# BC Forest Discovery Centre

A hundred acres outside Duncan where the geared locomotives that hauled British Columbia's old growth still run, including the last working Climax in Canada.

**Location:** 48.8019, -123.7150

**Categories:** Museums, Heritage Railways, Vancouver Island, British Columbia, Logging History

## Introduction

One of the steam locomotives here spent its working life helping fill in North Vancouver's harbour. It is now more than a century old, and it spends its days hauling visitors across a trestle over Somenos Lake. That reversal is the whole idea of the BC Forest Discovery Centre, a hundred-acre site on the edge of Duncan on Vancouver Island where the machinery that took British Columbia's forests apart has been kept alive, in steam, on two and a half kilometres of operating narrow gauge track. Nothing here is behind glass. Most of it moves.

## A Collector Who Did Not Stop

The centre exists because of G.E. Wellburn, known to everyone as Gerry, a collector in the serious and slightly obsessive sense of the word. He gathered the equipment, the buildings and the artefacts of the coastal logging industry at a moment when that industry was busily discarding them, and in 1965 the result opened to the public as the Cowichan Valley Forest Museum. It carried the name BC Forest Museum for a period before settling on its present one. The distinction between a private accumulation and a public museum is largely a matter of somebody deciding the material belongs to everyone. Wellburn made that decision, and a great deal of machinery that would otherwise have gone for scrap is still turning because of it.

## Shays, Climaxes and One Survivor

Logging railways were not like other railways. The track was temporary, steep, roughly laid and frequently terrible, which ruled out conventional locomotives and produced a whole family of geared machines built to crawl up impossible grades with heavy loads. The centre holds seven steam locomotives, and the roster reads like a directory of the coastal industry: Mayo Lumber Company No. 3, a Shay that spent 1995 to 2009 on loan to the Kettle Valley Steam Railway; Hillcrest Lumber Company No. 1, another Shay; a Shay from Bloedel Stewart and Welch; a Climax from Shawnigan Lake Lumber; and two Vulcan tank engines named Suzie and Samson. The standout is Hillcrest Lumber Company No. 9, a Climax, and the only operational locomotive of its type left in Canada. Diesels round out the collection, among them an eighty-ton Whitcomb from British Columbia Forest Products and an eight-ton Plymouth called Sandy.

## Two and a Half Kilometres

The railway is the spine of the visit, and the route was assembled with an eye to what it would pass. Riders cross a trestle over Somenos Lake, which is the kind of structure that photographs well and that nobody builds any more. They pass one of the earliest prefabricated schoolhouses, a small building with a surprisingly large story about how quickly instant communities were erected in the woods. There is an operating water wheel. Elsewhere on the site sit a reconstructed logging camp, a ranger's station and a forest fire lookout tower, rebuilt and climbable, from which the view takes in both the grounds and the marsh beyond.

## The Work It Represents

It would be easy to present all of this as picturesque, and it is, but the logging camp is worth a slower look. The men who lived in camps like it felled and moved timber of a size that is difficult to imagine from the second-growth forests standing today, in conditions that were dangerous by any measure. The trucks and the locomotives survived because steel survives. The camps are reconstructions because canvas and cut lumber do not, and because nobody at the time thought a bunkhouse was worth preserving. Walking through one is the closest most visitors will come to the daily reality behind a century of provincial prosperity.

## The Marsh Next Door

The centre's boundary runs against the Somenos Marsh Wildlife Refuge and a Ducks Unlimited pond, and the wetland does something useful to the experience. Waterfowl work the shallows in numbers, other birds move through the fringes, and red-eared slider turtles haul out to sun themselves in the field beside the marsh. Stand at the top of the fire lookout tower and the composition arranges itself: preserved logging machinery in the foreground, open water and reeds beyond, forest closing the horizon. The industry and the ecosystem it worked in, side by side, both still here.

## Flight Context

The BC Forest Discovery Centre lies at 48.8019 N, 123.715 W on the northern edge of Duncan in the Cowichan Valley, southern Vancouver Island. Best viewing altitude is 1,500 to 3,000 feet, enough to resolve the site's open grounds, the loop of narrow gauge track and the trestle crossing at Somenos Lake. Key visual landmarks: Somenos Lake and the surrounding marsh immediately north of the site, the town of Duncan just south, the Cowichan River running east to the sea at Cowichan Bay, and the Strait of Georgia with Salt Spring Island beyond to the east. Cowichan Lake sits about 28 km west up the valley. Duncan's small airport lies just south of the site; Victoria International (CYYJ) is roughly 30 nm southeast and Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) about 25 nm north. Expect valley fog on calm autumn and winter mornings, and watch for rising terrain west of the valley floor.
