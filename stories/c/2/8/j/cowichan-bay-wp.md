# Cowichan Bay

A Vancouver Island fishing village that once billed itself the Salmon Capital of the World, employed the future poet of the Klondike as a farmhand, and became North America's first Cittaslow town.

**Location:** 48.7500, -123.6000

**Categories:** Ports and harbours of British Columbia, Populated places in the Cowichan Valley Regional District, Designated places in British Columbia, Populated places on the British Columbia Coast

## Introduction

On 1 October 1903 a farm labourer finished his last day at Corfield Farm on Cowichan Bay. He had been working the farms around this stretch of Vancouver Island on and off since June 1896, and he was, by every visible measure, a young man with no particular prospects. His name was Robert W. Service, and within a few years he would be the most widely read poet in the English-speaking world on the strength of verses about a gold rush he had not yet seen. A memorial bench now sits in Robert Service Wayside Park, its plaque carrying a few lines of 'Heart o' the North.'

## Salmon Capital of the World

From the early 1900s, sportsmen came here from across the British Empire, drawn by the salmon running in the bay and up the Cowichan and Koksilah rivers. For a time the place styled itself the Salmon Capital of the World, and the claim carried more than fishing: fine sailing water, an annual regatta, and grass tennis courts that were said in that era to be the oldest anywhere but Wimbledon. That last boast has a real institution behind it. The South Cowichan Lawn Tennis Club was founded in 1887, making it one of the oldest lawn tennis clubs in the world and the oldest in Canada - a club with more than a century of white-flannel history on a coast better known for gumboots.

## A Bowl in Bloomsbury

Long before any of that, First Nations people lived on this bay for thousands of years, harvesting the salmon and shellfish that filled its coves, tidal flats and fast rivers. In the late nineteenth century a rare steatite anthropomorphic bowl was found here - a carved stone vessel in human form, one of only about fifty known, attributed to the Marpole Culture and dated somewhere between 400 BC and 400 AD. It is now in the collection of the British Museum. Two thousand years after it was carved on this shoreline, it sits in a case in Bloomsbury, roughly seven and a half thousand kilometres from the tidal flats that gave it its subject matter.

## Bypassed Twice, Thriving Anyway

Cowichan Bay was the gateway for European settlement of the Cowichan and Chemainus valleys from the early 1860s, when a steamer service from Victoria was the main link for people and goods. Then the Esquimalt and Nanaimo Railway went elsewhere, and later the Island Highway did too. Being bypassed twice should have finished a place this small. It did not. The village kept going on sport and commercial salmon fishing and on log and lumber exports, developing the slightly stubborn character of a community that transport planners have twice declined to notice. Today the Island Highway and the Island Rail Corridor both run nearby, and Mount Tzouhalem rises to the north with its hiking trails and ecological reserve.

## Deepwater and Derelicts

The bay is a noted deepwater port, and tankers and container ships still swing at anchor off the village. That same depth has a downside: vessels get abandoned here and left to rot. The most notorious was the Dominion, still full of fuel, which dragged her anchor during a winter storm before finally being towed away for scrap after five years. Her replacement in the anchorage was a replica of the Beaver, brought in by the same tug that had removed the Dominion. Meanwhile the old economic base has been steadily giving way - to recreational boating, to a revived interest in boatbuilding, to an appreciation for the bay's history and ecology, and to visitors.

## Slow, Officially

The village's modern turn began in 2004, when a European-inspired bakery opened at the centre of the seaside strip and did well enough that others followed: an organic clothing store, a shop selling locally made cheese, an old-style ice cream parlour, an upscale restaurant, a potter's studio, and a run of small eateries and shops. Kayak rentals and whale-watching tours now work out of the same waterfront. Depending on the season you may see bald eagles, seals or herons without leaving the dock. In 2009 Cowichan Bay became the first Cittaslow community in Canada and North America - an international designation for towns that deliberately choose a slower pace, which for a village twice passed over by the fast route is a fairly satisfying outcome.

## Flight Context

Cowichan Bay lies at 48.75 N, 123.60 W on the east coast of southern Vancouver Island. Fly it at 2,000-3,500 ft: the bay opens east into Satellite Channel with Saltspring Island beyond, Mount Tzouhalem stands immediately north of the estuary, the Cowichan River mouth spreads into tidal flats at the head of the bay, and Duncan sits a short distance inland. Ships at anchor in the deepwater anchorage are a reliable visual marker. Victoria International (CYYJ) is roughly 20 km southeast over the Saanich Peninsula and Nanaimo (CYCD) about 40 km northwest. The channel funnels afternoon winds in summer, morning fog forms readily over the estuary in autumn, and the ridge north of the bay can hold cloud after the water has cleared.
