# Crofton, British Columbia

One morning in 2001, this Vancouver Island town woke up grey — a layer of mill ash on nearly every house. In December 2025, after sixty-eight years, the mill shut for good.

**Location:** 48.8638, -123.6457

**Categories:** Populated places in the Cowichan Valley Regional District, Ports and harbours of British Columbia, Southern Vancouver Island, Company towns in Canada, Populated places established in 1902

## Introduction

One day in 2001, Crofton woke up grey. A layer of ash had come down in the night and settled over the town, staining nearly every house on every street. The cause was not mysterious for long: improper burning at the pulp and paper mill on the waterfront, the single enterprise that had employed this place for four decades. The mill owners paid for a thorough cleanup and changed how they burned. But the incident crystallised something residents had lived with for years — a relationship with an industry that provided the paycheques and also, sometimes, the air. Twenty-four years later that argument ended in the only way it could. In December 2025, the mill closed for good.

## Henry Croft's Two Years

Crofton exists because one man had a copper mine and needed somewhere to process the ore. In 1900, Henry Croft bought land at Osborne Bay for a smelter and a townsite. Construction started in 1901 under the Northwestern Smelting and Refining Co., and 1902 was the year everything happened at once: the village was founded, the smelter fired up, and Croft extended the Lenora Mt. Sicker Railway down from his mine to the new works. It lasted months. By late 1902 the mining company was in receivership and the digging had stopped. The smelter ran on other people's ore until that ran out too, and closed in 1903. Britannia Mining & Smelting bought it in 1906 and a customs office operated in town from 1906 to 1909, but when copper prices collapsed in the Panic of 1907 the smelter shut again, in January 1908. Rumours of a big sawmill came to nothing. Henry Croft died in 1917 with his namesake town struggling to survive.

## Logs in the Water

What saved Crofton was not metal but timber. By the 1910s the Westholme Lumber Co. was hauling logs into town over the narrow-gauge line, and by the mid-1920s the Esquimalt and Nanaimo Railway had built in over a new Osborne Bay subdivision — later called the Crofton Spur — to load logs at the wharf. The process had its own rough theatre. Logging trains brought raw logs down out of the bush, the logs were barked in the water, and then they were lifted aboard ships bound for foreign mills. Industrial Timbers Ltd., which became British Columbia Forest Products, Western Forest Industries, and MacMillan Bloedel all shipped through here. The town settled into the role it would hold for a century: a deep-water loading point on a forested coast.

## Sixty-Eight Years of Pulp

The pulp and paper mill opened in 1957 and passed through a series of corporate owners, as mills in this province invariably do. It was the town — the reason for the ferry schedule, the tax base, the shift whistle that organised the week. After the 2001 ash fall, concern about emissions did not go quiet. On 17 September 2004, the musician Randy Bachman organised a Clean Air Concert to fund an independent study of what the mill was putting into the air, and the bill he assembled was extraordinary for a community of this size: the Barenaked Ladies, Neil Young, Tal Bachman, and Bachman himself. It had to be staged in Duncan, because Crofton had no venue big enough. The mill closed permanently in December 2025, its owner, Domtar, citing poor pulp prices and a lack of affordable raw material — the same two forces that have been closing mills up and down this coast.

## A Kilometre of Shoreline

The town has been quietly building an alternative for thirty years. Crofton's smelter wharf was converted in 1955 into the mainland-side terminal for the Salt Spring Island ferry, and BC Ferries still runs the route daily, which means a steady trickle of travellers passes through town with time to spare. In the early 1990s the Crofton Community Centre Society began building a scenic walkway along the shore. Phase one opened in 2002, the town's hundredth anniversary; all three phases were complete by 2014, and the Seawalk now runs more than a kilometre from the wharf and ferry terminal to Crofton Beach. Other pieces of ordinary infrastructure arrived on their own timetable: the town water supply switched from Crofton Lake to the Cowichan River in 2000, and in September 2006 Crofton was finally added to a regional bus network linking it to Duncan and Chemainus. Before that, the only public transit was a Victoria-to-Nanaimo bus that did not stop. For a community of roughly 1,450 people facing life after the mill, the walkway and the ferry are not decoration. They are the plan.

## Flight Context

Crofton lies at 48.8638 degrees north, 123.6457 degrees west, on Osborne Bay on the east coast of southern Vancouver Island, about 74 km north of Victoria and east of the Trans-Canada Highway (BC 1). Best viewed from 2,000 to 3,500 feet on a north-south track along the coast — the industrial waterfront, its deep-water wharf, and the ferry terminal make the town easy to identify from well offshore, and the BC Ferries route running east to Salt Spring Island gives a clean visual line across Stuart Channel. Salt Spring Island fills the horizon to the east; the Cowichan Valley opens inland to the west. Nearest airports: Nanaimo (CYCD) about 40 km north, Victoria International (CYYJ) about 65 km south, and Victoria Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH). Morning fog forms readily over Stuart Channel in autumn; afternoon inflow winds from the northwest are common through the summer months.
