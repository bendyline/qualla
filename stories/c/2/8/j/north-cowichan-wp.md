# North Cowichan

A district municipality of 32,000 that owns six mountains and a logging operation, and whose best-known town answered a mill closure by covering itself in paintings.

**Location:** 48.8247, -123.7192

**Categories:** Municipalities, British Columbia, Vancouver Island, Forestry, Public Art

## Introduction

In May 1982 the MacMillan Bloedel sawmill in Chemainus shut down, taking roughly 700 jobs with it. For a single-industry town on the east coast of Vancouver Island, that is usually the end of the story - the mill goes, the young people go, and what remains slowly empties out. Chemainus did something else. That same year the town launched a mural project, commissioning artists to paint the community's history directly onto its building walls: the logging camps, the sternwheelers, the people who had worked here. Thirty-three murals went up over the following years and thirty-two survive, and the tourists who come to walk between them now underwrite a good part of the local economy. It is one of the more unlikely municipal decisions in Canadian history. Faced with the collapse of its only industry, a company town hired painters.

## Six Mountains and a Logging Company

Chemainus is one of five communities inside the District of North Cowichan, and the district itself owns something no other municipality in the province does on this scale: a working forest. The Municipal Forest Reserve covers about 5,000 hectares - roughly a quarter of North Cowichan's entire land base - and takes in six mountains: Prevost, Sicker, Richards, Maple, Tzouhalem, and Stoney Hill. The municipality has logged it for decades, run it as a revenue source, and in recent years found itself in a public argument with its own residents about whether it should keep doing so. Many people who live here only discovered in the course of that debate that they were, collectively, shareholders in a logging operation. The reserve is simultaneously a source of municipal income, a recreation network, a research subject, and an unresolved question about what a community owes to the ground it stands on.

## What the Ice Left

The shape of this valley was set by the Fraser Glaciation, which ran from roughly 29,000 to 15,000 years ago and left ice up to thirty metres thick grinding through what is now the Cowichan Valley. What the glaciers left behind was fertile, workable ground, and the climate that settled over it afterward is close to the best in Canada for growing things. Sheltered from Pacific storms by the mountains of central Vancouver Island and the Olympic Peninsula beyond, North Cowichan sits in a genuine rain shadow: winters mild and damp with little snow, summers warm and dry. The Köppen classification is Csb - warm-summer Mediterranean, a category that applies to almost nowhere else in the country. Agriculture followed naturally and has never left.

## Five Places, One Council

At 193.98 square kilometres, North Cowichan is the largest incorporated municipality by land area on Vancouver Island, and it has the loose, distributed feel that implies. The district was established in 1873 and now holds 31,990 people spread across Chemainus in the north, Crofton on the water, Westholme in the farming middle, Maple Bay on its inlet, and the South End pressed up against the city of Duncan. Highway 1 runs the length of it as a divided arterial; Highway 18 branches west toward Lake Cowichan. Maple Bay's water aerodrome puts floatplanes on the water to downtown Vancouver, the Vancouver airport, Ganges on Salt Spring, and Bedwell Harbour. And in a small municipal signature that says a good deal about local temperament, North Cowichan has quietly built fourteen roundabouts.

## The Second Economy

The old drivers were furs, fish, minerals, and above all trees. Forestry still matters here, but it now shares the ledger with retail, construction, manufacturing, and health care - the mix of a place that has stopped depending on one employer. Much of what draws visitors is unabashedly about the first economy anyway. The BC Forest Discovery Centre interprets the industry that built the valley. The Pacific Northwest Raptors centre flies birds of prey for audiences. The Chemainus Theatre Festival stages comedies, musicals, and dramas in the town the murals saved, and the Cowichan Theatre programs year-round. It is a version of the resource-town afterlife that worked: the trees still come down, but they are no longer the only thing holding the place up.

## A Valley With Old Names

The names carried by the landscape here run much deeper than the 1873 incorporation date. Cowichan, Chemainus, Tzouhalem - these are Indigenous names attached to this valley long before a district council existed to administer it, and they remain in daily municipal use on road signs, mountain summits, and letterhead. The Municipal Forest Reserve debate has been one place where that older relationship to the land keeps surfacing, because a working forest is not only an asset on a balance sheet. It is also a watershed, a habitat, and a place people have walked for a very long time. North Cowichan has spent the last four decades learning, in public, how to hold several answers to that at once.

## Flight Context

North Cowichan spans 194 square kilometres centred near 48.825 N, 123.719 W on the east coast of southern Vancouver Island, between Duncan and Ladysmith. A viewing altitude of 3,000-5,000 feet MSL takes in the whole district: the Stuart Channel shoreline with Chemainus and Crofton on the water, the agricultural flats around Westholme, Maple Bay's sheltered inlet, and the forested ridges of the Municipal Forest Reserve - Prevost, Sicker, Richards, Maple, Tzouhalem, and Stoney Hill - rising inland. Mount Tzouhalem's cliff face above Cowichan Bay is the most distinctive terrain landmark; Highway 1 runs north-south as a clear divided line through the middle. Salt Spring Island lies east across the channel. Maple Bay water aerodrome (YAQ) handles regular floatplane service, Duncan Airport (CAM3) sits just south, Nanaimo (CYCD) is about 20 nm north, and Victoria International (CYYJ) about 35 nm southeast. Conditions are unusually favourable - this is a rain-shadow valley with dry, clear summers and mild winters - but morning valley fog is common in autumn.
