# Cowichan River

A Canadian Heritage River on Vancouver Island that ran so low its salmon had to be trucked upstream, and whose chinook then came back from a few hundred fish to record returns.

**Location:** 48.7667, -123.6330

**Categories:** Rivers of Vancouver Island, Southern Vancouver Island, Cowichan Valley

## Introduction

There was a stretch of years when the salmon had to be driven. In the decade leading up to 2014 the Cowichan River dropped so low in late summer that returning fish could not get up it, and the response - undignified, expensive, and entirely necessary - was to catch them and haul them upstream by truck. Meanwhile the Cowichan Valley Regional District put residents on water restrictions. A river on the wet side of a wet island, in a province people picture as permanently rain-soaked, could not reliably deliver its own fish to their spawning grounds. The Cowichan is a Canadian Heritage River, one of the country's officially recognised waterways. It has also been, for twenty years, one of its most closely watched patients.

## Lake to Bay

The river begins at Cowichan Lake and runs east to salt water at Cowichan Bay, dropping over Skutz Falls and cutting through Marie Canyon on the way. Its drainage basin covers 795 square kilometres - modest, which is part of the problem, because a small catchment has little stored water to spend in a dry September. Along the middle reaches lies Cowichan River Provincial Park, home to hundreds of animal species including the endangered Vancouver Island ermine and more than two hundred kinds of bird. Anglers know the river better than most Canadians know any river: rainbow and brown trout, three species of Pacific salmon, and the steelhead that people plan entire winters around. In 2024 it was ranked the second-best fishing destination in the country. There is even a Cowichan Herald Extraordinary at the Canadian Heraldic Authority, named for the river.

## Quw'utsun

This is Quw'utsun territory and has been for a very long time. Cowichan Tribes is the single largest First Nations band in British Columbia, with thousands of members and a core traditional territory of roughly 3,750 square kilometres, centred on this valley. The people speak Hul'q'umi'num, a dialect of Halkomelem. Historically the densest settlement ran along the lower river, from what is now Duncan down to Cowichan Bay - exactly where a salmon river is most generous. When the Indian Act imposed its administrative categories, seven distinct peoples with their own villages and their own names - Quamichan, Clemclemaluts, Comiaken, Khenipsen, Kilpahlas, Koksilah and Somena - were consolidated into a single band. The river those seven communities were built around is the same one now being nursed back to health, largely with their involvement.

## A Few Hundred Fish

In 2009, only a few hundred Cowichan chinook came back. For a river of this size and history that is close to the end of the line - not a bad year, but the sort of number that precedes a local extinction. What followed is one of the better stories on this coast. The Department of Fisheries and Oceans, Cowichan Tribes, and a stack of conservation and stewardship organisations worked the problem together over more than a decade: habitat, flows, harvest, hatchery practice. The fish responded. Returns climbed, and then kept climbing, until 2022, 2023 and 2024 each produced historically high chinook counts. Recovery on that scale is rare enough that people in the watershed talk about it carefully, as though saying it too confidently might jinx the run.

## Low Water, Warm Water

The pressures have not gone away, though one of them recently did: the pulp and paper mill at Crofton drew river water for decades before Domtar permanently closed it in early 2026. The valley itself still draws its own water, and summers keep arriving hotter. In July 2023, during drought, the Cowichan suffered a fish kill. Flow at the time was down to roughly 4.5 cubic metres per second. The provincial investigation found dangerous nutrient levels and floating brown algae below the wastewater outflow, and noted that fisheries staff who had previously described the effluent as resembling watery coffee were now calling it green and goopy. The Town of Lake Cowichan's sewage treatment facility was found to have been outside its permit requirements on multiple occasions that year. The penalty issued was $700. The chinook comeback and the fish kill happened on the same river, two summers apart, which is a reasonable summary of where things stand.

## Flight Context

The Cowichan River runs roughly east from Cowichan Lake to Cowichan Bay on southeastern Vancouver Island, with a midpoint near 48.7667 N, 123.6330 W. Follow it at 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL: the lake is a long finger of water at the western end, the river threading east through forest and the cut of Marie Canyon before opening into the farmland of the lower valley around Duncan and finally into the tidal flats and log booms of Cowichan Bay. Mount Tzouhalem stands over the estuary; the Strait of Georgia and Salt Spring Island lie beyond. Duncan Airport (CAM3) sits about 3 nm from the mid-river reference point, Victoria International (CYYJ) roughly 11 nm southeast, and Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) about 20 nm north. Expect valley fog on cool mornings, channelled winds along the river corridor, and low cloud against the hills west toward the lake through the winter months.
