# Mount Maxwell Provincial Park

The Royal Navy named this Salt Spring Island mountain for an admiral; the neighbours named it for a cattle rancher — and both names won, on a summit guarding Canada's largest Garry oak meadow.

**Location:** 48.8042, -123.5158

**Categories:** Provincial parks of British Columbia, Salt Spring Island, 1938 establishments in British Columbia, Protected areas established in 1938

## Introduction

The Royal Navy got there first. Around 1859 the Admiralty surveyor George Henry Richards put this Salt Spring Island summit on the chart as Mount Baynes, honouring Admiral Sir Robert Lambert Baynes. The people who actually lived beneath it had other ideas. John Maxwell registered a claim near Burgoyne Bay in 1861 and ran cattle on the slopes with his partner James Lunney until his death in 1887, and to everyone on the island it was simply Maxwell's mountain. Officialdom held out for half a century before giving in: Mount Maxwell became the accepted name in 1911. When the park was established in 1938, someone brokered a peace. The mountain is Maxwell. The summit is Baynes Peak.

## What You See From Baynes Peak

The park exists because of the view, and that is stated plainly in its founding purpose. From 580 metres the whole inland sea opens up at once: the mountainous spine of Vancouver Island across Sansum Narrows to the west, Burgoyne Bay directly below like a green slot cut into the island's flank, the Gulf Islands scattered north and east across Trincomali and Stuart channels, and on a clear day the Coast Mountains of the British Columbia mainland standing up behind all of it. Ferries thread the channels far below, small and silent from up here. There are two ways to arrive — a gravel road that climbs to near the top, or trails coming up from the northeast or from Burgoyne Bay Provincial Park to the south — and which one you choose largely determines whether the view feels earned.

## The Rarest Forest in Canada

The mountain's western flank holds something scarcer than the view. Garry oak savannah — open, sunlit meadow studded with gnarled oaks and carpeted in spring camas — once covered much of this rain-shadowed corner of the coast. It is now among Canada's most threatened ecosystems, squeezed out by development, agriculture, invasive grasses, and the simple fact that the land it likes is the land people want. It is also not, in the deepest sense, a wild landscape: Coast Salish peoples maintained these meadows for millennia through deliberate burning and the cultivation of camas, whose bulbs were a staple food. Left alone, Douglas fir crowds in and the oaks lose. The Mount Maxwell Ecological Reserve was created on 2 June 1972 to protect these stands, and expanded in 2004 to take in what is now the largest Garry oak meadow in the country.

## Small Park, Large Landscape

Mount Maxwell Provincial Park is only 231 hectares, which by the standards of BC parks is barely a rounding error. Its significance comes from what it touches. The ecological reserve lies immediately to the west and Burgoyne Bay Provincial Park to the south, and together the three form a continuous block of more than 1,100 hectares running from an alpine-feeling summit down to tidewater. That continuity is the whole point. Species do not respect boundary lines, and an oak meadow protected in isolation is a fragment waiting to fail — but a protected corridor from ridge to shoreline gives deer, raptors, pollinators, and the oaks themselves room to persist. On an island where waterfront land carries the prices it does, assembling that block took decades and a good deal of stubbornness.

## The Cliff Edge

Baynes Peak ends abruptly. The summit's west side drops away in bluffs above Burgoyne Bay, and the updraft along that face is reliable enough that turkey vultures and bald eagles work it without seeming to move a feather. Arbutus trees lean out over the edge with their peeling cinnamon bark, a species that grows almost nowhere else in Canada. Standing there in late spring, with camas turning the meadow blue behind you and a thousand feet of air in front, it is easy to understand why a government in 1938 — not an era known for setting land aside — decided this particular hilltop should belong to everybody.

## Flight Context

Mount Maxwell rises to 580 metres at 48.804°N, 123.516°W on the southwest side of Salt Spring Island, the largest of British Columbia's Gulf Islands. Fly it at 3,000 to 5,000 feet for the best sense of the landscape, or lower along Sansum Narrows if conditions allow — but be aware that the bluffs generate mechanical turbulence and strong updrafts on windy days, and the terrain rises fast from the water. Landmarks are unmistakable: Burgoyne Bay is a narrow finger of water cutting northeast into Salt Spring directly below the summit, Sansum Narrows separates the island from Vancouver Island to the west with the Cowichan Valley beyond, and Ganges Harbour sits on the island's east side. Mount Tuam anchors the island's southern tip. Ganges Harbour Water Aerodrome (CAX6) serves Salt Spring directly with regular floatplane traffic from Vancouver; Victoria International (CYYJ) is roughly 25 nautical miles southeast, and Nanaimo (CYCD) lies to the northwest. The Gulf Islands sit in the Olympic and Vancouver Island rain shadow, giving this area some of the driest, sunniest weather on the BC coast — summer visibility is often excellent, though morning fog forms readily in the channels through autumn.
