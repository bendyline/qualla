# Quamichan

The largest and wealthiest of the eight Cowichan villages, stretching five kilometres along the Cowichan River, whose name comes from a figure in a Cowichan story and whose population was cut down by epidemic disease within two generations.

**Location:** 48.7784, -123.6718

**Categories:** Coast Salish, Southern Vancouver Island, First Nations history, Cowichan Valley

## Introduction

Quamichan was not a village in the sense the word usually carries. It ran five kilometres. Houses and structures lined the Cowichan River from a point a kilometre above Quamichan Creek almost the whole way down to the neighbouring village of Comiaken, and the first census taken after European contact put roughly 1,700 people there. That made Kw'amutsun the largest and wealthiest of the eight Cowichan villages at the opening of the colonial period — a nation with a river, a fishery and a reputation that made its neighbours and the Hudson's Bay Company both pay attention.

## The Warm Place

The Cowichan Valley on southeastern Vancouver Island takes its English name from Qu'wutsun, which means warm place — an accurate description of a broad, sheltered, south-facing valley with a temperate microclimate and a large salmon river running through it. Quamichan sat near what is now the city of Duncan, with Salt Spring Island and the Gulf Islands out to the east. The Quamichan are one of several Cowichan-area peoples now governed together as Cowichan Tribes. The land around the old village carries a second, purely bureaucratic name as well: the Quamichan Land District, a unit of the cadastral survey system by which the province records land titles. Two naming systems lie over the same ground, and they describe quite different things.

## The Name in the Story

The village's original name, kwómetsen, means humpback or hunchback, and it comes from a figure in a Cowichan story — a hunchbacked cannibal giantess who kept children in a basket and put pitch over their eyes before eating them. Every culture keeps a story like this, told to children who are old enough to be warned and not yet old enough to wander off safely, and the details survive precisely because they are frightening. What is unusual is that the name of the creature became the name of the place, and then, in the hands of English speakers, was flattened into Quamichan. The word on the road sign is an anglicization of an anglicization. Underneath it is a story that the Cowichan have been telling far longer than anyone has been signposting.

## Tzouhalem at Fort Victoria

Quamichan's standing among the Cowichan villages rested partly on wealth and partly on military capability, and no figure embodied the latter more than Tzouhalem. Trained as a warrior by his grandmother and known through the region for combative, unruly conduct, he commanded the First Nations force that assaulted the Hudson's Bay Company post at Fort Victoria in 1844 — not a raid in passing but a sustained two-day attack on the most fortified European position on the island. The Company had arrived, in Cowichan eyes, without asking, and had begun behaving as though the coast were something it could administer. Tzouhalem's fighting reputation is generally credited with helping make Quamichan the largest and wealthiest of the eight villages. His name still sits on the landscape: the mountain east of Duncan that the Cowichan called Shkewetsen was renamed Mount Tzouhalem after the Quamichan chief who lived on it.

## What the Epidemics Did

The most consequential thing that happened to Quamichan was not a battle. Smallpox and other introduced diseases moved through the Cowichan Valley in the nineteenth century and did what they did everywhere on this coast. A population of roughly a thousand in the mid-1800s stood at three hundred by 1901 and two hundred and sixty by 1909. Those are not casualty figures from a single event; they are the shape of two generations of repeated loss — families, houses, entire lines of knowledge, the people who knew the songs and the fishing places and the protocols that made a five-kilometre village work. A community that had been the wealthiest of eight was reduced by roughly three-quarters while the paperwork of land districts and reserves was being drawn up around it.

## The Dances Continued

Missionaries and government agents spent decades trying to end the potlatch and the ceremonies they classified as pagan, and Canada made the potlatch illegal outright. The Cowichan Winter Dances went on anyway. Observers through the early twentieth century described them as vibrant, and the annual gathering of kin and village for dance and ceremony continued well into that century — which is a plain statement of fact and also a measure of stubbornness. Daily life changed around the ceremonies. Where people had lived by fishing, hunting, and gathering wild berries and roots, by the 1900s they were also farming, working on the railway, and taking shifts in the canneries that lined the coast. The Quamichan adapted to an economy that had been imposed on them without conceding the part of life that was not for sale.

## Flight Context

Quamichan lies in the Cowichan Valley on southeastern Vancouver Island at roughly 48.78N, 123.67W, immediately east of the city of Duncan. From the air, follow the Cowichan River from Cowichan Lake southeast to its estuary at Cowichan Bay; the old village site occupies the lower river between Quamichan Creek and Comiaken. Quamichan Lake sits just north of Duncan as a clear landmark, with Mount Tzouhalem (536 m) rising about 4km east-northeast of Duncan above the valley floor. The broad farmed flats of the valley contrast sharply with the forested ridges on either side. Nearest airports: Duncan Airport (CAM3) about 2nm west, Victoria International (CYYJ) roughly 25nm southeast, and Nanaimo (CYCD) about 20nm north, with floatplane traffic into Cowichan Bay and Maple Bay. Best viewed between 2,000 and 4,000 feet. Valley fog is common on autumn mornings and typically burns off by late morning.
