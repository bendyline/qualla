# Mount Tzouhalem

A mountain above Duncan named first for sunlight, then for the Cowichan Valley's most argued-over man, and topped for sixty years by a cross that someone cut down with a grinder in July 2021.

**Location:** 48.7783, -123.6140

**Categories:** mountains, indigenous-history, coast-salish, vancouver-island, contested-monuments

## Introduction

The first name this mountain carried was about light, not people. Shkewetsen, the local First Nations called it -- basking, or warming in the sun -- which is exactly what the south-facing bluffs above Duncan do all summer, holding heat in the rock long after the valley below has gone into shadow. The name it carries now belongs to a man who was banished to it, and who has been argued about ever since. The mountain itself is modest, well under a thousand metres, rising four kilometres east-northeast of Duncan in the Cowichan Valley. Almost nothing else about it is modest.

## Fire and Camas

Long before the mountain acquired its current name, its slopes were a working landscape. This is a historical harvesting site for Vancouver Island Coast Salish peoples, and specifically a camas ground -- the blue-flowered lily whose bulb was a staple food and a trade commodity across the region. Camas needs open, sunlit meadow to thrive, and the meadows here did not simply occur. They were deliberately and repeatedly burned to raise yields and maintain the open, park-like habitat the plant requires. Early European visitors to southern Vancouver Island described the landscape as looking like an English gentleman's park and assumed they had found wilderness. They had walked into a garden that was several thousand years old and tended with fire.

## The Man They Renamed It For

Tzouhalem was a Quamichan chief, born to a Quamichan father and a Comiaken mother, and trained in warfare by his grandmother. The accounts that reached print describe a man of extraordinary combativeness whose fighting probably helped make Quamichan the largest and wealthiest of the Cowichan villages -- and whose killings eventually led his own people to banish him. He spent his last years in a cave on the side of this mountain, with fourteen wives, many of them widowed by his own hand. In 1859, attempting to take another wife from Penelakut Island, he was killed by her or by her husband. Those are the stories as the written record has them, filtered through nineteenth-century settlers and generations of retelling. They are not the whole account. Tzouhalem came of age while smallpox was gutting the southern Island nations, leaving survivors exposed to raids, at precisely the moment British colonists began pressing for Cowichan land. In 2021, the Cowichan Tribes filmmaker Harold Joe -- Xulputstun -- co-directed a feature documentary that put the legend under examination rather than simply retelling it, playing Tzouhalem himself in the reenactments. Joe's view is blunt: the figure handed down as a tyrant who hated his own people is not the man his community remembers.

## Target Practice and Spelling Reform

The Royal Navy found the mountain useful in a different way. HMS Trincomalee, of the Pacific Squadron, used Mount Tzouhalem as the target for her cannon to announce her arrival in Cowichan Bay -- a naval calling card fired at a sacred hill, with whatever that communicated to the people watching from below. British surveyors then spent a century and a half failing to spell it. The maps give Tzohailim Hill in 1855 and Tzohailin Hill in 1864. In 1911 the Geographical Names Board of Canada settled on Tzuhalem Mountain; in 1950 that became Mount Tzuhalem. Only in 2000 was the spelling changed to Mount Tzouhalem, adopting the local traditional form supported by the Cowichan Tribes band government. It took ninety years of official cartography to arrive at the version the people who live here had been using all along.

## The Cross, and the Grinder

Since the 1970s the bluff at the top carried a cross, first wooden, hauled up as part of a Good Friday stations-of-the-cross pilgrimage. Weather and vandals took the wooden ones repeatedly, and in the late 1980s a steel cross went up in their place. It became a valley landmark, visible for miles, and a long-running source of unease -- a Christian monument standing on a mountain at the heart of Cowichan Tribes traditional territory. During the night of 16 to 17 July 2021, someone went up with an angle grinder and cut it off at the base. No one claimed responsibility, and the reason has never been established. The timing was its own sort of statement: the removal came days after the Penelakut Tribe, on an island visible from this coast, announced the location of more than 160 undocumented graves at the former Kuper Island residential school. North Cowichan's mayor called the cutting a sad piece of vandalism. In February 2022, a replacement cross appeared at the summit, installed without the landowners' permission, and the argument resumed where it had left off.

## What You Climb It For

Today the mountain is one of the best-used pieces of recreational ground on southeastern Vancouver Island, laced with hiking and mountain-biking trails climbing through second growth to the open, sun-warmed bluffs that gave the mountain its first name. From the top the Cowichan Valley spreads out below -- farmland, Quamichan Lake, the estuary and the boats at Cowichan Bay, the Gulf Islands beyond, and on a clear day the mainland mountains behind them. The bluffs remain culturally significant to Cowichan Tribes members, who continue to use the area. Most people who come up here for the view do not know whose cave they are walking above. The mountain has been, in turn, a food source, a refuge, a place of exile, a gunnery mark, a pilgrimage, and a flashpoint. It is still warming in the sun.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.78 N, 123.61 W, four kilometres east-northeast of Duncan in the Cowichan Valley of southeastern Vancouver Island. The mountain is well under 1,000 m but stands out sharply because it rises directly from flat farmland: look for a forested ridge with pale, open rock bluffs on its south and southwest faces, immediately north of the Cowichan Bay estuary and east of Quamichan Lake. Duncan's grid sits to the west, Maple Bay and Sansum Narrows to the east, and the Gulf Islands beyond. Best viewed at 2,500-4,000 ft AGL in afternoon light, when the south-facing bluffs are lit and the estuary mudflats show their channels. Victoria International (CYYJ) lies about 13 nm southeast across Saanich Inlet; Nanaimo Airport (CYCD) is roughly 19 nm north-northwest. Expect valley inflow winds in the afternoon, mechanical turbulence in the lee of the ridge, and morning fog pooling over the Cowichan estuary.
