# San Juan Island National Historical Park

Two army camps at opposite ends of an island, kept there for twelve years by a boundary dispute that began with a pig in a potato patch and ended with a German emperor.

**Location:** 48.4642, -123.0204

**Categories:** National historical parks of the United States, Parks in San Juan County, Washington, Pig War (1859), National Historic Landmarks in Washington (state), National Park Service areas in Washington (state), Coast Salish history

## Introduction

On 15 June 1859, Lyman Cutlar found a pig eating his potatoes and shot it. He was twenty-seven, an American squatter on land the Hudson's Bay Company was running as a sheep farm, and the pig belonged to the company's agent, Charles Griffin. Cutlar offered to replace the animal or pay for it. Griffin wanted a hundred dollars, an absurd sum for a hog and a deliberate assertion of who owned this island. Within six weeks the United States Army had sixty men ashore under Captain George Pickett, the Royal Navy had warships in the channel, and two of the most powerful countries on earth were one nervous officer away from a shooting war over a boundary line nobody had bothered to settle. They stayed like that, camped at opposite ends of San Juan Island, for the next twelve years. The pig remained the only fatality.

## Eleven Thousand Years Before the Pig

None of this was empty country. People have lived in these islands for roughly eleven thousand years, since the continental ice sheet withdrew at the end of the last glaciation, and the six central Coast Salish nations descend from them. Archaeological evidence places hunting and gathering here six to eight thousand years ago, and shell middens lie under the ground at both American Camp and English Camp, which means both armies pitched their tents on top of villages. Smallpox arrived with European contact in the 1770s and killed people here in numbers the record can only estimate. By the time Britain and the United States began arguing over who owned the island, the population that had actually occupied it longest had already been devastated by a disease neither country brought deliberately and neither did much about.

## Sixty Men and a Squadron

The American homesteaders asked for military protection after the Cutlar affair and got it, which is how a dispute over livestock became an international incident. Pickett landed his infantry at the island's southern end. The British answered with Royal Navy ships, and the officer commanding them had orders that, if executed literally, would have started a war. He did not execute them literally. That refusal, repeated on both sides by men who understood what was at stake and what was not, is the actual story of the Pig War. Cooler heads arranged a joint military occupation: both nations would keep a camp on San Juan Island until diplomacy could sort out the boundary. Nobody expected that arrangement to last more than a season.

## Twelve Years of Neighbours

It lasted from 1859 to 1872. British Royal Marines held a camp on the island's northwest shore from 1860, in a sheltered cove with a formal garden and a blockhouse, while the American garrison occupied a wind-scoured prairie above the Strait of Juan de Fuca. The two sides built a road between them. They visited each other. The Americans in San Juan village did business with both camps, which was excellent for the village. Among the American officers was Henry Martyn Robert, a young engineer who would later write Robert's Rules of Order, the manual that governs how committees argue without descending into chaos. It is difficult to imagine a better apprenticeship for that particular book than twelve years of armed men agreeing not to shoot.

## Arbitrated in Berlin

The 1871 Treaty of Washington finally handed the question to a neutral party, and the neutral party was Kaiser Wilhelm I of Germany. He considered the two competing channels and in 1872 ruled that the boundary ran through Haro Strait, the western channel, which put the San Juan Islands in the United States. The British struck their camp and left. Both sites were designated a National Historic Landmark in 1961, and on 9 September 1966 President Lyndon Johnson signed the law creating San Juan Island National Historical Park to preserve them together, an American park that exists to commemorate a dispute with Britain that both countries chose not to win by force.

## The Butterfly That Came Back

The prairie at American Camp turns out to be doing something no other place on earth is doing. It holds the world's only viable population of the island marble butterfly, a small pale insect considered extinct for ninety years until it was rediscovered in 1998 and listed as endangered in 2020. Foxes hunt rabbits across the same grass. Bald eagles, northern harriers, ospreys, great horned owls, harlequin ducks and goldfinches work the park's edges, and orca, humpback and gray whales pass offshore. Trails climb Mount Finlayson at American Camp and Young Hill at English Camp, with Bell Point and the Mitchell Hill network below. South Beach opens onto the Strait of Juan de Fuca with the Olympic Mountains filling the southern horizon, and Fourth of July Beach faces the calmer water of Griffin Bay, named for the man whose pig started all of it.

## Flight Context

San Juan Island National Historical Park occupies two separate sites on San Juan Island at roughly 48.464N, 123.020W, the westernmost of the main San Juan Islands in northwest Washington. American Camp sits on the open, treeless prairie of the island's southern tip between the Strait of Juan de Fuca and Griffin Bay, easily identified from the air by the pale grassland and the long sweep of South Beach. English Camp lies about 15 kilometres northwest on Garrison Bay, a sheltered green cove on the island's northwest side. Haro Strait separates the island from Vancouver Island, with Victoria visible on the far shore about 20 kilometres west. The Olympic Mountains rise across the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the south, and Mount Baker stands 10,781 feet to the east on clear days. Friday Harbor Airport (KFHR) is on the island itself, with Bellingham (KBLI) and Victoria International (CYYJ) nearby. Best viewed at 2,000 to 4,000 feet AGL. Expect strong afternoon westerlies funnelling through Haro Strait and frequent low stratus in summer mornings.
