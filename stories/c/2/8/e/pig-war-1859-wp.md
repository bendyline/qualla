# Pig War (1859)

Thirteen years to the day after a treaty left the water boundary ambiguous, an American farmer shot a British pig in his potato patch and brought two empires within one order to war over the San Juan Islands.

**Location:** 48.4616, -123.0066

**Categories:** military-history, san-juan-islands, washington-state, border-disputes, national-parks

## Introduction

On 15 June 1846 the Oregon Treaty settled where Britain ended and the United States began, running the line west along the 49th parallel and then south through the middle of the channel separating the continent from Vancouver Island. On 15 June 1859 - thirteen years later to the day - an American settler named Lyman Cutlar walked out to his potato patch on San Juan Island, found a black pig eating his crop again, and shot it. The pig belonged to Charles Griffin, the Hudson's Bay Company man who ran a sheep ranch on the island for the British. Cutlar offered ten dollars. Griffin said the animal was a prize breeder and wanted a hundred. Cutlar refused to pay, and within two months there were 461 American soldiers with fourteen cannon on that island facing five Royal Navy warships carrying 2,140 men and seventy guns. No one was killed. The only casualty of the Pig War was the pig.

## Two Straits, One Sentence

The trouble was a single clause. The treaty put the boundary through the middle of the channel between the mainland and Vancouver Island, then southerly through that channel and the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the sea. But there were two channels it could plausibly mean: Haro Strait, running along the western side of the San Juan Islands, and Rosario Strait, running along their eastern side. Choose Haro and the islands are American. Choose Rosario and they are British. In 1846 nobody negotiating in Washington or London had reliable charts of the place. The best maps available were George Vancouver's of 1798 and the American naval survey published by Charles Wilkes in 1845, and both are muddy precisely where clarity was needed - around southeastern Vancouver Island, the Gulf Islands, and the approaches to Haro Strait. Two governments signed a description of a channel neither of them could locate.

## Talking Themselves to a Standstill

A joint Boundary Commission was appointed in 1856 to sort it out. The British sent James Charles Prevost as First Commissioner with George Henry Richards and William A. G. Young; the Americans sent Archibald Campbell with John Parke and William J. Warren. They first met on 27 June 1857 aboard the Royal Navy warship HMS Satellite. Prevost argued that the treaty's channel had to satisfy three tests - it must separate the continent from Vancouver Island, it must carry the boundary southerly, and it must be navigable - and that only Rosario qualified. Campbell answered that Rosario separated the San Juans from Lummi, Cypress, and Fidalgo Islands rather than the continent from Vancouver Island, that southerly was meant loosely, and that Haro was in any case the wider and more direct passage. Prevost produced American maps drawn by John C. Fremont and by Oregon's Surveyor General John B. Preston that showed the line running through Rosario. At their sixth meeting on 3 December 1857 he offered a compromise: a line through San Juan Channel, giving the Americans every major island except San Juan itself. Campbell declined. The commission adjourned, having established only that neither man would move.

## Escalation Over a Potato Patch

Sovereignty being unsettled, both sides simply acted as though it were settled in their favour. The Hudson's Bay Company turned San Juan into a sheep station; by mid-1859 somewhere between twenty-five and twenty-nine American settlers had also taken up ground there. Cutlar's pig ended the pretence of coexistence. Captain George Pickett - a Virginian who four years later would lead the doomed charge at Gettysburg for the Confederacy - landed the first small American force. Reinforcements followed under Colonel Silas Casey until the American position held 461 men and fourteen guns. Facing them was a British flotilla under Captain Geoffrey Hornby, a rising officer who would end his career as Admiral of the Fleet. Hornby had orders that could have started a shooting war and declined to act on them before his admiral arrived. When Rear Admiral Robert L. Baynes reached the scene he was blunt with Governor James Douglas: he would not involve two great nations in a war over a squabble about a pig.

## Twelve Years of Sharing an Island

President James Buchanan sent General Winfield Scott, the commander-in-chief of the US Army, west to defuse it. Scott proposed joint military occupation until someone settled the boundary properly, and both governments agreed that November. For the next twelve years a British camp and an American camp coexisted on one small island, reportedly on cordial terms, while the United States fought a civil war that pulled Pickett south and made the whole affair look trivially small. One of the American officers stationed there, Second Lieutenant Henry Martyn Robert, built an earthwork on the island that still stands as the best-preserved fortification of its kind in the country - and later, applying a different sort of discipline to a different sort of dispute, published the first edition of Robert's Rules of Order in 1876. The boundary finally went to arbitration under Kaiser Wilhelm I, whose three-man commission sat in Geneva for nearly a year and ruled for the United States on 21 October 1872: the line would run through Haro Strait. The Royal Marines withdrew on 25 November 1872. The Americans were gone by July 1874. Both camps are preserved today inside San Juan Island National Historical Park.

## Flight Context

The American Camp end of the Pig War story lies at 48.462 degrees north, 123.007 degrees west, on the southern tip of San Juan Island, Washington; the British camp sits at Garrison Bay on the island's northwest side. A viewing altitude of 3,000 to 5,000 feet takes in the whole geography of the dispute at once: San Juan Island in the middle, Haro Strait to the west with Vancouver Island beyond it, and Rosario Strait to the east toward Anacortes. That single frame is the argument. American Camp's open prairie and the long gravel spit of Cattle Point are unmistakable against the forested rest of the island. Friday Harbor (KFHR) is on the island itself, Orcas Island (KORS) is a few miles north, Bellingham International (KBLI) lies 30 miles east on the mainland, and Victoria International (CYYJ) is 20 miles west in British Columbia - and crossing between them means crossing an international border. Expect strong tidal rips visible in Haro Strait, frequent summer morning fog burning off by midday, and brisk channel winds through the passes.
