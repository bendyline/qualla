# Equality Colony

A socialist colony founded near Edison in 1897 by people who intended to convert Washington State — and then the continent — and who lost it to an argument, a barn fire, and a court order.

**Location:** 48.5889, -122.4229

**Categories:** Bellamyism, History of Skagit County, Washington, Politically motivated migrations, Populated places established in 1897, Utopian communities in Washington, 1897 establishments in Washington (state)

## Introduction

The plan was to convert the United States to socialism, and it was going to begin with fifteen people on a muddy quarter-section outside Edison, Washington. This was not a commune withdrawing from the world. The Brotherhood of the Cooperative Commonwealth had a strategy: plant enough cooperative colonies in one sparsely populated western state to swing its elections, socialize it through its own legislature, and let the example spread. The first fifteen settlers arrived on 1 November 1897, named the place Equality after a novel published that year, and began building.

## A Strategy Borrowed From Kansas

The idea came out of New England reform circles in the mid-1890s. F.G.R. Gordon of the Socialist Labor Party proposed massing socialist colonies in one western state to win local control; he had Texas in mind, but Cyrus Field Willard, Richard J. Hinton, and Wilfred P. Borland settled on Washington instead. Norman Wallace Lermond, a journalist and farmer from Warren, Maine, and Ed Pelton took up the organizing. Lermond's explicit model was the New England Emigrant Aid Company, which had shipped abolitionists into Kansas before the Civil War to keep it a free state. Henry Demarest Lloyd gave modest money, and the first local union was organized at Warren on 18 October 1895.

## Seven Officers and a Mail Referendum

The Brotherhood was constituted the way a scattered movement had to be in 1896 — by mail referendum, conducted through the pages of the Coming Nation. A constitution and seven officers were announced that September, with three stated aims: educate people in socialism, unite socialists in one association, and plant colonies in one state until that state was socialized. Lloyd, elected president, promptly declined to serve. New elections in January 1897 installed the radical minister Myron W. Reed and attempted to draft Eugene V. Debs as national organizer. Debs never served, though the idea interested him enough that he met Lermond in Terre Haute that May. When Debs's circle, pulled toward electoral politics by Victor Berger, founded the Social Democracy of America that June, the Brotherhood declined to join — rejecting the class-struggle premise outright, preferring a movement open to middle-class professionals and, as they put it, "everybody who believes in cooperation." The refusal cost them Debs and a share of their membership.

## Two Miles Northeast of Edison

By mid-1897 the Brotherhood had about 2,200 members across 130 local unions. Lermond toured Tennessee and Arkansas, then announced Washington as the target: thin population, liberal inclinations, a Populist governor rumoured to be sympathetic, and local unions already on the ground with sites available. The South was ruled out as too thoroughly settled and, in the organizers' own phrase, burdened with "the Negro question" — the language of their era, and a revealing admission from a movement promising universal cooperation. Pelton left Maine on 1 September and on 15 October put $100 down with Mathias Decker, a conservative Skagit County farmer, for 280 acres at Blanchard. Further purchases brought the colony to 600 acres by the following summer. The first structure was named Fort Bellamy.

## Mud Versus Headquarters

The trouble started almost immediately, and it was about who was sleeping where. The national board leased a headquarters in the town of Edison and stayed there, while the colonists lived in crude, half-finished buildings on the site and watched their contributions fund an administration in town. They also thought the board too preoccupied with founding further colonies when Equality itself was unfinished. At a contentious meeting that April, a proposal for a second colony at Edison went down, and an amendment granting Equality full independence in its internal affairs carried 298 to 176. Lermond resigned in August and went back to Maine. By February 1899 the headquarters had moved to the colony and the Brotherhood as a national movement was effectively over: membership collapsed from three thousand to around three hundred.

## What They Actually Built

As a settlement rather than a revolution, Equality worked for a while. The colonists put up two large apartment buildings, a barn, a dining hall, a school, a public hall, a printing office, a sawmill, a blacksmith shop, an apiary, and a bakery. Around a hundred people were still there in the spring of 1903. One of them was Harry Ault, who arrived at fourteen in April 1898, learned printing and journalism on Industrial Freedom, and rose to edit it before it folded in December 1902. Two decades later he was editing the Seattle Union Record during the 1919 general strike. Whatever else the colony failed to produce, it produced him.

## The Barn

The end arrived through a schism. In autumn 1904 Alexander Horr turned up promoting Theodor Hertzka's "Freeland" scheme; he returned the next year with followers who swelled the population by 30 percent, and by summer 1905 had pushed through drastic revisions to the constitution. The older residents fought it, the colony divided bitterly, and the disputes occasionally turned violent. On the night of 6 February 1906 someone set fire to several buildings. The worst loss was the barn, which burned to the ground and killed most of the colony's cattle. No one was ever identified, and each faction accused the other. A suit to dissolve was filed in Skagit County, the Brotherhood went into receivership, and on 1 June 1907 the land sold for $12,500. A few families stayed in the district on plots they owned outright — which was, in the end, exactly the arrangement the colony had been founded to replace.

## Flight Context

The Equality Colony site lies at roughly 48.589°N, 122.423°W, at Blanchard in Skagit County, Washington, about two miles northeast of the hamlet of Edison. Best viewed from 1,500 to 3,000 feet. Nothing of the colony stands, so navigate by the landscape: this is the northern edge of the Skagit delta, a table-flat grid of diked farm fields ending abruptly against the forested wall of Blanchard Mountain and the Chuckanut range, with Samish Bay and Padilla Bay opening west toward the San Juan Islands. Chuckanut Drive threads the narrow shelf between the hills and the water just north — one of the most recognizable stretches of road on this coast from the air. Bow and Edison are two small crossroads communities in the fields below. Skagit Regional (KBVS) at Burlington is about 8 nautical miles south, Bellingham International (KBLI) roughly 12 nautical miles north, and Anacortes (KOTS) about 12 nautical miles southwest. Blanchard Mountain is a well-used paragliding launch — expect free-flight traffic on good thermal days. Conditions bring dense delta fog on calm autumn mornings, strong outflow winds down the Skagit and Samish valleys in winter, and reliable ridge lift along the Chuckanuts in a westerly.
