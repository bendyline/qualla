# Chuckanut Bay

A crescent of water south of Bellingham folded into a syncline of fifty-million-year-old floodplain sandstone, where nine different industries were tried in eighty years and the honeycombed rock outlasted every one of them.

**Location:** 48.6804, -122.4957

**Categories:** bays-and-coastline, washington-state, geology, local-history, indigenous-place-names

## Introduction

Stone. Salmon. Logs. Sailing ships. Bricks. Bootleg whiskey. Coal. Chickens. Between 1880 and 1964, somebody tried each of these as a way to make a living out of Chuckanut Bay, and every one of the ventures failed, moved, or simply stopped. The quarry ran from 1880 to 1908. The cannery went bankrupt two years after it opened. The brick and tile plant lasted a few years after 1915. The chicken ranch, improbably the most durable of them, made it from 1936 to 1964. What has outlasted all of it is the thing the industries were chewing on: a three-mile crescent of water on the eastern shore of the Salish Sea, hemmed by pale sandstone eaten into honeycomb by salt and wind.

## Fifty Million Years of Floodplain

The bay is a fold. It formed along the axis of a plunging syncline as the Chuckanut Mountains rose, and the rock doing the folding is the Chuckanut Formation - an extraordinarily thick stack of sandstone, conglomerate, mudstone, and coal laid down about fifty million years ago, in the Eocene, on subsiding floodplains near a much warmer Washington coast. That origin explains almost everything about the place. It explains the coal that was mined at Governors Point in 1921, because coal is what buried swamp forests become. It explains the building stone that came out of the quarries here and went into the Armory and the Pike Block in Bellingham, and into structures all around Puget Sound. And along the shoreline it produces tafoni: pockets and cavities where salt crystals, left as seawater evaporates, wedge between sand grains and prise them loose one at a time. The rock at Teddy Bear Cove and along the bay's edge looks chewed, honeycombed, almost organic.

## A Name for What the Water Does

Lieutenant Juan Francisco de Eliza put the bay on a Spanish chart in 1791 as Puerto del Socorro - the harbour of relief or help. That name did not stick. On 1 December 1852 Captain Henry Roeder chose the one that did, borrowing it from the Coast Salish languages already spoken here. In Nooksack the word refers to Chuckanut Creek and to the Nooksack camping ground at its mouth, and according to the anthropologist Allan Richardson and the linguist Brent Galloway it carries the sense of a beach where the tide goes way out. The linguist William Bright traced the English spelling through the neighbouring Lummi dialect of North Straits Salish, which had taken it from Nooksack in the first place. In 2005 Wayne Suttles proposed a different root in Northern Straits, and allowed that both forms may simply have coexisted. Three languages and two centuries later, the most likely translation of the bay's name is still a description of its mudflats at low water.

## Eight Ways to Make a Living

The Chuckanut Stone Quarry opened in 1880 and closed in 1908. Chuckanut Mountain was logged through the 1890s, and the bay itself became the workspace - a place to raft logs and load them into ships. In the early 1900s, three- and four-masted sailing vessels were built here, and in 1902 Pleasant Bay just to the south served as a winter mooring for ships laid up until spring. A salmon cannery opened in 1900, went bankrupt in 1902, and was reborn as the Astoria-Puget Sound Cannery; when fish traps were outlawed in 1934 the whole operation was picked up and moved to Excursion Inlet in Alaska, leaving buildings that served as warehouses until 1948. A brick and tile plant arrived in 1915. Coal and stone came off the west side of Governors Point in 1921. And during Prohibition, moonshiners and rum-runners made good use of a quiet bay with a lot of rock to hide behind and an international border a short run north.

## The Clubhouse That Became a Restaurant

Between 1935 and 1941 a ferry ran from Chuckanut Bay out to the San Juan Islands and up to Sidney, British Columbia, and a small community grew around the landing - about sixty people by the 1940s. The ferry stopped. The landing did not go to waste: it became the foundation of the Chuckanut Bay Yacht Club. The club closed in 1946, and the clubhouse was moved wholesale to La Conner, where it operates today as a waterfront restaurant. In 1954 the estate of Charles F. Larrabee, whose family had already donated the land for Larrabee State Park, deeded five beaches here to a residents' association, under a quit-claim that conveyed them for park, boat haven, beach, and playground purposes and explicitly permitted the new owners to leave them entirely undeveloped. The Chuckanut Bay Community Association, founded in 1992 to support the local volunteer firefighters, now counts more than four hundred households. State Route 11 - Chuckanut Drive - threads the whole length of it, between the cliffs and the water.

## Flight Context

Chuckanut Bay lies at 48.680 degrees north, 122.496 degrees west, in southwestern Whatcom County, Washington, immediately south of Bellingham at the southeast end of Bellingham Bay. A viewing altitude of 2,500 to 4,000 feet shows the full crescent, with Mud Bay at its northern head, Pleasant Bay to the south, Governors Point on the outer western arm, and the little wooded knob of Chuckanut Island, also called Dot Island, sitting offshore with Chuckanut Rocks beyond it. The Chuckanut Mountains rise abruptly east of the water, and Chuckanut Drive traces a shelf cut into their seaward face. Lummi Island fills the view west across Bellingham Bay, with the San Juans beyond. Bellingham International (KBLI) is about 10 kilometres north, Skagit Regional at Burlington (KBVS) 30 kilometres south, and Orcas Island (KORS) west across Rosario Strait. Expect autumn and winter fog pooling in the bay, strong northeasters funnelling out of the Fraser Valley in cold snaps, and generally clear stable air on summer afternoons.
