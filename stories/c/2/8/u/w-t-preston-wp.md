# W. T. Preston

A sternwheel snagboat built from the parts of her predecessors, who cleared the rivers of Puget Sound for half a century and once hauled a sunken bomber off the bottom.

**Location:** 48.5161, -122.6092

## Introduction

Among the jobs listed on her service record - pulling snags, light dredging, firefighting, general work - is the retrieval of a sunken military bomber. Also several automobiles. The W. T. Preston was a snagboat, a sternwheeler built for the unglamorous and genuinely dangerous business of taking obstructions out of rivers, and over half a century in the water she took out whatever was in the way, whether or not it had originally been a tree. The work kept a commercial waterway open, and almost nobody outside the river towns ever thought about it.

## What a Snagboat Was For

Dead trees came down the rivers of western Washington and reached Puget Sound, where many of them floated half-submerged. A waterlogged log riding low in the water is called a deadhead, and it will punch through the hull of a wooden vessel without warning. In an era when the rivers were commercial highways, that was not a nuisance but an economic problem, and the federal government answered it by building specialist boats to clear the channels. The first to work these rivers was the Skagit. She was replaced in 1914 by the Swinomish, and the Swinomish by the W. T. Preston, launched in 1929 and named for the only civilian engineer then working for the Army Corps of Engineers.

## A Ship Made of Other Ships

Sternwheeler machinery was simple, rugged, and routinely outlived the shallow-draught hulls it was bolted into, so snagboats were rebuilt rather than replaced. The Preston took her main single-expansion reciprocating steam engines, her pumps and much of her hardware from the Swinomish. Then in 1939 she did it again in her own right: the Corps built a new superstructure on a welded steel hull and moved the stern wheel, the main engines, the smokestack and the foredeck equipment across from the original 163-foot wooden-hulled boat. Her Canadian counterpart went further still. The Samson V, built in 1937 at New Westminster, carried engines and a paddlewheel shaft from the Samson II of 1905, A-frame crane parts from the Samson III of 1914, and a steam winch from the Samson IV of 1924; when she retired in 1980 she was the last steam-powered paddlewheeler running in Canada.

## Olympia to Blaine

Her territory ran the length of the sound, from Olympia in the south to Blaine on the Canadian border, and up the Skagit, Stillaguamish and Snohomish rivers. As the decades passed, the rivers carried less and less freight and the job changed underneath her. Clearing channels for commerce gave way to dredging, to fighting fires, to whatever a large steam-powered crane platform with a shallow draught could usefully do. The Army Corps operated her out of the Hiram M. Chittenden Locks in Seattle, and she worked Puget Sound for more than forty years before the Corps retired her in 1981. Her replacement, the Puget, still ties up at the same dock - an unbroken line of work stretching from the first snagboat on the Skagit to a vessel still on the roster today.

## Dry Land

She did not go for scrap. The W. T. Preston is now permanently dry-berthed on the Anacortes waterfront near Cap Sante, which is a strange thing to see - a full-size sternwheeler sitting on land, wheel and all, well above the tide that carried her for fifty years. She was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1989 and remains an official City of Seattle historic landmark as well, a legacy of her old home port. The City of Anacortes Museum owns and runs her as a museum ship, and in 2005 the Snagboat Heritage Center opened just north of her, holding artifacts, models, maps and the documentary record of the boats that kept these waterways navigable. It is a small museum about an unheroic job, which is precisely why it is worth the stop.

## Flight Context

The W. T. Preston sits at 48.5161 N, 122.6092 W on the Anacortes waterfront, near Cap Sante on Fidalgo Island. Fly it at 1,500-2,500 ft: Cap Sante's blunt headland and the marina below it are the anchor, with Fidalgo Bay and the March Point refineries to the east, Guemes Channel and Guemes Island to the north, the Anacortes ferry terminal on the western point, and Mount Erie rising south of town. The dry-berthed hull and its white superstructure are visible from the air right at the shoreline. Skagit Regional (KBVS) lies about 15 km east, Orcas Island (KORS) across the water to the northwest, and NAS Whidbey Island (KNUW) roughly 20 km south - check its NOTAMs before routing through. Marine fog forms quickly in Guemes Channel, and refinery stacks give a reliable read on the low-level wind.
