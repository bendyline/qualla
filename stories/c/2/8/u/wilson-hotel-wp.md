# Wilson Hotel

A Tacoma speculator bet a million locally made bricks that the Great Northern Railway would end at Anacortes. The railway went elsewhere, the bricks turned out to be bad, and the building is still standing.

**Location:** 48.5176, -122.6130

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Skagit County, Washington, Hotel buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Washington (state), Defunct hotels in Washington (state), Hotel buildings completed in 1890, 1890s architecture in the United States, National Register of Historic Places in Skagit County, Washington

## Introduction

In 1890 nobody knew where the Great Northern Railway was going to stop. Somewhere on Puget Sound a transcontinental line was going to meet salt water, and whichever town it chose would become a city while the others stayed villages. So the towns built as though they had already won. David Wilson, a Tacoma capitalist who had followed the Northern Pacific to its terminus in the early 1880s and financed hotels and business blocks in Yakima and Davenport on the same logic, picked Anacortes and started buying property. Then he commissioned one of Tacoma's leading architectural firms to design him a large brick hotel on the corner of Avenue P and Eighth. He was wrong about the railway. The building outlasted the mistake by a hundred and thirty years.

## A Million Bad Bricks

Wilson needed roughly a million bricks - enough for the hotel and for a bank building on Q Avenue that never got built - so he hired another Tacoman, contractor and brick mason David See, to set up a brickyard in Anacortes purely to supply him. See arrived in May 1890 and had the yard running inside a month, which tells you everything about the tempo of a speculative boom. Time was less kind to the product. The brick proved to be of very poor quality, and most buildings made from it, the Wilson included, eventually had to be stuccoed or veneered to keep the wet Puget Sound climate from eating them. Construction began on 4 June 1890. Local papers called it one of the largest and costliest buildings yet raised in Anacortes, at an estimated $42,000.

## Sixty-Two Rooms and a Parlour

The building was finished by December 1890 and took its first guests in February 1891. Pickles & Sutton had given it a Romanesque front - rough-faced stone bases, rounded windows, Roman arches, stone and tin trim, and a large central pediment carrying the hotel's name, which deterioration removed long ago. Inside were sixty-two sleeping rooms above and, on the corner of the second floor, a substantial parlour. The interiors were Eastlake in style, finished in local red cedar and red cherry. Four store rooms faced Commercial Avenue at street level, with a dining room, the hotel offices and a bar room taking the southern two. When Anacortes got its first telephone service in 1890 and electric light in 1891, the Wilson was among the first buildings connected to each.

## The Railway Went to Everett

The Great Northern chose Everett, and ultimately Seattle. Anacortes got a consolation prize: the Northern Pacific ran a branch line up from Sedro-Woolley, which made the town a minor terminus rather than no terminus at all. That was enough to keep the Wilson busy, since it stood within a few blocks of the depot and quickly became the hotel visiting businessmen and dignitaries asked for - outperforming the grander Hotel Anacortes, which had been built further out and found itself stranded in a district the boom never reached. But a hotel cannot outrun a national contraction. The Panic of 1893 wiped David Wilson out. By the end of that year he was broke, the hotel was closed to guests, and the building was sold at a sheriff's auction to cover unpaid property taxes.

## The Funks, and After

Valentine Funk bought the building in 1901, and the family held it for generations - his grandson Wallie ran the hotel into the 1960s while also running the local paper, the Anacortes American. In 1911 proprietors from Everett put $18,000 into a renovation, refurnishing every room and cutting the count from sixty-two to fifty-one so that twelve could have private bathrooms. It reopened that October as the New Wilson Hotel, advertised as the finest between Seattle and Bellingham, with electricity, steam heat and hot and cold running water. The Funks came back in May 1926 with $50,000 and replaced the old kitchen wing with a four-storey reinforced concrete annex that matched the three-storey original in height, linked by an elevator stopping on floors in both. The Wilson has looked much the same ever since. It served as a hotel into the 1970s, became apartments, was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 15 December 2004, and emerged from a full restoration and earthquake retrofit in 2007. Today it holds retail below and low-income housing above.

## Flight Context

The Wilson Hotel stands at 48.5176 N, 122.6130 W in downtown Anacortes, on Fidalgo Island at the northern edge of the Skagit delta. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, pick out the compact downtown grid running back from the waterfront along Commercial Avenue, with Guemes Channel and Guemes Island immediately north, the Anacortes ferry terminal to the west, and the two refineries on March Point to the east - the Wilson sits in the heart of the old commercial blocks. Mount Erie rises just south of town. Anacortes Airport (74S) is about 2 nm southwest, Skagit Regional (KBVS) roughly 8 nm southeast, and Bellingham International (KBLI) about 17 nm north-northeast. NAS Whidbey Island (KNUW) lies about 10 nm south with active military airspace and frequent jet traffic - check restrictions and NOTAMs carefully. Marine fog and low stratus are common off Rosario Strait in summer mornings and through the winter.
