# Guemes Island

The island the Samish called 'many dogs' for the wool-bearing dogs kept here, renamed for a Spanish viceroy, and emptied of its original village in 1912.

**Location:** 48.5490, -122.6291

**Categories:** Islands, Indigenous Culture, Local History, Coastal, Washington

## Introduction

The Samish name for this island is Qweng7qwengila7, and it means many dogs. That is not a joke about strays. Coast Salish peoples kept a distinct small, long-haired dog whose coat was sheared like a sheep's, spun into yarn, and woven into blankets - the Salish Wool Dog, a breed maintained deliberately for generations and kept apart from ordinary village dogs, often on islands where the separation was easy to enforce. The dogs are gone now, lost during the nineteenth century as trade cloth and upheaval displaced the weaving economy that sustained them. The name stayed on the island long enough to be written down. It is the oldest thing about this place still in the record, and it describes an industry almost nobody remembers.

## Sxwalimet

Guemes Island sits within the traditional territory of the Samish Indian Nation, and the Samish had a village here called Sxwalimet, standing near where the ferry landing is today. It lasted until around 1850. Everything that makes an island livable was concentrated at that spot - the beach for canoes, the protected water, the approach from the Anacortes side - which is exactly why the ferry terminal ended up in the same place. Look at the modern dock and you are looking at the site people chose first, for the same reasons.

## Moved, Then Moved Again

The Samish did not leave Guemes voluntarily, and they did not leave once. Displaced by the United States government, they established a new village on the island around 1873, near Potlatch Beach. It occupied the only natural spring on Guemes - the single reliable source of fresh water on the whole island, which tells you both why they chose it and what was at stake in holding it. In 1912 white settlers forced them off the island altogether. Those are the facts in the record, stated plainly, and they cover a span of roughly sixty years in which a community was uprooted twice from ground it had occupied for far longer than the government doing the uprooting had existed. The Samish Indian Nation is still here, on the mainland and throughout these waters. The village sites are not.

## Isla de Guemes

The name on modern charts arrived in 1791, when the Spanish explorer Jose Maria Narvaez, sailing under Francisco de Eliza, worked through these channels and labelled the island Isla de Guemes. He was honouring Juan Vicente de Guemes Padilla Horcasitas y Aguayo, 2nd Count of Revillagigedo, Viceroy of New Spain - a man who never saw the Pacific Northwest and whose connection to this particular island is that he approved the budget. It is a common enough story on this coast, where half the headlands carry the names of European administrators, but the contrast here is unusually sharp. One name describes what the island was for. The other describes who was paying.

## Island Life Now

Guemes today is quiet and stubbornly rural, reached by private boat or by the little ferry that Skagit County runs across the channel from Anacortes. Facilities are limited on purpose. There are two parks - Schoolhouse Park in the middle of the island and Young's Park on North Beach, near the Guemes Island Resort - and a handful of public beaches, though most of the shoreline is private and marked as such. The community, unpretentiously, is just called Guemes. Fewer than three hundred households occupy the island, and they are small ones, averaging under two people each, with a substantial share of residents living alone and past sixty-five. It is the demographic profile of a place people retire to and hold onto, a short crossing and a whole world away from the traffic on Fidalgo.

## Flight Context

Guemes Island lies at 48.5490 N, 122.6291 W in western Skagit County, Washington, immediately north of Fidalgo Island and the city of Anacortes, separated from it by the narrow Guemes Channel. From the air the identifying features are the channel itself with the small Skagit County ferry shuttling across it, the island's largely wooded interior with a scatter of shoreline homes, and the open beaches along its northern side. Fidalgo Island with the Anacortes refineries and ferry terminal sits immediately south, Cypress Island lies northwest, and Deception Pass with its bridge is about 8 nm southwest. Anacortes (74S) is the nearest field, roughly 3 nm south; Skagit Regional (KBVS) lies about 10 nm southeast and Bellingham (KBLI) about 20 nm northeast. Naval Air Station Whidbey Island (KNUW) is to the south and its Class C airspace and military traffic affect the area, so check NOTAMs. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 feet MSL takes in the island, the channel and the Anacortes waterfront. Marine fog is common on summer mornings; westerlies through the channel can be brisk in the afternoon.
