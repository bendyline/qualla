# Edison, Washington

A Skagit flats hamlet of 240 people, named for America's most famous capitalist inventor, that spent a decade as the national headquarters of a socialist utopia - and that later sent Edward R. Murrow off to high school.

**Location:** 48.5625, -122.4360

**Categories:** Small Towns, Washington, Utopian Communities, Social History, Skagit County, Historical Figures

## Introduction

There is a fine irony buried in this village of 240 people. Edison, Washington, carries the name of Thomas Alva Edison, the inventor who industrialized invention itself and built the research laboratories at Menlo Park and West Orange that turned discovery into a business. Less than three decades after the town took his name, it became the national headquarters of an organized effort to abolish the economic system he epitomized. For ten years, a socialist colony on the Samish flats published a newspaper for supporters across the country, and its return address said Edison.

## Settled, Then Named

The settlement came first. Ben Samson put down stakes here in 1869, on the rich diked bottomland where the Samish River spills toward the bay - some of the most productive farm ground in Washington, reclaimed from tideflat and still flat enough that a bicycle can see for miles. The name came later, borrowed from the inventor who was by then a household word across the country. A great many small American places named themselves after Edison in those years; this one kept the name through everything that followed, and now covers a tidy 0.6 square miles of Skagit County.

## The Brotherhood of the Cooperative Commonwealth

On November 1, 1897, E. G. Pelton and a group of fellow socialists founded Equality Colony on 280 acres just outside town. The venture belonged to a national organization, the Brotherhood of the Cooperative Commonwealth, and its animating idea came from Edward Bellamy, whose enormously popular novel of a cooperative future had convinced a great many Americans that a better arrangement was not only possible but imminent. The plan was to build it in one place first and let the example spread - starting, of all places, in the mud of the Samish flats. Edison became the headquarters. What went up was an actual working community: farming, timber milling, a school, a blacksmith shop, and copper-working shops.

## Industrial Freedom

The Brotherhood understood that a demonstration is worthless if nobody sees it, so from Edison it published a newspaper. Industrial Freedom circulated nationally to roughly three thousand supporters - people who had not moved to Washington but who sent money, read the reports, and believed something was being proved on their behalf. That is a striking thought to hold while standing in Edison today: a tiny farming village at the edge of Puget Sound serving as the editorial centre of an American movement, its arguments about work and ownership printed here and mailed east.

## How It Came Apart

Utopias are usually undone from inside, and Equality was no exception. Its founding leader died in 1901, taking with him whatever authority had held the arrangement together. A faction leaning toward anarchism arrived and raised tensions that hardened into a schism. Then, in 1906, an arsonist set the colony barn on fire, killing most of the livestock and destroying the farm equipment - a catastrophe for a community whose entire economic case rested on being able to feed itself. Equality Colony succumbed to its internal divisions in 1907, ten years after its founding. There is very little left to see. What remains is the odd fact of it, and the names on a map.

## The Boy Who Went to Edison High

Edison's other claim is a person. Edward R. Murrow, born in 1908 and dead in 1965, graduated from Edison High School - the broadcaster whose wartime reports from London put American listeners on a rooftop under the Blitz, and who later turned a television camera on Senator Joseph McCarthy at considerable professional risk. The school building he attended stayed in service as Edison Elementary until it was replaced by a larger one in 1996, put up on the same site. That new K-8 Edison Elementary is now the only school in town. A village that once tried to reinvent the American economy ended up producing one of the people who defined American journalism, which is a different kind of influence and probably the more durable one.

## Flight Context

Edison sits at 48.5625°N, 122.4360°W on the Samish flats in Skagit County, Washington, a few miles inland from Samish Bay. The terrain makes it easy: this is dead-flat reclaimed farmland cut by dikes and drainage sloughs, bounded on the north by the wooded ridge that Chuckanut Drive climbs along the shoreline, and on the west by Samish and Padilla bays with Fidalgo Island and the Anacortes refineries beyond. The Samish River winds past the village; Mount Baker dominates the eastern horizon. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500-2,500 ft MSL to read the village grid, the dike pattern, and the farm sections around it; 6,000-7,000 ft frames the Skagit delta against the San Juan Islands and the Cascades. Skagit Regional (KBVS) is roughly 6 nm south-southeast and Bellingham International (KBLI) about 14 nm north; NAS Whidbey Island (KNUW) lies some 15 nm southwest with Class C airspace and military traffic. The flats are a major wintering ground for snow geese, swans, and raptors - expect substantial bird activity at low altitude from November through March, along with frequent winter fog and low stratus.
