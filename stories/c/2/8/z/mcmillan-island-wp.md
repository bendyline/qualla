# McMillan Island

A Fraser River island that the current welded together out of two, named for a fur trader who passed through twice, and home to the Kwantlen First Nation's main village for nearly two centuries.

**Location:** 49.1764, -122.5700

**Categories:** Islands of British Columbia, Islands of the Fraser River, Landforms of Lower Mainland, First Nations, Indian reserves in British Columbia, Fur trade

## Introduction

Two islands became one because somebody built a bridge somewhere else. Into the 1920s the land north of Fort Langley was a pair — Brae Island on the northwest end, McMillan on the southeast, split by a channel narrow enough to throw a line across. Then the Jacob Haldi Bridge went up over the wider Bedford Channel, the current behind it slackened, and the Fraser did what the Fraser always does: dropped its load of silt until the upstream end of that little channel simply sealed shut. Charts have said McMillan Island ever since. The seam is invisible now under grass and cottonwood, and yet the two halves have gone on living entirely separate lives.

## A River That Builds and Unbuilds

Ten thousand years ago this was not an island at all but a peninsula reaching off the shore of what is now Maple Ridge. The Fraser gnawed at its neck until the connection failed, leaving an island far larger than anything here today. Then the process reversed. With a channel open on the far side, the water slowed along the southern flank, and silt and debris settled out until the island had nearly grafted itself onto the bank at Fort Langley. Finally the river changed its mind again and tore a section loose.

What survives measures 1.78 square kilometres — silt and debris, essentially, held in place by root systems and luck. It is a landform in the middle of an argument, and the argument is still running. Anyone who has watched a Fraser freshet in June understands that the shoreline drawn on today's map is a working draft.

## Why the Kwantlen Moved Upriver

The island sits within Kwantlen traditional territory, which overlaps that of the Kwikwetlem, Tsawwassen and Katzie nations and runs up the Fraser as far as the Stave River. For generations the Kwantlen's principal village stood far downstream at Qayqayt — also recorded as Kikait — across the water from what became New Westminster, on ground later called Brownsville and known today as Surrey's Bridgeview.

After the Hudson's Bay Company built Fort Langley in 1827, the Kwantlen moved their main village to this island. The reasoning was both commercial and defensive: from here they could command trade with the post, and they gained shelter under the fort's guns from raiding parties that came down the coast from the north. Those raiders — the Euclataws of Quadra Island — took captives from lower Fraser villages. Company men and Kwantlen fighters repelled them together, and the raids on this stretch of river eventually ended.

## The Name on the Chart

James McMillan of the Hudson's Bay Company gave the island the name it still carries. He came through the lower Fraser in 1824, returned in 1827, and founded Fort Langley on the south bank — originally about four kilometres downstream from the site visitors know today, before the post was rebuilt along Bedford Channel.

It is a modest basis for a place name. Two visits, a mandate from a fur company, and a career that carried him elsewhere. The people who have raised families on this ground for close to two centuries had their own names for it long before a surveyor wrote his down, and both truths sit on the same island without much apparent friction.

## Three Owners, One Road

Glover Road crosses Bedford Channel and runs the length of the island — the same road that is the main street of Fort Langley. What it passes through changes partway along.

The southeastern portion, the original McMillan Island, is reserve land: McMillan Island 6, 191 hectares, the largest and most populated of the Kwantlen First Nation's reserves and the site of the Nation's band office on Glover Road. The 2016 census counted roughly 94 residents in 22 private dwellings.

The northwestern end, the vanished Brae Island, went a different way. In 1995 the Greater Vancouver Regional District acquired about 68 hectares of it, and most of that became Brae Island Regional Park; a portion is leased out as a private campground. The Township of Langley holds the remaining 1.5 hectares. One island, one road, and three sets of keys.

## The Slip at the North End

Docks line the island's edges, and the ones on the north side used to matter a great deal. This was the southern terminus of the Albion Ferry, which began running to Albion in Maple Ridge on 2 June 1957 and carried commuters, log trucks and school kids across the Fraser for fifty-two years.

The Golden Ears Bridge opened on 16 June 2009 and made the crossing redundant within weeks. The last sailings of the Kulleet and the Klatawa came just after noon on 31 July 2009. The ramps are still there at the water's edge, aimed at nothing in particular — the kind of leftover infrastructure that tells you a route existed here long before the bridge did, and that the river was once something you waited for rather than something you drove over.

## Flight Context

McMillan Island lies at 49.176°N, 122.570°W, an elongated low island in the lower Fraser River roughly 5 nm northeast of Langley Regional Airport (CYNJ) and about 6 nm east-southeast of Pitt Meadows (CYPK). Fly it at 1,500–2,500 ft AGL: the island reads as a green sliver between the Fraser's main channel to the north and the narrower Bedford Channel separating it from Fort Langley to the south, with Glover Road and its bridge the clearest human line on the ground. The Golden Ears Bridge, 3 nm downstream to the west, is the standout navigational landmark — its cable-stayed towers are visible for many miles. Abbotsford International (CYXX) sits about 14 nm east and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 24 nm west. Expect valley fog on autumn and winter mornings and be alert for float traffic and low-level transits along the river corridor; the Vancouver VTA is essential reading here.
