# Greater Vancouver Zoo

It began in 1970 with one llama in a Fraser Valley field, and has spent the half-century since arguing - sometimes with itself - about what a zoo is for.

**Location:** 49.0923, -122.4870

**Categories:** Zoos in British Columbia, Langley, British Columbia (district municipality), Tourist attractions in British Columbia, Wildlife conservation, 1970 establishments in British Columbia

## Introduction

The first resident was a llama named Dennis. He arrived in 1970 from Mount Vernon, Washington, to 120 acres of Fraser Valley farmland at Aldergrove that a businessman named Pat Hines had bought to build a game farm. Paddocks went up around Dennis over the following months and filled with animals of every size and description. On 20 August 1970 the gates opened to the public. More than half a century later the place is still there, on the same ground, still argued over - a zoo that has changed its name twice, its owners three times, and its stated purpose more fundamentally than either.

## A Family in a Field

Hines had first registered the venture as World Wide Game Farm Ltd., but it opened as the Vancouver Game Farm and he ran it with his wife Ann, other family members, and their staff. Their daughter Eleanor and her husband, Hugh Oakes, eventually took over the management and held it until the sale in 1991. The new owners rebuilt. In 1995 the name changed to the Greater Vancouver Zoological Centre - a correction as much as a rebrand, since Aldergrove sits well outside Vancouver proper - and then to the Greater Vancouver Zoo in 1999, after another change of hands. Enclosures were replaced, a narrow-gauge train was laid, a picnic park with gazebos and barbecues went in, the entrance was remodelled. The North American Wilds exhibit opened in 1998: a narrated bus ride through one habitat shared by black bears, coyotes and Vancouver Island wolves, then another where Roosevelt elk, black-tailed deer and plains bison grazed.

## The Frog That Redefined the Place

In 2000 the zoo joined the Oregon Spotted Frog Recovery Program. The frog was, at that point, the only species in Canada ever given an emergency listing as endangered - a designation reserved for animals whose disappearance is not a decades-away worry but a near-term likelihood. The work is unglamorous. Frogs are bred, weighed, measured, tagged and released into wetlands, and most of what happens afterward is unknowable. It earned the zoo the Colonel G.D. Dailley Award, given for captive-breeding work that meaningfully improves a species' odds of surviving. Two more British Columbian natives followed under a partnership with Wildlife Preservation Canada: Taylor's checkerspot butterfly and the western painted turtle. The zoo has also bred addax and Père David's deer, and was the first in western Canada to breed red pandas. And because the Salmon River runs through the property, it takes part in the restoration program for that tributary too - conservation that begins at the fence line.

## Animals That Cannot Go Back

Not everything released can be. A grizzly named Shadow, born in 1999, came in as an abandoned cub - too young to have learned from a mother how to find food, avoid people, or survive a winter, and therefore unreleasable for life. Three more orphaned grizzly cubs arrived from Alberta in 2020. Much of the collection has arrived by similar routes: reptiles, exotic birds and several cat species seized from the illegal pet trade or surrendered when owners discovered what they had bought. These are animals whose lives were shaped by human decisions long before they reached Aldergrove. The longest tenure belonged to Tina, an Asian elephant who arrived at the opening in 1970 and died in 2004 - thirty-four years in a Fraser Valley field, a span that traces almost exactly the era in which zoos everywhere were being forced to rethink what they owed the animals they held.

## Under Scrutiny

That rethinking did not come easily here. In 1997, Zoocheck Canada and a visiting British veterinarian published reports calling for better conditions for the rhinoceros and the big cats; by 2003 little had changed. Crown counsel laid formal charges in May 2006, then stayed them in January 2007 on the grounds that a new hippopotamus habitat had opened and prosecution no longer served the public interest - explicitly not a finding that the charges had been unjustified. The zoo lost its Canadian Association of Zoos and Aquariums accreditation for two years. Worse came in March 2009, when four zebras died shortly after two African buffalo were introduced to their enclosure; the likely cause was exertional myopathy, the muscle damage that follows extreme stress or struggle. The zoo did not disclose the deaths at the time. In July 2020, photographs of an emaciated eight-year-old moose named Oakleaf circulated online; she was euthanised the following day as her health continued to fail.

## The Night the Wolves Got Out

On 16 August 2022, someone cut a hole in the fence around the habitat holding nine adult grey wolves and six cubs. The wolves found it. Most stayed inside the grounds, where staff and responders spent an anxious day tracking them between exhibits - one was eventually darted in the animatronic dinosaur display, an image that captures something about the modern zoo. British Columbia Conservation Officer Service officers recovered two more near a creek. But two wolves left the property altogether, and one of them, a three-year-old female named Chia, was struck by a car and found dead at the roadside. The other, a yearling called Tempest, was missing for three days before she was safely returned. Nobody has been held responsible for the cut fence. For an institution that spends its budget keeping some animals in and releasing others back to the wild, it was a hard lesson in how thin the line between those two categories can be.

## Flight Context

The Greater Vancouver Zoo occupies roughly 49 hectares at 49.0923°N, 122.4870°W, in Aldergrove within the Township of Langley - open Fraser Valley farmland about 10 km north of the Canada-US border. Fly it at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL; the enclosures read clearly from low altitude as a cluster of paddocks and curving paths set among rectangular section-line fields. Navigation is easy here because the valley floor is a grid: Highway 1 runs east-west a couple of kilometres north of the site, 264th Street runs north-south past the gate, and the wooded corridor of the Salmon River cuts across the property. The Golden Ears and the north-shore peaks of the Coast Mountains stand up sharply to the north, and Mount Baker dominates the southeastern horizon on clear days. Nearby airports: CYXX Abbotsford International roughly 11 km east-southeast, CYNJ Langley Regional about 10 km west, CZBB Boundary Bay some 38 km west, and KBLI Bellingham International around 33 km south across the border. Abbotsford's control zone abuts this area - check the boundary before manoeuvring, and stay clear of the international border. Summer afternoons give the cleanest air; autumn and winter bring persistent valley fog that can leave the floor socked in while the mountains stay clear.
