# St. George's Anglican Church (Fort Langley)

A fifty-dollar lot, a carpenter's idea of Gothic, and an iron cross bearing the names of two Hawaiians who died working the Fraser River fur trade.

**Location:** 49.1687, -122.5775

**Categories:** Anglican church buildings in British Columbia, Langley, British Columbia (district municipality), Churches completed in 1901, Carpenter Gothic architecture in Canada, Hudson's Bay Company history, Hawaiian diaspora

## Introduction

Above the front door of a small wooden church in Fort Langley hangs an engraved iron cross. It carries two names: Pion Pion and Katrina. They were Hawaiians, contracted to the Hudson's Bay Company in the nineteenth century and buried in the ground next door, and beyond the cross itself almost nothing of them survives. Somebody at St. George's decided that the marker should not be left to rust flat in the grass. So it went up over the entrance, where anyone walking into a Sunday service has to pass beneath it. For a building whose chief claim is that it is pretty and old, that single decision is the most interesting thing about it.

## The Ground Came First

The Hudson's Bay Company cemetery next to the church was in use by about 1840, which makes it the second oldest known European burial ground in Langley Township - older than the church beside it by more than sixty years, older than the province. The fur trade that filled it was never a purely European enterprise. The Company recruited heavily in the Hawaiian Islands, and the men who signed those contracts were known throughout the Pacific Northwest by the Hawaiian word for a person: Kanaka. They paddled the brigades, cut timber, worked the fort. Some married into local First Nations families and stayed. Pion Pion and Katrina are two of the very few whose names made it onto anything durable, and the cross that carries them is now, by a long margin, the most quietly remarkable object in the building.

## The Congregation That Outlived Its Town

St. George's did not begin here. In 1859, the Hudson's Bay Company chaplain, the Reverend W. B. Crickmer, established a church at Derby, three miles west, in a townsite briefly imagined as the capital of the mainland colony. The capital went elsewhere. By 1871 Derby had thinned out and Crickmer had gone home to England. In 1879 the original building was floated across the Fraser to Maple Ridge, which leaves an odd situation: a parish with a founding date and no building, a congregation without an address. The Diocese of New Westminster was formed that same year under Bishop Sillitoe, the Reverend William Bell took charge, and for two decades services happened wherever a room could be found. St. Alban's went up in Milner in 1889. Fort Langley waited.

## Fifty Dollars and a Carpenter's Gothic

The land came from Alexander Mavis, who sold it for fifty dollars. Duncan Bule built the church in 1901, and Bishop John Dart conducted the first service in it on October 15 of that year; consecration followed in 1908. What Bule produced is a textbook of Carpenter Gothic - the style that happens when a cathedral idea meets a sawmill and a good set of chisels. Cathedral stonework becomes wood frame. Soaring becomes steep: one storey, a sharply gabled roof, tall pointed windows, gable screens, and rafter ends drilled into decoration for no structural reason at all. A small bell tower joined the roofline in 1914. Step inside and the interior is all fir - tongue-and-groove paneling laid in a herringbone pattern, and the original pews and altar rails, in place since 1909.

## Nine Windows

The stained glass arrived slowly, one memorial at a time, across ninety-four years. The first of the nine windows went in during 1912; the last in 2006. Each was given in someone's memory, which means the collection is less an artistic program than a ledger of a small community's losses, assembled by people who mostly did not know one another and never saw the finished set. It is widely considered the finest collection of stained glass in the Fraser Valley. On a clear morning the east windows throw coloured light down the herringbone fir, and the effect in a room this small is disproportionate - the reason Carpenter Gothic works at all is that modest dimensions make ordinary light feel deliberate.

## Still Open

The Township of Langley formally recognized St. George's as a local heritage site on January 1, 2006, and it was added to the Canadian Register of Historic Places that October 24. Heritage listing often reads like a polite form of retirement, but not here. The parish still holds Sunday Eucharist at 9160 Church Street, and in 2026 it is marking 125 years of ministry in this building. Fort Langley itself has become a weekend destination - the reconstructed National Historic Site up the road draws the crowds, the main street sells coffee and antiques. The church does not compete. It sits on its lot beside the cemetery, doors open, iron cross overhead.

## Flight Context

St. George's stands at 49.1687 N, 122.5775 W, on Church Street in Fort Langley on the south bank of the Fraser River. At 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL the church itself is a small white rectangle, so navigate by its neighbours: the palisade and bastions of the reconstructed Fort Langley National Historic Site sit a few hundred metres north, the Bedford Channel separates the village from wooded McMillan Island, and the Jacob Haldi Bridge is the single link across. The Fraser runs broad and braided here with gravel bars showing at low water, and the Golden Ears massif fills the horizon to the north. Nearby airports: Langley Regional (CYNJ) roughly 6 nm southwest; Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 10 nm northwest; Abbotsford International (CYXX) about 13 nm southeast. Valley fog is common on autumn and winter mornings and can leave the village socked in while the ridges above stay clear.
