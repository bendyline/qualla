# Kanaka Creek Regional Park

A narrow green ribbon along eleven kilometres of salmon creek in Maple Ridge, named for the Hawaiian families who settled its mouth in the 1850s.

**Location:** 49.2006, -122.5860

**Categories:** Maple Ridge, British Columbia, Parks in Greater Vancouver, Waterfalls of British Columbia, Salmon, Conservation

## Introduction

The creek is named for men who crossed most of an ocean to reach it. In the 1850s Hawaiian and Pacific Islander families settled the lower reaches of a stream on the north bank of the Fraser, most of them drawn in by the fur trade running out of Fort Langley just across the water. Kanaka is a Hawaiian word meaning man — what Hawaiians called themselves before anyone else used it for them. Many of those men married First Nations women, and their descendants still live in British Columbia. The creek kept the name. Today Kanaka Creek Regional Park follows it for roughly eleven kilometres through Maple Ridge, from the Fraser confluence east of Haney up to Webster's Corners: a green ribbon barely wider than the valley it protects.

## Before the Name

Kanaka Creek runs through the unceded traditional territory of six First Nations — Katzie, Kwantlen, Matsqui, Musqueam, Semiahmoo and Tsleil-Waututh — and the record of their use of this watershed reaches back more than nine thousand years. That span is hard to hold in the mind. People fished, travelled and lived along this creek for something like four hundred generations. Then, within a few lifetimes, the population collapsed: epidemics swept the lower Fraser around 1750 and again in the early 1800s, arriving ahead of most of the settlers themselves, and the communities here lost catastrophic numbers of people to diseases against which they had no defence and no warning. The Katzie remain. When Metro Vancouver sat down to write the management plan the park operates under today, the Katzie First Nation helped write it.

## Sandstone, Slickrock and Two Sets of Falls

Walk 1.2 kilometres up the Canyon Trail from the hatchery and the creek changes character completely. The soft valley floor gives way to a sandstone canyon, and the water drops through it at Cliff Falls — really a pair of falls, one on each upper fork, joining below. Sandstone weathers into smooth chutes and ledges, and the rock around the falls has been polished into the slickrock slides that generations of Maple Ridge teenagers have treated as the best swimming hole in the district. Spring runoff makes the canyon loud enough that conversation stops working. By late August the same stretch is a quiet place of green light and dripping moss, the pools clear enough to count stones on the bottom. Roughly 11.8 kilometres of maintained trail run through the park overall, much of it flat, with parking and washrooms at the main access points.

## The Creek That Feeds Everything

Chum and coho salmon return up Kanaka Creek, along with cutthroat trout, and the Bell-Irving Hatchery inside the park exists to keep those runs going. It is operated by KEEPS — the Kanaka Education and Environmental Partnership Society — which pairs the enhancement work with free open houses, classroom visits and an annual Return of the Salmon day. What the salmon bring, the bears follow. Black bears use this corridor constantly, because it links mature forest to the Fraser River and they need both. Cougar, bobcat, coyote, red fox and deer move through as well, river otters work the water, and more than 130 bird species have been recorded in the park. The rarest resident is the one almost nobody sees: the Pacific water shrew, endangered, hunting the slow shaded margins of small streams — exactly the wet ground that development drains first.

## A Park Shaped Like an Argument

The park's odd ribbon geometry is the residue of a fight. In the late 1970s Maple Ridge began buying out private property inside the proposed boundaries, and the people who owned land in the watershed pushed back hard. An agreement struck in 1981 tried to thread the needle: protect the creek, displace as few residents as possible. What emerged from that compromise is the long, narrow shape the park still has, following the water rather than claiming the basin around it. The final parcel was acquired in 1993. A twenty-year management plan arrived in 2004, written jointly by Metro Vancouver Regional Parks, the Katzie First Nation, KEEPS, the Alouette Field Naturalists and the municipality, with targets as granular as planting at least ten native species and thickening salmonberry and snowberry cover to support the ecosystems that depend on them.

## Loved Too Hard

Popularity turns out to be its own kind of pressure. Unsanctioned trails cut through the understorey. Riders built bike jumps off-trail. Plant life vanished under boots and tires in places where nothing was meant to be travelling at all. In 2022 a University of Victoria team completed a restoration project on the worst of the damage, replanting native species, closing off the trampled ground and fencing the areas most likely to be re-entered. The project worked, and the team's closing recommendation had nothing to do with plants: teach people how to use a trail. It is a fair note on which to leave the place. Over 130 bird species, two runs of salmon and an ecosystem nine thousand years in the making all survive here inside a growing suburb, held together by a strip of land narrow enough to walk across in a few minutes.

## Flight Context

Kanaka Creek Regional Park centres on 49.2006°N, 122.5860°W, on the north bank of the Fraser River in Maple Ridge, British Columbia. Because the park is a narrow corridor, 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL gives the best read on its shape — the creek shows as a dark seam of forest cutting northeast from the Fraser through otherwise open suburban and agricultural land. Navigation landmarks: the Fraser River and Highway 7 mark the southern edge, the Maple Ridge Fairgrounds sit just east of the lower park with Albion beyond, Derby Reach Regional Park lies directly across the river in Langley, and the peaks of Golden Ears rise sharply to the north. Pitt Meadows Airport (CYPK) is about 5 nm west and is the practical base for low-level sightseeing here; Langley Regional (CYNJ) lies roughly 6 nm southwest across the river, and Abbotsford (CYXX) about 14 nm southeast. Vancouver International (CYVR) is some 27 nm west. Valley fog is common on autumn and winter mornings; the clearest light on the canyon comes mid-morning in late summer, when the sun reaches into the sandstone gorge.
