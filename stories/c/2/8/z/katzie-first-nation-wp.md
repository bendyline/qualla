# Katzie First Nation

Their name means land of the moss, and it comes from an instruction: pile up moss, and build the village on that.

**Location:** 49.2000, -122.6667

**Categories:** First Nations governments in the Lower Mainland, Sto:lo governments, Pitt Meadows, Indigenous history, Coast Salish

## Introduction

Pile up moss, the sky woman said, and build the village on that. The instruction sounds absurd until you have stood in the Pitt Meadows wetlands in November rain, where the ground is more sponge than soil and moss grows deep enough to take a person's weight. From that instruction comes a name. The Katzie First Nation calls itself q̓ic̓əy̓ - land of the moss - and the anglicized spelling, Katzie, now appears on road signs, reserve boundaries, bridge agreements and federal registry forms across the Lower Fraser Valley. The word is older than every one of those things, and so are the people who carry it.

## The Lake That Breathes

The heart of Katzie territory is a lake that rises and falls like the sea, because it is connected to it. Pitt Lake sits only three metres above sea level, twenty kilometres up the Pitt River from its junction with the Fraser, and twice a day the Pacific tide climbs that far inland and shoves the lake's outflow backwards. It is one of the few genuinely tidal lakes anywhere on Earth and among the largest: twenty-five kilometres long, four and a half wide at its broadest, a hundred and fifty metres deep at its floor. The Katzie name for it, sq̓ə́yc̓əyaʔɬ x̌acaʔ, simply means the lake of the Katzie. Around and below it lie the sloughs, marshes and wet meadows that fed the Nation for millennia - fish, waterfowl, berries, roots - and the traditional territory reaches out across what are now Pitt Meadows, Maple Ridge, Surrey, Langley and Delta.

## What Swaneset Made

Katzie oral tradition begins with five communities established by the Creator across what is now Greater Vancouver, each with a chief appointed to lead it: θéłəctən at Pitt Lake, Swaneset at Sheridan Hill in present-day Pitt Meadows, Xwoe'pecten at Port Hammond, C'simlenexw at Point Grey, and Smakwec at Point Roberts. The Katzie descend chiefly from Swaneset and θéłəctən. From the other three came the Kwantlen, the Musqueam and the Snokomish - the last of whom were destroyed by the smallpox that swept the coast in the eighteenth century, an entire people gone inside a generation. Swaneset, the tradition holds, shaped his stretch of country until it grew berries and roots in abundance. He then travelled to the sky and came back with a wife who told the people to build on moss, released eulachon into the Stó꞉lō, and taught them how to take and prepare the fish. A later marriage, to the daughter of the chief of the sockeye people, secured the salmon runs. By the end of Swaneset's life there were twelve Katzie villages.

## Three Hundred Forty-One Hectares

Set the reach of that territory beside what the Crown eventually left in Katzie hands, and the arithmetic tells its own story. There are five reserves. Katzie 1 runs 44.10 hectares along the north bank of the Fraser west of Port Hammond; Katzie 2 holds 23.10 hectares directly across the river; Barnston Island 3 takes 54.6 hectares of that island's south shore; Pitt Lake 4, the largest at 218.50 hectares, sits where the lake empties into the Pitt River; and Graveyard 5, north of Port Hammond, is 0.40 hectares - four thousand square metres, a burial ground and little else. Together they come to roughly 341 hectares, about three and a half square kilometres, scattered in five pieces across a valley the Nation once moved through freely. The fragments are not the territory. They are what the reserve system made of it.

## A Language the Schools Did Not Take

Children taken into the residential school system were punished for speaking their own languages, and across the coast that policy did exactly what it was designed to do. hənq̓əmínəm̓ - the downriver dialect of Halkomelem, one of the Coast Salish languages - survived anyway, carried by Katzie speakers through the decades when carrying it was dangerous. It is still spoken in the community today, still taught, still used in the names the Nation uses for its own places. Language work is slow and unglamorous: elders recording word lists, young people learning sounds that English does not contain. It is also the clearest measure of what was attempted here and what failed.

## Teachings Written on the Land

The Katzie describe their teachings as written on the land, which makes any development across that land a question rather than a formality. In September 2004 the Nation signed a benefit agreement with TransLink over the Golden Ears Bridge, the crossing that links Langley with Pitt Meadows and Maple Ridge directly through Katzie territory; some members raised concerns about the bridge's effect on historical burial grounds. The bridge opened on 16 June 2009 and has been toll-free since 1 September 2017. The Nation today describes itself as a growing community of 614 members, led by Chief Grace George and a council of three, with its own departments for health, social services, early-years education, lands management and territorial guardianship. Federal figures from February 2024 recorded 310 members living on reserve. Most Katzie still live within the territory their oral history says Swaneset shaped for them.

## Flight Context

Katzie territory centres on 49.20 N, 122.67 W in the Lower Fraser Valley east of Vancouver. The single best orientation feature is Pitt Lake: a long dark finger of water running roughly north from the Pitt River, hemmed by steep forested walls, with the Golden Ears peaks of Garibaldi Provincial Park rising beyond its head. Fly it at 2,500 to 4,500 feet AGL and the whole story lays out at once - the lake at the top, the braided sloughs and diked farmland of Pitt Meadows in the middle, the Fraser River and Barnston Island at the bottom, and the Golden Ears Bridge crossing the Fraser to the southwest. Pitt Meadows Airport (CYPK), itself one of the busiest general aviation fields in Canada, sits directly beneath the territory - expect heavy circuit and helicopter traffic and monitor the mandatory frequency. Other nearby fields: Vancouver International (CYVR) about 25 nm west, Abbotsford (CYXX) about 20 nm southeast, and Boundary Bay (CZBB) to the southwest. Valley fog and low cloud are routine from October through March, and the mountain walls north of Pitt Lake can hide in cloud while the delta below stays clear; summer afternoons give long visibility and a westerly inflow wind.
