# Fort Langley National Historic Site

The Hudson's Bay Company came to the Fraser for beaver pelts and ended up making its money on salted salmon, cranberries, and one November morning that created a province.

**Location:** 49.1681, -122.5720

**Categories:** National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Hudson's Bay Company forts, History of British Columbia, Open-air museums in British Columbia, Forts in British Columbia

## Introduction

The most valuable cargo leaving Fort Langley was never fur. It was fish. Coopers inside the palisade turned out barrels by the hundred, and Kwantlen fishers filled them with salmon that sailed to Hawaii; cranberries went south to California. In 1831 alone the fort put up nearly three hundred barrels of salted salmon. The Hudson's Bay Company had come to this bend of the Fraser for beaver pelts and a defensible border, and found instead that the river's real wealth was the thing the Stó:lo had been harvesting here for thousands of years. Today ten buildings stand inside the reconstructed walls. Exactly one of them is old.

## Before the Palisade

The Fraser Valley had been Stó:lo territory for millennia before a European saw it. The river was road, larder and marketplace at once, carrying people and goods between neighbouring communities and returning salmon by the million every year. Historians describe the pre-contact valley as one of the most economically productive regions of the Pacific Northwest, and the societies it supported were correspondingly elaborate - complex social hierarchies, distinctive artistic traditions, substantial architecture. This was not empty country awaiting a fort. It was a working economy with its own rules, and the Kwantlen, whose villages lay along this stretch of river, would spend the next several decades deciding on their own terms how much of the newcomers' trade they wanted.

## A Fort Built Against a Clock

The fort exists because of a deadline. Britain and the United States had agreed in 1818 to share the Oregon Country, and that arrangement was set to expire in 1827. Fort George, the Company's Pacific depot, sat on the south bank of the Columbia - almost certainly American ground in any eventual boundary line. After the North West Company merged into the Hudson's Bay Company in 1821, Governor George Simpson wanted a bigger northern hub near the mouth of the Fraser, a river he believed to be more navigable than the Columbia. He sent Chief Trader James McMillan to look. McMillan liked the ground near the Salmon River: farmable, and thick with fish. The party that arrived at the end of July 1827 was in rough shape, several men ill and the horses dead, crippled or spent, but the first bastion went up by mid-August and the palisade was closed in early September. The post was named for Thomas Langley, a Company director who never saw it.

## Salmon in Barrels

The first fort stood four kilometres downstream at a place the Kwantlen knew as snaqwamex - later Old Fort Langley, renamed Derby in 1858, and now simply farmland. In 1839 operations moved upstream to the present site above Bedford Channel, across from McMillan Island, and a fire the following year forced much of it to be rebuilt. What made the place profitable was partnership rather than conquest. The Kwantlen, led by Chief Whattlekainum, brought furs and fish and took blankets and trade goods in return, and the fort's coopers - among them William Cromarty and a Kwantlen barrel-maker recorded as Peopeo - built the casks that carried the catch to Honolulu and San Francisco. That trade came at a cost the ledgers never showed. As settlement spread along the river, Stó:lo families lost reliable access to fishing sites they had used for generations, and later legal restrictions on selling their own catch made it steadily harder to earn a living from the river that had always fed them.

## Nineteen November

Gold changed everything in a single season. When word got out in 1858 that the Fraser bars were paying, something like thirty thousand miners poured north, most of them American, and Britain moved fast to establish a mainland colony before the question of who owned the place answered itself. Fort Langley was the obvious stage and the wrong fortress: Colonel Richard Moody of the Royal Engineers judged it militarily indefensible and put the new capital at New Westminster, on the high north bank downstream. Still, it was here on 19 November 1858 that James Douglas, freshly sworn as the colony's first governor, read out the proclamation creating the Colony of British Columbia - a pointed gesture, made on his way upriver to face down American miners after the Fraser Canyon War. The fort's own decline followed within thirty years. Paddlewheelers carried trade past it to Hope and Yale, the capital moved on to Victoria, and competitors dismantled the Company's monopoly. In 1886 Fort Langley stopped being a trading post.

## One Building, Then Ten

By the 1920s the storehouse was all that remained. Canada designated the site in 1923, and from 1931 the Native Sons and Daughters of British Columbia ran a museum in that lone survivor, continuing until 1956. Parks Canada took over in 1955; three buildings were reconstructed in time for the province's 1958 centennial, and the rest followed across the second half of the century - the blacksmith shop with its working forge in the 1970s, a cooperage in 1992, the Depot in 1997. A single acre in 1924 has grown to 8.5 hectares. In 2019 students from Langley Fine Arts School worked with Kwantlen First Nation members on a project called This is Kwantlen, mounting photographs and biographies of living Kwantlen people outside the fort and through the village. The gesture was pointed too: the people who made this place profitable had, for a long time, been the hardest to find inside its walls.

## Flight Context

Fort Langley sits at 49.1681 N, 122.5720 W, on the south bank of Bedford Channel opposite McMillan Island, where the Fraser River braids around a string of low wooded islands roughly 50 km upstream from its mouth. Fly it at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL: the reconstructed palisade and its bastions are small but distinct, a pale rectangle set against the dark green of the riverbank with the village street grid immediately south and the Golden Ears peaks filling the horizon to the north. Langley Regional Airport (CYNJ) is about 4 nm south-southwest and the natural departure point; Pitt Meadows (CYPK) lies roughly 6 nm northwest across the river, Abbotsford (CYXX) about 12 nm southeast, and Vancouver International (CYVR) 24 nm west. Radiation fog fills the Fraser Valley on clear autumn and winter mornings and can obscure the whole river corridor until mid-morning; summer afternoons bring the cleanest light on the north-facing palisade.
