# Ruskin, British Columbia

A co-operative sawmill village at the mouth of the Stave River, named for a Victorian social critic, undone first by a drought and later by a government order.

**Location:** 49.2000, -122.4330

**Categories:** Historical communities, Utopian and intentional communities, British Columbia, Fraser Valley, Industrial history, Japanese Canadian history

## Introduction

In the spring of 1897, thirty children walked to a brand-new schoolhouse at the mouth of the Stave River. Their parents, fifty-four members of a co-operative society, had reason to believe they were building something that would last: a sawmill, a general store, a smithy, a shoemaker's shop, a dairy and a vegetable farm, houses and barns and a boarding house, all of it held in common. They had called the settlement Ruskin Mills, after John Ruskin, the English art critic and social thinker whose ideas about work and dignity several of the leading members liked to argue about in the evenings. The following summer, it did not rain.

## The Summer the River Failed

Everything in the operation depended on water. Horses and oxen dragged logs out of the bush to the bank of the Stave, and the river carried them down to the mill. That was the whole system, simple and cheap and entirely at the mercy of the weather. Through the rainless summer of 1898 the Stave shrank until the logs would not float, and the mill sat idle with nothing to cut. The Society had no cash reserve. Facing bankruptcy, its members handed their assets to E. H. Heaps and Company, the Vancouver firm that had supplied the mill machinery on credit. Most of them packed up and left. A few stayed on as employees of the company that now owned what they had built. The name stuck, though. The post office had opened on the first day of January that year, and an official name is a hard thing to unmake.

## Steam, Fire, and Receivership

Heaps had no interest in utopia and considerable interest in lumber. The company expanded the mill, retired the oxen, and replaced them with steam donkeys and a logging railway that pushed northwest until it reached Dewdney Trunk Road. Across the CPR tracks on the Fraser shore stood the Heaps building, which housed the office, the store, the post office and quarters for senior staff. Then the mill burned in the winter of 1904 and 1905. It was rebuilt, and in 1910 it burned again. There were plans for something larger, and promises, but when Vancouver's building boom collapsed in 1913 the money evaporated and the Ruskin operation went into receivership. By then a rival had appeared half a mile upriver, just across the Mission boundary: the American-owned Stoltze Manufacturing Company, whose shingle mill opened in 1912 and sold into the United States rather than the flattened local market. Through the 1920s it was the largest shingle mill in British Columbia.

## Raspberries on 280th Street

Stoltze's success rested substantially on Japanese labour, in the woods and on the mill floor. After the First World War, Japanese families also began farming here, planting raspberries and strawberries on the benches above the river. By the 1930s about thirty Japanese landowners were registered in Ruskin, and roughly three-quarters of the community's population was Japanese. Class photographs from the Ruskin elementary school in those years show only a handful of children of European descent among them. There were Japanese logging outfits, a couple of small Japanese sawmills, and a community hall of their own on 280th Street where the events of a full community life took place. All of it ended in 1942, when the Canadian government expelled Japanese Canadians from the coast and interned them. The farms, the mills, the hall, the neighbours: gone in a season, by order. With those families departed any significant agriculture in Ruskin. Nothing replaced it.

## The Water That Stayed

The river that failed the co-operative became the district's most valuable asset. A power company formed in 1899 and won provincial permission to harness Stave Falls; it took more than a decade and a successor company before power finally flowed from the Stave Dam plant in December 1911. To reach the construction site, the British Columbia Electric Railway built the Stave Falls Branch in 1910, connecting it to the CPR line at Ruskin, and the traffic it generated finally persuaded the CPR to build Ruskin a station of its own that same year. In 1930, Ruskin Dam was completed at the narrowest point of what had been the Stave River canyon, flooding it into Hayward Lake, roughly four and a half miles long and named for one of the company's engineers. Stave Falls itself is a dry falls now, water diverted, the drop that gave the place its name reduced to bare rock.

## Reading the Grade

The trains stopped coming. Passenger numbers dwindled until the scheduled stop no longer made sense, and in 1961 the station building was dismantled; with it went the resident CPR agent, whose services local industry had insisted were absolutely essential. The Cash Grocery lasted longer, serving the community from its building north of the tracks for more than half a century, until the widening of the Lougheed Highway took it down. The school closed in 1998 and Ruskin's children now go to Whonnock. What survives is mostly infrastructure repurposed as memory: the railway grade still traceable through the trees, part of it now a walking trail along the shore of Hayward Lake, some of its trestles standing in ruins and deliberately cut back so nobody climbs them. And there is the community hall, its facade carrying the date 1922 for the building that burned, though the structure people actually gather in opened in 1924 and has been theirs ever since.

## Flight Context

Ruskin lies at 49.2000 N, 122.4330 W on the north bank of the Fraser River about 35 miles east of Vancouver, where the Stave River comes down to meet it. Best viewed between 2,500 and 5,000 feet. The navigational giveaway is the Stave system stacked in a line running north from the Fraser: the concrete of Ruskin Dam at the canyon narrows, the long thin finger of Hayward Lake behind it, then Stave Falls Dam and the much larger Stave Lake beyond, with the Golden Ears peaks rising steeply to the north. The Lougheed Highway and the CPR main line run together along the Fraser shore beneath. Nearest airports are Abbotsford International (CYXX) about 11 nm south-southeast, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 11 nm west, Chilliwack (CYCW) roughly 19 nm east, and Vancouver International (CYVR) some 27 nm west. Fraser Valley fog and low stratus are frequent from October through March and can fill the valley floor while the ridges stay clear; summer afternoons give the cleanest view of the reservoirs.
