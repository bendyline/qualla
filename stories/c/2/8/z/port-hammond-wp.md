# Port Hammond

For a few months it was the western end of the Canadian Pacific Railway; a century later Hammond is a riverside mill village of cottages, ballfields and film crews.

**Location:** 49.2000, -122.6500

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods, Railways, History, Company towns, British Columbia

## Introduction

For a while, this was the end of the line. When Canadian Pacific track first reached the north bank of the Fraser here, Port Hammond was the railway's westward terminus, and two brothers who had bargained their land away for it could reasonably believe they had bought a city. William Hammond, a civil engineer, and his brother John, a farmer, had held the property since an 1862 Crown Grant, shared with a Mrs. E. H. Mohun. Their deal with the CPR traded right-of-way, station ground and yards for one condition: the first station in Maple Ridge would be theirs. Then the rails kept going west to Burrard Inlet, and the city that was going to be Hammond became Vancouver instead.

## The Port That Wasn't

The "Port" in the name was not wishful thinking, at least not entirely. As Hammond's Landing, the place handled steamships and mail for all CPR shipping out of New Westminster and Victoria, and the brothers believed a deep-water port would grow out of it. The Fraser had other ideas, and so did the railway. What settled here instead was a working village strung along the riverbank, most of its street grid squeezed between the CPR mainline and the water - and, consequentially, outside the main dyke that protects the rest of the area from the Fraser's spring freshet. Hammond has always lived a little closer to the river than is entirely comfortable.

## Who Built It, and Who Stayed

The railway brought Chinese labourers and merchants to the Fraser Valley, and when construction moved on, some stayed - working farms, working in households, making lives in a country whose laws were actively hostile to them. Their part of Hammond stood near what is now 207th Street and Maple Crescent, in the same stand of big-leaf maples that eventually gave Maple Ridge its name. After the First World War another cluster of houses acquired the nickname "Swede Row," for the Swedish millworkers and loggers who filled them. A village of a few hundred people, and already two distinct immigrant neighbourhoods inside it.

## The Graveyard on the North Side

On the north edge of Hammond is a parcel of just 0.40 hectares that carries the official name Graveyard Indian Reserve No. 5 - the bureaucratic language of another century, preserved in the land registry. It exists because the other cemetery in the area was for white settlers. Indigenous people and Chinese residents were not permitted there, and so the ground was purchased from the Hammond brothers to give them somewhere to bury their dead. A few Hawaiian workers - Kanakas, in the term of the day - had been buried in the white graveyard, which tells you the line was drawn by custom rather than any consistent rule. The reserve is small enough to walk across in a minute and is held today by the Katzie First Nation, whose ancestors were here long before the Crown Grant of 1862.

## Company Town

From 1910 onward a major sawmill defined the place, and Port Hammond was a company town in the full sense: the mill cut the lumber, and the mill built the houses its workers slept in. In 1946 the operation came under British Columbia Forest Products, then among the largest milling concerns in the province. Interfor bought the Hammond division in 1991 and closed the operation in 2019. The little cottage-scaled mill houses are still there, tight-set on small lots near the river, and they are the reason Hammond looks nothing like the subdivisions that fill the rest of Maple Ridge. A neighbourhood built for shift workers keeps the proportions of shift work.

## Doubling for Somewhere Else

These days the cameras come to Hammond. Series including Arrow and The Flash have filmed in the Maple Meadows Business Park, using the river-edge industrial landscape as a stand-in for whatever fictional city the script requires. The more local passion is baseball. Hammond Stadium is a year-round training ground for minor ball, and on 30 July 2016 the City of Maple Ridge unveiled a public artwork there called "Play Ball," a nod to how long the game has been part of the neighbourhood's summers. Between the ballpark, the soccer pitches, the golf and the riverside cycling, a place that failed to become a port has settled comfortably into being a village.

## Flight Context

Port Hammond lies at 49.2 N, 122.65 W on the north bank of the Fraser River, in the west end of Maple Ridge and just east of the Golden Ears Bridge, which is the single easiest landmark in the picture: a cable-stayed span crossing to Langley. From 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the tight grid of small houses pinched between the CPR mainline and the water, with the broad Fraser curving south of it and the Pitt River meeting the Fraser a short way west. Pitt Meadows Regional (CYPK) sits immediately west and is the natural field for a close look; Langley Regional (CYNJ) is across the river to the south, Abbotsford International (CYXX) east up the valley, and Vancouver International (CYVR) downstream to the west. The peaks of the Golden Ears stand to the north and make an unmistakable bearing in clear weather; in spring, the Fraser runs high and brown with freshet melt, which is exactly the season this townsite was built outside the dyke to face.
