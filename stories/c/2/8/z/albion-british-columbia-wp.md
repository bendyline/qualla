# Albion, British Columbia

A Fraser River neighbourhood whose train stop was won by one heavy man on a stormy night, and whose orchards and ferry have since given way to subdivisions.

**Location:** 49.1833, -122.5500

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in Maple Ridge, British Columbia, Populated places on the Fraser River, Local history, Transportation history

## Introduction

The trains did not stop at Albion. They rattled past the orchards and the sawmill sheds on their way to Haney, and anyone living out here walked the difference. Then one stormy evening a crew made an exception for a single resident, a man whose weight, in the delicate phrasing of the local record, "was not to be lightly estimated," and let him down at Albion rather than watch him trudge home through the rain. Within a week other men were demanding the same courtesy. The railway gave in. That is how a community on the north bank of the Fraser River got itself onto the map - not by petition, but by one awkward act of mercy that everyone else wanted a share of.

## Orchard on the North Bank

Albion is the oldest non-Indigenous settlement in what is now Maple Ridge, only slightly younger than Fort Langley directly across the water. Its first European settler was Samuel Robertson, who came over from the fort with his Indigenous wife, Julia, and planted what is credited as British Columbia's first fruit orchard two years before he moved here for good. The place was called East Haney at first. It became Albion in 1907, borrowing the old poetic name for Britain. Another early settler, Peter Baker - Boulanger before the name was anglicized - gave his to the road that is now 240th Street, the spine the neighbourhood still hangs from. Robertson's name survives too, on the technical secondary school that draws students from across the district.

## River Road

Between the Canadian Pacific tracks and the water runs River Road, and it has been working industrial ground since the railway arrived in the 1880s. Sawmills. Canneries. Fishboats tied up where the current slowed. For most of a century Albion faced the river, because the river was the reason to be here. Langley Indian Reserve No. 5, which contains a cemetery, lies just east along the same bank. It is one of several reserves administered by the Kwantlen First Nation, whose main community sits directly across the Fraser on McMillan Island at Fort Langley - a reminder that the two shores were one neighbourhood long before either settlement had a name in English.

## The Boat, Then the Bridge

From 1957 onward, crossing to Langley meant queueing for the Albion Ferry. It charged forty cents a car at first, dropped its tolls in 1972, and spent the next thirty-seven years as one of the few genuinely free rides in Metro Vancouver. Locals planned their days around it. Then the Golden Ears Bridge opened on 16 June 2009, a 2,410-metre extradosed span - the longest of its kind in North America - and the little ferries became redundant almost overnight. The Kulleet and the Klatawa made their last crossings just after noon on 31 July 2009 and were sold two years later. The bridge carried a toll for its first eight years; the province lifted it on 1 September 2017, so the crossing is free again, just not in a way that requires anyone to wait on a dock.

## The Hall and the Vinyl Monster

In 1923 the Albion Community Association built itself a hall, and for decades it was the answer to a specific problem. "The people of Albion didn't travel very far afield," one supporter recalled, "but took pleasure in getting together with one another. Of necessity their lives were lived within walking distance of their homes." The municipality bought the hall and demolished it in 2011. What replaced the old rural Albion came faster and in beige: so much new subdivision housing that residents coined a name for the spread, the "vinyl monster," after the siding on the newer homes filling the former farm fields east of 240th. The arguments that followed were the ordinary ones: crowded schools, where the shopping should go, what to do with the last open hills. Maple Ridge now lists an Albion Community Centre among its recreation facilities, which is a century-late reply to a hall built because nobody could drive anywhere.

## What Still Stands

The Hill House holds the high ground on 240th just north of the Lougheed Highway: four storeys, roughly 4,000 square feet, Edwardian, built in 1912 and still reading as the grand house of a farming community. Down at the fairgrounds, Country Fest fills each July with the kind of event that only works when a neighbourhood still thinks of itself as rural. In December 2010 the municipal council voted thirty-nine acres of the old John Jackson farm into parkland, the trade being approval for 112 new homes elsewhere on the same property. That transaction is Albion in miniature: a little of the old landscape kept by agreeing to build over the rest.

## Flight Context

Albion sits on the north bank of the Fraser River at 49.1833 N, 122.5500 W, inside the City of Maple Ridge. Fly it at 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, low enough to pick out the Canadian Pacific line and River Road between the tracks and the water. The clearest landmark is the Golden Ears Bridge immediately downstream to the west, its extradosed towers crossing to Langley; across the river lie McMillan Island and Fort Langley. Pitt Meadows Regional Airport (CYPK) is about 8 nm west, Abbotsford International (CYXX) roughly 15 nm southeast, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 25 nm west. The Golden Ears peaks rise steeply to the north, so expect rapid cloud formation on the ridges even when the valley is clear.
