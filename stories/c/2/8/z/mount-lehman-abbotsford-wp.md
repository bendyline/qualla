# Mount Lehman, Abbotsford

A Fraser Valley farming community that began at a riverboat wharf in the 1870s, moved itself to meet the interurban railway in 1910, and kept its school, church and hall running for well over a century after the town around them dissolved.

**Location:** 49.1186, -122.3800

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in Abbotsford, British Columbia, Fraser Valley, History of British Columbia

## Introduction

A few weathered pilings still stand in the Fraser River at the foot of a bluff east of Fort Langley. They are what remains of Lehman's Landing, once the only way into the Matsqui country — the stop where paddle-wheelers working the run between Yale and New Westminster put in to set down settlers, mail and hardware, and take on milk. The road that climbed from the wharf to the farms on the plateau above carried vehicles until 1950. The last few hundred metres are a footpath now, and even that is shut: landslides have eaten sections of the route, Abbotsford has declared the ground unstable, and a sign at the trailhead asks people to stay off.

## Why Anyone Chose the Bluff

Mount Lehman is not a mountain. It is a plateau of upraised land sitting between the flat expanse of Matsqui Prairie to the east and Glen Valley to the west, and in 1874 it was covered in cedar and fir heavy enough to discourage anyone thinking about agriculture. That looked like a poor bet for a farming settlement. It turned out to be the opposite.

The advantage was elevation. Down on the prairie the Fraser flooded every summer and the mosquitoes arrived in clouds; up on the escarpment neither reached. Clearing the timber was brutal, slow work — but underneath it lay soil that repaid the effort in pasture and crops. The first settlers were choosing high ground over easy ground, and the arithmetic held.

## Four Names on the Escarpment

Royal Engineer Alben Hawkins came to survey in April 1874 and stayed, taking a plot on the lip of the escarpment overlooking Matsqui Prairie. He was not quite first. Charles Malcolm Nicholson, his wife Priscilla and their eight children had been living about a kilometre to the southeast since roughly 1872 — but under the colony's pre-emption system, which granted land to those who filed on it and cleared it, the Nicholsons held no title. Presence and ownership were different things, and the records remember the filers.

In 1875 the cousins Samuel and Isaac Lehman pre-empted land here, Isaac alongside his brother-in-law Christian Musselman. Isaac's quarter-section lay toward the Fraser, at the northeast corner of section 11, township 14. Musselman died unexpectedly, and Samuel came with his wife Katherine and their six children to take up the ground he had left. The community that grew around them carries Isaac Lehman's name.

## Everything Arrived by Water

The wharf came first, because everything did. Built at the foot of the bluff, Lehman's Landing became the important riverboat stop east of Fort Langley, and the trail hacked up from it to the settlement was called, with no ceremony at all, Landing Road. Settlers logged, burned and grubbed out the underbrush, and a network of trails spread across the plateau.

Then the institutions arrived, in a tight run of years that still defines the place. Thomson's General Store was operating at the landing by 1883. The post office opened on 1 May 1884, and the first one-room school went up the same year. Mount Lehman United Church followed in 1894, the community hall in 1904. School, hall and church have all run continuously ever since — the school is the oldest in continuous operation anywhere in Abbotsford.

## The Milk Train

In 1910 the British Columbia Electric Railway ran a line from Chilliwack to Vancouver straight through Mount Lehman, and the community quietly picked itself up and moved. The store went from the landing to the school, then to the railway crossing, with the post office following it; a hardware store and service station grew up at the same spot. Farmers turned to vegetables and dairy, because now they could get them to market without waiting on a paddle-wheeler.

From 1910 to 1952 there were passenger and freight runs twice a day, an extra market train on Fridays, and every morning a milk train. By the 1930s the crossing supported a hardware store, a feed co-operative, a bank, two general stores, a post office, a shoemaker, a butcher, a library and a station. For a community of a few hundred farmers on a wooded bluff, that is a remarkable amount of town.

## Farms, and a Room Full of Books

Abbotsford grew, and most of that main street went with it. Mount Lehman joined the District of Matsqui in 1892; Matsqui was folded into the City of Abbotsford in 1995, and the supermarkets and gas stations are now a ten-minute drive away rather than a walk. What survived is the part that was never commercial. The hall still takes bookings for weddings, dinners and the Fall Fair. There is a fire hall, a credit union, the post office, and a branch of the Fraser Valley Regional Library so small it fits in a single room. The school teaches about 110 children, kindergarten through grade five, on the ground it opened on in 1884.

Around it: blueberry and raspberry fields, orchards, tree farms, estate wineries, boarding stables, and greenhouses raising cucumbers, tomatoes and peppers. Chickens, cattle, and — genuinely — llamas. The railway still runs, though it answers to Southern Railway of British Columbia now. The trains carry freight and do not stop.

## Flight Context

Mount Lehman lies at 49.1186°N, 122.3800°W on the north edge of Abbotsford, British Columbia, about 8 nm northwest of Abbotsford International (CYXX). From 2,500–4,000 ft AGL the plateau is unmistakable: a raised, tree-fringed table of small acreages and greenhouse roofs standing above the dead-flat, ditch-ruled green of Matsqui Prairie to the east and Glen Valley to the west, with the Fraser River wrapping its northern foot. Pick up the Highway 1 corridor to the south and follow Mount Lehman Road north; the Southern Railway line crosses it near the old settlement centre. Nearby fields: CYXX (Abbotsford International) southeast, CYNJ (Langley) west, CYPK (Pitt Meadows) northwest, and CYVR (Vancouver International) about 30 nm west. Fraser Valley fog and low stratus are common from late autumn through winter and frequently blanket the river flats while the plateau stays clear — a striking sight from above. Mount Baker sits on the southeastern horizon on good days and makes an easy visual anchor.
