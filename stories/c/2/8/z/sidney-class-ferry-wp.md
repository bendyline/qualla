# Sidney-class ferry

Two ships built in 1960 to end a strike became the first fleet of BC Ferries, ran for four decades almost unaltered, and ended as hulks on the Fraser River.

**Location:** 49.1470, -122.3906

**Categories:** Maritime history, Ferries of British Columbia, Ships built in British Columbia, Transport in British Columbia, Shipwrecks and hulks

## Introduction

There is a burned ship on the Fraser River at Mission, British Columbia, and its last name was Bad Adventure. She was launched in 1959 as Sidney, spent forty years as Queen of Sidney carrying cars between Vancouver Island and the mainland, and was sold off for a hundred thousand Canadian dollars in 2002 to owners with plans that never came to anything. Shortly before two in the morning on May 3, 2025, fire took hold aboard her and the city of Mission opened its Emergency Operations Centre. The RCMP called the blaze suspicious. It is an undignified end for a vessel that once represented a province deciding it would no longer depend on anyone else to reach its own capital.

## A Strike Builds a Fleet

In the summer of 1958, employees of CP Steamships and the Black Ball Line walked off the job, and the water route between Vancouver and Victoria simply stopped. Vancouver Island discovered how thin the thread was. Premier W. A. C. Bennett's response was not to negotiate but to build: a provincial ferry service, publicly owned, with ships of its own. Two were ordered from British Columbia yards and both keels went down on the same day, May 9, 1959. Sidney took shape at Victoria Machinery Depot in Victoria as yard number 85, launched that October. Tsawwassen rose at Burrard Dry Dock in North Vancouver as yard number 309, launched in November. Each was named for the terminal at the far end of the other's run, one at Swartz Bay near Sidney on the island, the other at Tsawwassen in Delta. They entered service together on June 15, 1960.

## Borrowed Lines

The design was not invented from scratch, because there was no time. Black Ball Transport already had a ferry on the ways in Seattle for its Port Angeles to Victoria run, the Coho, drawn by the naval architect Philip F. Spaulding. Spaulding and his Canadian partner Arthur McLaren took those lines and reworked them so the new provincial ships could load vehicles through the bow as well as the stern, which in a busy terminal is the difference between turning a ship around and turning it around fast. The result ran 102.4 metres overall, 93.8 metres between perpendiculars, with a beam of 22.6 metres. Twin sixteen-cylinder Mirrlees diesels put roughly 4,500 kilowatts through two screws for eighteen knots. They carried 108 cars at first, then 138 after platforms were added to the car decks in 1971. The Coho, remarkably, is still working the Strait of Juan de Fuca.

## Queens, Not Princesses

Canadian Pacific had spent half a century calling its coastal steamers Princess this and Princess that, and the upstart provincial fleet was not about to sail in second place. A naming policy settled the matter: larger vessels would take Queen at the front of their names, smaller ones at the end. Tsawwassen became Queen of Tsawwassen in 1962, Sidney became Queen of Sidney in 1963, and the fleet had its answer. Beyond the new lettering, the ships changed almost not at all across four decades. There was one significant alteration, and it is the most human detail in the whole record: the restaurants had to be enlarged shortly after service began, because passengers ate far more than anyone had forecast. Nobody had allowed for the appetite of people crossing the Strait of Georgia with an hour and a half to kill.

## Laid Up on the Fraser

Queen of Sidney was retired in November 2000 and laid up in the Fraser River the following year. In 2002 she was sold for CAN$100,000 to private buyers, renamed Bad Adventure, and moved to a yard at Mission, where she has stayed ever since, upriver and out of sight. Her sister lasted longer. Queen of Tsawwassen came out of service in 2007, no longer able to satisfy current regulations, with a refit priced beyond what she was worth, and went up for sale in 2008. Neither ship was preserved. This is the ordinary fate of working vessels: the ones that get museums are warships and liners, while the boats that actually moved a province's daily life go quietly to a mudbank. The 2025 fire only made visible what had already happened to Queen of Sidney years before.

## Flight Context

The surviving hull lies at roughly 49.147 N, 122.391 W, in the river at Mission, British Columbia, about 40 nautical miles east of Vancouver. Fly the Fraser Valley eastbound at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL and the geography does the navigating for you: the Fraser River runs broad and silt-brown through flat farmland, with the Coast Mountains rising abruptly along the north bank. At Mission, look for two crossings close together, the Highway 11 Mission Bridge and the older Mission Railway Bridge with its central swing span, and for the white tower of Westminster Abbey on the bluff above the north bank, which is the single most conspicuous landmark for miles. The ferry hull sits in a river yard near the waterfront and is small at this scale; you will find the town first and the ship second. Nearest airports are Abbotsford International (CYXX) about 10 nm south, Chilliwack (CYCW) about 19 nm east, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 14 nm west, Langley Regional (CYNJ) about 16 nm southwest, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 32 nm west. Valley fog is common on autumn and winter mornings and can sit in the river channel long after the uplands clear; summer afternoons bring good visibility, with Mount Baker standing out sharply to the south-southeast.
