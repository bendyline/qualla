# Fort Langley

The Hudson's Bay post where British Columbia was proclaimed a colony in 1858 — then lost its capital, became a mill town, and reinvented itself as the Fraser Valley's favourite film set.

**Location:** 49.1685, -122.5800

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in Langley, British Columbia, Langley, British Columbia (district municipality), Populated places on the Fraser River, 1827 establishments in the British Empire, Colony of British Columbia (1858–1866), Former colonial capitals in Canada, Hudson's Bay Company forts, Fur trade, Heritage villages

## Introduction

British Columbia was proclaimed into existence inside a fur-trade post that smelled of salted salmon and cedar. On 19 November 1858, James Douglas stood in the Hudson's Bay Company fort on the south bank of the Fraser River, read out the proclamation establishing the Colony of British Columbia, and was named its first governor. Within months the capital had moved downriver and the fort's moment had passed. What remains is a village of roughly 3,800 people where Glover Road runs down to the water between restored storefronts — and where the most consequential thing that ever happened was finished in an afternoon.

## A Fort Built Against a Map

The post existed because of a line nobody had drawn yet. In the 1820s the country between the Rockies and the Pacific was the Columbia District to the British and Oregon Country to the Americans, and Sir George Simpson, governor of the Hudson's Bay Company, could see how the argument might end. If the boundary followed the 49th parallel instead of the Columbia River, Fort Vancouver — the company's western headquarters, across the water from present-day Portland — would land on the wrong side of it. So in 1827 Simpson ordered a new post built on the Fraser, about four kilometres downstream from the present village and deliberately on the south bank. Should the Columbia go to the Americans, a fort on the far shore would secure British claims to both sides of the Fraser. They named it for Thomas Langley, a director of the company. The buildings moved upstream in 1839, and fire forced a rebuild the following year.

## Barrels Bound for Honolulu

The fur trade turned out to be the least of it. Salmon made the fort's reputation: coopers packed the catch in casks of Douglas fir and white pine, nearly 300 barrels in 1831 alone, and by 1830 the post was also shipping cedar lumber and shingles, much of it toward the Hawaiian Islands, then the commercial crossroads of the Pacific. The farm filled out the ledger: by 1840, potatoes in quantity, 750 bushels of wheat, 500 of oats, 600 of peas. An overland route, the Cowlitz Portage, tied the post to Fort Vancouver by way of Fort Nisqually on Puget Sound. None of it would have worked without the people already living here. The Kwantlen and neighbouring nations were the fort's essential trading partners, and many of the men inside the palisade married women from those communities — kinship that held the whole enterprise together. The Kwantlen First Nation still administers a reserve on McMillan Island, directly across Bedford Channel.

## The Colony and the Colonel

Gold changed everything in 1858. Thousands of prospectors pushed up the Fraser, most of them American, and Douglas answered by laying a colony over top of them. A townsite called Derby had already been surveyed and its lots sold beside the fort's original location, with a barracks for the Royal Engineers under construction. Then Colonel Richard Moody arrived and studied the ground. American territory lay only a few miles away across easily crossed country; Derby, he concluded, could not be defended. On 14 February 1859 he chose a new site at the mouth of the Pitt River on the north bank and proposed the name Queensborough. That July, Douglas announced that Her Majesty had settled on New Westminster instead. Derby emptied. The fort's own trade shifted to Bedford Channel, sheltered from the current by McMillan and Brae islands, and that is where the village sits today, at the point where Glover Road meets the river.

## Eighty-Eight Acres of Sawdust

For most of the twentieth century this was a mill town. The railway had stripped away the community's shipping role, so when a sawmill opened on 88 acres of riverfront in 1921 it effectively rebuilt the local economy, and the neighbourhood that grew around it stayed resolutely working class into the 1970s. That same year, Dr. Benjamin Marr planted horse chestnuts along his Glover Road frontage; they still shade the approach into the village. By the late 1980s the machinery was aging and the operation redundant. Interfor cut staff and tried to reinvent the site as a value-added venture, but the mill shut for good in the mid-1990s. The land waited until 2005, when a contentious rezoning opened it to medium-density housing. Construction on the Bedford Landing community began the following year, eventually adding roughly 1,500 residents to a village that had held steady near 3,000 for generations.

## A Town That Plays Other Towns

Fort Langley now earns part of its living by looking like itself. New construction must follow strict guidelines to match the heritage streetscape, and the commercial core of about eighty businesses runs heavily to independents. The Coulter Berry Building at Mavis and Glover, completed in 2016 — three storeys of shops, offices and restaurants, certified LEED Gold — took the mixed-use award at that year's Fraser Valley Commercial Building Awards. The yellow Community Hall is the most photographed thing in town. Built in 1931 by hired hands and volunteers to a design by Archibald Campbell Hope, it remains privately held by the Fort Langley Community Improvement Society, which the local Women's Institute founded in 1924 to build it. Film crews adore the place. The hall has stood in for a sheriff's station in The X-Files, and the streets have played other towns in Air Bud, Once Upon a Time, Bates Motel, and Wyoming in the second season of The Last of Us. The reconstructed fort itself draws about 60,000 visitors a year.

## Flight Context

Fort Langley sits at 49.1685° N, 122.58° W on the south bank of the Fraser River, roughly 45 km east of Vancouver at the top end of the Township of Langley. Between 2,500 and 4,000 feet the geography reads clearly: the braided Fraser, the long green wedge of McMillan Island directly across Bedford Channel, Brae Island beside it, and the reconstructed palisade of the national historic site standing out as a rectangle of wooden walls near the waterfront. Glover Road runs south from the river toward Highway 1. Look north across the Fraser for the Golden Ears Bridge, and past it the twin summits of Golden Ears in the Coast Mountains — a dependable heading reference in clear air. Nearby fields are CYNJ (Langley Regional) 8 km south, CYPK (Pitt Meadows) northwest across the river, CYXX (Abbotsford) east up the valley, and CYVR (Vancouver International) to the west; a private airstrip with floatplane facilities lies just east of the community. Valley fog is common on autumn and winter mornings, while summer afternoons usually offer excellent visibility toward the Coast Mountains and Mount Baker.
