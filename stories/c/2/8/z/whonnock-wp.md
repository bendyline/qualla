# Whonnock

A hilly, half-forested community on the Fraser River that exists because a railway surveyor needed a station ten miles from the last one - and that carries, in its name, the one salmon that climbs its creek.

**Location:** 49.1833, -122.4670

**Categories:** Communities, Rivers and lakes, History, Indigenous history, British Columbia

## Introduction

The railway put a station here for a reason no romantic could love: the spot happened to be ten miles from Hammond, and ten miles was how far apart stations went. That was it. That was the whole calculation behind a community. The surveyors spelled it Wharnock at first, misheard from a Halkomelem word the local people had used far longer than anyone had been laying rail, and when the trains began running regularly in 1885 the settlers came up the line behind them. Whonnock has been rural and hilly and heavily treed ever since, 56 kilometres east of downtown Vancouver on the north bank of the Fraser, minding its own business in the eastern corner of Maple Ridge.

## The Fish in the Name

Whonnock comes from a Halkomelem word for the humpback salmon - the pink - which is the only salmon that ascends Whonnock Creek. Not the chinook, not the coho. One species, and the place took its name from it. The creek runs down from above Dewdney Trunk Road, past Whonnock Lake, and empties into the Fraser. The lake itself is a bog lake, fed regularly from one direction only, tea-coloured at the margins, ringed by mature timber. Beavers and muskrats work its edges. Loons breed on it, and the annual stocking of rainbow trout keeps a steady procession of anglers on its small sandy beach. It is not a dramatic body of water. It is the kind you learn by returning to it, which is what people here have done for a very long time.

## Before the Line Was Drawn

People have lived at this bend of the river for more than ten thousand years. Roughly twenty-five years before Simon Fraser came downriver in 1808, smallpox swept through and emptied the villages here and around Boundary Bay - arriving ahead of the men who carried it, killing people who had never seen a European. After Fort Langley was established around 1827, survivors and newcomers began repopulating the abandoned sites. The Whonnock tribe of the Kwantlen lived here in the years that followed, with their own chief. The last member of that tribe living on the reserve died in 1951. Whonnock Indian Reserve No. 1 still sits where the creek meets the Fraser, under Kwantlen First Nation jurisdiction from their headquarters on McMillan Island at Fort Langley. The name on every road sign in this valley is theirs.

## Shetland, Norway, and a Lot of Rain

Robert Robertson came from Shetland and settled in 1861 with his First Nations wife, raising a family beside the Whonnock village for twenty-five years with no other settler households nearby. Then the station arrived and the trickle became a stream. Whonnock gained a post office, a school, a general store and a string of churches before its neighbours had any of them. Most newcomers were British, from elsewhere in Canada, but Norwegians made up a notable share and shaped the place. Nearly everyone fished or logged; the farming was subsistence farming on poor ground, with a few residents scraping together small commercial orchards or poultry operations. A handful of comfortable families kept summer places and paid others to do the work. In 1912 the women of the community built and ran a hall, and for forty years it was where Whonnock happened.

## The Berry Slopes

From the 1920s, Japanese Canadian families made the south-facing slopes above the river into extensive berry farms - the warmest, best-drained ground in the district, and a large share of the community's population. In 1942 they were removed. Federal orders that January and March cleared everyone of Japanese ancestry out of a hundred-mile strip along the coast, more than 22,000 people, over 90 percent of Japanese Canadians and some 14,000 of them born in this country. Their land and belongings were seized and, from 1943, auctioned off well below value. They were not permitted back to the coast until April 1, 1949. Ottawa apologized in 1988 and paid redress to those still living. The berry fields on those slopes changed hands and changed crops, and the families who had cleared and planted them did not come back.

## What Held On

Whonnock has had a post office and a postmaster continuously since 1885 - the general store's owner held both jobs until 1914, when the post office moved to the site it still occupies, in a building dating from about 1932. The Whonnock postmaster is the last in the Lower Mainland to carry that title. The general stores did not fare as well: the first burned in 1918 and was never rebuilt, and the rest - Graham's, Luno's, Showler's Red and White - gave out after the Second World War, when cars and supermarkets made a village shop redundant. The McFli Feed Store now occupies the old Red and White building beside the post office. Mills still run on the waterfront, smaller than before. People keep horses, poultry, sheep, goats, llamas; weavers, potters and leather workers keep studios in the trees. The station is gone. The community the station accidentally created is not.

## Flight Context

Whonnock sits at 49.1833 N, 122.467 W, on the north bank of the Fraser River in eastern Maple Ridge, roughly 30 nm east of downtown Vancouver. Fly it at 2,000-3,500 feet AGL to read the terrain that defines the place: the wide braided Fraser to the south, the abrupt rise of forested hills to the north, and Whonnock Lake as a small dark oval about a mile inland, the most reliable visual fix in the area. Whonnock Creek drains south from the lake to the river; the Lougheed Highway and the CP main line run parallel along the flat river terrace. Stave Lake and the Stave River mouth lie a few miles east, with Golden Ears rising sharply to the north as a hard-to-miss reference peak. Nearby airports: Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 12 nm west, Abbotsford International (CYXX) about 12 nm southeast, Chilliwack (CYCW) about 24 nm east, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 30 nm west. Fraser Valley fog is frequent on autumn and winter mornings and can fill the river corridor while the hills above stay clear - one of the best reasons to come at first light.
