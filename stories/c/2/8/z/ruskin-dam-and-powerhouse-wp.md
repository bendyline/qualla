# Ruskin Dam and Powerhouse

A 1930 concrete gravity dam on the Stave River that drowned a canyon and its waterfall, and has since stood in for half the fictional dams on North American television.

**Location:** 49.1958, -122.4080

**Categories:** dams, engineering, rivers, british-columbia, pacific-northwest

## Introduction

In November 1930 the businessmen and politicians of the Fraser Valley sat down to dinner inside a powerhouse. Tables were set on the generator floor, under machinery that had been running for only a matter of days, beside a river that had just been stopped. It was an odd place to eat and an entirely deliberate one: the British Columbia Electric Railway wanted its guests to feel the scale of what money and concrete had done to the Stave River. Ninety-five years later the building still hums. The dam upstream of it is 59.4 metres of unreinforced mass holding back a lake that did not exist before the engineers arrived, and beneath that lake, somewhere under the flat green water, is a canyon nobody alive has seen.

## The Drowned Canyon

Before 1930 the lower Stave ran through a gorge that ended in a modest waterfall roughly where the dam now stands. Neither survives. The reservoir that replaced them is called Hayward Lake, named for the first superintendent of the Stave Falls Dam upstream, and it holds 42 million cubic metres of water across three square kilometres. It reaches 5.6 kilometres north, filling the old canyon to the base of the next dam in the chain, and it gathers runoff from a catchment of 953 square kilometres - most of it steep, forested Coast Mountain country that sheds rain in enormous quantities each winter. What looks like a placid recreational lake today is really a canyon with the lid closed on it, kept full on purpose, its shoreline drawn by an engineer's decision rather than by geology.

## Seven Gates and a Truss Bridge

Ruskin is a concrete gravity dam, which means it does its work by sheer weight - no arches, no clever transfer of load into the valley walls, just 110 metres of mass sitting on bedrock and refusing to move. Its spillway is an ogee curve fitted with seven radial gates, capable of passing 4,430 cubic metres per second when the Stave is in flood. The powerhouse stands immediately downstream on the river's eastern bank, holding three Francis turbine-generators, each fed by its own penstock rather than sharing a header. Getting to work means crossing the river: staff reach the powerhouse from the company offices on the west bank by a truss bridge, a detail that gives the whole installation the feel of a small industrial village with a single street.

## One River, Three Dams

Ruskin was never meant to stand alone. The Western Canada Power Company began developing the Stave Valley in the first decade of the twentieth century and finished Stave Falls Dam in 1912; the British Columbia Electric Railway bought the company in 1921 and completed Alouette Dam in 1928, then broke ground on Ruskin in 1929 about 5.6 kilometres below Stave Falls. Water now works its way down the system in stages. A tunnel 1,067 metres long moves water from Alouette Lake into Stave Lake, where an 8 MW powerhouse takes a first bite. Stave Falls, with 90 MW installed, takes the next. What comes out flows into Hayward Lake, and Ruskin takes the last. The original 1911 Stave Falls powerhouse was designated a National Historic Site of Canada in 2003 and now runs as a visitor centre - the rare industrial building that outlived its usefulness and was kept anyway.

## Reeves Dam

There is a second reason people recognize this place, and it has nothing to do with electricity. Ruskin's spillway face, its catwalks and its brooding 1930s powerhouse have drawn film crews for decades. The X-Files shot here. So did MacGyver, Dark Angel, The Boy Next Door and The Invisible. Smallville used it repeatedly and gave it a name of its own: in three episodes the structure appears as Reeves Dam, a nod to George Reeves and Christopher Reeve, the two actors most associated with the character the series was reimagining. It is a strange sort of fame. Millions of people have watched something dramatic happen at this dam without ever learning which river it holds back.

## Rebuilding a Ninety-Year-Old

Concrete poured in 1929 was not designed with modern seismic standards in mind, and the Stave Valley sits in a part of the world that expects a very large earthquake eventually. Two of the three generators went into service at the opening; the third was not added until 1950. Beginning in 2012, BC Hydro started an upgrade budgeted at roughly C$800 million and scheduled to run through 2018 - replacing the generating units, reinforcing the dam's right bank, rebuilding the intake and penstocks, replacing the spillway piers and gates, and relocating the switchyard. Three new 38 MW turbine-generators lifted the station's capacity to 114 MW, a modest gain for the money. But capacity was never the point. The point was to make sure the lid stays on the canyon.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.196N, 122.408W, on the Stave River about 2 km above its confluence with the Fraser, between Maple Ridge and Mission, British Columbia. Best viewed at 2,000-3,500 ft AGL; the dam is small in plan (110 m long) but the spillway face and the abrupt change from green reservoir to grey tailrace make it easy to pick out. Follow the Fraser east from Vancouver, find the Mission Bridge, then track the narrow north-south sliver of Hayward Lake upstream 5.6 km to the much larger Stave Lake beyond Stave Falls Dam. The Golden Ears peaks rise to the north-northwest and Mount Baker stands about 33 nm southeast. Nearest airports: Abbotsford (CYXX) roughly 10 nm south-southeast, Langley (CYNJ) about 11 nm southwest, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 12 nm west, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 30 nm west. Valley fog fills the Fraser lowland on clear autumn mornings and can hide the dam while the ridgelines above it stay in sun.
