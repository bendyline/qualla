# Derby, British Columbia

A crossroads in a Langley farm field is all that remains of the townsite that was briefly meant to be the capital of British Columbia.

**Location:** 49.2000, -122.6333

**Categories:** ghost-town, history, british-columbia, colonial-history, rivers, heritage

## Introduction

There is nothing at Derby. An intersection between farm fields on the northern edge of Langley, a line of trees, and the Fraser River sliding past in a long northward arc. No street grid survives. No foundations, no barracks, not even the church - the church was taken across the water to the other bank, where it still stands. What makes the emptiness worth stopping for is what was very nearly built here. For a few months spanning 1858 and 1859, this ground was the intended capital of the brand-new Colony of British Columbia. Had one colonel's judgment gone the other way, the province might have grown up in this field.

## The Company Gets There First

In 1827 the Hudson's Bay Company planted a fort on the south bank of the Fraser at this spot, for reasons that had less to do with the river than with a line on a map. Governor George Simpson expected the 49th parallel to become the boundary between British and American territory, and he wanted a Company post standing north of it before anyone else could argue the point. Fort Langley was the first Hudson's Bay post established in Coast Salish territory - among people who had fished and lived along this reach of the Fraser for thousands of years, and whose consent formed no part of the Company's calculation.

The fort became a business rather than a garrison. By 1830 it was shipping barrels of salted salmon, along with cedar lumber and shingles, as far as the Hawaiian Islands. In 1839 the Company picked the whole operation up and moved it four kilometres upriver, to the stretch of Bedford Channel where the reconstructed fort stands today, because ships could get at it more easily there.

## Gold, and a Capital on Paper

Then came 1858. Gold on the Fraser bars pulled thousands of miners north out of California in a single season, and Britain answered by converting the district into a Crown colony on 2 August 1858. The new Colony of British Columbia needed a seat of government, and Fort Langley served as its first.

A townsite was surveyed on the old fort's ground and given a name chosen with an eye on London: Derby, after Edward Geoffrey Smith-Stanley, 14th Earl of Derby, who happened to be British prime minister that year. Royal Engineers arriving in 1858 raised barracks on the site. On paper the place had everything a capital requires - a survey, a garrison, a name with a title behind it, and deep water at the door. What it did not have was high ground.

## One Colonel's Verdict

Colonel Richard Clement Moody reached Esquimalt on 25 December 1858 and was sworn in at Victoria as the colony's first lieutenant governor on 4 January 1859. He commanded the Columbia Detachment of the Royal Engineers - 150 men at first, later 172 - and his instructions were to build a colony capable of holding its ground.

He looked at Derby and disliked what he saw. Flat, low, and wide open from the American side, it could not be defended if the United States ever decided the gold fields were worth a war. Moody wanted a port with a bluff behind it, and he found one downriver. On 14 February 1859 Governor James Douglas proclaimed the new capital under the name Queensborough; Queen Victoria renamed it New Westminster on 20 July 1859. Derby, a few weeks into its career as a capital, was simply over. The engineers packed up and went to build somebody else's city.

## What the River Kept

Everything raised here is gone. The barracks, the fort, the surveyed lots - none of it stands, and no ruin marks the spot. The Church of St. John the Divine, the one structure anyone thought worth saving, was moved across the Fraser to what is now Maple Ridge, where it remains in use. The rest quietly stopped being a place at all. British Columbia's ghost-town lists carry Derby's name without being able to point at anything, which may be the purest form of ghost town there is.

What survived was the word itself. Derby Reach is the name of that long northward bend in the Fraser south of Haney, and Derby Reach Regional Park runs along the bank, with trails through cottonwood bottomland and gravel bars where people fish. Walkers cross ground that was once drawn up into government blocks and numbered for sale. It is an odd afterlife for a capital that never happened: remembered by a curve in a river.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.20N, 122.63W on the south bank of the lower Fraser River, in the northwestern corner of the Township of Langley. From the air the site reads as a rectangle of farmland where the Fraser swings north - the Derby Reach - with Maple Ridge and the community of Haney on the opposite bank. There is no townsite to spot; look instead for the river bend, the dyke and the narrow band of cottonwood along the shore, and the historic village of Fort Langley with its reconstructed palisade roughly four kilometres upstream. The Golden Ears peaks rise sharply to the north across the valley, and Mount Baker stands to the southeast on clear days. Nearby airports: Pitt Meadows Regional (CYPK) about 10 km northwest, Langley Regional (CYNJ) about 12 km south, Abbotsford International (CYXX) about 35 km east, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 45 km west. Best viewing altitude is 1,500-3,000 feet. The Fraser Valley holds thick radiation fog on autumn and winter mornings, which can erase the entire floodplain until midday.
