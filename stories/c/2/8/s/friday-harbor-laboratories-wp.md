# Friday Harbor Laboratories

The University of Washington marine station where a jellyfish from these waters gave up the green fluorescent protein - and, eventually, a Nobel Prize.

**Location:** 48.5461, -123.0130

**Categories:** washington, san-juan-islands, science, marine-biology, university, research-station

## Introduction

If you have ever seen a photograph of a glowing cell - a neuron lit up green, a tumour tracked through a living mouse, a zebrafish with its nervous system rendered visible - the technique behind it began with a jellyfish scooped out of the water here. Green fluorescent protein was discovered at Friday Harbor Laboratories in 1962, isolated from Aequorea victoria, a small crystal jellyfish common in these channels. It took decades for anyone to work out what to do with it, and in 2008 the Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Osamu Shimomura, Martin Chalfie, and Roger Tsien for the discovery and development of the protein. A sculpture on the campus marks the spot. The jellyfish themselves have had a harder time: their numbers in Friday Harbor collapsed, which has made studying GFP's role in the animal's own life considerably more difficult.

## Trevor Kincaid's Station

The laboratory is older than the discovery by more than half a century. It was founded in 1904 as the Puget Sound Biological Station by Trevor Kincaid, a zoology professor at the University of Washington who became its first director.

The choice of location was not sentimental. The San Juan archipelago sits where tidal exchange between the Strait of Juan de Fuca and the Salish Sea drives cold, nutrient-rich water through narrow channels, which produces one of the most biologically productive temperate marine environments anywhere. A researcher can stand on the dock and be over water a hundred feet deep and full of things nobody has looked at properly. That is the whole argument for a marine station, and it has held for over a century.

## Ten Weeks and an Original Question

What distinguishes FHL is the teaching model. The summer classes are intensive and selective, drawing graduate students from around the world who compete for the places, and the autumn and spring terms run roughly ten weeks with courses aimed at advanced undergraduates as well as graduate students.

The defining feature of those terms is that each one carries an original research component. Students do not merely repeat established experiments; they are expected to ask a question nobody has answered and then go get the data. The subject list reads like a map of what this coast makes possible - marine algae, conservation biology, invertebrate zoology, comparative invertebrate embryology, the functional morphology and ecology of marine fishes, larval ecology, paleontology, oceanography. Much of it depends on being able to collect a live organism in the morning and have it under a microscope before lunch.

## Living at the Lab

A field station is a place people stay, not just visit. FHL keeps a small resident scientific staff and offers laboratory space, a library, and housing year-round to visiting researchers - and, importantly, to their families. That last detail changes who can actually do this kind of work. A postdoctoral researcher with two young children is not going to spend a summer on a remote island unless the island has somewhere for the children to sleep.

The result is a community that reassembles each season: the same people returning year after year, overlapping with a rotating cast of students, all of them oriented around the same tide tables. Careers get built in these places. So do collaborations, marriages, and the long-running arguments that move a discipline along.

## The Long View

In 2004, the zoologist Patricia Louise Dudley, who had spent many summers at Friday Harbor, created an endowment for the study of systematics, the structure of marine organisms, and marine invertebrate ecology. She attached conditions: recipients must spend significant time at the laboratory, and she expressed a hope that the findings would contribute to understanding evolutionary relationships.

Those are unfashionable subjects in an era of high-throughput sequencing and grant cycles measured in months. Systematics - the patient work of establishing what a creature is and how it is related to everything else - is the foundation the rest of biology stands on, and it does not produce quick results. An endowment that insists on residence at a particular dock, studying particular animals, is a bet that some kinds of knowledge still require you to be in the room with the organism. The jellyfish that changed cell biology were not found in a database.

## Flight Context

Friday Harbor Laboratories sits at 48.546N, 123.013W on the northern shore of Friday Harbor, San Juan Island, occupying a wooded point across the water from the town and ferry terminal. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet look for the harbour itself - a busy anchorage in the San Juans, thick with sailboats in summer - with the ferry docks on the southwest side and the laboratory's cluster of buildings and dock on the point opposite. Friday Harbor Airport (KFHR) is less than a mile west, directly above the town; Roche Harbor (WA09) lies northwest, Orcas Island (KORS) north, and Bellingham (KBLI) east across Rosario Strait. Victoria (CYYJ) is west across Haro Strait and the international boundary. Expect heavy summer floatplane and ferry traffic, morning fog in the channels, and gusty conditions where wind funnels between the islands.
