# Crane Island (Washington)

A 236-acre private island in the San Juans with a grass airstrip, no public services, and a permanent population that may have fallen to four.

**Location:** 48.5971, -122.9983

**Categories:** Islands, San Juan Islands, Aviation, Local History, Washington

## Introduction

The tricky part of landing on Crane Island is the last hundred feet. The strip is 1,500 feet of gravel and grass, private, for residents only, and the prevailing wind usually blows across it rather than down it. Pilots set up crabbed into that wind, and then, as the aircraft sinks below the tops of the surrounding trees, the wind simply stops. Whatever correction you were holding is suddenly wrong. In February 2022 a Cessna 207 flying a commuter run from Bellingham came in here and ran out of usable runway, damaging the aircraft; nobody was hurt, and the operator's own recommendation afterwards was that the 207 should not be used on this strip at all. That is Crane Island in one incident: small, beautiful, and not to be taken casually.

## The Rock Pile

Crane is the largest of the Wasp Islands, a cluster scattered in the channel off the southwestern shore of Orcas Island, opposite the northwest corner of Shaw. Locals call the group the rock pile, which is less poetic than accurate - the shorelines here are littered with rock, above and just below the surface, and the boating requires attention. The name came from Charles Wilkes, whose United States Exploring Expedition charted these waters between 1838 and 1842 and handed out names commemorating American naval history. Wasp honoured a sloop-of-war commanded by Jacob Jones in the War of 1812. Crane itself covers 0.956 square kilometres - 236 acres, a shape you can walk across in an afternoon.

## Cadwell's Orchard

The first settler arrived in 1879. Walter P. Cadwell came from a pioneer family living near Pole Pass, the narrow channel between Crane and Orcas, and he planted a fruit and vegetable farm on the island. He held it until 1906, when he sold to John C. Hammond of Seattle. Over the following decades the island tried on a series of trades, none of which lasted: a lime quarry on the west side, a pheasantry, sheep ranching, and logging that continued until 1959. The list reads like the standard repertoire of small-island economics in the San Juans - work whatever the ground will give you, and when it stops giving, try the next thing. Lime, birds, wool, timber: each of those trades left something behind, a cut bank here, a fence line there, and then moved on.

## Twenty People, Then Four

In 1960, Island Properties began subdividing Crane for development, and the shape of the modern island was set: houses ringing the shoreline, a small private community dock on the east side near Pole Pass, and no public services of any kind. The census counted twenty permanent residents in 2000. Since then, the pattern has been one that repeats across desirable islands everywhere - the original full-time residents have largely moved off, and their places have been taken by younger part-time owners, so that the number of people who actually live here year-round may now be as few as four. The houses do not stand empty in July. In February they mostly do.

## What the Middle Holds

The centre of the island is a different story from its edges. Most of the interior is owned by the Crane Island Nature Preserve, which means the developed ring encloses a core of protected ground - forest and rock left alone while the shoreline was parcelled out. Meanwhile the Washington State ferry runs past the island's southern side through Wasp Passage, threading the rock pile on its way between Orcas and San Juan Island. Passengers on deck watch Crane slide by at close range: a wooded hump, a few roofs among the trees, a dock, a mown line of grass that turns out to be a runway. Almost nobody aboard will ever set foot on it. That is roughly the arrangement everyone involved seems to prefer. Crane is visible from the water, from the ferry deck and from the air, and it stays that way: a place you observe in passing rather than arrive at, 236 acres of wooded rock in the middle of a sea lane.

## Flight Context

Crane Island lies at 48.5971 N, 122.9983 W in the heart of the San Juan archipelago, San Juan County, Washington, tucked between the southwestern shore of Orcas Island and the northwest corner of Shaw Island. From the air it is identifiable as the largest of the Wasp Islands - a wooded island roughly 236 acres in size, with a ring of shoreline houses, a small dock on its eastern side at Pole Pass, and a mown 1,500-foot gravel and grass strip cut through the trees. Wasp Passage runs along its southern side, carrying the Washington State ferry between Orcas and San Juan Island. The airstrip is PRIVATE, restricted to island residents, and is not a landing option for visitors: prevailing winds are typically crosswind to the runway and drop off abruptly on short final as the aircraft descends below the treeline, and a 2022 accident here resulted in the operator recommending against using Cessna 207s on the field. Orcas Island (KORS) is about 4 nm northeast and Friday Harbor (KFHR) about 5 nm southwest; Bellingham (KBLI) lies roughly 25 nm east. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 feet MSL shows Crane, the Wasp group and the surrounding channels clearly. Summer morning fog is common throughout the archipelago.
