# The Whale Museum

A toll-free phone line in Friday Harbor has been collecting whale sightings from the public since 1976 — three years before the museum that runs it even existed.

**Location:** 48.5357, -123.0170

**Categories:** Natural history museums in Washington (state), Museums in San Juan County, Washington, Friday Harbor, Washington, Whale conservation, Orca conservation

## Introduction

The database is older than the museum. Since 1976, ordinary people around the Salish Sea — fishermen, ferry passengers, someone on a porch with binoculars — have been calling a toll-free number to report what they just saw, and every one of those calls has gone into a record of where the whales were and when. It is citizen science that predates the phrase by decades. The Whale Museum in Friday Harbor, Washington, opened in 1979 and took the hotline in with it, and almost fifty years of accumulated sightings now sit behind the exhibits. That is the thing worth understanding about this place. It looks like a small-town natural history museum. It functions as a research institution that happens to have a gift shop.

## What Is Inside

The galleries do what good natural history galleries do: they make an animal too large to comprehend at sea comprehensible on land. There are life-sized whale models and articulated skeletons hung so you can walk the length of a spine. There are displays devoted to the endangered Southern Resident killer whales — not as a species in the abstract, but as specific family groups, the pods that people here can name on sight. Other exhibits cover the pinnipeds, porpoises, and other marine mammals of the Salish Sea, alongside wildlife video and material on Northwest Native American culture and the artifacts connected to marine mammals. The museum is a registered non-profit governed by a board of directors, which is to say that the admission you pay is the research budget.

## Listening at Lime Kiln

Since 1983 the museum has maintained a research laboratory at Lime Kiln Point State Park, on the west side of San Juan Island where the water runs deep close to shore and the orcas pass within easy hearing. The instrument that matters there is a hydrophone. As a partner in the Salish Sea Hydrophone Network, known as SeaSound, the museum records not only whale vocalizations but also vessel noise and everything else moving through the water column. That second category is the point as much as the first. The Southern Residents hunt by echolocation, and one of the three principal threats to their survival — alongside a shortage of Chinook salmon and the accumulated chemical contamination in their own tissue — is underwater noise loud enough to drown out the sound they are listening for. You cannot manage a problem you have not measured.

## When a Whale Washes Up

In 1981 the museum started a Marine Mammal Stranding Network, authorized by NOAA to respond when a marine mammal comes ashore in these islands, alive or dead. Live animals go to the Wolf Hollow Wildlife Rehabilitation Center. The dead ones go to researchers at the University of Washington's Friday Harbor Labs, where a necropsy can reveal what the population as a whole is up against — what the animal had eaten, what had accumulated in its blubber, what killed it. The museum was part of the investigation into Sooke, catalogued as L-112, a three-year-old orca that washed up near Long Beach, Washington, in 2012. Work like this is unglamorous and often grim. It is also the only way anyone learns anything from a death.

## Soundwatch

In 1993 the museum launched Soundwatch, a boater education program aimed squarely at the problem of well-meaning people getting too close. Boaters and kayakers who encounter whales are not usually trying to cause harm; they are trying to see something extraordinary, and they frequently have no idea that federal and state law governs how they approach, or that the Be Whale Wise guidelines exist at all. Soundwatch meets them on the water and tells them. Alongside it, the museum runs a Marine Naturalist Training Program twice a year — an in-depth course in the natural history of the Salish Sea — and education programs for children and adults covering orca acoustics, gray whales, cetacean identification, and Puget Sound's seals and sea lions.

## Adopting a Whale

The Orca Adoption Program is the museum's most direct answer to the distance problem. Individual Southern Resident orcas are identifiable by the shape of the dorsal fin and the pattern of the grey saddle patch behind it, and each one has a designation, a known mother, and a documented life history. Adopting one means learning to pick that animal out — its markings, its family line, where it has been seen. The population is small enough that this is genuinely possible: seventy-three animals in the most recent census, listed as endangered in the United States in 2005 and protected under Canada's Species at Risk Act. The gamble the museum is making is that a person who can recognise one whale will care about all of them. In a town of a few thousand people at the edge of their range, that is not a bad theory of change.

## Flight Context

The Whale Museum is at 48.5357 degrees north, 123.0170 degrees west, in the town of Friday Harbor on San Juan Island, Washington. Best viewed from 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL — Friday Harbor is easy to identify from the air as the only sizeable settlement on the island, wrapped around a sheltered east-facing harbour crowded with moored boats and served by the Washington State Ferries terminal. Brooks Point and Turn Island frame the harbour entrance; San Juan Channel runs north-south just offshore. On the island's west side, about 12 km away, Lime Kiln Point and its lighthouse mark the museum's hydrophone research station on Haro Strait, where the orcas pass close to shore. Nearest airports: Friday Harbor Airport (KFHR) is immediately west of town, Bellingham International (KBLI) lies about 40 km northeast on the mainland, and Victoria International (CYYJ) sits across Haro Strait in Canada. NOTE: the international border runs down Haro Strait just west of the island — check current airspace and border requirements. Summer marine fog is common in the mornings around the San Juans.
