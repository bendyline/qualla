# Fort Rodd Hill National Historic Site

Three Victorian gun batteries guarding the mouth of Esquimalt Harbour, built around guns that dropped out of sight after every shot - and that never once fired at an enemy.

**Location:** 48.4323, -123.4504

**Categories:** Historic Sites, Military History, British Columbia, Fortifications, Lighthouses, Parks Canada

## Introduction

The gun weighs five tons. It throws a hundred-pound shell roughly six miles. And the moment it fires, the recoil drives it down out of sight behind a concrete parapet, where the crew reloads it in cover before cranking it back up to fire again. The disappearing gun was the Victorian answer to an obvious problem - a gun you can see is a gun an enemy can hit - and one of the three originals installed at Fort Rodd Hill still sits in firing position on the Upper Battery, aimed out over the entrance to Esquimalt Harbour at a fleet that never arrived.

## A Harbour Worth the Trouble

Britain's Royal Navy had been using Esquimalt since the 1840s, at first only for anchorage, water, and timber. The turn came during the Crimean War, when three hospital huts went up here between 1854 and 1856 - the modest beginning of a base that the Royal Canadian Navy still operates. In 1862 the Pacific Squadron moved its station from Valparaíso, where it had made do with floating storeships, to Esquimalt, and once there were storehouses and workshops ashore there was something worth attacking. Two other pressures sharpened the point: the Fraser River gold rush of 1858, which flooded the colony with American miners, and the 1859 Pig War standoff on San Juan Island, whose underlying boundary dispute stayed unresolved until 1871. Guns first went onto Rodd Hill in 1864. The hill itself was named for John Rashleigh Rodd, a young lieutenant serving under Captain John A. Duntze who ended his career as a full admiral before his death in 1892.

## Concrete Sunk into the Hill

The fort that survives today is a product of the 1890s, built by the Royal Marine Artillery as part of a wider scheme to shield Victoria and the naval base. The Upper Battery's sunken concrete emplacements were poured between 1895 and 1897 for three six-inch disappearing guns. The Lower Battery followed on the same pattern, with two six-inch Mark 6 guns on Mark 4 disappearing carriages. Everything about the design assumes an enemy warship steaming toward the harbour mouth: thick concrete cut into the hillside rather than raised above it, magazines tunnelled beneath the guns, shells and charges moving up by hand through narrow passages. Standing inside those passages, the fort feels less like a castle than like a machine with rooms.

## Belmont's Quick-Firers

The big guns could not solve every problem. A six-inch gun is slow, and the naval threat of the era included fast torpedo boats that could slip into a harbour before a heavy gun could be laid on them. The answer went up between 1898 and 1900 as Belmont Battery: two single-barrelled twelve-pounder quick-firing guns mounted on top of the wall, capable of about fifteen rounds a minute against small, fast, dodging targets. Belmont came with a lookout tower and a searchlight generating plant, because the boats it was built to stop would come at night. The fort, in other words, was built in layers, each answering a different way the sea might be used against it.

## The Light Next Door

Directly beside the fort, on a rock joined to shore by a causeway, stands Fisgard Lighthouse - the first lighthouse on Canada's west coast, lit in 1860 to guide ships into Esquimalt. The pairing is an odd one and a telling one: one structure built to welcome vessels in, the other built to stop them. Both are now National Historic Sites, managed and presented together by Parks Canada. Visitors walk from the keeper's quarters and the white tower over to the gun emplacements in a few minutes, crossing from one century's idea of coastal infrastructure into another's.

## Sixty Years of Waiting

The Royal Canadian Artillery held Fort Rodd Hill continuously until 1956, through two world wars and the early Cold War, updating guns and equipment as threats changed. What did not change was the fundamental condition of the place: generations of gunners drilled, watched, maintained, and stood their shifts against an attack that never came. That is the ordinary experience of coastal defence, and it is not nothing - deterrence works invisibly, and the men who served here had no way of knowing their war would stay offshore. When the guns were finally declared obsolete, the fort passed into public hands rather than being cleared, which is why the concrete, the magazines, the searchlight positions, and one enormous disappearing gun are all still here to be walked through.

## Flight Context

Fort Rodd Hill sits at 48.4323°N, 123.4504°W, on the west side of the entrance to Esquimalt Harbour in Colwood, just west of Victoria. The visual key from the air is Fisgard Lighthouse - a white tower on a small rock connected to the point by a causeway, marking the harbour mouth; the fort's batteries occupy the wooded rise immediately behind it. Esquimalt Harbour opens north with the naval dockyard and its grey hulls clearly visible; the Strait of Juan de Fuca stretches south toward the Olympic Mountains, and Victoria's Inner Harbour lies a short hop east. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500-2,500 ft MSL to distinguish the Upper, Lower, and Belmont battery emplacements and the lighthouse causeway, or 5,000-6,000 ft to frame the whole of Esquimalt and Victoria against the strait. Victoria International (CYYJ) is roughly 9 nm north-northeast; Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is about 4 nm east. Note the active naval base airspace and heavy floatplane and helicopter traffic in the area. Mornings can bring marine fog off the strait in late summer and autumn; winter fronts arrive from the southeast with low cloud and rain.
