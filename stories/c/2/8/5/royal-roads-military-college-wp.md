# Royal Roads Military College

Canada bought a coal baron's castle for roughly the price of its fence, meant to hide the British royal family in it, and instead spent fifty-five years turning young Canadians into officers on its lawns.

**Location:** 48.4344, -123.4728

## Introduction

Seventy-five thousand dollars bought the Dominion Government a castle. In 1940 Ottawa acquired Hatley Park, almost in its entirety - Samuel Maclure's stone mansion completed in 1908 for the coal magnate, premier and lieutenant governor James Dunsmuir, along with most of the 650-acre estate around it. By one contemporary reckoning the purchase price was roughly what the fence enclosing the property was worth. The buyer had a specific plan. If Britain fell, the royal family would need somewhere in the Commonwealth to go, and a defensible waterfront estate on Vancouver Island looked like an answer. The answer came back from London in the voice of the Queen: the children would not go without her, she would not go without the King, and the King would never go. Canada was left holding a castle.

## HMCS Royal Roads

What the navy did with it was more useful anyway. In December 1940 the estate opened as an officer training establishment, commissioned as HMCS Royal Roads and named for the anchorage lying offshore in Juan de Fuca Strait. Its job was to take civilians and turn them into short-term probationary sub-lieutenants of the Royal Canadian Navy Volunteer Reserve at the speed the war demanded. Roughly six hundred volunteer reserve officers passed through during those years, and many of them went straight into the Battle of the Atlantic - convoy escort work in the North Atlantic winter, which was among the longest and coldest fights of the war. In 1942 wartime expansion produced the Royal Canadian Naval College on the same grounds. The Dunsmuir ballroom had become a classroom for men who would be at sea within months.

## The Institution That Kept Changing Its Name

Peace brought a series of re-badgings that trace the shape of Canada's postwar military. In 1947 the site became the RCN-RCAF Joint Services College, training naval and air force personnel together. The following year it broadened again into Canadian Services College, Royal Roads, running a two-year programme for navy, air force and army alike. In 1968 it settled on Royal Roads Military College, and in 1975 it finally began granting its own degrees. The curriculum was unforgiving by design. Cadets had to meet the standard in four separate components - academic, second-language, physical fitness, and military - and failing any one of them meant leaving without the degree, no matter how the other three had gone. A great many otherwise capable people discovered which of the four was theirs.

## Squadrons, Flights and a Pipe Band

The undergraduate body was the Cadet Wing, divided into four squadrons run day to day by senior cadets. The squadrons had no names, only embroidered patches bearing mythological figures, worn on the sleeve of the cadet workdress. Below them came flights, and the flights were named for explorers. Squadrons competed against each other at drill and intramurals with the ferocity that small closed communities reserve for internal rivalries. The music came early: a drum and bugle corps existed by 1955, a Brass and Reed Band by 1975, and the Pipes and Drums turned out for parades, mess dinners, sporting events, recruiting trips, weddings, funerals, Christmas and the Graduation Ball. Between the squadron patches and the pipes, the college manufactured tradition at a rate that its actual age never quite justified - and it worked.

## The Last Parade

In February 1994, with the Cold War over and defence budgets being cut hard, the Department of National Defence announced that Royal Roads would close. The final class graduated in May 1995. That same year Hatley Park and the former college were declared a National Historic Site of Canada, commemorating both the Dunsmuir era and the college's fifty-five years; the plaque placed in 2000 cites the grounds as a nearly intact Edwardian park. The site did not go quiet. On 21 June 1995 the province passed the Royal Roads University Act, and the campus is now leased from the federal government for a dollar on a fifty-year agreement, with the university holding about 55 hectares and National Defence retaining 175. Hatley Castle houses the Royal Roads Military College Museum - and, when film crews arrive, doubles as Professor Xavier's school and the Luthor mansion.

## Flight Context

The former Royal Roads Military College sits at 48.4344 N, 123.4728 W in Colwood, on the west side of Esquimalt Harbour just outside Victoria. From 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL the estate is easy to pick out: Hatley Castle's grey stone mass and formal gardens set in mature parkland, with a clean edge where the grounds meet the waterfront and Royal Roads - the open anchorage in Juan de Fuca Strait that gave the college its name - lying immediately south. CFB Esquimalt and its dockyard are just east across the harbour, and the Olympic Mountains fill the horizon across the strait. Victoria Inner Harbour Water Airport (CYWH) is roughly 3 to 4 nm east and Victoria International (CYYJ) about 13 nm north-northeast. This is busy, tightly controlled airspace with military activity around Esquimalt; check NOTAMs and restrictions. Summer visibility is usually excellent, while autumn and winter bring low stratus and morning fog off the strait.
