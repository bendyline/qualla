# Sooke Potholes Regional Park

Meltwater and trapped boulders spent thousands of years drilling round swimming holes into the bedrock of the Sooke River — and a failed 1980s resort left its concrete skeleton in the trees above them.

**Location:** 48.4280, -123.7120

**Categories:** Parks in British Columbia, Capital Regional District, Swimming holes, Geological formations

## Introduction

The swimming holes were drilled by rocks. When the glaciers that buried southern Vancouver Island began retreating roughly fifteen thousand years ago, they dropped their load of boulders into the Sooke River valley, and the meltwater did the rest — spinning those stones in eddies, hour after hour, century after century, grinding cylindrical basins down into the sandstone of the valley floor. The result is a stretch of river that looks engineered: smooth round pools, some of them deep enough to jump into, linked by chutes and narrow canyon walls. On a hot July afternoon the potholes are full of teenagers doing exactly what the geology invites. The process that made them is still running, just far too slowly to watch.

## Sixty-Three and a Half Hectares

Sooke Potholes Regional Park covers 63.5 hectares — about 157 acres — strung along the Sooke River north of the town of Sooke, on the southwest coast of Vancouver Island. It is not a large park, and its shape follows the river rather than any tidy boundary, which is the point: what is being protected here is a corridor, not a block. At the southern end, the smaller Sooke Potholes Provincial Park runs up against the regional park's boundary, so that two levels of government protect one continuous run of riverbank. From the park you can reach the westernmost end of the Galloping Goose Regional Trail, the rail-to-trail route that runs all the way back into Victoria. The canyon walls throw shade over the water for much of the day, which keeps the pools cold even in August.

## The Flowline

Cut into the hills east of the river is one of the more unusual pieces of engineering on Vancouver Island. The Sooke Flowline, built in 1915, was a gravity-fed water conduit that followed the contours of these hillsides for kilometre after kilometre before reaching the Humpback Reservoir near what is now Mount Wells Regional Park. Nothing about it is dramatic. That is what makes it impressive: a line of flowing water that crosses a mountainous landscape purely by following the shape of the ground, laid out by surveyors with instruments and patience more than a century ago. It can still be found in the hills east of the Sooke River, threading through the second-growth timber.

## Deertrail

Up in the trees above the potholes stands a concrete ruin, and the story behind it is one of the most-told in the park. Much of this land was once part of the Deertrail Resort — a development proposed in the 1980s that was to become a substantial destination in the Sooke hills. It never opened. Construction got as far as raising at least one structure, and then the money, the permits, or the ambition ran out, and the forest began taking the site back. The remains of that building are still there, roofless and grown over, and they have acquired the particular gravity that unfinished buildings always do. A ruin is usually evidence of something that ended. This one is evidence of something that never quite started.

## Spring Salmon Place

This valley lies within the traditional lands of the T'Sou-ke Nation, who were here for a very long time before the river became a swimming destination. Following European colonization in the nineteenth century, the land around the potholes was worked hard — farmed, fished, mined, and logged — and only later turned over to recreation. The park exists because of two purchases: The Land Conservancy of British Columbia and the Capital Regional District bought the property from private owners in 2005 and 2007, and the two organizations now plan and manage it together. At the northern end of the park sits the 67-site Spring Salmon Place Campground, known by its T'Sou-ke name, KWL-UCHUN, and operated seasonally by the T'Sou-ke Nation. It is a small but meaningful arrangement: the Nation running the visitor facilities on a river that has been theirs far longer than anyone has been camping beside it.

## Flight Context

Sooke Potholes Regional Park lies at 48.4280 degrees north, 123.7120 degrees west, along the Sooke River about 4 km north of the town of Sooke on southern Vancouver Island. Best viewed from 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL on a north-south track following the river valley inland from Sooke Basin — from the air the park reads as a narrow green corridor with the pale, exposed bedrock of the river channel visible through the canopy where the canyon opens up. Sooke Basin and the Strait of Juan de Fuca lie to the south; the Sooke Hills and the ridgeline carrying the old Flowline rise to the east. Nearest airports: Victoria International (CYYJ) about 40 km east, Victoria Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH), and William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) at Port Angeles, Washington, across the strait. This valley traps morning fog and low cloud well after the coastline has cleared, particularly in autumn; summer afternoons are usually the most reliable window, with good visibility inland toward the Sooke Hills.
