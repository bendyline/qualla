# Puget Sound Navigation Company

The Black Ball Line ruled cross-sound traffic for half a century, then shut itself down to win a fare dispute - and accidentally handed Washington its state ferry system.

**Location:** 48.4025, -123.3998

**Categories:** Puget Sound Navigation Company, Ferries of British Columbia, Transport companies established in 1898, Defunct shipping companies of Canada, 1898 establishments in Washington (state)

## Introduction

In 1951 a private ferry company stopped its boats in order to make a point. Costs had risen after the war, the state utilities commission would not allow higher fares, the courts had dragged on, and when the unionized crews finally walked off, the Puget Sound Navigation Company did not hire replacements. It simply tied everything up and waited for the public to demand that Olympia relent. The public demanded something else. Washington bought the company's domestic fleet and routes outright for $4.9 million, and on 1 June 1951 the state began running the boats itself. The gamble had produced the exact opposite of its intent: a permanent public ferry system.

## A Flag Borrowed From the Atlantic

The name came from a long way off. The original Black Ball Line began scheduled passenger and freight service in 1818, four sailing ships working between New York and Liverpool, and its house flag was famous enough to be worth inheriting. In 1884 Charles Peabody, grandson of one of those founders, came west to Port Townsend, and under a modified Black Ball flag he started the Alaska Steamship Company. Fourteen years later, in 1898, he founded the Puget Sound Navigation Company. Locals almost never used the formal name. To everyone on the water it was simply the Black Ball Line, and by the early 1900s it was running something like sixteen vessels.

## Owning the Crossings

Before the bridges, the water was the highway. Puget Sound is a shattered coastline of peninsulas and islands, and getting from one side of it to the other meant boarding somebody's boat. The Black Ball Line made sure it was theirs. By the 1930s the company held what has fairly been called a virtual monopoly on cross-sound traffic, and it pushed north as well, competing with the Canadian Pacific Railway's steamships on routes in the Strait of Georgia. Steamboats gave way to car ferries as the automobile arrived, and the company adapted, because the fundamental business never changed. People kept needing to be on the other shore.

## The Standoff

What broke the arrangement was arithmetic. Operating costs climbed steadily after the Second World War, and the company petitioned the State Utilities Commission to raise fares. It was rebuffed. A long run of court battles followed, and then the crews struck. The company's response was unusual - rather than bring in strike breakers, it halted operations altogether, betting that stranded commuters would force the state's hand. The state declined to intervene. In early 1951 the Puget Sound Navigation Company sold its domestic assets to Washington's Department of Transportation, and Washington State Ferries was born from the wreck of a bluff that had failed.

## North of the Line

The company kept its Canadian business and carried on as Black Ball Ferries, Ltd., a much smaller operation of five ships. It ran between Vancouver and Nanaimo and across Howe Sound and Jervis Inlet, and on 11 August 1951 it opened its first all-Canadian route, from Horseshoe Bay in West Vancouver to Gibsons Landing on the Sunshine Coast - the same crossing that still carries most of that coast's traffic today. The reprieve lasted a decade. BC Ferries had started up in June 1960 as a division of the British Columbia Toll Highways and Bridges Authority, and in November 1961 Black Ball Ferries sold it most of what remained. On both sides of the border, the same story had played out: private boats, public system.

## One Ship Still Running

The line is not quite gone. Black Ball Ferry Line, the direct descendant of the company Peabody founded in 1898, still operates a single route - Port Angeles to Victoria, across the Strait of Juan de Fuca, an international crossing with customs at both ends. The ship is the MV Coho, built in 1959, older than most of the vehicles she carries. She was launched while Black Ball Ferries still ran boats on the Canadian coast and while Washington State Ferries was less than a decade old, and she has outlasted the circumstances of both. Every sailing is a working remnant of the mosquito fleet, still charging fares, still crossing the same water.

## Flight Context

The Coho's route runs across the Strait of Juan de Fuca near 48.4025 N, 123.3998 W, between Victoria and Port Angeles. Fly it at 2,000-4,000 ft and the whole operation is legible: Victoria's Inner Harbour and the Ogden Point breakwater on the Canadian shore, the long curve of Ediz Hook sheltering Port Angeles on the American one, with the Olympic Mountains rising behind. The international border runs down the middle of the strait, so a crossing here means customs planning on both sides. Victoria International (CYYJ) and Victoria Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) serve the north shore; William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) sits at the base of Ediz Hook to the south. This strait generates persistent marine fog and low stratus, particularly on summer mornings, and afternoon westerlies can funnel through hard - the same conditions that made these crossings a business worth monopolizing.
