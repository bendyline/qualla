# Esquimalt Royal Navy Dockyard

Britain's only naval base in western North America began as three wooden huts built to receive casualties from a Crimean War battle fought on the Kamchatka Peninsula, and is still a working dockyard after 180 years.

**Location:** 48.4311, -123.4317

**Categories:** naval-history, british-columbia, victoria, military-installations, national-historic-sites

## Introduction

In February 1855 the governor of the Colony of Vancouver Island, James Douglas, received a letter asking him for a hospital. It came from Rear Admiral Sir Henry William Bruce, commanding the Royal Navy's Pacific Station, and it was prompted by a war on the other side of the planet. The Crimean War had a forgotten North Pacific theatre, and the previous August an Anglo-French squadron had thrown itself against the Russian port of Petropavlovsk on the Kamchatka Peninsula and been badly handled. Bruce needed somewhere in the eastern Pacific to put the wounded. Douglas put up three wooden buildings on seven acres at Duntze Head, at the mouth of Esquimalt Harbour. They were never meant to be permanent. They became the first shore establishment the Royal Navy ever built at Esquimalt, and the yard that grew from them is still operating today.

## A Harbour Chosen by Sounding Line

The harbour had been on British charts since about 1842, when a hydrographic survey concluded that Esquimalt's depth and shape would serve as a naval port on the west coast of North America - an assessment made with a lead line and a notebook, decades before anyone knew what it would be used for. The judgement was sound. Esquimalt is a deep, sheltered, awkwardly entered inlet immediately west of Victoria, easy to defend and hard to surprise. What it lacked was a reason to exist. The Fraser gold rush and the wars of the 1850s supplied one, and in 1859 the colony began lighting the approaches: Fisgard Light first shone on 16 November 1860, the first permanent lighthouse on Canada's west coast, and Race Rocks followed on 26 December. The lights served the navy and the gold-rush traffic equally, and they served one more purpose besides. A British lighthouse at the harbour mouth was a statement, in brick and glass, that this coast was not going to be annexed.

## Valparaiso Moves North

For most of the nineteenth century the Royal Navy's Pacific Station was headquartered at Valparaiso in Chile - logical enough when British commerce in the Pacific ran on Chilean copper and Peruvian guano. As British economic interest shifted northward, so did the fleet. In 1865 Esquimalt was formally recognised as an alternate base for the station, and the emphasis kept drifting up the coast until the Vancouver Island yard was the centre of gravity rather than the outpost. It was the only Royal Navy base in western North America, which is a way of saying that from Cape Horn to the Bering Sea, British naval power in the Pacific ran through one small harbour on one island. Then it ended, and it ended precisely. The Royal Navy vacated Esquimalt at sunset on 1 March 1905, leaving the yard to the Canadian government.

## Two Docks, a Century Apart

The dockyard's heart has always been its ability to lift a ship out of the water. An 1871 agreement between Canada, Britain, and the brand-new province of British Columbia committed to building the first graving dock here; construction began in 1876 and the dock was commissioned in 1887, and it remains the oldest operating dry dock on the west coast of North America, still in regular use servicing Her Majesty's Canadian ships as part of the Fleet Maintenance Facility. It was built for the largest vessels in the British Pacific fleet of its day, and ships kept getting bigger. So the dominion government built another one about five hundred metres away, working from 1921 to 1926 and completing it in 1927. That dock could take vessels too large for the Panama Canal, and at the time it opened it was the second largest dry dock in the world. It remains the largest solid-bottom commercial dry dock on the west coast of the Americas.

## Troopship, Cruise Ship, National Historic Site

In February 1942 the RMS Queen Elizabeth came into the Esquimalt Graving Dock for two weeks and left with three thousand extra berths welded into her - a liner converted to carry soldiers, at a moment when the Allies needed hulls more than they needed comfort. The same dock now has stabiliser pockets cast into its concrete walls so that cruise ships can extend their fins for inspection without leaving the basin. The military side consolidated in the 1960s, when the unification of Canada's armed forces reorganised the yard as Canadian Forces Base Esquimalt, home of the Royal Canadian Navy's Pacific Fleet. In 1995 the dockyard and three neighbouring places - the old Royal Navy Hospital, the Veterans' Cemetery, and the Cole Island Magazine - were designated together as the Esquimalt Naval Sites National Historic Site of Canada. The cemetery is the quietest of the four, and the most direct answer to what a naval base actually costs.

## Flight Context

Esquimalt Dockyard sits at 48.431 degrees north, 123.432 degrees west, on the east side of Esquimalt Harbour immediately west of downtown Victoria, British Columbia. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 3,500 feet frames the harbour, the graving docks, the naval jetties, and Victoria's Inner Harbour a few kilometres east. Navigate by the shape of the coast: Esquimalt Harbour is a deep, narrow, north-trending inlet with Duntze Head and the white tower of Fisgard Lighthouse marking its entrance, and Fort Rodd Hill's gun batteries on the western shore. Grey hulls alongside the jetties are the giveaway. Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 20 kilometres north, Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is 4 kilometres east and busy with floatplanes, and William R. Fairchild International at Port Angeles (KCLM) lies 30 kilometres south across the Strait of Juan de Fuca. Note that the harbour and dockyard lie under restricted and controlled airspace associated with CFB Esquimalt and Victoria's terminal area; check current charts before operating low nearby.
