# Fisgard Lighthouse National Historic Site

The first lighthouse on Canada's west coast, lit in 1860, kept by twelve people including a widow who took over the job the week her husband died on station.

**Location:** 48.4304, -123.4475

**Categories:** Lighthouses completed in 1860, Lighthouses in British Columbia, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Maritime museums in British Columbia, Classified Federal Heritage Building, Buildings and structures in Victoria, British Columbia

## Introduction

William Bevis kept the Fisgard light for eighteen years and died on the island in 1879. His widow Amelia took over the job. She held it for a year, which is the entire official record of her: a name and two dates on a list of keepers, a woman running a lighthouse at the mouth of Esquimalt Harbour in the year her husband died. The Fisgard light had been burning for nineteen years by then, the first on the west coast of Canada, thrown up in a hurry by a colonial government that had suddenly realized how much shipping was arriving and how many rocks were waiting for it. It is still burning. Everything else about the place has changed at least once, including the building itself, which burned down to its floor tiles in 1957.

## Ordered From England, Assembled on a Rock

The Colony of Vancouver Island built Fisgard between 1859 and 1860, and the project was an exercise in long-distance logistics. Architects John Wright and Hermann Otto Tiedemann drew the tower and the adjoining keeper's house, a picturesque gothic in red brick that looks transplanted from an English village and, in a sense, was. Joseph Despard Pemberton, the colonial surveyor and engineer, won the construction contract. Excavation on Fisgard Island began in the autumn of 1859. The lens, the lamp apparatus, and the lantern room came out from England in the care of the first keeper, George Davies, who travelled with the equipment he would spend the next year operating. The cast-iron spiral staircase was cast in sections in San Francisco and shipped north. At sunset on 16 November 1860, the tower showed a light for the first time.

## Twelve Names on a List

From 1860 until automation, twelve people kept this light. The list is short enough to read in a minute and tells a longer story than it means to. George Davies lasted a year. John Watson lasted months. William H. Bevis held the station from 1861 until his death there in 1879, followed by Amelia Bevis. Joseph Dare kept it from 1884 until 1898, when he drowned in Esquimalt Harbour, the water he had spent fourteen years warning other people about. Three keepers cycled through in the remainder of that year alone. Then Douglas MacKenzie, Andrew Deacon, George Johnson, and finally Josiah Gosse, who stayed from 1909 to 1928. The light was automated in 1929, and the profession that cost Bevis and Dare their lives here simply ended.

## Two Seconds of White

The signal itself is a precise piece of information, and mariners read it the way you read a road sign. Fisgard shows a white isophase light on a two-second period, equal intervals of light and dark, from 21.6 metres above mean sea level, across a sector running from 322 degrees around to 195. Outside that arc, red shutters cover the lamp, which tells an approaching vessel it has drifted into water it should not be in. The tower stands 14.6 metres and is floodlit below the balcony at night so it registers as a landmark even when the light itself is not the thing you need. It has run continuously since 1860, with one interruption.

## Gutted, 1957

That interruption was a fire, possibly set by vandals, which tore through the interior and left almost nothing. What survived was the checkered floor and the steel staircase, the two things that would not burn. The lighthouse was dark for a year. The interior was rebuilt, and in a turn that says something about how Canada felt about the place by then, the fire was followed almost immediately by recognition rather than demolition. Fisgard Lighthouse and the keeper's dwellings were declared a National Historic Site of Canada on 3 November 1958, with Parks Canada taking over administration. It is also a Classified Federal Heritage Building, the highest federal heritage designation available.

## Guns on the Next Headland

Fisgard does not stand alone. Just inland on the same shore is Fort Rodd Hill, a coastal artillery fort the Royal Marine Artillery built between 1895 and 1897 to defend the Esquimalt naval base, three gun batteries that stayed in service with the Royal Canadian Artillery until 1956. The two sites are presented together by Parks Canada, and the pairing is honest about what this harbour mouth has always been: a working entrance to a naval base, guarded and lit. The lighthouse sits on a low rocky island reached by a man-made causeway, so visitors walk out over the water to get to it. On a southeast gale the spray comes right across.

## Flight Context

Fisgard Lighthouse stands at 48.430N, 123.448W, on Fisgard Island at the mouth of Esquimalt Harbour in Colwood, just west of Victoria on southern Vancouver Island. From the air it is a white tower with a red brick house at the end of a short causeway, marking the harbour entrance; the grassy earthworks and gun batteries of Fort Rodd Hill sit immediately inland on the same point. Esquimalt Harbour behind it is the Canadian Pacific Fleet's base, usually with naval grey hulls alongside. Look south across the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the Olympic Mountains in Washington, about 30 kilometres away and often the clearest reference point on the horizon. Race Rocks light lies about 15 kilometres southwest. Victoria International Airport (CYYJ) is roughly 25 kilometres north, Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) about 8 kilometres east, and Port Angeles (KCLM) sits across the strait in Washington. Best viewed at 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL. Note that Esquimalt has active military airspace and restrictions nearby; check current NOTAMs before planning a low pass.
