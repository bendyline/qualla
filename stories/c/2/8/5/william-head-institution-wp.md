# William Head Institution

A headland southwest of Victoria that held quarantined immigrants for 75 years, hid tens of thousands of Chinese labourers during the First World War, and is now a prison with a public theatre season.

**Location:** 48.3401, -123.5367

**Categories:** Prisons in British Columbia, Quarantine facilities in Canada, 1959 establishments in British Columbia, Federal government buildings in British Columbia, Correctional Service of Canada institutions

## Introduction

By August of 1917 thirty thousand men had passed through this headland. The quarantine station had been built to handle about a thousand at a time, so at any given moment most of those present lived in tents on the grass above the water, held behind a fence they could not cross, in a country almost none of them had chosen to visit. They were members of the Chinese Labour Corps, and they were here because Britain had decided the safest route to the Western Front ran across Canada instead of through the Suez Canal. Today the same ground holds a minimum-security federal prison whose inmates stage plays that the public buys tickets to see. Few pieces of shoreline anywhere have been asked to do so many different things.

## The Men Who Were Not Supposed to Exist

In total, about 84,000 Chinese labourers passed through Canada as part of the Chinese Labour Corps -- most of them through William Head -- out of roughly 140,000 who served the British and French war effort overall, recruited largely from Shandong province to dig trenches, unload ships, repair roads, and handle the enormous physical work behind the front lines — releasing Allied soldiers to fight and die further forward. The first group reached William Head on 18 April 1917: 2,056 men aboard the Empress of Russia. Secrecy was absolute. From here they were loaded onto sealed trains and moved across Canada without being allowed off, so that Germany would not learn what was happening. Most spent about ten days inside the fenced portion of the station before their train came. Twenty-one of them never left. They died en route to or from a war that was not theirs, and they are buried on this headland, the far edge of a journey that began on the other side of the Pacific.

## Seventy-Five Years at the Door

The station opened in 1883 to replace the older facility at Albert Head, and it was named for Sir William Edward Parry, the Arctic explorer. Its job was simple and unsentimental: every ship arriving on Canada's west coast could be stopped here, and anyone aboard suspected of carrying smallpox, cholera, typhus, or plague could be held until the incubation period ran out. For people at the end of a long ocean crossing, the sight of the headland meant weeks more waiting, disinfection, separation from family, and the possibility of dying within sight of the country they had travelled to reach. It stayed in operation until 1958 — seventy-five years of being the place where arrival was deferred.

## Duplexes Instead of Cellblocks

In 1959, a year after the last quarantine, the site reopened as a prison. William Head Institution does not look like one. Rather than cellblocks and tiers, the design is residential: five neighbourhoods of clustered duplexes, four duplexes to a neighbourhood, each meant to function as a small community where men cook their own meals, manage their own space, and deal with their own neighbours. The rated capacity is 200; as of March 2024 the count was 130, with 101 staff. The statistic that surprises people is this one — 55 percent of the men there are serving life sentences. Minimum security is about assessed risk of escape and of harm, not about the length of a sentence, and a man twenty-five years into a life term is often the most stable person on the compound.

## Curtain Up, Every Fall

Since 1981 the men at William Head have run a theatre company. It began when Simon Fraser University and the University of Victoria offered credit drama courses inside; when policy changes cut the program in 1993, the participants refused to let it die, and with help from staff and volunteers out of Victoria's theatre community they kept producing. William Head on Stage is Canada's longest-running prison theatre company and the only one in the country where members of the public can buy a ticket, clear security, and sit down inside a federal institution to watch incarcerated artists perform work they have often written themselves. Audiences run as high as 2,500 across a fifteen-night fall run. The value is not only artistic — rehearsal demands showing up, taking direction, and carrying a responsibility other people depend on, which is most of what release requires. And for one evening, the traffic on this headland goes the other way: outsiders coming in, voluntarily, to listen.

## Flight Context

William Head sits at 48.340°N, 123.537°W, on a blunt peninsula in Metchosin about 25 km southwest of Victoria at the southern tip of Vancouver Island. Best viewed from 1,500 to 3,000 feet along the Juan de Fuca shoreline. The headland is easy to identify: a wooded point projecting south into the strait, with Pedder Bay cutting in immediately to the east and Parry Bay to the northeast, and the distinctive scatter of Race Rocks and its black-and-white banded lighthouse about two nautical miles southwest. The Olympic Mountains fill the horizon across the strait, often snow-topped into July. Note that this is an active federal correctional institution — observe any published airspace restrictions and do not loiter low overhead. Victoria International (CYYJ) is roughly 15 nautical miles northeast, Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) serves floatplane traffic, and William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) at Port Angeles is directly across on the Washington side. Expect strong westerly flow down the strait, frequent summer fog that can arrive fast off the open Pacific, and excellent visibility on clear winter days after a frontal passage.
