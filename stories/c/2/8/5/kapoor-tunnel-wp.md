# Kapoor Tunnel

Greater Victoria's drinking water travels 8.8 kilometres through a tunnel that a boring machine could not cut, finished by hand by crews who met in the middle only six inches off line.

**Location:** 48.5095, -123.6913

**Categories:** Tunnels in British Columbia, Water infrastructure, Engineering history, Greater Victoria

## Introduction

Two crews started digging toward each other from opposite ends of a mountain on southern Vancouver Island, 8.8 kilometres apart, with no laser guidance, no satellite positioning, and -- for most of the job -- no working machinery. They had survey instruments, compressed air, hand drills, and a narrow-gauge rail line. When they finally broke through and the two headings met, the tunnel was six inches off line. That is a margin of error of roughly one part in fifty-eight thousand, achieved underground, in crumbling shale, by men who could not see more than a few metres in any direction. Nearly every glass of tap water in Victoria still passes through what they cut.

## The Machine That Couldn't

The plan had been easier than the execution. A consultant on the earlier Sooke Flowline, Arthur Adams, had proposed a tunnel through this ridge decades before anyone built one, but the technology of his era was not equal to it. By the 1960s it looked as though it finally was. A miniature tunnel boring machine was built in Vancouver for $258,000 -- serious money then -- and set to work chewing through the rock. The rock declined to cooperate. The shale here is soft, crumbly, and unstable, and instead of cutting cleanly it packed into the machine's teeth and gears until the motors burned out. The contractor eventually quit the job altogether. The Greater Victoria Water District picked up the work itself and finished the tunnel the old way, by hand, completing the excavation in 1967.

## Three Men at the Face

Everything about the bore constrained the work. The cross-section was small enough that only one cart could move in the tunnel at a time, which meant that hauling muck out and bringing timber, rail, and cement in had to be sequenced like a single-track railway. Only three men could stand at the rock face, so progress ran to about seven metres a day. Fresh air had to be pumped from the surface down the full length of the heading, and the same narrow gauge that limited the muck cars limited the concrete, holding the lining crews to roughly twenty-four metres a day. Given the conditions, the safety record was remarkable: the project was nearly incident-free. The one serious injury happened when a worker drilling into an existing hole struck an undetonated stick of dynamite and lost the use of an eye.

## What Flows Through It

The tunnel runs from a head tank near Sooke Lake, which keeps the water at constant pressure, to the Japan Gulch treatment plant near Goldstream Provincial Park. It replaced the Sooke Flowline, an ageing surface conduit that leaked and lay exposed to everything a coastal forest could drop on it -- falling timber, rockslides, winter storms. The tunnel is immune to all of that, and it is vastly larger: it can carry 580 million litres a day, about ten times what the flowline managed. Final cost came to $5.6 million. Water reaches Victoria, Esquimalt, the Westshore, and the Saanich Peninsula through it, serving every municipality in the region except Sooke and the Highlands. It opened for service in 1970, three years after the last round was fired.

## Every January, Somebody Walks It

A tunnel this important cannot simply be trusted. Once a year, in January, the flow is shut off and the region switches to the district's secondary Goldstream system for a few days. Then workers go in on foot and walk the entire 8.8 kilometres in the dark, headlamps on, looking for cracks, spalling, seepage, and anything else the mountain might have done to the lining since last winter. It is one of the more unusual commutes in British Columbia -- a straight line under a forested ridge, no daylight at either end for most of the walk, the concrete ticking and dripping around you. Nothing above ground marks the route. The people drinking the water have no idea it exists, which is the highest compliment a piece of infrastructure can be paid.

## Flight Context

The Kapoor Tunnel runs beneath the ridge at roughly 48.510N, 123.691W, northwest of Victoria on southern Vancouver Island. There is nothing to see of the tunnel itself -- the visible landmarks are its endpoints and the terrain between them. Sooke Lake Reservoir, a long dark finger of water in the hills, marks the western end; the Goldstream valley and Goldstream Provincial Park, where the Japan Gulch plant sits, mark the eastern. The Malahat highway corridor climbs the ridge just north of the alignment, and Finlayson Arm cuts south from Saanich Inlet to the east. Best viewed at 2,500-4,000 feet, which puts the reservoir, the ridge, and the Goldstream outlet in one frame. Nearest airports: Victoria International (CYYJ) about 18nm east, Victoria Inner Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) about 17nm southeast, and Duncan Airport (CAM3) about 20nm north. The Malahat ridge generates its own cloud and can be socked in while Victoria is clear.
