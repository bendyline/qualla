# Goldstream Provincial Park

A small park twenty minutes from downtown Victoria where thousands of salmon return each November to spawn and die, feeding eagles and some of the largest western red cedars in British Columbia.

**Location:** 48.4667, -123.5500

**Categories:** Provincial parks of British Columbia, Greater Victoria, Nature centres in British Columbia, 1958 establishments in British Columbia, Protected areas established in 1958, Salmon

## Introduction

By the second week of November the park smells of death, and that is the good news. The chum salmon have come back into the Goldstream River from the sea, pushed upstream past the picnic tables, dug their redds in the gravel, spawned, and begun to fail. Their bodies pile in the shallows and along the banks. Bald eagles drop out of the cottonwoods by the dozen. Gulls quarrel over the leftovers. And every one of those carcasses is nitrogen and phosphorus hauled in from the open Pacific, delivered by fish, and absorbed by trees standing on the floodplain — which is how a river feeds a forest, and why some of the largest western red cedars in British Columbia happen to be growing here, twenty minutes from downtown Victoria.

## The Gold Nobody Found

In 1863 a rumour went around Vancouver Island that there was gold in this creek. The rush that followed was short and fierce, as these things tend to be when the rumour is thin. Men drove tunnels into the rock along the valley walls, worked them, and left. What they did not find is why the park exists at all — had the ore been real, this would have become a mine site rather than a stand of old-growth twenty kilometres from a capital city. The workings are still there. Walk the trails on the west side and you pass the dark mouths of abandoned adits set into the hillside, half-screened by sword fern, and the name Goldstream remains attached to a river whose actual wealth was always swimming up it.

## Who Owned the Water

The park's history is mostly a property fight. Lot 6 belonged to James Phair, the earliest settler in what is now Langford, who ran the original Goldstream Hotel — today a pub still arguing with Six Mile over which is the oldest in British Columbia. Phair sold his section to the Esquimalt Water Works Company in 1908. The upstream half had gone out as a Crown grant to the National Electric Tramway and Lighting Company, 319 acres for $319, and the water company bought that too. Their goal was not scenery. Financed by a mortgage held by the Dunsmuir estate of Hatley Castle, the company set out to own the entire Goldstream watershed and with it a monopoly on the water rights, specifically to block the City of Victoria from claiming them. It is a very Victorian story: a private firm cornering the drinking water of a provincial capital.

## Saved by a Renegotiated Contract

The monopoly ended badly for everyone who built it. To retire the Dunsmuir mortgage in 1925, the water company sold a large block of the watershed's timber to the Continental Timber Company for around $150,000. Within the same year Victoria passed the Esquimalt Water Works Company Winding Up Act and expropriated the land and the works outright. What happened next is the reason there are big trees to walk under: the city, now holding both the land and an inherited logging contract, went back to the table and renegotiated the agreement to carve the Goldstream area out of the ground to be cut. A mayor and council in 1925 made a decision about timber that nobody could reverse a century later, which is roughly the only way old-growth survives near a growing city.

## The Swiss Village That Never Was

In 1926 the Goldstream Gardens Company arrived with a proposal to lease the two sections for forty-nine years and turn them into a commercial attraction. The plan listed motor camp accommodations, tea rooms, a miniature golf course, riding stables, gas stations, flower gardens, a fernery, and — for reasons lost to the record — a Swiss Village. Council said no. A confectionary and a tearoom did operate in the park at intervals through the 1930s and 1940s, which was as far as commerce got. In 1946 the Rod and Gun Club was permitted to build a clubhouse, and that building still stands; it is now the Goldstream Nature House, where school groups come to look at displays about the salmon run. In 1958, British Columbia's centennial year, the city handed the whole thing to the province and it became a provincial park.

## Four Square Kilometres, Vertically Arranged

The park covers only 3.79 square kilometres, but it stacks a remarkable amount into that. On the floodplain, Douglas-fir and western red cedar tower over western hemlock, black cottonwood, bigleaf maple, and red alder, which in turn shade western yew in the understorey. Climb the steep ridges on either side and the forest changes entirely — arbutus with its peeling cinnamon bark, western flowering dogwood, lodgepole pine, all of it adapted to thin soil and summer drought. Trails run from the salt marsh at the estuary up to Mount Finlayson, a hard, rocky scramble that rewards you with the whole of the Saanich Peninsula and the Olympics beyond. One warning: the railway trestle on the highway's west side, marked on old maps as the Goldstream or Niagara Trestle, is active private property on the Island Rail Corridor, permanently barricaded since 2021 and closed to the public. It is not part of the trail system, whatever the photographs suggest.

## Flight Context

Goldstream Provincial Park sits at 48.467°N, 123.550°W in Langford, at the head of Finlayson Arm about 16 kilometres northwest of downtown Victoria. Best viewed from 1,500 to 3,000 feet. The navigational key is Finlayson Arm — a narrow, deep, fjord-like inlet running roughly 8 kilometres north off Saanich Inlet, with the Goldstream estuary and its salt marsh at the very head. The Trans-Canada Highway climbs out of the valley immediately west of the park toward the Malahat, a visible notch in the ridgeline. Mount Finlayson rises steeply on the park's east side and is the most prominent rock summit in the immediate area. Victoria International (CYYJ) lies about 12 nautical miles east on the Saanich Peninsula; Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is roughly 10 nautical miles southeast and handles heavy floatplane traffic; Nanaimo (CYCD) is about 45 nautical miles northwest up the island. Expect terrain-induced turbulence around the Malahat in strong westerlies, low cloud hanging in Finlayson Arm on winter mornings, and a distinct visual change in late autumn when the cottonwoods along the river turn yellow against the dark conifers.
