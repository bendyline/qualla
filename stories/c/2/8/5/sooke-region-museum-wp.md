# Sooke Region Museum

The small Vancouver Island museum that won a referendum, adopted an abandoned lighthouse lantern, and drew 40,000 visitors a year to a town of a few thousand.

**Location:** 48.3843, -123.7065

**Categories:** Museums in British Columbia

## Introduction

Museums do not usually win elections. Ask any community whether it would like to pay more property tax and the answer tends to be a long, eloquent silence. In Sooke, on the southern tip of Vancouver Island, the question was put to a vote and 83 percent of residents said yes - raise our taxes, fund the museum. The local governments eventually went further and made it a service, so the money would arrive reliably rather than by periodic act of goodwill. It is a strange and rather moving thing to find in a town's financial records: a community that decided its own history was infrastructure.

## A Light That Never Worked

The most conspicuous object on the museum grounds began its life 45 kilometres off the far northern end of Vancouver Island. Triangle Island is 119 hectares of rock in open Pacific, and between 1909 and 1919 Canada tried to run a lighthouse on it. The attempt failed comprehensively. Fog sat on the island for weeks. The wind arrived at hurricane force with dispiriting regularity. The station was abandoned as unfit for human habitation, and the island reverted to what it does best - hosting British Columbia's largest seabird colony, including the largest population of Cassin's auklets anywhere on Earth. In 2004 the lantern room came south and was installed at the Sooke Region Museum, the most conspicuous thing on the grounds and the only surviving working part of a light that spent a decade shining into cloud.

## From East Sooke to Port Renfrew

The museum's territory is the whole southern tip of the island, a ragged stretch of coast running from East Sooke west to Port Renfrew where the Strait of Juan de Fuca gives way to open ocean. Human and natural heritage both fall within its brief, which in practice means logging, fishing, shipwrecks, settlement and the ecology all get equal standing. Behind the exhibits sits an archive of documents and photographs, the unglamorous engine of any museum that intends to be useful. The institution publishes books as well; the most recent is Bringing Water to Victoria, an account of how a city on an island solved the oldest problem a city has.

## Bake Ovens and Two Small Engines

Some of the museum's best work has happened outdoors and off-site. Staff and volunteers helped track down a historic bake oven out in the bush, and located a second one on their own grounds - the kind of find that rewrites a small piece of a region's story about who was living where, and how they fed themselves. Two small rail engines are currently under restoration, destined for display. Outdoor exhibits fill the space around the main building, and a temporary exhibit gallery keeps the indoor content turning over. None of this is on a grand scale. All of it is the sort of thing that only gets done when somebody local decides it matters.

## Salmon, Art, and a Night Market

For an institution this size, the museum's reach is remarkable: it averages more than 40,000 visitors a year and doubles as the region's official Visitor Information Centre, which means most people passing through Sooke walk through its doors whether they meant to or not. It runs a gift shop, hosts the annual Sooke Summer Night Market, and puts on special tours that can include a traditional salmon barbecue. It also founded the Sooke Fine Arts Show, the largest juried art show on Vancouver Island, and ran it for twenty years before handing it to a separate society - the rare case of a small organisation successfully raising something bigger than itself and then letting it go.

## The Curator Who Stayed

The museum opened in 1977, and its first curator was Elida Peers. She received the Order of British Columbia, the province's highest honour, for that work and for others like it. What makes the detail worth keeping is what happened afterward: she stayed. She continues to volunteer at the museum as its historian, which is to say the person who remembers, who can tell you which photograph in the archive shows which family standing in front of which vanished building. The Sooke Region Historical Society runs the place - incorporated under the provincial Societies Act, registered as a federal charity, and dependent, like every small museum, on people who do not stop.

## Flight Context

The Sooke Region Museum stands at 48.3843°N, 123.7065°W, on the southern shore of Vancouver Island where Sooke Basin opens into the Strait of Juan de Fuca. Approaching from the east along the coast at 1,500-2,500 ft, the landmarks are Sooke Harbour, the long sandspit that nearly closes its entrance, and Sooke Basin behind; the museum grounds sit near the highway at the head of the harbour, with the white lantern room from the Triangle Island light visible as a small bright structure. The Olympic Mountains of Washington fill the southern horizon across the strait, often the only feature visible when marine layer covers the water. Race Rocks and its banded tower lie east along the coast toward Victoria. Victoria International (CYYJ) is the nearest field of size, about 22 km north-northwest of Victoria in North Saanich; William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) at Port Angeles sits across the strait. Expect summer fog pushing in from the open Pacific and strong westerly channelling along the strait.
