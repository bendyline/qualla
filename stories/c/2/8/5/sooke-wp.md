# Sooke

Where Vancouver Island's road system starts to run out, a debt-ridden Scottish captain bought the colony's first private farm, three broom seeds took root, and a gold rush came and went in a single season.

**Location:** 48.3761, -123.7380

**Categories:** District municipalities in British Columbia, Greater Victoria, Juan de Fuca region, Gold rush history, Populated places on the British Columbia Coast

## Introduction

Three seeds sprouted. In October 1850, Captain Walter Colquhoun Grant stopped at the Sandwich Islands, now Hawaii, where the British consul handed him a few seeds of Scotch broom as a gesture of home. Grant planted them in front of his house at Sooke. Only three came up. Those three are the reason that every May, from Victoria to Campbell River, the roadsides of Vancouver Island turn a violent chrome yellow and hay-fever sufferers curse a man who has been dead since 1861. Broom is now the island's most notorious invasive plant, and the whole infestation traces back to a homesick gift and a garden bed in Sooke.

## The Worst Farmer in the Colony

Grant was an aristocrat with debts and no agricultural experience whatsoever, which made him an unpromising candidate for the Hudson's Bay Company's colonization scheme. He bought a hundred acres from the company in 1848 and took up residence at Sooke in 1849, making him the first immigrant settler to purchase land in what would become British Columbia, and the first European on Vancouver Island to set up independently of the company. He chose the site with a sawmill in mind, wanting timber and fast water, and with an eye to putting some distance between himself and James Douglas at Fort Victoria, since he suspected they were not going to get along. He was right about that. He was wrong about nearly everything else, and he left the colony within a few years, having failed at farming and succeeded, unknowingly, at botany.

## T'Sou-ke

None of this was empty country. The T'Sou-ke people had been living around this harbour and river for a very long time before any of it was surveyed, and the town's name is theirs. It comes from a word that refers to the stickleback, a small spiny fish that fills Sooke harbour and the river running into it. Sooke Harbour is unusually protected, closed off from Juan de Fuca Strait by the long curving finger of Whiffin Spit, which makes the inner basin calm when the strait outside is anything but. That combination of shelter, a salmon river, and a tidal flat full of fish is why people settled here in the first place, and it is still the shape of the place. The T'Sou-ke Nation remains in the community today.

## Four Months of Leechtown

On 18 July 1864, members of the Vancouver Island Exploring Expedition found payable gold on a river northeast of Sooke and named it for Peter Leech, one of their own. What followed was the classic compressed madness of a gold rush. By 9 November, less than four months after the discovery, roughly 1,200 miners were working the ground, supplied by an estimated six general stores and three hotels in a town that had not existed in the spring. Leechtown was the largest settlement on Vancouver Island outside Victoria for a moment, and then the gold thinned out and the moment ended. Almost nothing is left. The Galloping Goose Regional Trail, once a railway and now a cycling route running out from Victoria through Sooke, ends at the site.

## Muir's Mill

The man who made something of Grant's land was John Muir, no relation to the naturalist, a Scottish coal miner who had come out under contract to the Hudson's Bay Company and put in his time at Fort Rupert. When his contract expired in late 1851 he moved to Sooke, took over the farm Grant had abandoned, and named it Woodside. There he built the first steam-powered sawmill on Vancouver Island, turning the timber Grant had coveted into a business that actually worked. The Muir family became the backbone of Sooke for generations. The elementary school in the west end of town carries the name John Muir, and the yellow broom that his family found growing in front of the house they bought has since occupied the entire island.

## Where the Pavement Thins

Sooke today is the last real town before Vancouver Island's southwest coast turns wild. Highway 14 carries on west past French Beach, Sandcut, Fishboat Bay, China Beach, and Mystic Beach, delivering hikers to the Juan de Fuca Marine Trail and eventually to the trailhead of the West Coast Trail. In town, the Sooke Potholes are the summer institution: a chain of deep bedrock basins scoured into the Sooke River, cold enough to make a swimmer gasp and popular enough to fill two parks, one regional and one provincial. The Sooke Region Museum keeps the local record. The Sooke Fall Fair has run on Labour Day weekend since 1913. And for all the wilderness at its back door, Sooke is increasingly a commuter town, absorbing people priced out of Victoria half an hour to the east, which is its own kind of gold rush and considerably slower.

## Flight Context

Sooke sits at 48.376N, 123.738W, on the north shore of Juan de Fuca Strait about 35 km west of Victoria on southern Vancouver Island. From the air the defining feature is Whiffin Spit, a long hooked sandbar nearly closing off Sooke Harbour from the strait; inland, the Sooke River valley and the Sooke Hills rise to forested ridges, and Highway 14 threads west along the coast past a string of beaches. The Olympic Mountains stand across the strait to the south. Victoria International Airport (CYYJ) lies roughly 25nm northeast, Victoria Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) about 18nm east, and William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) at Port Angeles about 20nm southeast across the boundary. Best viewed at 2,000 to 4,000 feet following the coastline westward. Fog forms readily in the strait and can cover the harbour while the hills behind stay clear, and westerly winds funnel strongly along this shore on summer afternoons.
