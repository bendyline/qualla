# Esquimalt Lagoon Migratory Bird Sanctuary

A shallow tidal lagoon behind a driftwood-strewn sand spit outside Victoria, protected since 1931, where more than 190 bird species stop over on a shoreline the Esquimalt and Songhees Nations have used for thousands of years.

**Location:** 48.4278, -123.4681

**Categories:** Migratory Bird Sanctuaries of Canada, 1931 establishments in British Columbia, Important Bird Areas of British Columbia, Coastal wetlands

## Introduction

Somebody keeps carving the driftwood. Along the spit at Esquimalt Lagoon, among the bleached logs the winter storms pile up, there are herons and owls and eagles worked out of the grey wood by an artist who has been quietly populating the beach for years. Visitors find them by accident, which is the point. It is an unusual welcome for a federal bird sanctuary — official protection on one hand, an anonymous carver on the other — but it suits a place that has always worked as several things at once: a highway rest stop for migrating birds, a suburban beach a fifteen-minute drive from downtown Victoria, and a shoreline that people have been harvesting from for far longer than either.

## A Road Between Two Waters

The geography is simple and unusually satisfying. A narrow sand spit called the Coburg Peninsula runs across the mouth of a shallow basin, sealing it off from the Strait of Juan de Fuca except for a single tidal channel at one end. A road runs the length of the spit. Drive it and the open strait is on your right, cold and grey and rolling in from the Olympic Peninsula, while on your left the lagoon sits nearly flat, warmed by the sun and drained twice a day into mudflats and eelgrass. The sanctuary takes in about 126 hectares of wetland, salt marsh and beach. At its head stands the Royal Roads property with Hatley Castle behind the treeline, and just around the point are the Fort Rodd Hill and Fisgard Lighthouse national historic sites — a lot of designated history packed into a very small stretch of coast.

## Older Than the Sanctuary By Several Thousand Years

The lagoon and the Coburg Peninsula lie in the traditional territories of the Esquimalt and Songhees Nations, with the Beecher Bay Nation nearby, and the archaeology here records a connection stretching back thousands of years. These were subsistence grounds and places of spiritual significance, used continuously rather than occasionally. Under the Douglas treaties, the Esquimalt and Songhees retain Aboriginal title and the right to hunt on unoccupied land — rights that survive on paper. The Esquimalt Nation has named the practical difficulty directly: what a treaty right to a resource means when the resource itself is being squeezed out by urban development, by pressure on forests and fisheries, by a shoreline that keeps getting busier. It is not an abstract question in a place where a protected lagoon sits inside a growing municipality, and it has not been answered.

## What Comes Through

The Canadian Wildlife Service established the sanctuary in 1931 for a straightforward reason: birds travelling between North and South America needed somewhere to stop, and this was one of the good places. It is one of only seven migratory bird sanctuaries in British Columbia, and more than 190 species have been recorded here. Gulls, ducks and shorebirds use the lagoon year-round. Waterfowl raft in the shallows through the winter; raptors work the margins. Beneath the surface the lagoon is just as busy, with bivalves, sand dollars, sea lettuce and eelgrass beds that feed the whole arrangement. Coho salmon and cutthroat trout move through the tidal channel. The great blue heron — unmistakable in flight, all folded neck and trailing legs — is listed as a species of concern under the Species at Risk Act, and its numbers here peak through spring and summer.

## A Working Compromise

What makes Esquimalt Lagoon interesting is that it never became a wilderness. It stayed a neighbourhood. Dog walkers and beachcombers use the spit constantly, cars park along the seawall, and the sanctuary functions anyway. The intertidal marsh is regarded as notably resilient in the face of climate change, holding its structure while other coastal wetlands on this strait lose ground. That resilience buys time, not immunity. Sea level, storm surge, and the steady conversion of surrounding land all keep applying pressure, and a spit is by definition a temporary landform — sand arranged by waves, subject to rearrangement by the same. For now the herons stand in the shallows at low tide, the carvings appear and weather and are replaced, and a hundred and twenty-six hectares of mud and marsh keep doing the work they have done since long before anyone wrote it down.

## Flight Context

Esquimalt Lagoon lies at 48.43N, 123.47W in Colwood, British Columbia, on the west side of Esquimalt Harbour about 5nm west of downtown Victoria. From the air the shape is unmistakable: a thin pale sand spit — the Coburg Peninsula — arcing across the mouth of a shallow lagoon, with a single tidal channel breaching it at the northeast end. Use Fisgard Lighthouse and the earthworks of Fort Rodd Hill on the point immediately east as your fix, with the Royal Roads estate and Hatley Castle grounds inland behind the lagoon. The Strait of Juan de Fuca opens south toward the Olympic Mountains. Nearest airports: Victoria International (CYYJ) about 12nm north, Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) about 5nm east with heavy floatplane traffic, and Port Angeles (KCLM) 18nm south across the strait. Maintain at least 2,000 feet over the sanctuary to avoid flushing waterfowl. Clearest viewing is on a winter high-pressure morning, when the lagoon is full of rafting ducks and the Olympics stand up sharply across the water.
