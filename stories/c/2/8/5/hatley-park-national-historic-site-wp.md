# Hatley Park National Historic Site

A coal baron's 40-room castle near Victoria that Canada bought in 1940 as a bolt-hole for King George VI, spent half a century as a naval college, and now serves as a university campus and Hollywood's favourite stand-in for Professor Xavier's school.

**Location:** 48.4343, -123.4720

**Categories:** Historic Sites, Castles and Estates, Gardens, Military History, Film Locations, British Columbia

## Introduction

In 1940 the Canadian government bought a castle for a king who never came. With Britain bracing for invasion, contingency plans were drawn up to move King George VI, Queen Elizabeth and the two princesses out of the country if German troops reached the Midlands - and the destination chosen for that grim scenario was Victoria, British Columbia. The federal Crown purchased Hatley Castle in Colwood and prepared it as a royal residence. In the end the King and Queen stayed in London, the princesses went to Windsor, and the forty-room mansion on the edge of Esquimalt Harbour was handed to the navy instead. It has been finding new uses for itself ever since, most of them stranger than the last.

## A Coal Fortune, Set in Stone

James Dunsmuir bought the property in 1906, the year he became British Columbia's Lieutenant Governor. He had inherited the coal business his father Robert built on Vancouver Island and run it for decades, and the house he and his wife Laura commissioned was designed to look like the money had always been there. The architect was Samuel Maclure, one of the most accomplished designers working in western Canada, and what he produced was Scottish baronial in outline and Edwardian Tudor Revival in its detail - forty rooms, deep panelling, and a picturesque silhouette that photographs well from every angle. They called it Hatley Park, borrowing the naming convention of British country estates. The family's later years there were not gentle ones. Their son James Jr. died in 1915 when the Lusitania went down. Dunsmuir himself died in 1920, and descendants held the castle until 1937, when the Depression finally loosened their grip on it.

## Nine Garden Rooms

In 1912 the Dunsmuirs brought in Franklin Brett and George D. Hall, Boston landscape architects trained under Frederick Law Olmsted, and asked them to design not just gardens but the entire estate. The plan they delivered reads like a diagram of Edwardian social life: nine formal garden rooms pressed close to the castle, then recreational grounds, then working farmland, and finally forest closing the whole thing off from the world. It covers 565 acres. Keeping it required roughly a hundred gardeners and groundskeepers in the Dunsmuir era, and about fifty when the Department of National Defence held it - a remarkable commitment for a military institution. Today the number is five full-time gardeners, an arborist, a curator, seven seasonal staff and a manager, and the arithmetic shows. With no federal, provincial or municipal funding for the site, the team concentrates on what can be kept to standard: the Japanese, Rose and Italian gardens are the showcases, and the rest gets what is left.

## The Price of Admission

That funding gap produced one of the odder local controversies of the century. In June 2006, citing the unfunded cost of heritage preservation, the university began charging admission to the main heritage gardens - an area making up less than five per cent of the campus. Adults paid twelve dollars in summer, six in winter. Greater Victoria residents could buy a fifteen-dollar summer pass, and Colwood residents got in free. The numbers never worked. Maintaining the site ran around $550,000 a year; the gate brought in about $40,000. People objected on principle to paying for access to a national historic site, and the revenue barely dented the problem it was meant to solve. In 2019 the fee was dropped. The gardens are free again, and the maintenance bill has not gone anywhere.

## Cadets, Students, Supervillains

The navy adapted the mansion as a training establishment in December 1940, opening as HMCS Royal Roads and becoming the Royal Canadian Naval College in 1942. In 1948 it turned tri-service, training navy, army and air force cadets together under the name Canadian Services College, Royal Roads, and in 1968 it took the name it's best remembered by: Royal Roads Military College, for the stretch of water east of the grounds that forms the entrance to Esquimalt Harbour from the Strait of Juan de Fuca. Generations of cadets marched past Maclure's stonework. The college closed in 1995, the estate was leased to the province, the castle and grounds were designated a National Historic Site of Canada, and that September Royal Roads University opened as a public degree-granting institution, leasing the campus from National Defence for one dollar a year and taking on every stewardship obligation that came with it. Nine recognized heritage buildings and one classified building stand on the grounds. Meanwhile the castle had acquired a second career. Film crews have worked here for more than eighty years, and the building has served as Lex Luthor's mansion in Smallville, the Queen family home in Arrow, Auradon Prep in the Descendants films, and - most persistently - Xavier's School for Gifted Youngsters across the X-Men series and both Deadpool films. A house built to impress the British Empire now mostly plays home to fictional billionaires and mutants. It is very good at it.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.434 N, 123.472 W in Colwood, at the western edge of Greater Victoria on southern Vancouver Island, British Columbia. The estate sits just inland of Royal Roads, the open water forming the approach to Esquimalt Harbour from the Strait of Juan de Fuca, and the castle itself is identifiable from the air as a large stone building with a distinctive tower set in dark mature forest and formal green terraces - a conspicuous block of old timber and lawn in an otherwise suburban landscape. Esquimalt Harbour and the naval dockyard lie immediately east, Victoria and its inner harbour about 10 km east-southeast, and the Olympic Mountains fill the southern horizon across the strait. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500-3,000 ft MSL for the estate and gardens; 5,000 ft and above frames the whole Juan de Fuca approach. Victoria International (CYYJ) is roughly 20 km north, Victoria Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) about 10 km east, and Port Angeles (KCLM) sits 35 km south across the strait in Washington. Note the restricted and controlled airspace associated with CFB Esquimalt and Victoria's harbour operations. Morning stratus off the strait is common; afternoons in summer are usually clear with excellent visibility toward the Olympics.
