# New Westminster Bridge

A single-track swing bridge from 1904 that still pivots open for Fraser River traffic - and has now outlived the road bridge built to replace it.

**Location:** 49.2082, -122.8940

**Categories:** Bridges, Railways, Engineering, History, British Columbia

## Introduction

The toll was twenty-five cents, and the farmers worked out the arithmetic fast. Walk your cattle across the upper deck of the new Fraser River bridge and you paid a quarter a head. Load the same animals into a truck and the whole truck cost a quarter. The complaints were loud and, in the end, short-lived: after March 1910 the passenger and vehicle tolls stopped altogether. What has not stopped is the bridge. Waddell & Hedrick's swing span opened on 23 July 1904, and more than a century later it is still the only railway crossing of the Fraser at New Westminster - single-tracked, slow, and swinging open for ships through a good part of every day.

## Before the Bridge, a Ferry

Getting from New Westminster to the south bank once meant waiting for the K de K or the Surrey, the little ferries that docked at Brownsville - the community that became the South Westminster neighbourhood of Surrey. The 1904 bridge ended that, and it did two jobs at once. Trains ran on the lower deck, road traffic on the upper. Underneath the arrangement was a piece of railway diplomacy: the rail deck tied Great Northern track on the American-facing south side to Canadian Pacific track along the New Westminster shore, stitching two national networks together over one set of trusses. The north end of the bridge widens strangely as it reaches shore, flaring from 19 feet to 136 feet, because a single track had to split and turn there to meet the CPR line running along the waterfront.

## Losing the Top Deck

By the 1930s the upper deck was simply too small for the cars piling onto it. In January 1936 the federal Department of Public Works, guardian of navigable water, set terms: British Columbia could replace the swing span with a vertical-lift span giving ships far more room, or it could tear the road deck off, hand the bridge to Ottawa, and build a new road crossing just downstream. Premier Duff Pattullo chose the second option quickly. The bridge that carries his name opened in November 1937; the old upper deck came down; and in October 1939 the rail bridge passed to the federal government, which owns it still. For decades afterward, trains over a Canadian river were dispatched from a Burlington Northern office in Seattle.

## Boxing Day, and Other Bad Days

On 26 December 1975 a log barge drifted loose, struck the bridge and went through it, tearing out the 116-metre main span. The fix came from an unlikely direction. Engineers adapted a just-finished design by the Kansas City firm Howard, Needles, Tammen & Bergendoff for the Rulo Rail Bridge over the Missouri River in Nebraska - a span only five feet shorter than the one the barge had destroyed - and had trains running again by late April. Fire closed the bridge for most of a month in 1982. In November 1987 a barge hit the swing span; while it sat out of service, a second barge hit it again in December. Repairs ran to about $2 million, and the lawsuit that followed, Canadian National Railway Co. v. Norsk Pacific Steamship Co., became one of the Supreme Court of Canada's landmark decisions on who may sue for economic loss.

## The Bridge That Was Never Replaced

Almost everyone who has dealt with this crossing has wanted a taller one. The lumber company Crown Zellerbach pressed for a lift span from 1936 into the late 1960s, because tides, freshets and the narrow gap through the swing span kept oceangoing ships from reaching its mill upstream. In 1964 the engineer William George Swan, who had designed Pattullo's road bridge, lobbied Ottawa for a wider lift bridge. A year later there was talk of salvaging the lift span from the doomed Second Narrows Bridge and dropping it in here; instead the old Second Narrows went for scrap in 1970. Studies in 2004 and 2010 priced replacements from $110 million to $600 million. Each time, the cheaper answer won: coordinate the railways better and keep the 1904 bridge working. It now handles about 33 trains a day, freight alongside Amtrak's Cascades, Via Rail's Canadian and the Rocky Mountaineer.

## Outliving the Neighbour

Two hundred feet downstream, the story ended differently. The stal̕əw̓asəm Bridge (Riverview), the replacement road crossing, opened fully in January 2026, and on 6 February the Pattullo Bridge closed to traffic for good after 89 years, the last walkers and cyclists crossing ten days later. The road bridge built in 1937 to take pressure off the old swing span is gone; the swing span, seismically upgraded in the early 2020s, is still turning. Stand on the New Westminster quay at almost any hour and you will eventually see it: a green steel truss, more than 120 years old, pivoting slowly out of the way of a ship.

## Flight Context

The bridge crosses the Fraser River at 49.2082 N, 122.894 W, roughly 15 miles upstream of the Strait of Georgia, linking New Westminster on the north bank to Surrey on the south. From 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL it is unmistakable: a long, low, dark truss set at a slight angle to the river, immediately upstream of the wide new stal̕əw̓asəm Bridge deck, with the New Westminster quay, the SkyTrain line and Surrey's Scott Road Station as supporting fixes. If the centre span is sitting parallel to the current rather than across it, the bridge is open for marine traffic. Vancouver International (CYVR) lies west-southwest down the river, Abbotsford International (CYXX) east up the valley, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) and Boundary Bay (CZBB) are the nearest general-aviation fields. The Fraser valley holds fog and river haze in autumn and winter mornings; afternoons in summer give the cleanest look at the span.
