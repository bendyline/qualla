# British Columbia Penitentiary

For 102 years the BC Pen held the West's federal prisoners on a hillside above the Fraser - and when it finally emptied in 1980, 80,000 people queued up to walk through it.

**Location:** 49.2182, -122.8946

**Categories:** Defunct prisons in Canada, Prisons in British Columbia, History of New Westminster, Sapperton, New Westminster, Canadian criminal justice history

## Introduction

For two weeks in May 1980, more than 80,000 people lined up in Sapperton to tour a prison. The British Columbia Penitentiary had just been emptied - the last inmate left on 15 February - and before the wreckers arrived the federal government threw open the gates. Visitors walked cell blocks whose internal walls prisoners had torn apart by hand four years earlier, and stepped into the solitary cells. When the institution had taken its first prisoners 102 years before, it opened without a ribbon, a speech, or any public notice at all. It closed on 10 May 1980 with dignitaries and a formal ceremony.

## A Prison Before the Railway

British Columbia joined Confederation in 1871, and the arithmetic of punishment immediately became awkward. With no transcontinental railway yet, shipping convicted men east to federal institutions was slow and expensive. Ottawa began planning its own western penitentiary in 1874 and chose a hillside above the Fraser River in the Sapperton neighbourhood of New Westminster. The first prisoners arrived in 1878, into the first federal penal institution west of Manitoba - and into a building that barely worked. The basement flooded, the plumbing and heating failed, bars were missing from some windows and loose in the walls of others, and there were no proper medical facilities. Repairs stretched over years, carried out largely by inmate work crews: men rebuilding the place that held them. A wooden fence gave way to thirty-foot rock walls, and those to forty-foot concrete ones, with a guard tower at each corner.

## Twenty-Three and a Half Hours

There was no dining hall. That single omission shaped everything: men collected food from the kitchen and carried it back to eat alone, three times a day, for as long as their sentence lasted. Rise at seven; work, count, lock-up, work again; dinner in the cell; a few hours of evening leisure. Far worse waited upstairs. The segregation unit built in 1963 - officially the Special Correction Unit, known to everyone as the Penthouse - replaced basement cells called the Hole. Three concrete walls, no window, a steel door with a five-inch square opening. A cold-water basin, a radio with two channels and no volume control, a sheet of plywood on a concrete pad four inches off the floor. The lights stayed on around the clock, dimmed to 25 watts at night. Prisoners spent 23 and a half hours a day inside; the remaining half hour was spent walking the corridor alone. One man accumulated 1,421 days there between 1970 and 1974, 754 of them consecutive.

## The Sons of Freedom

The Doukhobors are a pacifist Russian Christian people who settled in British Columbia; the Sons of Freedom sect among them answered perceived government interference with public nudity and arson. Mass arrests produced mass convictions, and the Pen could not absorb them. In 1931, after more than 600 Doukhobors were sentenced to three years, a satellite prison was built on Piers Island simply to hold them. A second wave came in 1950. The compound built alongside the main penitentiary was burned twice in 1951, the second fire largely destroying it; the men were moved inside, where they held a 35-day hunger strike that ended in forced feeding. Arsons in 1961 and 1962 produced Agassiz Mountain Prison, fireproofed down to its concrete and metal furniture. The hunger strike that began there in August 1962 ran more than a year, and one man died of malnutrition. The last Doukhobor prisoner was released in 1970.

## Forty-One Hours in June

In 1975 the place came apart. A February hostage-taking prompted a commission of inquiry, which found that each of the men involved had come straight out of segregation into the general population without supervision. It also found correctional-officer turnover of 61.2 percent the previous year, a backlog in training, buildings unfit for modern use, and serious overcrowding. Then, on the morning of 9 June, prisoners took fifteen staff hostage. The standoff lasted 41 hours. When a tactical squad stormed the room in the small hours of 11 June, Mary Steinhauser, a 32-year-old classification officer, was killed by gunfire from the rescue party. The largest came the following autumn. On 27 September 1976, inmates released for showers seized the prison and, over several days, destroyed most of the cell blocks, pulling down century-old interior walls by hand. Police and soldiers ringed the perimeter. A newly formed Citizens' Advisory Committee joined the negotiations, and a nine-point agreement ended it peacefully on 2 October.

## Boot Hill

Closure was announced on 12 March 1979, and the men were moved to Kent Institution over the following year. Most of the site is now housing and parkland. Four pieces survive: the gatehouse, later a sports bar; the Coal House; the original Centre Block, turned into offices; and the cemetery. Boot Hill opened officially in 1913. Prisoners dug its graves, built its coffins, cut its markers and kept its grass. Roughly fifty men remain buried there, most under small concrete stones bearing nothing but a prison number, some in graves with no marker at all - the early record-keeping was too careless to trust. Among them is Joseph Smith, 24, executed at the penitentiary on 31 January 1913 after a guard was killed during an escape attempt the previous October, the only execution ever carried out on the site. The cemetery sits inside Glenbrook Ravine Park today, maintained by the city, with a plaque. Most people walking the ravine have no idea it is there.

## Flight Context

The former BC Penitentiary site lies at 49.2182°N, 122.8946°W, on the slope above the north bank of the Fraser River in the Sapperton neighbourhood of New Westminster. Fly it at 2,000-3,500 ft AGL. The Fraser itself is the navigation anchor: follow the north arm upstream past Annacis Island and the ground rises sharply on the left bank where the prison stood. The new stal̕əw̓asəm Bridge, fully open since January 2026, crosses to Surrey just downstream, with the decommissioned 1937 Pattullo truss span beside it - closed to traffic on 6 February 2026 and no longer a through route. Royal Columbian Hospital's block is the most conspicuous structure on the hillside, and Glenbrook Ravine Park is the wooded notch cutting down toward the river just east of it; the Expo Line viaduct threads the slope below. Nearby airports: CYVR Vancouver International about 20 km west, CZBB Boundary Bay roughly 20 km southwest, CYPK Pitt Meadows around 18 km northeast, and CYNJ Langley Regional some 25 km southeast. This is busy controlled airspace beneath Vancouver's terminal environment - obtain clearance before manoeuvring. Autumn and winter bring low stratus and Fraser Valley fog; the sharpest views come on cold, dry outflow days.
