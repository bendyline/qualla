# Queensborough, New Westminster

The name a queen rejected ended up on a flood-prone tip of Lulu Island, where mill workers from four continents built the most polyglot corner of British Columbia's oldest city.

**Location:** 49.1833, -122.9330

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in New Westminster, Populated places on the Fraser River, Immigration history, Flood management, British Columbia history

## Introduction

On February 14, 1859, the capital of the new Colony of British Columbia was proclaimed under the name Queensborough. Colonel Richard Clement Moody of the Royal Engineers had chosen it. Queen Victoria, consulted as a courtesy, preferred New Westminster - and a queen's preference is not a suggestion. The rejected name did not vanish. It drifted downriver and came to rest on the eastern tip of Lulu Island, a low table of silt that the Royal Engineers had set aside as a military reserve and that nobody else particularly wanted. More than a century and a half later, Queensborough is home to roughly eleven thousand people, no single group among them a majority, on ground that the Fraser River has never fully conceded.

## Leftovers of the Survey

The Royal Engineers surveyed Lulu Island in the 1860s and designated its eastern tip a military reserve for the defence of New Westminster. Nothing military ever much happened there. When the Township of Richmond incorporated in 1879, the reserve was pointedly left out of it, and the land sat in administrative limbo until the growing city across the river annexed it in 1889. The province handed over title, the city subdivided the whole reserve for sale in 1890, a bridge went up to reach it, and the lots went to auction. The name only became official in 1911, when an Italian immigrant and community leader named Anthony Sprice opened the Queensborough Post Office. In Chinook Jargon, the trade language of the coast, an adaptation of that same discarded name - "Koonspa" - became the usual word for New Westminster as a whole. The capital got the queen's name. The jargon kept Moody's.

## Mills, Canneries, and the People Who Came for Them

Cheap lots and steady work built the neighbourhood. Lumber mills and salmon canneries lined the riverbank, and the people who filled those jobs arrived from almost everywhere: Japanese, Chinese, Punjabi, Italian, Greek, Slovak, Ukrainian and Finnish families, each group raising its own hall, its own church or temple, its own mutual aid society. A substantial South Asian community had settled here by 1919, and a Sikh temple stood in Queensborough by 1931. What is striking is that the fragmentation never hardened. In 1911 the whole community organized itself under the Queensborough Ratepayers Association, still operating today and often cited as the oldest ratepayers association in British Columbia. Separate halls, shared drainage ditches and shared dikes: on low ground behind a shared dike, cooperation is less a virtue than an engineering requirement.

## The Komagata Maru Docks

The small passenger ferry that shuttles between Queensborough and the Quayside boardwalk takes about five minutes and costs $2.50. Both of its landings carry the same name: the Komagata Maru Docks. On May 23, 1914, a chartered Japanese steamship of that name reached Vancouver carrying 376 passengers from British India - 340 Sikhs, 24 Muslims, 12 Hindus - all of them British subjects, all of them legally entitled to nothing, as it turned out. Immigration authorities kept the ship anchored in the harbour for two months. Fewer than two dozen people were permitted to land. On July 23 the vessel was escorted back out to sea under naval guard, and when it finally reached Budge Budge near Calcutta that September, troops fired on the passengers and twenty of them died. Prime Minister Justin Trudeau delivered a full apology in the House of Commons on May 18, 2016. Naming the docks is a quieter gesture, and arguably a better one - thousands of people a year step onto that name without ceremony, in a neighbourhood built by the community the ship was carrying.

## Bridges and the Shape of Now

Queensborough spent its first century as a place you had to mean to visit. The current Queensborough Bridge, a 924-metre arch carrying Highway 91A, opened on August 26, 1960, replacing a span that had stood for 51 years; crossing cost a quarter until the provincial government bought out the toll in November 1966. The Derwent Way Bridge links the neighbourhood east to Annacis Island in Delta, a working industrial island that is not part of New Westminster at all. The waterfront that once held mills is now Port Royal, a redeveloped district of townhouses built to resemble the heritage homes they replaced. Queensborough Landing, a shopping centre, arrived in 2004. At the centre of it all sits Ryall Park, with the community centre, two schools, a spray park and the Boro All Wheel Park, where the skateboards and the bicycles have had to negotiate for territory with the dogs.

## Living at Three and a Half Metres

Every conversation about Queensborough's future eventually becomes a conversation about water. Most living space here is built above the city's flood design level of 3.5 metres, and 6.5 kilometres of dike hold the Fraser back. The numbers the city works from are sobering without being hysterical: roughly a 0.7 percent chance in any given year that a major flood overtops the dikes, which works out to about a 40 percent chance across a seventy-year lifetime, inundating perhaps a fifth of the houses to depths of up to 1.7 metres. A one-in-five-hundred-year event today would cause around $300 million in building damage - about 85 percent of all such damage in New Westminster - rising toward $400 million under mid-century climate projections. Upgrading the dikes would cost an estimated $84.6 million, roughly $55.9 million of it construction and $28.7 million seismic. The cost-benefit ratio comes out near 6.89. Few neighbourhoods can state their own odds so precisely.

## Flight Context

Queensborough occupies the eastern tip of Lulu Island at 49.1833 N, 122.933 W, roughly 12 nm southeast of downtown Vancouver. From 2,000 to 3,500 feet the layout is unmistakable: the Fraser River splits around Lulu Island, and Queensborough sits at the upstream point of the wedge, ringed by a visible dike line and dense low-rise housing. The blue-grey arch of the Queensborough Bridge crosses the North Arm at the neighbourhood's northeast corner, with the Derwent Way Bridge reaching east to the rectangular industrial grid of Annacis Island. Downstream the island widens into Richmond; upstream, the heritage streets of New Westminster climb a steep north bank. Nearby airports: Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 8 nm west on Sea Island; Pitt Meadows (CYPK) lies about 12 nm northeast; Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 10 nm south. Expect low ceilings and river fog on calm autumn and winter mornings, and heavy VFR traffic in the CYVR terminal area - check airspace before descending for a look.
