# Technical University of British Columbia

For three years British Columbia's newest university held classes in a converted department store in Surrey, waiting for a tower that opened four years after the university itself was shut down.

**Location:** 49.1860, -122.8500

**Categories:** Defunct universities and colleges in British Columbia, Education in Surrey, British Columbia, Simon Fraser University, Universities and colleges established in 1999, Educational institutions disestablished in 2002, Canadian history

## Introduction

It held classes in a dead department store. From September 1999 until the summer of 2002, the Technical University of British Columbia — TechBC, to everyone who went there — ran an entire institution out of leased space in a former Zellers at Surrey Place Mall, in premises the university cheerfully called "betaspace." The permanent campus was going up next door, a Bing Thom design that would become the tallest building in Surrey. TechBC never moved in. The province closed the university before the tower was finished, and by the time anyone occupied those floors, the name on the door belonged to somebody else.

## The Valley Asks for a University

The demand came from below. Surrey and the Fraser Valley were growing faster than almost anywhere in Canada, and they had no university of their own — a gap that showed up bluntly in the statistics. British Columbia ranked ninth among Canadian provinces for university participation, and the valley was dragging the number down. In 1991 residents formed the Fraser Valley University Society specifically to apply pressure, and one of its first moves was to lobby Simon Fraser University's president, William Saywell, for a satellite campus. Successive governments preferred a different answer: hand degree-granting powers to the colleges already there, the institutions that became Kwantlen Polytechnic University and the University of the Fraser Valley. By the mid-1990s that answer was wearing thin. On 2 February 1995, Mike Harcourt's government announced something more ambitious — a free-standing technical university on a hundred-million-dollar campus in Cloverdale.

## No Senate, No Tenure

The enabling legislation passed in July 1997, and it left out something universities elsewhere treat as structural. The Technical University of British Columbia Act made no provision for an academic senate, the body through which faculty normally control curriculum. Word also spread that the new institution would operate without tenure. The Canadian Association of University Teachers responded with a boycott that lasted a year — an unpromising way for a university to begin its life, since a boycott aims directly at the thing a new school most needs, which is people willing to teach at it. TechBC recruited anyway, and recruited well. Tom Calvert, a prominent computer scientist, came over from SFU as vice-president of research and external relations. Alice Mansell, an artist and academic leader, arrived as the first vice-president academic and rebuilt the leadership structure around planning and operations rather than disciplines. Tim Collings, who invented the V-chip, was among those who followed.

## TechOne

Under Glen Clark's government the mandate narrowed toward high technology, which was where the economic optimism of the late 1990s pointed. What emerged was genuinely unusual. Every student began with TechOne, a common and intensive first year blending artistic, technical, mathematical and communication skills, delivered through a hybrid of online and face-to-face work that most universities would not seriously attempt for another two decades. Three streams followed at the September 1999 launch: Interactive Arts, built around computers in art, design and new media; Information Technology, a hybrid of computer engineering and computer science; and Management and Technology, pairing business curriculum with the technical skills the sector actually required. TechOne's strength was also its trap. Because it matched nothing at any other institution, it largely would not transfer — and a first year that strands you if you leave is a hard thing to sell to an eighteen-year-old choosing between schools.

## The Tower That Ate the University

The Cloverdale site proved impractical, so Surrey offered twelve acres in Whalley instead, and in July 1998 the campus moved to the urban core. Then the financing got creative. The development arm of the provincial auto insurer, ICBC, had quietly bought the adjacent mall, and ICBC president Bob Williams — a long-serving NDP figure — proposed a three-storey galleria above it topped by a landmark office tower: TechBC in the podium, ICBC upstairs. The appeal was accounting. Rather than the promised hundred million in capital spending, the university would simply pay rent — $426,000 a month, more than $178 million across a twenty-five-year term once other charges were counted. Every comparison with other BC institutions, all of which had been gifted their buildings outright, now ran against TechBC. The construction delays meant the university could not admit students fast enough, which produced the charge that it was missing enrolment targets. The finished tower rose 118.9 metres and twenty-six storeys, and stood as Surrey's tallest building from 2003 to 2017.

## Seven February

Gordon Campbell's Liberals took office in 2001 promising a ten per cent cut across government, and a Core Services Review went looking for it. Advanced Education Minister Shirley Bond ordered a review of TechBC's future while deputy minister Gerry Armstrong quietly asked other institutions what they might do with it. SFU answered first. On 7 February 2002, at a televised Open Cabinet meeting, the government announced the closure. TechBC shut at the end of July after three waves of layoffs; SFU hired most of the teaching staff and reopened the same campus with the same programs that September. The agreement funded 860 full-time-equivalent students, against the 2,353 TechBC had proposed in its final business plan. SFU finally moved into the Bing Thom building in September 2006. The Surrey campus now serves more than five thousand students across thirty-plus programs, and in August 2026 the first students entered SFU's new Stephens Family School of Medicine there — the valley eventually got its university, under a different name.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 49.186° N, 122.850° W in Surrey's City Centre, about 20 km southeast of downtown Vancouver on the south side of the Fraser River. Fly it at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL: the Central City tower is the obvious mark, a 118.9-metre glass-and-wood cylinder rising from a low mall roof amid otherwise mid-rise development, with the SkyTrain Expo Line running past it on elevated guideway toward the Pattullo and Port Mann bridges. Boundary Bay (CZBB) is roughly 10 nm southwest, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 12 nm north across the Fraser, Langley (CYNJ) about 12 nm southeast, Abbotsford (CYXX) about 22 nm east and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 18 nm west. This is congested, controlled airspace beneath the Vancouver terminal area — check the VFR terminal chart before entering. Fraser Valley fog is common on autumn and winter mornings and can hold well past sunrise; summer afternoons are typically clear with good visibility toward the North Shore Mountains and Mount Baker.
