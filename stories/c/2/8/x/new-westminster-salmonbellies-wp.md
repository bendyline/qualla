# New Westminster Salmonbellies

A lacrosse club born on a cricket ground in 1888, named after an insult, and holder of more national championships than any team in the sport.

**Location:** 49.2147, -122.9058

**Categories:** Sports history, Lacrosse, British Columbia, New Westminster, Canadian culture

## Introduction

It began as an insult. In the club's early years a fan of an opposing side looked at the men from New Westminster and called them salmonbellies, which on the Fraser River in those days meant something close to peasants. Salmon was cheap. Salmon was what you ate when you could not afford beef. The players considered the jab and decided the heckler had chosen his detail badly, because the belly happens to be the best part of the fish. They took the name. More than a century later they still carry it, and they still refuse to spell it with a y, even when writing about a single Salmonbellie.

## Formed on a Cricket Ground

On May 11, 1888, the Daily British Columbian ran a notice of almost comic brevity: "All lacrosse players are requested to meet on the cricket grounds this evening for the purpose of forming a lacrosse club." They met. The club was founded the next day, May 12, 1888, with a Mr. Glover in the chair and a Mr. Lewis handed the combined offices of secretary and treasurer. Practices were set for Wednesday and Friday evenings, and the committee resolved to try for a match against Vancouver on the first of July. The first home game in the Royal City did not come until June 8, 1889, and it went badly, a 3-1 loss to those same Vancouver rivals. Two years after the founding, the three British Columbia clubs organized themselves into an amateur association to standardize the rules, and New Westminster won all six games of the inaugural season.

## Eggs, a Pistol, and a League That Kept Collapsing

Lacrosse in the Lower Mainland was not a polite pastime. In 1903 some eleven thousand people packed Brockton Oval to watch New Westminster play Vancouver in a match the papers of the day called the greatest ever played in western Canada; by the end of that season the two clubs were barely speaking, and New Westminster sat out 1904 entirely. The low point came in 1908, when a New Westminster supporter began throwing eggs at Vancouver players and the stands erupted. A member of the Vancouver club's staff drew a pistol and fired into the crowd. Nobody was killed, but Vancouver refused to play New Westminster for some time afterward. That same year the Salmonbellies toured east and played professional clubs in Montreal and Ottawa, which compromised their amateur standing and collapsed the provincial association altogether. What replaced it had two members. Over the next decade the league folded, reformed, cancelled seasons, and folded again, held together mostly by the fact that neither club could quite live without the other.

## Amsterdam, and a Gold Medal Nobody Won

As defending national champions, the Salmonbellies were selected to represent Canada in field lacrosse at the 1928 Olympic Games in Amsterdam, where the sport appeared as a demonstration event rather than a medal one. Three teams entered. Canada lost 6-3 to the United States, represented by the Johns Hopkins University squad, then beat Great Britain 9-5. Britain in turn beat the Americans, which left all three nations level in the standings with no mechanism to break the tie and no appetite for inventing one. All three were awarded gold medals. Back home the consequence was stranger still: with the city's senior club on the other side of the Atlantic, no senior season was played in the Lower Mainland that year at all.

## Twenty-Four Times

The Mann Cup arrived in New Westminster for the first time in 1915 and kept coming back. In May 1932 the senior league in British Columbia abandoned the field game for box lacrosse, played on a floor inside an arena, and the Salmonbellies made the switch with everyone else; their first Cup under the new rules came in 1937. By the time the run ended they had won it twenty-four times, more than any club in the history of the trophy, with at least one championship in every single decade from the Cup's inception until the 2000s. The names are still hanging at Queen's Park Arena: Paul Parnell, Wayne Goss, Eric Cowieson, Jack Bionda, Cliff Sepka, Dave Durante, Geordie Dean. More Salmonbellies have entered the Canadian Lacrosse Hall of Fame than players from any other team, and in 2004 the great teams of 1968 through 1972 were inducted together, as a single unit.

## Thirty-Five Years of Almost

The last one came in 1991. Since then the Salmonbellies have reached the Mann Cup final nine times and lost every one, including three years running from 2008 to 2010. The Six Nations Chiefs, who entered the Ontario league in 1993, have become the particular obstacle. In 2023 they took the series in New Westminster four games to one. When the two met again in the Royal City in 2025, the hosts won the opener on the last shot of the game and the second by four goals, and for two nights it looked as though the drought might finally break. It did not. Six Nations pushed the series to a seventh game and won it. The 2026 season was harder still, a 6-12 record and no playoffs. There is a particular loyalty that attaches to a club like this, older than the province's highways, named after a fish nobody respected. It does not require a trophy every year. It has waited longer.

## Flight Context

Queen's Park Arena, the Salmonbellies' home since long before the current drought, sits at 49.2147 N, 122.9058 W in New Westminster, British Columbia, on the north bank of the Fraser River about 10 nm east-southeast of downtown Vancouver. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet the geography reads plainly: the broad brown ribbon of the Fraser, the Pattullo and Port Mann bridges spanning it, and the green rectangle of Queen's Park set into the street grid just north of the river. Nearby airports include Vancouver International (CYVR) roughly 11 nm west, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 9 nm south-southwest, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 10 nm northeast, and Abbotsford International (CYXX) some 24 nm east. This is busy Class C and terminal airspace beneath Vancouver's arrivals, so coordination is essential. Autumn and winter bring persistent low cloud and rain funneling up the Fraser Valley; clearest viewing is on summer afternoons when the North Shore mountains stand up sharply behind the city.
