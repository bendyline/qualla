# Pattullo Bridge

For eighty-eight years a Depression-era arch carried Surrey across the Fraser River on lanes too narrow for the traffic it inherited, until a cable-stayed successor with a Halkomelem name took the job in 2026.

**Location:** 49.2076, -122.8950

**Categories:** Bridges in Greater Vancouver, Bridges over the Fraser River, Transport in New Westminster, Transport in Surrey, British Columbia, Engineering history

## Introduction

On February 6, 2026, the last car crossed the Pattullo Bridge. Ten days later the walkers and cyclists were gone too, and the long burnt-orange arch over the Fraser River stood empty for the first time since Premier Thomas Dufferin Pattullo cut the ribbon on his own namesake in November 1937. Few structures in Metro Vancouver were so thoroughly disliked and so completely indispensable. Four lanes, no median barrier, a deck that had carried something like seventy-five thousand cars a day into its ninth decade: the Pattullo was an eighty-eight-year-old answer to a question nobody was asking anymore. Its retirement was a relief. It was also the end of something.

## The Premier Who Put His Name On It

Duff Pattullo had crossed the Chilkoot Pass in 1897 as secretary to the Yukon Commissioner, served as acting Gold Commissioner in Dawson City, sat on its council, then moved south in 1908 to become mayor of Prince Rupert. He entered provincial politics in 1916 and took the premiership in November 1933, at the bottom of the Depression, on a promise of work and wages. What followed was British Columbia's own small New Deal: a Special Powers Act in 1934, a higher minimum wage, money for schools and relief, and concrete poured into roads and bridges to put men back on payrolls. The bridge across the Fraser was the showpiece. Built under supervising engineer Major W. G. Swan by the Dominion Bridge Company and Northern Construction, it cost $4 million and ran 1,227 metres. It opened to traffic on November 15, 1937, at a toll of twenty-five cents, which the province lifted in 1952.

## Before the Steel

Crossing the Fraser here was once a matter of timing and nerve. From 1882 a steam ferry called the K de K shuttled residents and livestock between Brownsville on the south bank and New Westminster on the north, and by the late 1890s it was plainly outmatched by the traffic it was asked to carry. The answer arrived in 1904: a two-level steel bridge, road above, railway below, begun in 1902. That structure served both jobs for three decades until the Pattullo opened and took the cars away, leaving the older span to the trains alone. It still stands, still works, and is now called the New Westminster Rail Bridge. Three crossings within sight of one another, each a generation apart, make this stretch of river a fairly legible diagram of how a region grows.

## Four Lanes, No Barrier

The trouble with the Pattullo was arithmetic. It had been laid out for 1937 vehicles, and the lanes never widened. Its central divider was a row of plastic pillars installed by TransLink to make the centre line harder to miss, which is not the same as making it harder to cross, and head-on collisions were a chronic hazard in rain or at speed. Lanes were closed overnight, from ten at night until five in the morning, as a safety measure. Every proposed fix ran into another. Counterflow operation in the manner of the Lions Gate Bridge would have strangled Surrey and New Westminster at the approaches. A concrete median would have narrowed the lanes further and forced a truck ban. Photo radar stayed politically impossible after the province scrapped it in 2001. Oversized commercial vehicles were simply prohibited, and the bridge carried on.

## A Name in Halkomelem

At three in the morning on January 18, 2009, fire broke out in the wooden trestle under the south approach, and a span meant to last weeks in repair reopened in eight days because engineers borrowed a temporary structure from the Canada Line construction site. That improvisation bought time; the replacement bought the future. The new crossing is cable-stayed, 1,235 metres long with a main span of 332 metres and a structure rising 167 metres above the river, built for $1.637 billion with four lanes that can become six, and with paths for people on foot and on bicycles that the old bridge never properly had. It carries no toll. The Kwantlen First Nation and Musqueam Indian Band gave it the Halkomelem name stal̕əw̓asəm, posted on the signage alongside the English name Riverview. One lane opened on Christmas Eve 2025. All four were running by February 14, 2026.

## Flight Context

The crossing sits at 49.208 N, 122.895 W, where the Fraser River bends between New Westminster on the north bank and Surrey on the south. Fly it at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL on a westbound or eastbound river track: from the air this reach of the Fraser is one of the most distinctive transport clusters in Metro Vancouver, with three parallel crossings within a few hundred metres of each other. From upstream to downstream you will pick out the new cable-stayed stal̕əw̓asəm (Riverview) Bridge with its harp of cables, the retired Pattullo arch beside it, the SkyTrain SkyBridge, and the swing-span New Westminster Rail Bridge. Note that the Pattullo closed to all traffic in February 2026 and no longer carries vehicles; do not use lit traffic as a cue for which span is which. The Fraser itself is a wide brown ribbon, sharply different in colour from the Strait of Georgia it feeds. Nearby airports: Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 9 nm southwest, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 13 nm west, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 10 nm northeast, Langley Regional (CYNJ) about 12 nm southeast, and Abbotsford International (CYXX) about 25 nm east. This is busy, layered airspace beneath the Vancouver terminal area, so check the current charts before overflying. Autumn and winter bring river fog and low ceilings; summer afternoons are typically clear, with the North Shore Mountains visible to the north and Mount Baker standing to the southeast.
