# Green Timbers Urban Forest Park

British Columbia's first reforestation plantation went into the ground here in 1930, and the 560-acre forest that grew from it survives inside Surrey only because its neighbours voted, twice, to keep it.

**Location:** 49.1807, -122.8194

**Categories:** 1974 establishments in British Columbia, 1930 establishments in British Columbia, Nature centres in British Columbia, Parks in Surrey, British Columbia, Surrey, British Columbia

## Introduction

On 15 March 1930, a crew from the British Columbia Forest Service walked into a field of stumps on the edge of Surrey and began pushing seedlings into the ground. Nothing like it had been attempted in the province before. The land they worked had been, only a few years earlier, one of the most photographed forests on the West Coast — a corridor of Douglas fir so tall that motorists on the Pacific Highway pulled over simply to stand beneath it. Logging took almost all of it. What went into the ground that March was British Columbia's first reforestation plantation, and the place is still here: 560 acres of second-growth woodland with a city grown tight against every edge.

## The Forest That Stopped Traffic

Before the saws, Green Timbers covered roughly 5,000 acres, and it earned its reputation the easy way — by standing directly beside the road. The Pacific Highway ran through it, and in the early 1900s this was the only old-growth stand on the West Coast a traveller could reach without leaving the pavement. Postcards went out from here. Families posed against trunks wide enough to swallow them.

People saw what was coming. Attempts to set the forest aside reach back as far as 1860, and they failed, one after another, for the reason such attempts usually failed: the timber was worth more in a ledger than in the ground. By the late 1920s the great trees were gone. What remained was a scoured, stump-studded plain that looked less like a forest than the memory of one.

## Fifteenth of March

The Forest Service chose that cleared ground for an experiment. Could a working forest be deliberately re-grown? On 15 March 1930 the first plantation went in, seedlings set out in tidy rows — a plantation rather than a woodland. The site became a forestry school and a nursery, training the people who would repeat the work across the province, and Green Timbers picked up the title it still carries: British Columbia's Birthplace of Reforestation.

Those rows are still legible if you know to look. Walk the interior trails and the big conifers — Douglas fir, grand fir, Sitka spruce, western hemlock, western redcedar, western white pine — line up in a way no forest manages on its own. By 2024, the practice begun in this one field had put more than ten billion trees into British Columbia soil.

## Saved by Referendum

Being a symbol did not make the ground safe. In 1966, 33 acres were carved off for the site of Douglas College; later, more was sold to the City of Surrey and built over as housing. The forest became a park in 1974, which sounds like the end of the story and was not. In 1987 residents formed the Green Timbers Heritage Society for a specific reason: someone had proposed putting a football stadium in the trees.

What followed was the least romantic and most effective kind of conservation — meetings, petitions, and two municipal votes, in 1988 and 1996, in which Surrey chose the forest. There is no dramatic standoff to recount, no protester chained to a trunk. A neighbourhood simply refused, repeatedly and in writing, to let the place be converted. That refusal is why there is anything left to walk through.

## Shallow Roots and a Wandering Stone

The logging left a mark you cannot see from the trail. Stripped of its canopy, the forest floor lost its humus layer to erosion, and the trees that came back sit on thinner ground than their ancestors did — root systems spread wide and shallow through loam and clay, gripping rather than anchoring. The result is a forest that feels mature and is, structurally, still young.

Near the middle of the park the trees open onto a meadow and Green Timbers Lake, stocked with rainbow trout in spring and fall and ringed most mornings by people in no hurry at all. Elsewhere in the woods sits a glacial erratic, a boulder that rode ice here from somewhere else entirely and was set down when the ice gave out. It predates every argument anyone has had about this land by thousands of years.

## What Moved Back In

Plant a forest and then wait to see who accepts the invitation. Bald eagles and red-tailed hawks work the airspace above the second growth. Great blue herons stand in the shallows with the patience of statues, buffleheads and wood ducks take the open water, and Steller's jays announce themselves from the understory whether you asked or not. At ground level: coyotes, raccoons, Douglas squirrels, Townsend's voles, and, after rain, Pacific banana slugs crossing the path at a pace that invites you to slow down and watch.

Over ten kilometres of trail thread the whole of it. Salal and red huckleberry crowd the edges, skunk cabbage lights the wet hollows in spring, and Pacific trillium opens white before fading to purple. Surrey residents call this their Stanley Park. The comparison undersells what happened here: this one was made on purpose, from nothing, by hand.

## Flight Context

Green Timbers sits at 49.1807°N, 122.8194°W in central Surrey, British Columbia, roughly 20 nm east-southeast of Vancouver International (CYVR). From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL it reads as a dark rectangular block of conifer in an otherwise pale grid of suburb — the sharpest green-to-grey edge for miles. Use Highway 1 to the north and the Highway 10 / King George Boulevard corridor to the south as bracketing lines; the small open water of Green Timbers Lake and the adjacent meadow break the canopy near the centre. Nearby fields: CYVR (Vancouver International) to the west, CZBB (Boundary Bay) to the southwest, CYPK (Pitt Meadows) to the north, and CYXX (Abbotsford International) about 25 nm east. Fraser Valley stratus and winter fog routinely hide the whole area below 1,000 ft; late-afternoon light in autumn gives the best contrast between the planted conifer rows and the deciduous fringe.
