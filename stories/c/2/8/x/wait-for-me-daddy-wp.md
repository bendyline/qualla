# Wait for Me, Daddy

On a New Westminster hill in 1940, a five-year-old broke from his mother's hand and ran into the most famous photograph Canada took during the Second World War.

**Location:** 49.2011, -122.9114

**Categories:** Photography, World War II, Military history, Monuments and memorials, British Columbia, History

## Introduction

The secret destination was Nanaimo. Three hours across the strait, still in British Columbia, still within sight of the same mountains - that was where the British Columbia Regiment was bound on October 1, 1940, when it marched down Eighth Street to board the SS Princess Joan. Nobody on the sidewalk knew that. The wives and mothers lining Columbia Street were saying goodbye the way you say goodbye to men going to a war that had already swallowed Poland and France, and one of them lost her grip. Five-year-old Warren Bernard, called Whitey, broke free and ran at the column with both arms out. A newspaper photographer had his camera up.

## One Frame, and the Luck in It

Claude P. Dettloff of The Province had positioned himself near the top of the Eighth Street hill for a reason: from there the whole column filled the frame, rank behind rank, dropping toward the Columbia Street intersection and the river beyond. It was a composition photograph, a piece of craft. Then the boy entered from the left. Dettloff pressed the shutter and caught Whitey mid-stride reaching for Private Jack Bernard, who has turned his head, who is already half out of the line. There is no second frame that matters. Everything the picture does - the receding soldiers, the blur of the child's momentum, the mother's hand still open behind him - happened in the fraction of a second in which the photographer did not have time to think. Craft put him on that hill. Accident gave him the rest.

## A Year of Waiting on the Coast

The regiment's war had begun more than a year earlier, at quarter past four in the morning on August 26, 1939, when the adjutant took a call from Ottawa telling him to call out the British Columbia Regiment. Germany was threatening Poland and demanding Danzig. Soldiers fanned out to guard bridges, docks, power stations - the vulnerable points of a coastal city bracing for something it could not yet picture. Canada declared war on September 10. Then, while other Canadian units shipped for the United Kingdom, the BC Regiment stayed put. Months of drill and guard duty followed, the specific tedium of men trained to leave who are not permitted to. When the order finally came, it delivered them to Vancouver Island. The real departure came later: the regiment converted to armour and fought through France and the Netherlands.

## The Woman in the Dark Coat

Look to the left edge, past Whitey's mother, at the second woman back - dark coat, necklace, staring straight into Dettloff's lens. That is Agnes Confortin, born Agnes Power, who had come along that morning to keep her friend Phyllis Daem company while Phyllis saw the men off. Agnes had no one in this column. She had two brothers already enlisted with the North Nova Scotia Highlanders, and the expression she wore has the weight of that arithmetic in it. Larry Power came home in 1944 with severe post-traumatic stress. Wilfred Power was killed in action near Arnhem in March 1945, weeks before the city was liberated. A crowd photograph is never only about its subject. Behind every celebrated face in this one stands someone doing private accounting about people who are somewhere else.

## What a Picture Becomes

Life and Time ran it. War-bond drives used it, because it did in one image what a page of copy could not: it made the abstraction of enlistment into a child's outstretched hands. Jack Bernard came home at the end of the war, and Dettloff was there again to photograph the reunion. The marriage did not survive the years that followed - an ordinary ending that the famous photograph, frozen at the moment of parting, has no way to hold. New Westminster made the moment permanent anyway. On October 4, 2014, the city unveiled a bronze by Veronica and Edwin Dam de Nogales at Hyack Square, at the foot of the same hill the regiment marched down. The Royal Canadian Mint struck three coins from the scene - two dollars in alloy, three and ten dollars in silver - and Canada Post put it on a stamp. Stand at Hyack Square today, look uphill, and the geometry of the photograph is still there in the street.

## Flight Context

The intersection of Eighth and Columbia Streets in New Westminster sits at 49.2011 N, 122.9114 W, on the steep north bank of the Fraser River's main arm. Fly it low and slow - 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL - because the story here is street-scale: a grid of blocks dropping sharply from the ridge to the waterfront, which is exactly the slope that let Dettloff frame an entire marching column in one shot. The river is the primary landmark, with the stal'ew'asem Bridge (opened January 2026 in place of the 1937 Pattullo Bridge, now closed to traffic) and the SkyBridge crossing to Surrey within a mile of the site. Queensborough and Annacis Island lie downstream to the southwest. Nearby airports: Vancouver International (CYVR) about 11 nm west, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 8 nm southwest, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 8 nm east-northeast, and Abbotsford International (CYXX) about 24 nm east-southeast. This is inside Vancouver's terminal airspace, so a clearance is required. Low cloud and Fraser Valley fog are common from late autumn through winter; the river reflects hard light on clear summer afternoons.
