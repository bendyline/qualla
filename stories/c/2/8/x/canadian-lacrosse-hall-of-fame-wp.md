# Canadian Lacrosse Hall of Fame

A Montreal dentist wrote down the rules of a game that had been played for centuries before him, called it Canada's national sport, and the shrine ended up on the Fraser River in a town that won the thing twenty-four times.

**Location:** 49.2015, -122.9114

## Introduction

In 1869 a Montreal dentist published a book called Lacrosse: The National Game of Canada, and inside it he explained exactly what he was doing. "Just as we claim as Canadian the rivers and lakes and land once owned exclusively by Indians," William George Beers wrote, "so we now claim their field game as the national field game of our dominion." He was not being coy. Beers had begun setting down written rules in 1860 - rubber ball, two-hundred-yard field, twelve players a side, first to five goals - and he spent the rest of his life promoting the result as a symbol of a young country. The game he codified was not his. The hall of fame that honours it sits today at 777 Columbia Street in New Westminster, British Columbia, four thousand kilometres from Beers's dental practice, in a town that earned the right to keep it.

## The Creator's Game

Long before anyone wrote a rulebook, Indigenous nations across the eastern half of the continent were playing versions of this game - some of them as early as 1100 AD. Teams could run to hundreds of men, sometimes as many as a thousand. The field was not a field but a stretch of country several miles long, and play went from sunup to sundown for two or three days running. It was ceremony as much as sport, a kind of symbolic warfare, and it was played for the Creator. Beers took that, reduced it to twelve players and a whistle, and offered it back to the settler nation as its own. The honest version of lacrosse's history holds both things at once: the modern sport exists because of what he organized, and it existed long before he arrived to organize it.

## Salmonbellies

Why New Westminster? Because of a club founded on 12 May 1888 whose players took their nickname from the fish that made the town's living. The New Westminster Salmonbellies have won the Mann Cup - senior lacrosse's national championship - twenty-four times, six more than any other club in Canada, and reached the final on forty-six occasions. They still play out of Queen's Park Arena, a wooden box of a building a few blocks up the hill from the Fraser. More Salmonbellies have been inducted into the Canadian Lacrosse Hall of Fame than alumni of any other team. Putting the hall anywhere else would have required an argument nobody in British Columbia was inclined to lose.

## Chartered, 1965

The Canadian Lacrosse Association chartered the hall in 1965 and inducted its first class the following year, with three categories: Field Players, Box Players and Builders. The box-and-field distinction reflects a genuine split in the sport - box lacrosse is the fast, walled, indoor Canadian variant; field lacrosse is the open outdoor game - and for half a century the hall kept separate shelves for each. A Team category arrived in 1988, a Veteran category in 1997 to rescue the stars of earlier generations from being forgotten. In 2016 the two player categories were merged into one, the hall deciding that a great lacrosse player is simply a great lacrosse player. Officials - the referees who work the floor - finally got a category of their own in 2022, alongside a new national selection committee meant to catch what the eastern and western committees kept missing.

## Newsy

Among that first group was Édouard Cyrille Lalonde, born in Cornwall, Ontario in 1887 and known to everyone as Newsy - a nickname earned not on any field but in a newspaper printing room, where he worked for the Cornwall Freeholder before sport became a living. He is remembered now mostly as a hockey player, an early giant of the game who entered the Hockey Hall of Fame in 1950. He made more money at lacrosse. In the years before the First World War, professional lacrosse drew crowds and paid wages that hockey could not yet match, and Lalonde scored 147 goals in 93 games for Vancouver. He died in Montreal in 1970, holding a place in both halls, a reminder that the ranking of Canadian sports has not always run the way it runs now.

## Upstairs at the Anvil

The hall's present home is the Anvil Centre, a civic arts and conference building on Columbia Street where it shares a roof with the New Westminster Museum and Archives and a contemporary media gallery. The neighbours are apt for a collection that is part sport, part civic memory. And the thing being remembered here is not a niche pastime: under the National Sports of Canada Act, given royal assent on 12 May 1994, "the game commonly known as lacrosse is hereby recognized and declared to be the national summer sport of Canada." Beers got his wish, 94 years after his death - written into statute, in the same sentence that names hockey, in a country that had to be argued into it.
