# New Westminster

British Columbia's first mainland capital burned to the ground in a single night in 1898, and the city that rebuilt itself on the Fraser has been quietly reinventing its name ever since.

**Location:** 49.2069, -122.9110

**Categories:** cities, history, rivers, british-columbia, pacific-northwest

## Introduction

Queen Victoria named this city herself, which is the sort of detail that sticks to a place for a hundred and sixty years. Colonel Richard Clement Moody and his Royal Engineers had proposed calling their new colonial capital Queensborough, and Governor James Douglas proclaimed it under that name on 14 February 1859. The Queen overruled them. She chose New Westminster instead, after the London borough, and the nickname that came with the honour - the Royal City - outlasted the Empire that bestowed it. In 2022 the city began quietly phasing the moniker out. A new logo arrived on 9 June 2025. Nothing about the place has ever been settled for long, including what to call it.

## A Second England on the Fraser

The Kwantlen First Nation lived on this bend of the river long before anyone drew a colonial boundary across it. Then gold was found in British Columbia, prospectors poured north from the American side, and the colonists grew nervous about who might follow them. Moody arrived in December 1858 at the head of the Columbia Detachment, hand-picked, in the phrase of the day, to found a second England on the shores of the Pacific. He was an engineer with an eye for ground. He rejected Fort Langley as too close to the border and chose this site instead: defensible, well served by water, positioned for the railways that had not yet been dreamed into existence, and blessed with an excellent port. He also wanted it beautiful - a city of beauty in the wilderness, laid out so that the authority of the Crown would be legible in its streets. It served as capital until 1866, when the mainland and island colonies merged and Victoria took the title.

## The Night of 10 September 1898

By the 1890s New Westminster had the second-largest Chinatown in the colony after Victoria's, and once Barkerville faded, the largest on the mainland. It sat along Front Street in a low, boggy stretch known simply as the Swamp - ground too soft and too close to the river and railway for the stone and brick blocks going up on Columbia Street above it. On 10 September 1898 fire took the downtown. It took Chinatown with it. The commercial core was rebuilt; the Chinese district was only partly rebuilt, and the community that had made it never recovered the ground it lost. A city can be reconstructed from insurance money and civic will. A neighbourhood that was already tolerated rather than welcomed is harder to put back, and New Westminster's was not.

## The Skookum House

In 1878 the federal government opened the British Columbia Penitentiary between Sapperton and what is now Queen's Park - the first federal prison west of Manitoba. Locals called it the Pen, or the skookum house, borrowing from the Chinook Jargon that still lubricated conversation across the early province. For 102 years it held maximum-security prisoners, closing at last in 1980. Demolition was only partial, which is why the site reads strangely today. The original centre block survives as offices. The gatehouse and its steps are still there, along with the old coal house, and down on the waterfront the prison armoury has been folded into a public park where the wharf once received steamboats. Townhouses and condominiums cover the rest. People buy groceries and walk dogs on ground that was, within living memory, the hardest address in the province.

## Sappers and Brewers

Sapperton takes its name from the Royal Engineers - sappers - who camped on the hill above it. Their dead and the province's early notables lie in Fraser Cemetery, a collection of monuments that rivals Victoria's Ross Bay for historical weight. The neighbourhood also gave British Columbia its first commercial brewery, the City Brewery, which passed through a succession of owners before Labatt's closed the plant in 2005. The condominium development that replaced it is called the Brewery District and contains, with perfect modern logic, no brewery. Beer did come back on its own terms: Steel & Oak opened in 2014 at the far end of the Quay, and Another Beer Co set up in 2019 near the old Labatt's site. Uptown, meanwhile, grew around Sixth and Sixth after Woodward's opened a department store there in 1954 and the library followed downtown's crowd up the hill in 1958.

## The River Turns

Front Street was a dockside market before the 1960s buried it under a truck route and a parkade; demolition of that structure began in 2016 as the city turned back toward its water. The street's grim mid-century texture made it useful to film crews, standing in for the Bronx, for a future Chicago, and for Philadelphia with the Fraser playing the Delaware. Westminster Quay arrived in the mid-1980s alongside the SkyTrain, faltered, and reopened as the River Market in September 2010. It also holds a 9.75-metre tin soldier that Guinness certified in 2002 as the world's largest. The most visible change is newer still: the orange arch of the 1937 Pattullo Bridge carried traffic to Surrey until 6 February 2026, when it closed for good, replaced by the stal̕əw̓asəm Bridge that had fully opened the month before. Roughly 79,000 people live in 15.62 square kilometres here, on a river that has never stopped rearranging what sits beside it.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.207N, 122.911W, elevation about 60 m (200 ft), on the north bank of the Fraser River where it swings southwest toward its estuary - roughly 19 km southeast of downtown Vancouver and near the geographic centre of Metro Vancouver. Best viewed at 2,500-4,000 ft AGL. The clearest navigational cue is the river itself: pick up the Fraser's sharp bend, then look for the new stal̕əw̓asəm Bridge crossing to Surrey (the orange through-arch of the old Pattullo alongside it closed in February 2026), the SkyBridge carrying SkyTrain across just downstream, and the Queensborough Bridge spanning the north arm to Lulu Island, where the Queensborough neighbourhood sits on the island's eastern tip. Queen's Park is the large green block on the uptown plateau. Nearest airports: Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 8 nm east-northeast, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 9 nm southwest, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 11 nm west, and Abbotsford (CYXX) about 24 nm east-southeast. Expect low ceilings and river fog on autumn and winter mornings; the Fraser valley funnels haze eastward on stagnant summer days.
