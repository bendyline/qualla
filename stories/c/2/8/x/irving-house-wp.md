# Irving House

Fourteen rooms of Gothic Revival timber on Royal Avenue, built in 1865 by a Scottish steamboat captain who fought a freight war down to fifty cents a ton - and the oldest fully intact house in the Lower Mainland.

**Location:** 49.2078, -122.9063

**Categories:** History of New Westminster, Buildings and structures in New Westminster, Museums in British Columbia, Historic house museums in Canada

## Introduction

There is a leafy neighbourhood in Portland, Oregon called Irvington, and it is named for the man who built this house in New Westminster. William Irving never quite stayed put. Born in Annan, in the Scottish border country, he sailed for Boston at fifteen, held a steamship captain's licence by twenty-five, and turned up in Oregon in 1849 by way of the Sacramento goldfields. He bought a block of the Portland townsite, ran lumber up from California, married an eighteen-year-old newcomer from Indiana named Elizabeth Dixon, and raised five children on the riverbank. Then, in 1859, he moved the whole enterprise north to a colony that barely existed yet.

## Fifty Cents a Ton

British Columbia in 1859 was a gold rush wearing the costume of a colony, and everything the miners needed had to travel by water. Irving settled first in Victoria, took a partnership in the Victoria Steam Navigation Company, and built two sternwheelers - the Governor Douglas and the Colonel Moody - to work the run between Victoria and New Westminster. Competition arrived promptly in the person of Captain William Moore, and what followed was less a business rivalry than a slow-motion collision. Freight rates on the route collapsed from twelve dollars a ton to fifty cents. Passenger fares fell just as hard. Neither captain could make money, and only after the Fraser Canyon rush faded did the surviving operators agree to stop destroying each other. When the Cariboo goldfields opened in 1862, Irving built the Reliance to push miners and supplies upriver to Yale, the head of navigation and the start of the Cariboo Wagon Road. Moore answered with the Flying Dutchman and the Alexandra. Irving kept the customers. Moore went bankrupt.

## Fourteen Rooms on Royal Avenue

By 1865 the lower Fraser carried only four sternwheelers, and Irving had made an arrangement with Captain Fleming to alternate years and split the profits evenly - the kind of truce men make after learning what the alternative costs. That was the year he moved his family to New Westminster and built the house at 302 Royal Avenue, on the corner of Merivale Street, where it still stands on its original ground. The timing was pointed. The Royal Engineers who had laid out the city had been disbanded only two years earlier, in 1863, and New Westminster was still deciding whether it would become a capital or a company town. Irving's answer was fourteen rooms and forty-seven hundred square feet of Gothic Revival ambition: a wooden house on a brick foundation, a storey and a half under steep gables, more space than a river captain strictly needed and exactly as much as a river captain wanted to be seen with.

## The Flags at Half-Mast

He had seven years in it. William Irving died at home on 28 August 1872, in his mid-fifties, and New Westminster shut down for him - every flag in town at half-mast, every store closed. He is buried at Fraser Cemetery, a few minutes' walk from the house. What happened next is the part that surprises people: his son John, eighteen years old, took over the river business and did not merely hold it together but expanded it, building the family's steamboat interests into something considerably larger than his father had managed. The house stayed in the family through three generations, until 1950. Nobody stripped it for parts, nobody subdivided the lot, nobody moved it to make room for something taller. That accident of continuity is why it survives today as the oldest house in the Lower Mainland still completely intact.

## Gold Behind the Paint

The City of New Westminster took the house on after 1950 and opened it as a museum, and for decades that was a fairly static arrangement - a preserved interior, a costumed tour, a schoolchild's field trip. Then in 2009 staff working in the upper hallway and the entrance found wallpapers underneath later finishes, dated to somewhere between 1897 and 1903. Rather than settle for a photograph, the museum had the patterns reproduced, printed in England, shipped back across an ocean the Irvings themselves had crossed, and hung where they had originally been. Since 2011 the restoration has run deeper and less visibly: modern fire suppression and electrical systems threaded through nineteenth-century framing, structural repair, exterior paint returned to its documented colours. In April 2018 the metallic gold wallpapers went back up. The work is not finished, and by the nature of an old wooden house it never entirely will be.

## What a House Remembers

Irving House is presented as a window onto colonial New Westminster, and it is honest enough to be one. The rooms are furnished the way a prosperous settler family with steamboat money furnished rooms - the parlour arranged for display, the service spaces arranged for people whose names appear in far fewer documents. A visitor moving through fourteen rooms is walking through the specific, narrow, comfortable world of one family who arrived with the gold rush and did well from it, in a city that was being invented around them as they unpacked. The house does not argue about that. It simply sits at the top of Royal Avenue, gables against the sky, older than the province it stands in.

## Flight Context

Irving House sits at 49.2078 N, 122.9063 W, on the bluff above the Fraser River in New Westminster, British Columbia, at the corner of Royal Avenue and Merivale Street. The building itself is small - a single house lot - so navigate to the setting rather than the structure: from 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL, follow the Fraser River east from its Strait of Georgia delta and look for the sharp bend where the river narrows and the north bank rises steeply into the New Westminster escarpment. The blue-glass towers of the New Westminster Quay and the SkyTrain bridge across the Fraser are the clearest local markers; Irving House lies a few hundred metres north and uphill of the waterfront, in the older residential grid near Queen's Park. Vancouver International (CYVR) is roughly 11 nautical miles west, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 12 nautical miles northeast, and Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 10 nautical miles southwest - all inside busy terminal airspace, so check current restrictions. Low cloud and river fog are routine from October through March; the crisp northeasterly outflow days of winter and clear summer evenings give the best light on the bluff.
