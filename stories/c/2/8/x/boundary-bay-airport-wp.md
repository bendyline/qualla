# Boundary Bay Airport

A delta farm field that trained Liberator crews, eavesdropped on the Soviet navy, sold for one dollar, and became the busiest airport in Canada without a single airline.

**Location:** 49.0742, -123.0070

**Categories:** Airports in Greater Vancouver, Certified airports in British Columbia, Buildings and structures in Delta, British Columbia, Transport in Delta, British Columbia, Military history of British Columbia, Cold War sites, Aviation history

## Introduction

For two years running, the busiest airport in Canada by takeoffs and landings was not Toronto Pearson or Vancouver International. It was a flat rectangle of reclaimed Fraser delta farmland in Delta, British Columbia, with no airline, no jet bridge and no departures board. Boundary Bay Airport logged roughly 248,000 aircraft movements in 2021, ranking first in the country that year and in 2020 as well. Almost all of those movements were the same small aeroplanes going around the same circuit, touching down and lifting off again. The place is busy the way a rehearsal room is busy. But the repetition is only its latest career, and not its strangest.

## Liberators for a Distant War

The runways were scraped out of delta farmland in the spring of 1941, and the first aircraft to use them were De Havilland Tiger Moths, fabric biplanes flown by No. 18 Elementary Flying Training School under civilian instructors. Fighters came next. Hawker Hurricanes of No. 133 Squadron stood ready through the months when a Japanese landing on the British Columbia coast seemed a live possibility, and the Curtiss Kittyhawks of Nos. 14 and 132 Squadrons followed them. Then, on 1 April 1944, the station's purpose changed entirely. No. 5 Operational Training Unit arrived to teach Commonwealth crews the Consolidated Liberator, a four-engined American bomber most of them had never touched, alongside North American Mitchells and target-towing Bolingbrokes. Those crews were bound for Southeast Asia and the Pacific. More than 6,000 students and personnel passed through the station before No. 5 OTU stood down on 31 October 1945.

## The Station That Listened

Peace nearly erased the place. The RCAF used it as a demobilization centre for troops coming home, then decommissioned the airfield in 1946. What saved it was not aviation but radio. In the late 1940s the Royal Canadian Corps of Signals took the site over as the Vancouver Wireless Station, a signals intelligence post whose operators monitored Soviet military communications and fleet traffic at the height of the Cold War. Around the listening gear grew an entire village: forty hectares of housing with a grocery store, a social club, a gymnasium and a sports field, all of it hidden in plain sight behind a farm-country address. Unification of the armed forces renamed it CFS Ladner in 1968. Three years later it closed, defeated by something mundane. New power lines strung nearby threw off enough electrical noise to spoil the reception. The married quarters are gone; the ground they stood on is now the North 40 Park Reserve, where Delta residents walk their dogs off-leash.

## Sold for One Dollar

After the signals station shut down, the old field belonged to whoever wanted it. Ladner held picnics and public fairs on the pavement, and cars raced on the runways. Then Vancouver International ran out of room. By the mid-1970s YVR could no longer comfortably host both heavy commercial traffic and the region's small aircraft, and Transport Canada concluded that reopening Boundary Bay as a reliever field was the cheapest answer available. Two of the three runways returned to service on 11 July 1983, with a new control tower west of the field. Ottawa later sold the airport to the City of Delta for a nominal one dollar. Management passed to a contractor, the buildings slid, and the municipality eventually vacated the contract. Alpha Aviation took over in December 2004, ended the drag racing, extended runway 07/25 for corporate jets with provincial grants, and opened a new terminal in February 2010, in time for the Winter Olympics. Delta extended the lease to 2099.

## Circuits and Bumps

What Boundary Bay does now, it does relentlessly. Five flight schools work the field: International Flight Centre, Insignia College, the Pacific Flying Club, Pacific Professional Flight Centre and Sea Land Air. Their students fly the pattern in a slow continuous rotation, which is why a field with no scheduled service out-counts the country's airline hubs. Scheduled flying has come and gone here more than once. Alpha Executive Air ran twice-daily service to Victoria until it ceased operations in 2010; on-demand charter companies worked out of the field until early 2023, and since then no hub-and-spoke operator has been based here. Canada Border Services officers still come out on call, because Boundary Bay is a designated airport of entry for arriving aircraft carrying no more than fifteen passengers. On the field, one wartime building survives: the old maintenance hangar, rehabilitated and designated a heritage site, the last standing witness to the Liberator crews.

## Flight Context

Boundary Bay Airport (ICAO CZBB, IATA YDT) lies at 49.0742° N, 123.007° W, just six feet above sea level on the Fraser River delta, 8.5 nautical miles south-southeast of Vancouver and 2.5 NM east of Ladner. Two paved runways cross: 07/25 at 6,015 feet and 13/31 at 5,605 feet. Approach from the south at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL and the layout reads instantly — a grid of dyked farm fields, the broad tidal flats of Boundary Bay to the south, Mud Bay to the east, and Highway 99 running arrow-straight northwest toward the George Massey Tunnel. Expect a full circuit: this is a training field, and the pattern is frequently busy with student traffic. Nearby fields are CYVR (Vancouver International) 8.5 NM north-northwest, CYPK (Pitt Meadows) northeast across the Fraser, CYXX (Abbotsford) east up the valley, and KBLI (Bellingham) southeast across the international border. Fog forms readily over the delta in autumn and winter; summer afternoons usually bring clear air and a westerly off the Strait of Georgia.
