# Woodlands (New Westminster)

For 118 years British Columbia sent its children with disabilities to an institution above the Fraser River; when the last of it came down in 2011, the people who had lived inside were the ones who wanted it gone.

**Location:** 49.2135, -122.8973

**Categories:** Former psychiatric hospitals, History of New Westminster, Disability history, British Columbia, Institutions

## Introduction

In October 2011, about 150 people came to watch a tower fall in New Westminster. Demolitions draw crowds, but this one drew a particular crowd. Heritage advocates had argued that the Centre Block Tower, the last standing piece of an institution that had opened in 1878, deserved to be saved. Former residents disagreed, and said so loudly enough that city council voted in July of that year to take it down. When the walls finally came in, some of the people watching had spent their childhoods behind them.

## The Names It Was Given

The institution opened in 1878 on roughly 40 hectares of crown land above the Fraser River, and the province called it, in the language of that era, the Provincial Lunatic Asylum. There were 65 residents by 1886 and 171 by 1896. In 1897 the name changed to the Provincial Hospital for the Insane, which was meant to sound more medical and did not change much else. The final renaming came in 1950: Woodlands School. The word school did real work. It implied teachers, lessons, graduation, a place children passed through on their way to somewhere. What Woodlands actually held by then were children with developmental disabilities, runaways, and wards of the state - people whose families had been told, often by doctors, that this was what a caring society did with them.

## Fourteen Hundred and Thirty-Six

In 1961 the resident population peaked at 1,436. That is a town's worth of people on 40 hectares, in buildings never designed for such a number, and the overcrowding grew severe enough that the province began shipping residents out to other institutions - Tranquille at Kamloops, Essondale and Valleyview in Coquitlam. British Columbia had built a small archipelago of these places, and a person could be moved between them without anyone outside asking why. Through the 1970s the tide reversed. Advocates, families, and a changing understanding of disability pushed toward community placements and group homes, and the resident count fell year by year. Woodlands closed for good in 1996, 118 years after it opened.

## One in Five

What had happened inside came out afterward, as it usually does. Investigation concluded that roughly twenty percent of residents had suffered physical, mental, or sexual abuse. Twenty percent of a population that had, in many cases, no reliable way to report it, no family visiting weekly, and no one outside the institution obliged to listen. A class action was filed in 2002 on behalf of former residents. It settled in 2003, with more than 850 people eligible for compensation ranging from $3,000 to $150,000 depending on what had been done to them. Reducing a childhood to a figure on a schedule is a grim exercise, but it was the first time the province had formally acknowledged that these were injuries done to individuals rather than unfortunate conditions in a building.

## The Line Drawn at 1974

The settlement carried a cut-off. Under the Crown Proceedings Act, the province could not be held liable for harm that occurred before 1974 - which meant that the survivors with the longest memories, the ones who had been there through the worst of the overcrowding years, received nothing. They were also, by then, the oldest. Every year of delay meant fewer of them left to receive anything at all. And there was delay: the case had settled in 2003, but the money did not reach survivors until 2018. Fifteen years is a long time to wait for an outcome you have already been told you have won. For many of the people entitled to it, the wait outlasted them.

## Victoria Hill

The abandoned buildings sat for twelve years. In July 2008 a major fire tore through several of them, gutting the second and third floors of the centre block, and the argument about preservation began in earnest - a familiar argument in a city proud of its Victorian stock, made unfamiliar by the people on the other side of it. The tower came down in 2011. The site is now called Victoria Hill, home to about 1,200 new houses and apartments on the bluff. One oddity remains from an earlier plan: the Expo Line was built through here with a station in mind, and the track geometry still allows for one, but the redevelopment never lined up with the timing and the platform was never constructed. Trains pass the site every few minutes without stopping. Given what stood here, that may be the right speed.

## Flight Context

The former Woodlands site - now the Victoria Hill neighbourhood - sits at 49.2135°N, 122.8973°W, on a bluff on the north bank of the Fraser River just west of downtown New Westminster. Fly it at 2,000-3,500 ft AGL. The Fraser is the anchor landmark: follow its north arm east from Annacis Island and the bluff rises on the left, with the Queensborough Bridge crossing to the south and the Expo Line's SkyBridge spanning the river a short way downstream. The SkyTrain viaduct runs along the site's edge between 22nd Street and New Westminster stations - look for the stretch of elevated track with no platform. Metro Vancouver's grid sprawls in every direction, so pick the river, not the streets. Nearby airports: CYVR Vancouver International roughly 18 km west, CZBB Boundary Bay about 20 km southwest, CYPK Pitt Meadows some 20 km east, and CYNJ Langley Regional 25 km southeast. This is dense controlled airspace under Vancouver's terminal environment - get clearance before manoeuvring. Autumn and winter bring persistent low stratus and Fraser Valley fog; the clearest views come on cold, dry outflow days.
