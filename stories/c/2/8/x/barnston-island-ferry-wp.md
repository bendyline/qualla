# Barnston Island Ferry

A tug shoves a barge across five minutes of Fraser River, free, on demand, because the bridge islanders have asked for since 1916 has never been built.

**Location:** 49.1919, -122.7250

**Categories:** Ferries of British Columbia, Crossings of the Fraser River, Transportation history, Metro Vancouver

## Introduction

Some winters the Fraser froze hard enough that Barnston Island farmers skated their produce across to the mainland. That was not charm; that was logistics. The island sits in a working river a few hundred metres off Surrey, and for more than a century the only way on or off has been a barge shoved across Parson's Channel by a tugboat. The crossing takes five minutes. It costs nothing. It runs on demand from a quarter past six in the morning until nearly midnight, and on school days the bus boards first. No bridge has ever been built, though islanders have been asking for one since before the ferry existed.

## The Year the Steamboats Stopped

In 1913 the Canadian Pacific Railway withdrew its steamboat service from the Fraser, and the farmers of Barnston Island woke up with no realistic way to get a crate of potatoes to market. A government wharf went up that same year. It was not enough. Petitions followed, and in May 1916 a government ferry finally began running - a secondhand cable ferry, retired from the Pitt River crossing and pressed into service here. It was the wrong boat for the job. Driftwood came down the channel in volume, and the underwater cable kept snagging fishermen's nets. The vessel was hauled out for a complete overhaul the next year. Even then, service stopped for six weeks every spring when the high water came, and again in winter when ice floes made the channel dangerous. Milk, grain, hay and potatoes do not wait for a river to settle down.

## The Boat That Went Around for the Milk

The fixes came one vessel at a time. A gasoline boat pushing a small scow arrived in 1918, enough for passengers and a little produce. Then, in 1921, a larger launch - a 38-foot former fishing boat - took over, and because the island's own roads were too poor to be trusted, it simply circled the island each day, pulling in at collection points to take milk churns aboard. By 1927 it was running flat out through the peak hours and wearing out. The 1930 replacement marked the real change: a 32-foot tug pushing a 36-foot barge that could carry two cars. Vehicles were multiplying on the island and the roads had improved, so the barge stayed at the slipway while the tug went off on its milk round anyway. By 1933 the crossing was free. Well into the 1950s, a truck still rode over each day to gather the churns and haul them to Dairyland.

## High Water

A river this size sets the terms. In the winter of 1936-37, floating ice rammed the ferry against the island slip and it had to be refloated; while it was under repair, only foot passengers got across. That September, a ferry worker backing a car at the mainland slip put it straight into the Fraser, fought his way out of the sinking vehicle, and surfaced. The worst came in 1948. As the flood rose, the ferry slip disappeared under fifteen feet of water, and the barges - fitted with extra railings to corral livestock - carried off 35 families, 500 head of cattle, 300 sheep, and all the poultry, pigs and horses that could be loaded. The B.C. Packers boat Salmon Prince worked the evacuation alongside them. The next year's freshet tore out the island landing ramp entirely, and for months the ferry simply ran itself up onto the beach.

## Same Crossing, Heavier Steel

In 1940 a new 35-foot wooden tug, the Barnston Island, took over, the daily circuit of the island ended, and the schedule settled into seven to seven. A barge capable of two trucks followed in 1944. In 1951 the upended wreck of a ferry that had sunk years earlier was finally blown apart with explosives to clear the slip. Then came steel: a 60-foot barge in 1954, the Barnston Island No. 3, good for three vehicles, and in 1963 the Barnston Island No. 1, a 66-foot steel tug that replaced the wooden one while the island landing was rebuilt around it. Through most of that, one man ran the boat. Len Thompson had been the ferry's captain since 1930 and retired in 1962, thirty-two years on a five-minute route. Western Pacific Marine won the operating contract in 1984 and started work that April.

## The Last Free Crossings

Today's barge takes six vehicles and 52 passengers, though a full load of cars leaves room for only 28 people. When the Albion Ferry was withdrawn in 2009, Barnston joined the Lytton and Big Bar crossings as the last free inland ferries left on the Fraser. Getting there means leaving Highway 1 at exit 53 into Port Kells and driving to the end of 104th Avenue, where the channel is narrow enough that the far bank looks like the other side of a wide street. The island itself is mostly farmland, named in 1827 for George Barnston, a Hudson's Bay Company clerk who came upriver with James McMillan to establish Fort Langley. Electricity reached it by underwater cable in 1938. At the southeast end is the Katzie First Nation's Barnston Island Indian Reserve No. 3. A century of asking has not produced a bridge, and the tug still comes when you turn up.

## Flight Context

The ferry crossing sits at 49.1919 N, 122.7250 W, on Parson's Channel where the Fraser River splits around Barnston Island between Surrey and Pitt Meadows. Best viewed from 1,500-2,500 ft AGL: the island reads as a flat green oval of field and cranberry bog, ringed by a dike and set in brown river water, with the Port Kells industrial flats and the Highway 1 corridor on the south bank. Pitt Meadows Airport (CYPK) lies roughly 6 nm northwest across the river and is the natural reference point; Langley Regional (CYNJ) is about 8 nm southeast, Abbotsford (CYXX) 20 nm east, and Vancouver International (CYVR) 20 nm west. Expect Fraser Valley fog on calm autumn and winter mornings, which can hide the island entirely while the North Shore mountains stay clear above it. The Golden Ears and Port Mann bridges upstream and downstream make the best large-scale landmarks.
