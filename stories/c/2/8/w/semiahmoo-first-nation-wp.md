# Semiahmoo First Nation

A small Coast Salish nation on 312 acres at the Canada-US border, where an international line, a municipal landfill, and sixteen years without drinkable tap water all landed on the same shore.

**Location:** 49.0000, -122.7667

**Categories:** Coast Salish governments, First Nations governments in the Lower Mainland, Indigenous history, British Columbia

## Introduction

For sixteen years, one of the smallest First Nations in Metro Vancouver could not drink from its own taps. The boil-water advisory went up in 2005 and did not come down until March 31, 2021 - and for all of that time, the City of White Rock's water ran clear a few hundred metres up the beach. The Semiahmoo reserve sits in one of the wealthiest, most serviced corners of Canada, wedged between a seaside resort town and the international border. Getting a pipe to it took three levels of government and most of two decades.

## A Line Drawn Through a Shoreline

Spanish ships reached this bay in 1791. George Vancouver's crews noted Semiahmoo fishing camps along it the following year. What neither expedition recorded was that they were sailing across the middle of a single people's territory, which ran from the Strait of Georgia down into what is now Washington State and inland along the lower Fraser. The Oregon Treaty of 1846 fixed the 49th parallel through all of it. Relatives who had shared the same beaches, the same reef-net sites, the same winter houses now lived in two countries. On the American side, the Point Elliott Treaty of 1855 made no provision for them; villages and fishing grounds around Point Roberts and Birch Bay slipped out of reach. Many Semiahmoo settled north of the new line, on the Canadian shore of the bay that still carries their name. The nation calls itself trans-boundary to this day, because the boundary is the newer thing.

## Counting Down

In 1790, European observers put the Semiahmoo population at roughly 300. By 1854, smallpox and warfare had cut it to about 250. The decline kept going, and it kept accelerating: 38 band members in British Columbia by 1909, 28 by 1963, 25 by 1971. These are not abstractions. Each drop represents families that did not continue, knowledge that did not get passed down, houses that emptied on a shore people had lived on for thousands of years. The Canadian government set aside the 312-acre reserve in 1887, a fraction of the territory and a fraction of what was needed. Today the band counts roughly a hundred members, about half of them living on the reserve - which means that for much of the past century, more non-Indigenous residents have lived on Semiahmoo land than Semiahmoo have.

## Half the Reserve, Buried

In 1942, the band leased 172 acres - more than half the reserve - to the Municipality of Surrey. The stated purpose was recreational parkland, and the maps duly labelled it Semiahmoo Park. What Surrey and White Rock actually put there was landfill and municipal infrastructure. The lease ran until 1996. When the land came back, it came back contaminated, and in 2014 the First Nation announced it was taking on the remediation itself. Consider the arithmetic of that: a nation of fewer than a hundred people, holding one of the most valuable waterfront parcels in the Lower Mainland, spending decades unable to use half of it because two cities had used it as a dump. The fence the band raised along its eastern boundary in 2010, unpopular with beach-walking neighbours, reads differently against that history.

## Sixteen Years of Boiling Water

The water problem was not obscure. Ottawa listed the Semiahmoo reserve as a high-priority community for drinking-water improvements in 2006 and promised help. Nothing arrived that fixed it. In 2015, after White Rock signalled it would end the connection that had been feeding the reserve, the band turned to Surrey instead. A deal came together in 2018, construction followed, and the pipes were finished in early 2021. When the advisory lifted that March, it had been in force for the whole childhood of anyone under sixteen on the reserve. Water is the least dramatic infrastructure there is, right up until you cannot drink it.

## The Long Table

Chief Harley Chappell has a line he uses about the fence and the neighbours and the border: people say fences make good neighbours, he says, but what is really needed is a long table. The nation now runs its own development corporation and has pushed plans for hospitality and conference development on the reserve near the crossing. The Haida artist Robert Davidson has long kept a studio here. Students at Earl Marriott Secondary in Surrey organize a powwow the First Nation sponsors. And for minor offences within the community, Semiahmoo uses an apology ceremony rather than a courtroom: the person who caused harm apologizes to those affected, gives a gift, and cooks a meal, with elders, council, and community present. Restitution, then food, then everyone at the same table. The metaphor is not accidental.

## Flight Context

Semiahmoo Indian Reserve lies at 49.0000°N, 122.7667°W, on the north shore of Semiahmoo Bay directly against the Canada-United States boundary. Best viewed at 2,500-4,000 ft AGL on a southbound or eastbound leg. Navigation landmarks are unusually clear here: the Peace Arch monument sits on Highway 99 at the border crossing immediately east of the reserve, the BNSF rail line traces the waterfront in a continuous curve, the Little Campbell River estuary marks the reserve's eastern edge, and Semiahmoo Spit reaches out from Blaine, Washington, across the bay to the south. White Rock's pier and its namesake boulder are a short hop northwest along the beach. Nearby airports: CZBB Boundary Bay roughly 20 km west, KBLI Bellingham International about 35 km south across the border, CYXX Abbotsford some 40 km east, and CYVR Vancouver International 35 km northwest. The international boundary runs along the reserve's southern limit - know your airspace before descending. Summer mornings often bring marine stratus off the Salish Sea that burns off by midday; winter brings low ceilings and long visibility beneath them.
