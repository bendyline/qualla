# Semiahmoo Spit

A mile-and-a-quarter finger of drifted sand where a cannery town, an ancient village, and an international border all crowd onto the same narrow beach.

**Location:** 48.9846, -122.7816

**Categories:** Landforms of Whatcom County, Washington, Headlands of Washington (state), Coastal geography, Indigenous history, Fishing and canning history

## Introduction

The bank along the beach is white. Not sand-white but shell-white, a packed seam of crushed clam and mussel running for yards through the cut bank where winter surf has chewed into the shore. This is midden, the compacted kitchen record of people who lived on Semiahmoo Spit for thousands of years, and the sea keeps opening it like a book nobody asked to have read aloud. A mile to the north, an invisible line crosses the water and becomes an international border. Four miles northeast, the apartment towers of White Rock, British Columbia, catch the afternoon light. In between lies a strip of drifted gravel barely wide enough for a road, holding back a harbour.

## Built Grain by Grain

A spit is an argument between wind and rock, settled slowly. Waves rolling down the Strait of Georgia strike this shoreline obliquely, at roughly thirty to forty-five degrees, and every breaking wave nudges sediment a little farther northeast. Geologists call the process longshore drift. What it means in practice is a conveyor belt: cobbles and gravel drop out first, close to the headland, because heavy material needs energy to move and the waves are spending theirs. Sand travels farther. Silt and clay travel farthest of all, settling in the quiet water behind. Repeat for a few thousand years and you get Semiahmoo Spit, about 1.25 miles long and roughly 125 acres in all, curving out from the Semiahmoo Peninsula to shelter Drayton Harbor from the open strait. The spit is not a permanent feature so much as a slow-moving one, a pile of sediment currently passing through.

## The City That Never Arrived

In 1858, word of gold on the Fraser River emptied San Francisco northward, and thousands of prospectors needed a way inland that did not run through the British authorities at Victoria. Semiahmoo looked promising. A trading post went up on the spit as a jumping-off point for the mainland, and its owner did what optimists did in 1858: he drew up a street plan and named the place Semiahmoo City. The boom never landed. The prospectors found other routes, the lots went unsold, and the spit settled instead into the work the water actually offered. Sheltered anchorage, a harbour at its back, salmon running past its tip - the town that failed as a gateway succeeded as a fishing village.

## The Cannery Years

Whatcom County's first salmon cannery opened at Semiahmoo in 1881, and canning became the spit's economy for very nearly a century. By the 1890s the operation here, run under the Alaska Packers Association, was reckoned among the largest salmon canneries anywhere in the world. Photographs from 1917 and 1918 show the reality behind that superlative: rows of workers at filling machines, the separate quarters where Indigenous and Chinese crews were housed apart from the rest, the long low sheds pressed against the water. Canning ended in 1980, when a development corporation bought the land. Some of the cannery structures survived the transition. One of them now houses a beach activities centre for the resort that opened here, which is a strange afterlife for a building that once smelled permanently of fish.

## What the Water Gave Back

Long before any of that, a major Coast Salish village stood on this spit - a place chosen for the same reasons everyone else eventually chose it, with the calm of Drayton Harbor on one side and the currents of the strait on the other. A burial ground lay here too, and it may be 4,500 years old. As many as three in ten Lummi people are estimated to have relatives buried in it. The City of Blaine nevertheless used the spit for industry and dumping for decades, and in 1999 a contractor building a sewage plant carted away tons of material containing bones and belongings; some of it turned up as far away as Colorado. A federal lawsuit ended in 2004 with the Lummi Nation receiving $3.5 million in compensation from the contractor and title to two acres of the spit. The land transfer was completed in 2017, more than twenty years after the disturbance began. Walk the eroding bank today and the midden is still there, still surfacing - evidence, and also a graveyard.

## Flight Context

Semiahmoo Spit lies at 48.9846 N, 122.7816 W, hooking northeast from the Semiahmoo Peninsula. Best viewed between 2,000 and 4,000 feet AGL, where the spit's curve and the enclosed water of Drayton Harbor read clearly as a single form. Look for the bright arc of beach separating the darker harbour from the open Strait of Georgia, with the Semiahmoo resort buildings and golf course on the peninsula behind. The Canada-United States border crosses the water one nautical mile north, with White Rock's waterfront and pier four miles northeast. Bellingham International (KBLI) is roughly 20 nm southeast; Abbotsford International (CYXX) lies about 30 nm east, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 30 nm northwest. Marine fog is common on summer mornings and can hide the spit entirely while the peninsula behind stays clear; afternoon westerlies through the strait often bring excellent visibility to the North Cascades.
