# Blaine, Washington

A town of fewer than six thousand people sits where Interstate 5 runs out of country, guarding a monument to peace and a border that has always leaked in both directions.

**Location:** 48.9881, -122.7440

**Categories:** Cities in Washington (state), Border towns, Maritime history, Pacific Northwest, Cities in Whatcom County, Washington

## Introduction

On May 9, 1970, roughly 450 Canadians walked south across the international line and staged what they called a symbolic invasion of Blaine. They came to protest the Cambodian campaign of the Vietnam War. Windows were broken, flags torn down, and the Peace Arch itself defaced before police and local volunteers pushed the crowd back toward Canada. The choice of target says something about this town of fewer than six thousand people. When you want to make a point about the United States and you happen to live in Vancouver, Blaine is the nearest available piece of it. The city's northern boundary is not near the border. It is the border.

## A Gate With No Fence

Samuel Hill dedicated the Peace Arch on September 6, 1921, a white concrete gateway planted squarely on the line between two countries. Around it grew something stranger than a monument: a park split down the middle, Peace Arch State Park on the Washington side, Peace Arch Provincial Park on the British Columbia side at Douglas, within the city of Surrey. Visitors entering from either country may walk to the far edge of the other park without clearing customs, wandering among flowerbeds and ponds and sculpture in a nation they have not technically entered. That arrangement survived the hardening of the border after 2001 untouched. Couples marry here. Nearby runs the only pedestrian crosswalk in the United States that crosses an Interstate freeway, because I-5 simply ends here and has to be walked across. Since 1937, Scouts from both countries have carried world flags through the arch at the Hands Across the Border celebration, which organizers cancelled in 2013 and public pressure restored in 2015.

## Salmon, Shingles, and a Boat Named Plover

Before it was a border town, Blaine was a working waterfront. The Alaska Packers' Association ran what was billed as the world's largest salmon cannery out on Semiahmoo Spit, and sawmills lined the shore, shipping lumber from Blaine's wharves to help rebuild San Francisco after the earthquake and fire of 1906. The forests went fast; the fish lasted longer. Into the 1970s, hundreds of purse seiners and gillnetters worked out of here, chasing salmon north along the British Columbia coast toward southeast Alaska. One survivor of that era still runs. The Plover, an eleven-ton wooden launch built in Seattle in 1944, carried cannery workers across Drayton Harbor until 1964, was rescued by volunteers, and earned a preservation award from Washington's Department of Archaeology and Historic Preservation in 1998. It now carries seventeen passengers at a time on summer weekends, about a kilometre each way, over water where the cannery site is a resort.

## The Traffic Both Ways

A border is a price difference with a fence around it, and Blaine has spent a century watching goods find the gap. When the Volstead Act dried out the United States in 1919, rum-runners worked this coastline hard, supplied in part by a large whiskey still on Texada Island up in the Strait of Georgia. Repeal arrived in 1933 through a congressional resolution called the Blaine Act, named not for this town or for James G. Blaine, its namesake senator from Maine, but for John J. Blaine of Wisconsin. Coincidences like that seem to cluster here. After Prohibition the current reversed, and British Columbia's restrictive liquor rules, including a ban on Sunday drinking, filled Blaine's taverns with thirsty Canadians. In the 1990s the cargo changed again: high-grade marijuana moving south by backpack and helicopter drop, cash, cocaine and handguns moving north. Enforcement after 2001 pushed most of that trade east into the mountains.

## Where America Begins

The city motto is "Where America Begins," which is either civic boosterism or a plain statement of geography depending on your mood. Blaine covers 8.62 square miles, more than a third of it water, wedged between Drayton Harbor and Semiahmoo Bay. On a clear day the view runs from Mount Baker and the North Cascades in the east to the southern tip of Vancouver Island in the west. The climate sits on the boundary between Mediterranean and oceanic, and with about forty inches of rain a year Blaine is drier and sunnier than much of the sodden country around it. The birds noticed first. Drayton Harbor, Semiahmoo Spit and Boundary Bay are all ranked as Important Bird Areas by the Audubon Society, and the tideflats fill each winter with waterfowl that recognize no line at all. The town's own airport closed at the end of 2008. The birds kept their flight paths.

## Flight Context

Blaine sits at 48.988 N, 122.744 W, at the exact point where Interstate 5 stops. Approach from the south at 2,500 to 4,000 feet AGL for the clearest read of the geography: the freeway runs dead straight north, widens into the inspection lanes of the Peace Arch crossing, and simply ends. The white Peace Arch is a small bright rectangle in the green strip of parkland immediately west of the lanes. Drayton Harbor is the near-circular bay southwest of town, closed off by the long pale hook of Semiahmoo Spit, which is the single most recognizable feature from the air and the best landmark for locating Blaine from twenty miles out. Boundary Bay opens to the northwest. Mount Baker stands 30 nautical miles east-southeast as an unmistakable snow reference. Note that Blaine's own airport (4W6) closed on December 31, 2008 and is no longer usable. Nearest fields are Bellingham International (KBLI) about 18 nm south, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 10 nm northwest in Delta, British Columbia, Abbotsford International (CYXX) about 27 nm east-northeast, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 22 nm north-northwest. Class B and restricted cross-border airspace complicate direct routing here; the international boundary is not a suggestion. Marine haze and low stratus are common from late spring through summer, often burning off by midday, while October through March brings frequent low ceilings and rain.
