# Lily Point Marine Reserve

On a bluff at the edge of a stranded piece of America, the richest reef net fishery on the Salish Sea survived a cannery, a golf course, and the border itself.

**Location:** 48.9775, -123.0263

**Categories:** Protected areas of Whatcom County, Washington, Coast Salish history, Marine reserves, Salmon fisheries, Point Roberts

## Introduction

Three nets, six hours, ten thousand salmon. That was one reported haul off these bluffs in 1881, and nobody at the time thought it extraordinary. Lily Point occupies the southeast corner of Point Roberts, a scrap of the United States marooned on the wrong side of the Canadian border, and its value has never had much to do with which flag flies over it. Every summer, sockeye returning to spawn in the Fraser River sweep around this headland in numbers that have fed families, funded canneries, and outlasted both. The bluffs are eroding. The pilings are rotting. The fish still come.

## An Accident of the 49th Parallel

The Oregon Treaty, concluded on June 15, 1846, ran the boundary between British and American territory west along the 49th parallel to the Pacific. Drawn on a map, the line looked clean. On the ground it sliced across the Tsawwassen peninsula and left its southern tip attached to nothing American at all. When British negotiators understood the problem, Captain James Prevost was instructed to ask that the fragment be ceded back. It wasn't. Point Roberts stayed in the United States, reachable from the rest of Washington only by a twenty-five mile drive through Canada, by boat, or by small aircraft. Lily Point claims 275 acres of that oddity: mature upland forest, steep feeder cliffs that shed sand onto the beach below, and 1.4 miles of shoreline bending east into Boundary Bay.

## Chelhtenem

The Coast Salish name for this place is Chelhtenem, which translates as "hang salmon for drying" - a name describing labor rather than scenery. That labor was reef netting, and it was a technology of real sophistication. An artificial reef suspended between two anchored canoes guided migrating salmon upward, and the net was lifted at the instant a school passed over it. Net locations were inherited, and the best of them were worth defending. In 1889, sixteen reef nets worked the water off Lily Point, and a single net could take as many as two thousand fish in a day. This was the most important reef net fishery of the Central Coast Salish. In 1994, Chelhtenem was entered on the National Register of Historic Places as a site of national cultural, traditional and spiritual significance - only the second place in Washington State to receive that designation.

## Cans and Pilings

Industrial fishing arrived with a cannery built on the point in 1883 and sold the following year to new owners, who ran it through the boom decades of the Fraser sockeye runs. Fish traps - enormous stationary nets strung from pilings driven into the seabed - intercepted the same salmon the reef nets had always worked, at a scale the older fishery was never built to match. The cannery shut down in 1917. What it left behind is still on the beach: rows of blackened pilings marching seaward from the gravel, exposed at low tide, and scattered debris that the bay has been slowly dismantling for more than a century.

## The Golf Course That Wasn't

Lily Point came close to disappearing a second time, and recently. After Welsh Development, Inc. acquired the property, Whatcom County approved a plan that would have covered it with condominiums, a golf course, 74 residential lots, and a regional recreational resort. Then, for reasons never publicly explained, the developer let the permits lapse in 2003 without turning a shovel. The land went back on the market. In 2008 Whatcom County bought it, with The Nature Conservancy and the Whatcom Land Trust helping carry the cost, and opened it as a park and marine reserve. Standing under the second-growth canopy above the bluffs, it is hard not to think about how narrowly all of this survived a missed paperwork deadline.

## What the Bay Keeps

Walk the switchbacks down to the beach and the argument for conservation makes itself. Bald eagles work the treeline. Herons stalk the tideflats, loons ride the chop offshore, and clams and crabs keep the intertidal busy. The eroding cliffs are not a defect but the mechanism - they resupply the beach with the coarse sediment that forage fish need for spawning, and those forage fish feed juvenile Chinook. Boundary Bay, whose ecological health is tied directly to this shoreline, is recognized as an Important Bird Area by BirdLife International, as a Western Hemisphere Shorebird Reserve Network site, and as a Ramsar wetland of international importance for waterfowl. Chelhtenem has changed remarkably little. The landforms, the vegetation, and the water that drew people to this corner are still doing exactly the work they always did.

## Flight Context

Lily Point sits at 48.9775 N, 123.0263 W, on the southeast corner of the Point Roberts peninsula where it meets Boundary Bay. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet AGL the geography reads instantly: the squared-off tip of the Tsawwassen peninsula, the razor-straight 49th parallel border cut running east-west across it, and the sharp forested bluff line dropping to a pale gravel beach. At low tide the cannery pilings show as a dark comb angling off the shore. Boundary Bay opens to the east as a broad shallow bowl, and the Tsawwassen ferry terminal causeway makes an unmistakable pointer to the northwest. Nearby airports: Boundary Bay (CZBB) roughly 10 nm northeast in Delta, BC; Vancouver International (CYVR) about 17 nm north; Bellingham International (KBLI) about 25 nm southeast across the bay. Summer mornings often bring marine layer cloud into the strait that burns off by midday; winter southeasters drive spray up the cliff face and make the beach approach unwise.
