# MV Plover

An eleven-ton wooden launch built in 1944 to carry cannery workers across Drayton Harbor, saved by volunteers and still running what may be the shortest ferry route in Washington.

**Location:** 48.9939, -122.7586

**Categories:** Ferries of Washington (state), Ships on the National Register of Historic Places in Washington (state), National Register of Historic Places in Whatcom County, Washington, Maritime history, Historic preservation

## Introduction

Seventeen passengers is the entire manifest. That is the legal capacity of the Plover, an eleven-ton wooden launch that has been crossing Drayton Harbor since 1944, covering roughly a kilometre of water between the Blaine harbor dock and the tip of Semiahmoo Spit. You can see the far dock from the near one. The crossing takes minutes, and it is claimed to be the shortest ferry run in Washington — a claim that sounds less like a boast than an apology until you understand what the boat was for. The Plover was never built to cover distance. She was built to get people to work, twice a day, in whatever the strait was doing that morning.

## Shift Change at the Cannery

Semiahmoo Spit curls a mile and a quarter into the water off Blaine, and for the better part of a century it was a factory. The Alaska Packers' Association ran a salmon cannery out there, an operation large enough to dominate Blaine's economy for decades and to pull labour from every direction. The trouble was geography: by road, the spit lies far around the head of the harbor. By water, it is barely a stone's throw. So in 1944, Bryants Marina of Seattle built a small ferry to close that gap. Every working day the Plover pushed off from Blaine loaded with people carrying lunch pails, ran across, and pushed off again. She did that until 1964, when the crossing stopped and the boat went quiet. The cannery itself outlasted her by a decade and a half; the land was sold to a development company in 1980, and a golf resort grew where the fish houses had been.

## The Spit Remembers Longer

None of that industry was the beginning. Long before the first cannery, a major Lummi village stood on Semiahmoo Spit, positioned exactly where anyone who understood salmon would put it. Whatcom County's first salmon cannery opened there in 1881, and the village site was taken for the works. The evidence has not gone anywhere. Wave action keeps cutting into both shores of the spit, and shell midden — the accumulated refuse of generations of people who lived and ate and buried their dead here — is visible in the exposed banks in many places. To the Lummi Nation the spit is not a former industrial parcel or a resort address but a burial ground of considerable antiquity, and that claim has outlasted both the fish houses and the boats that served them.

## A Hull Worth Saving

Boats of the Plover's size and vintage usually end as firewood or fill. This one did not, because a group of people in Whatcom County decided it should not. Volunteers from the Whatcom Maritime Historical Society took her on, and the restoration was thorough enough that the work itself became the story. The National Register of Historic Places listed the vessel on 4 June 1997. The following year, the Washington State Department of Archaeology and Historic Preservation gave the project a Valerie Sivinski Award, citing it as a demonstration of "the best of preservation practice" — a phrase worth noticing, because it is aimed at how the boat was repaired rather than at what she once did. Restoration of a working wooden hull is an argument with time that never quite concludes. Somebody has to keep making it.

## Second Oldest, Shortest Run

The Plover is owned today by the City of Blaine and operated by the nonprofit Drayton Harbor Maritime, running passengers across during the summer months from the harbor dock to the resort dock. She is the second-oldest operating foot-passenger ferry in Washington; the title belongs to the Carlisle II, built in Bellingham in 1917, twenty-seven years before the Plover slid into the water. Two survivors from an era when small boats were simply how you got from one side of the water to the other. The ride is unhurried and slightly absurd — a ferry with a route shorter than some airport concourses, crewed by volunteers, carrying tourists to a golf course across a harbor that once smelled overwhelmingly of fish. It is also the only way to make the crossing the way the cannery workers made it, which is a rarer thing to be able to buy a ticket for than it sounds.

## The Line on the Map

Blaine is the last city on Interstate 5 before the pavement runs out at Canada; the city's northern boundary is the international border itself, marked by the Peace Arch that was dedicated in 1921. Fewer than six thousand people live here. From the water, the border is invisible, and Drayton Harbor behaves as one body regardless of which flag is flying on the bluff. The Plover crosses none of it — she stays entirely inside the harbor — but the geography explains the town: a working port that ended up on the wrong side of a line for some purposes and the right side for others, with a small wooden boat still shuttling back and forth inside it.

## Flight Context

Blaine Harbor sits at 48.9939° N, 122.7586° W, immediately south of the Canada–United States border on Washington's northwest coast. Fly the harbor at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL: the shape is unmistakable from the air, with Semiahmoo Spit reaching a mile and a quarter northeast to enclose Drayton Harbor, the Blaine marina tucked at the inner shore, and the Peace Arch and the I-5 border crossing visible less than a mile north. The Plover's route is the short hop across the harbor mouth between the two dock complexes. Bellingham International (KBLI) lies about 18 nm southeast and is the practical base for the area; Abbotsford (CYXX) is roughly 20 nm northeast across the border, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 27 nm northwest.
