# White Rock

A granite boulder dropped by a glacier gave this Semiahmoo Bay beach town its name, its first navigation beacon, and its standing monthly appointment with a paint crew.

**Location:** 49.0209, -122.8037

**Categories:** city, british-columbia, coastal, beaches, indigenous-history, geology

## Introduction

For most of its life, the rock was painted by birds. The granite boulder that gave White Rock its name sits at the tide line below the promenade, and nineteenth-century sailors crossing Semiahmoo Bay steered by it because seabird guano kept it bright enough to work as a beacon. The birds have since lost that job to a paint crew. City parks staff now whiten the 486-ton boulder roughly once a month, covering whatever has been sprayed onto it since the last coat. It is a piece of the last Ice Age on permanent touch-up duty at the edge of the Salish Sea.

## Carried Here by Ice

The rock does not belong here, and that is the point of it. It is a glacial erratic - a slab of granite prised out of bedrock somewhere to the north, locked into the base of an advancing ice sheet, and hauled south through the last glaciation until the ice thinned and let it go. What the glacier abandoned on this shoreline is, by a wide margin, the largest thing on the beach.

Stand beside it at low tide and the number stops being abstract. Everything else here is in motion. Sandbars shift with each winter storm. The tidal pools fill and drain twice a day. The town on the bluff above has rebuilt itself several times over in a century. The rock has not gone anywhere since the ice released it, which makes it the one fixed point in a landscape otherwise defined entirely by tides.

## SEMYOME

Long before any sailor used the boulder as a landmark, this shoreline belonged to the Straits Salish. The Semiahmoo First Nation - SEMYOME in their own language - kept encampments along the bay across the whole span between 1791, the year of first European contact on this coast, and the 1850s, when settlement began in earnest.

What survives of that territory in law is small: a reserve of 312 acres pressed between the city and the international boundary, next to Peace Arch Provincial Park. In 2021 the nation counted 101 members, about half of them living on reserve. That same year brought something the surrounding suburbs had never thought about. A boil-water advisory that had stood since 2005 - sixteen years of not drinking from the tap, a few kilometres from downtown Surrey - was lifted on 31 March 2021, after the City of Surrey connected the reserve to municipal water and sewer.

## The Railway Made a Resort

White Rock became a destination because a railway needed a route. The Great Northern laid its track along the waterfront, and by the early 1900s it was delivering day-trippers from Vancouver and New Westminster to a beach that had been, until then, mostly empty. A station opened in 1913. The pier followed in 1914, built to give deep-water vessels somewhere to tie up, and almost immediately became the thing people came here to walk on.

The pattern set then has never really broken. Vancouverites still drive down on hot weekends, the sand still fills, and the restaurants along Marine Drive still trade on the view. The town itself took far longer to catch up. White Rock was not incorporated as a city until 15 April 1957, and it remains compact - 21,939 people at the 2021 census, hemmed in on three sides by Surrey and on the fourth by the water.

## The Night the Boats Came Loose

In December 2018, a windstorm drove hard into Semiahmoo Bay. Sailboats moored at the marina attached to the pier broke free, and the wind pushed them into the structure until a thirty-metre section of it was simply gone. Photographs taken the next morning show the walkway stopping mid-span, open water where the deck used to run.

Rebuilding took most of a year. The pier reopened on 28 August 2019, and people turned out for it - which tells you what the thing means to a town that has been walking out over the water on it since 1914. Nobody thinks of it as infrastructure. It is the local ritual, performed by nearly everyone who comes: out to the end, turn around, come back, preferably at low tide with the sun going down behind Point Roberts.

## Sea Level and the Bench

White Rock is really two towns stacked awkwardly on one another. Marine Drive and the beach sit at sea level, while the rest of the city occupies a bench several hundred feet above - which is why the trip between them looks trivial on a map and feels like a stair climb in practice.

Between the promenade and the town runs the rail line, still busy with freight, and it demands genuine attention. People distracted by headphones have been struck and killed walking on or across these tracks; the warning signs along the beach are not decorative. Past them, the promenade runs 2.2 kilometres along the sand. Because Vancouver Island shelters this whole stretch of coast, the water is warmer than the latitude has any right to deliver. When the tide pulls back, the flats open, the tidal pools appear, and the rock sits exactly where it has always sat, waiting for its next coat of paint.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.02N, 122.80W on Semiahmoo Bay, at the southwestern corner of mainland British Columbia. The town is unmistakable from the air: a narrow strip of waterfront at sea level backed by a steep forested bench, with the pier projecting south into the bay and the long curve of the beach running east toward Semiahmoo Park. The Canada-United States border lies barely two kilometres south, marked by the cleared boundary vista and the Peace Arch crossing on Highway 99. Boundary Bay opens to the west, Mount Baker dominates the southeastern horizon on clear days, and Point Roberts sits across the water to the southwest. Vancouver International (CYVR) is 35 km northwest; Abbotsford International (CYXX) 45 km east; Boundary Bay Airport (CZBB) 20 km west in Delta; Bellingham International (KBLI) 35 km south across the border in Washington. Best viewing altitude is 2,000-4,000 feet, low enough to pick out the pier and the promenade. Marine fog is common on summer mornings and can hide the shoreline entirely until mid-morning burn-off.
