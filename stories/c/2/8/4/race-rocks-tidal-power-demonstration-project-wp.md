# Race Rocks Tidal Power Demonstration Project

North America's first in-stream tidal generator spun beneath a granite lighthouse in one of the fiercest tidal races on the coast — and the same water that powered it ground its bearings apart.

**Location:** 48.2990, -123.5310

**Categories:** Energy infrastructure completed in 2006, Tidal power stations in British Columbia, 2006 establishments in British Columbia, 2011 disestablishments in British Columbia

## Introduction

The rocks are named for what the water does to you. Tides funnelling between Vancouver Island and the Olympic Peninsula squeeze through this cluster of low islets and accelerate to six, seven, eight knots — fast enough that small boats time their crossings to the minute, fast enough that there is barely a window in the day when the water here is doing less than three. In 2006 a consortium looked at that and saw a fuel supply. The turbine they lowered onto the seabed was the first in-stream tidal current generator in North America, and the story of what happened to it is a good lesson in the difference between an obvious idea and a working machine.

## Eighty Feet of Local Granite

People had been trying to survive this place long before anyone tried to harvest it. The British Admiralty ordered a lighthouse here after a run of shipwrecks, and on Boxing Day of 1860 the light was lit for the first time from an 80-foot tower built of granite shipped from Scotland and topped with sandstone quarried on nearby Gabriola Island. Keepers lived out there, on rock, in weather, until 1997 — the last of them among the final generation of staffed light stations on this coast. Pearson College took over management of the station and the surrounding marine protected area after they left. The waters had already become Ecological Reserve No. 97 in 1980, two years after Pearson College students started it as a marine science project. Harbour seals, California and Steller sea lions, and a growing colony of elephant seals haul out on the same rocks, in numbers loud enough to hear from a long way off.

## Sixty-Five Kilowatts and Two Diesels

The official name was a mouthful — the Pearson College – EnCana – Clean Current Tidal Power Demonstration Project at Race Rocks — and it bundled a college, an energy company, and a Canadian engineering firm into one experiment. The goal was modest and concrete: replace the two diesel generators that kept the light station running. Diesel on an offshore rock means fuel barged in, spills to worry about, noise in a marine reserve, and exhaust where nothing else makes any. The machine installed between July and September 2006 was a 65-kilowatt direct-drive, variable-speed permanent-magnet generator inside a bi-directional ducted horizontal-axis turbine — a shrouded rotor designed to produce power on both the flood and the ebb — set on the bottom at a depth of 19 to 22 metres.

## The Bearings Gave Out

It worked, and then it did not. The water-lubricated bearing system — elegant in principle, since seawater is free and there is no oil to leak into a protected reserve — did not perform as expected under the relentless loading of a current that reverses four times a day and never truly rests. By May 2007 the prototype was decommissioned so the bearings could be redesigned. Clean Current took it apart, reworked the bearings, the shroud, and the turbine itself, and put the unit back on the seabed on 17 October 2008. It ran on. Then, on 17 September 2011, the turbine and generator were pulled up for the last time and the project ended.

## What a Failed Prototype Is Worth

Five years, two installations, one permanent removal — judged as a power plant, that is not a success. Judged as what it actually was, a demonstration project, it is something else. Nobody in North America had put an in-stream tidal generator into real water before, and the thing the engineers learned is precisely the thing you cannot learn from a model: that the failure mode in a place like this is not the rotor or the generator but the humble, unglamorous bearing. Tidal energy's problem has never been whether the resource exists. Race Rocks moves an extraordinary amount of water past a fixed point, twice a day, forever, with a predictability no wind farm can match. The problem is building something that survives being in it. The light still turns above the reef, the sea lions still argue on the rocks, and the seabed where the turbine sat is quiet again — a small, honest monument to an experiment that answered its question.

## Flight Context

Race Rocks lies at 48.299°N, 123.531°W in the Strait of Juan de Fuca, roughly 10 nautical miles southwest of Victoria and about a kilometre and a half off Vancouver Island's Rocky Point. View it from 1,500 to 3,000 feet on a coastal track — low enough to pick out individual islets, seals on the rock, and the black-and-white banded tower that is the single most recognizable navigation mark on this stretch of water. The 80-foot granite lighthouse is unmistakable. Look for standing waves, overfalls, and visible current lines streaming off the reef on a big tide; they are dramatic enough to see clearly from the air and are the entire reason the project chose this site. William Head and Pedder Bay sit just to the northeast, Sooke to the west, and the Olympic Mountains fill the southern horizon across the strait. Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 20 nautical miles northeast, Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) is closer for floatplanes, and William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) at Port Angeles lies directly across on the Washington shore. Expect strong westerlies funnelling down the strait, summer fog banks that can close the area within minutes, and considerable turbulence near the shoreline on windy afternoons.
