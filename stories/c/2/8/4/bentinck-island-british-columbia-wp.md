# Bentinck Island, British Columbia

A 112-acre island within sight of Victoria's shipping lanes where Canada confined people with leprosy from 1924 until the last resident died in 1957.

**Location:** 48.3139, -123.5428

**Categories:** Greater Victoria, Islands of the Gulf Islands, Leper colonies, Medical and health organizations based in British Columbia, History

## Introduction

From the cottages you could watch the liners go by. Bentinck Island sits about twelve miles by sea from Victoria, a clover-leaf of rock and scrub grass at the southern edge of Vancouver Island, close enough to Race Rocks that the lighthouse is a near neighbour and close enough to the shipping lanes that freighters and passenger ships passed in plain view all day long. For thirty-three years the people living there watched that traffic and could not join it. Bentinck was Canada's Pacific leprosy station - a place built on the assumption that the humane response to an illness barely capable of spreading was to remove the people who had it and put them where the country would not have to look.

## Before Bentinck, There Was D'Arcy

The policy began in 1891 on D'Arcy Island, farther up the coast, where the men confined were Chinese immigrants. That detail is not incidental to the story; it is the story. Leprosy in late-Victorian British Columbia was treated less as a medical condition than as evidence about immigration, and the response was exile rather than treatment. The colony operated for thirty-three years, and the ruins of its buildings can still be found in the woods on D'Arcy today. In 1924 the federal government shut it down and moved the residents to Bentinck, the stated reason being proximity - Bentinck was nearer Victoria’s medical and quarantine facilities. Those facilities were close indeed: the quarantine station at William Head, which inspected and controlled everyone entering Canada for infectious disease, stood just along the same shore.

## Ten Cottages, Three Occupied

A Victoria newspaper reporter who visited in the spring of 1948 left the clearest surviving picture of the place. Ten neat, rain-washed cottages stood on the island for the use of patients, of which three were occupied. Two large blue-grey houses held the families of the nurse and the caretaker. There was a one-room hospital. There was also a modest two-room jail, which had been used exactly once, after a stabbing - an object that says a great deal about how the institution understood the people in its care. The three residents that spring were a middle-aged woman who had served as a missionary in Africa, an elderly Chinese man, and a Japanese Canadian of twenty-nine. They were not a category. They were three people on 112 acres, with a nurse, a caretaker, and a view of passing ships.

## The Eemdijk and the Hope

The island's other disaster arrived from the sea. On 15 October 1925 the Holland America cargo liner Eemdijk ran hard aground on Race Rocks, the reef just offshore whose currents and fog have wrecked ships for as long as ships have come this way. Salvage crews worked her for four days and refloated her on 19 October, beaching her on Bentinck Island to keep her from sinking. The rescue cost more than the ship was worth. The tug Hope was lost during the salvage attempt, and her seven crew went with her - men doing the ordinary, unglamorous, extremely dangerous work of getting other people's vessels off the rocks, in a strait that has never been forgiving about it.

## Ten Graves and a Closing

By 1948 ten patients had died on Bentinck and been buried in the colony’s graveyard, their graves marked with crosses. The newspaper account of that year described the dead, in the flat racial shorthand of its era, as "all Orientals" - which tells you who Canada had been confining, and how little the confining institution troubled itself to record about them as individuals. The station closed in 1957, when the last person there with leprosy died. The first effective drug, promin, had been available since the 1940s and dapsone since the 1950s; by the end, the isolation was a policy that had outlived its own medical rationale. The island is not a memorial and carries no monument. It is simply a small green shape off Metchosin, passed daily by the same ships, holding the graves of people whose names most records did not bother to keep.

## Flight Context

Bentinck Island lies at 48.3139 N, 123.5428 W, immediately off the southern tip of Vancouver Island at Metchosin, at the eastern entrance to the Strait of Juan de Fuca. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,000-3,500 ft AGL. From the air the island reads as a small, three-lobed landmass tight against the shore, with Race Rocks and its distinctive banded lighthouse tower less than 2 km southwest, the Rocky Point headland and Pedder Bay to the northeast, and William Head just east. The Olympic Peninsula fills the horizon across the strait to the south, and the main deep-sea shipping lane into Juan de Fuca Strait runs directly past. Race Rocks Lighthouse, built in 1861, is the most useful visual fix in the area. Nearest airports: CYYJ (Victoria International) about 25 km northeast, CYWH (Victoria Harbour water aerodrome) about 20 km east, and KCLM (William R. Fairchild International, Port Angeles) across the strait in Washington. Race Rocks generates heavy tidal rips and fog forms quickly here even when Victoria is clear.
