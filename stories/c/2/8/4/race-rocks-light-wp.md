# Race Rocks Light

Lit on Boxing Day 1860, Canada's westernmost stone lighthouse stands on eleven islets where the tide runs like a river, and its keepers' logbook is one long argument with the water.

**Location:** 48.2980, -123.5314

**Categories:** Lighthouses in British Columbia, Lighthouses completed in 1860, Maritime history, Marine ecology, Historic buildings and structures in British Columbia

## Introduction

On Christmas Day 1865, George and Rosina Davies stood on the rocks and waved at the skiff bringing her brother and sister-in-law out for the holiday. The boat came within about twenty feet of the jetty. Then a tide rip caught it, turned it over, and spilled the passengers and their wrapped gifts into water moving faster than anyone could swim. The light station had no boat of its own. The Davies family watched from the shore, and their visitors drowned in front of them. George Davies was the first keeper of Race Rocks Light, and he would not survive the following year either: he fell seriously ill in the winter of 1866, the station flew the Union Jack at half mast as a distress signal for nine days with no result, and he died shortly before Christmas.

## Why the Water Has a Name

Race Rocks is eleven low islets scattered off the southern tip of Vancouver Island, about 17 kilometres southwest of Victoria, sitting directly in the throat of Juan de Fuca Strait. Every tide cycle drives an enormous volume of the Pacific in and out through that gap, and where it accelerates over the shallows around these rocks it stops behaving like ocean and starts behaving like whitewater. Overfalls stand up. Eddies open and close. Boiling seams of current run for hundreds of metres. The name describes exactly what the water does. For vessels bound for Victoria and Esquimalt in the 1850s, this was the last and worst obstacle before safe harbour, which is why the British government paid for a light here.

## Scottish Granite, Gabriola Sandstone

Race Rocks Light and the smaller Fisgard light at the entrance to Esquimalt Harbour were the first two lighthouses on Canada's west coast, both financed from London. Fisgard was lit first; Race Rocks followed six weeks later, on 26 December 1860. Construction ran through 1859 and 1860, carried out by the crew of HMS Topaze alongside outside labourers hired under a contract awarded to John Morris. What they raised is unique on this coast: the only lighthouse here built of stone, its granite reportedly quarried in Scotland, capped with sandstone from Gabriola Island. The tower stands 24.4 metres, a cylinder painted in bold black and white bands so it reads against the grey water in daylight. It flashes white every ten seconds. Its foghorn gives three blasts at one-minute intervals.

## Eighteen Names

Between 1861 and 1997 the station had eighteen principal keepers, and reading the list is a compressed history of the coast. Thomas Argyle held the post for twenty-two years before retiring in 1888, then handed it to Albert Argyle for a year. W. P. Daykin lasted barely a year before transferring to Carmanah, which on this coast counts as a lateral move. Frederick Mercer Eastwood served twenty-eight years, from 1891 to 1919, which is a long time to live on five acres of rock with the horn going. Trevor Anderson put in sixteen years from 1966. Mike Slater was the last, leaving in 1997 when the light went automatic. The names are the whole record of what the job was: a family, a tower, a fixed schedule, and a great deal of weather.

## Handed to a School

When Canada automated Race Rocks in 1997, the station could easily have become another abandoned tower with a solar panel bolted to it. Instead the Canadian Coast Guard kept ownership and leased the station to Pearson College UWC, an international school on the shore nearby, which took over managing both the light and the surrounding Race Rocks Ecological Reserve. Students and staff have run the place since, treating the rocks as a working field station. The interior and exterior of the historic tower were restored in 2009, and the college has built an endowment specifically to keep paying for its upkeep. It is an unusual arrangement and a durable one, and it means a nineteenth-century navigational aid is now maintained largely by teenagers with an interest in marine biology.

## The Reserve

British Columbia protected the islets and the seabed around them as an ecological reserve in 1980, recognising that the same violent current that killed sailors also makes this one of the richest patches of water on the coast. The constant flow delivers a perpetual meal, and the result is a vertical wall of life: anemones, sponges, and rockfish below the surface, and above it a haulout crowded with Steller and California sea lions, harbour seals, and northern elephant seals, the bulls bellowing at each other through the fog. In 2010 the lighthouse and Fisgard both turned 150. One hundred and fifty years after George Davies watched his relatives go under, the same tide is still running, and the tower is still telling ships where it is.

## Flight Context

Race Rocks Light stands at 48.298N, 123.531W, on a cluster of eleven low islets in Juan de Fuca Strait roughly 17 km southwest of Victoria and about 1 nm off Rocky Point on Vancouver Island's southern tip. From the air it is easy to identify: a bold black-and-white banded stone tower 80 feet high on bare rock, with visible tide rips, overfalls, and current seams streaking the water around it even in light winds. Use the Victoria waterfront and the Esquimalt naval base to the northeast, and the Olympic Peninsula's mountain wall across the strait to the south, as your larger anchors. Victoria International Airport (CYYJ) lies about 20nm north, Victoria Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) about 12nm northeast, and William R. Fairchild International (KCLM) at Port Angeles roughly 18nm southeast across the international boundary. Best viewed at 1,000 to 2,500 feet. The strait generates fog on short notice and funnels strong westerlies in summer afternoons; the ecological reserve below is a protected marine area and a major pinniped haulout, so keep altitude and avoid low passes.
