# Point Roberts–Boundary Bay Border Crossing

The westernmost land crossing in the contiguous United States, and the only road into an American exclave that a 1846 treaty stranded on the wrong side of the line.

**Location:** 49.0022, -123.0681

**Categories:** Canada–United States border crossings, 1914 establishments in British Columbia, 1934 establishments in Washington (state), Exclaves, Whatcom County, Washington

## Introduction

A ninth grader living in Point Roberts crosses an international border four times before dinner. The bus leaves as early as 6:25 in the morning, exits the United States here at Tyee Drive, runs east across British Columbia, re-enters the United States at Blaine, and does the whole thing again in reverse that afternoon — more than eighty kilometres round trip, two countries, four inspections, to attend an American high school in the state you already live in. This crossing is the only road in or out of Point Roberts, and it is the westernmost land border crossing in the contiguous United States. Everything odd about the place traces back to one line drawn a long way from here.

## An Accident of 1846

The Oregon Treaty settled a long dispute by running the boundary along the 49th parallel to the Pacific. On a map that looks clean. On the ground it sliced straight across the narrow base of a peninsula hanging down from what is now Tsawwassen, leaving roughly five square miles of the United States dangling below the line with Canada across its only land approach. Nobody set out to create an exclave; the negotiators were working quickly and the local geography did not much interest them. The result is a piece of Washington State that touches no other piece of Washington State, reachable by road only by leaving the country, and by water only across Boundary Bay or the Strait of Georgia.

## Horseback, and a Canvas Tent

Formal control came slowly on both sides. Canada opened a customs office at Boundary Bay in 1914, and for years the job belonged to one man — E.T. Calvert, working out of his own house about two miles north of the line, covering the district by boat and on horseback because travel here happened along the shore rather than on any road. Purpose-built offices went up in 1935, one on the main road and another 2.2 kilometres east at Beach Road. The post was answering to New Westminster until 1948, when it was upgraded to the Port of Boundary Bay in its own right. The American side was thinner still. Customs officers came out to Point Roberts only occasionally in the early 1900s, and even after 1919 the visits ran to two or three a month, conducted from a canvas tent. A two-room wooden building finally went up at the border in 1934 and was rented to the government; Walter "Pa" Davis became the first Deputy Collector in 1936; and the United States did not actually buy the building and its one-acre site until 1941.

## Growing Into It

From there the story is a slow accumulation of lanes and rooms. The Beach Road post on the Canadian side closed permanently in 1975 — its heritage building survives, the interior since converted into a dental office — and the same year the Americans stopped intermittently staffing their own eastward post at Meadow Lane. Canada widened the main approach from one lane to two in 1977. The United States enlarged its building and added two approach lanes in 1979, one dedicated to trucks, then added a lobby and a search room in 1987, and opened the present facility in 1998. Both the CBSA and the American port now run twenty-four hours a day, which matters more than it sounds: for the people who live here, a closed border is a locked front door.

## Everything Arrives Sealed

Supplying an American town through a foreign country requires a specific legal fiction. Commercial goods moving between the US mainland and Point Roberts travel by truck under customs seals — loaded and sealed on one side, driven across British Columbia without the cargo being considered imported, and broken open on the other. Groceries, fuel, building materials, and mail all make that trip. It works smoothly enough that residents stop noticing, right up until something disrupts it, at which point the fragility of the arrangement becomes the only topic of conversation in town.

## Nineteen Months

In March 2020 the land border closed to non-essential travel, and Point Roberts discovered exactly how much of itself was on the other side. Roughly 85 percent of the local economy vanished more or less overnight. Businesses cut full-time-equivalent positions by 72 percent in aggregate, and 83 percent of surveyed firms saw monthly revenues fall by half or more. Before the pandemic the community had been receiving something like 1.5 million visitors a year, nearly all of them Canadians driving twenty minutes down from Metro Vancouver. The 2020 census counted 1,191 residents; through the closure the year-round population dropped by about a third. The land border stayed shut for nineteen months, and in November 2021 Point Roberts was granted a specific exemption allowing vaccinated residents to cross into Canada without a test — an acknowledgement, finally, that for the people living at the end of this road the border is not a frontier but a hallway.

## Flight Context

The Point Roberts–Boundary Bay crossing sits at 49.002°N, 123.068°W — essentially on the 49th parallel itself, where Tyee Drive on the American side meets 56 Street on the Canadian. Best viewed from 1,500 to 3,500 feet. The defining feature from the air is the border itself: a ruler-straight cleared swath running due east–west across the base of the peninsula, unmistakable against the forest and farmland, with Point Roberts hanging below it as a blunt squared-off tip into the Strait of Georgia. Boundary Bay opens to the east, the Tsawwassen ferry terminal and its long causeway project into the water just northwest, and the Fraser River delta spreads beyond that. Be careful here: this is a live international boundary directly under a busy terminal area, and cross-border flight requires proper clearance and reporting. Boundary Bay Airport (CZBB) in Delta is about 5 nautical miles northeast and is one of the busiest general-aviation fields in Canada; Vancouver International (CYVR) lies roughly 12 nautical miles north; Bellingham International (KBLI) is about 20 nautical miles southeast on the American mainland. Point Roberts has a small private airpark. Expect heavy traffic, frequent low stratus and delta fog in autumn and winter, and strong outflow winds from the Fraser Valley in cold snaps.
