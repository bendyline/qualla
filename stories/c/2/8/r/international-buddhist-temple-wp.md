# International Buddhist Temple

A Forbidden City roofline rises from the flat farmland of Richmond, BC — imperial gold and red on the outside, concrete and steel within, built from a single donation in 1979.

## Introduction

Beneath the gold and red of a Northern Chinese imperial palace, the Main Gracious Hall is holding itself up with concrete and steel. That sentence is the whole building in miniature. The International Buddhist Temple — 觀音寺, the Guan Yin Temple — sits on the flat delta farmland of Richmond, British Columbia, a few kilometres from one of Canada's busiest runways, and it is built in the traditional Chinese palatial style with a two-tiered roof of golden tiles and flared eaves modelled on the Forbidden City. Step inside the courtyard and the traffic stops mattering. The lotus ponds do most of the work.

## Two Donors, One Idea

In 1979, two Buddhists from Hong Kong gave land and money toward a single proposition: that North America should have an authentic Chinese Buddhist temple, not an adapted hall or a converted building but the real architectural article. The Venerable Guan Cheng and five others formed the International Buddhist Society in 1981 to carry it out. Two years later, with the Main Hall complete, the temple opened to the public. The formal inauguration came in 1986, and thousands turned up for it, including Richmond's mayor and the area's Member of Parliament — a useful marker of how firmly the project had planted itself in the civic life of a city that was changing fast around it. Expansion has continued in the decades since; the temple has never quite been finished.

## Nine Dragons and the Wisdom Fountain

The approach is deliberate theatre. The entrance is designed to evoke Deer Park, where Sakyamuni gave his first sermons, with gentle slopes, trees, and deer among them — sculpted rather than living, but the reference lands. Beyond the main courtyard lies a classical Chinese garden: lotus ponds, twin gazebos, rock landscapes, and a stone bridge. Water flows from the Wisdom Fountain at the pond's edge, which worshippers regard as having cleansing properties. In the Siddhartha Gautama Pool, nine white dragons spout upward, recalling the nine heavenly dragons that bathed the newborn prince. The abbot, who is a serious gardener, designs the plantings and flower arrangements himself according to both Chinese tradition and Buddhist aesthetics. There are bonsai, and Buddhist sayings carved into stone. For more than a decade the temple has taken first place in Richmond's landscape and garden contest in its category, which sounds like a small thing until you have seen it in June.

## The Halls

Five great Buddha and Bodhisattva statues stand in the Main Gracious Hall, among them a Sakyamuni Buddha described as the largest in North America. Across the Worshiping Square is the Seven Buddha Pavilion, housing the Four Heavenly Kings and Avalokitesvara — Guan Yin, the embodiment of compassion — depicted with a thousand hands and a thousand eyes, the iconography of a being who sees every suffering and can reach every one of them. Elsewhere on the grounds are the Thousand Buddha Hall, the Ksitigarbha or Ancestral Hall where families honour their dead, a Meditation Hall, and numerous smaller shrines. The Sangha holds daily prayer sessions in the Main Hall, so on almost any visit you will walk into chanting already in progress.

## Karma of the Brush

From 1986 to 1991 the Society ran a monthly Chinese art exhibition called Karma of the Brush, open to any artist in the Greater Vancouver area. It produced something permanent. Fung Kai Mun, one of the artists who helped found the exhibition, spent two years painting the Seven Buddha Mural, which the temple describes as the largest Buddhist mural in the world. On the wall facing the garden is the Amitabha Buddha mural, engraved with the Buddha's name — a standing reminder of the Mahayana aspiration to rebirth in Amitabha's Pure Land. Art here is not decoration added at the end of construction. It is part of how the place teaches.

## Open to Whoever Walks In

The Society practises Mahayana Buddhism, but the temple is explicit about welcoming Theravada practitioners and visitors of any faith or none. English-language meditation classes run Saturday mornings at nine. The Venerable Guan Cheng, who has led the Society and served as abbot since 1999, gives lectures and Dharma talks in English, Cantonese, and Mandarin, ranging from Sanskrit scripture to practical questions about living well; he holds a B.A. and an M.B.A. from the University of Toronto, has served as a Canadian delegate to the World Buddhist Forum, hosts radio programmes in Vancouver and Hong Kong, and has published several books. The charitable work is as real as the architecture: donations to the food bank, aid for students and the elderly, support for poor communities in rural China, and Seniors' Day celebrations with free vegetarian lunches. The City of Richmond calls the temple a Point of Pride. It has also received the Lieutenant Governor's 125th Centennial Award for service to the community — which is to say the neighbours, as well as the faithful, know what they have here.

## Flight Context

The International Buddhist Temple stands at 49.1332°N, 123.1234°W in south Richmond, British Columbia, on the flat alluvial plain of the Fraser River delta. This is busy, controlled airspace — Vancouver International Airport (CYVR) lies roughly 4 nautical miles northwest on Sea Island, and the temple sits beneath the general approach and departure environment, so review the terminal procedures and stay clear unless cleared. Viewed from 1,500 to 2,500 feet, the golden two-tiered roof and its courtyard garden stand out sharply against the surrounding grid of farmland, greenhouses, and low suburban development — an unmistakable landmark once you have seen it. Orient yourself on the South Arm of the Fraser River just to the south, with Steveston at the river mouth to the west and the Gulf of Georgia beyond; the North Shore Mountains rise across the city to the north, and Mount Baker often dominates the southeastern horizon. Other nearby fields include Boundary Bay (CZBB) to the southeast, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) to the east, and Vancouver Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYHC) downtown. Delta fog is a real factor from October through February; summer afternoons are typically clear with a westerly sea breeze.
