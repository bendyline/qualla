# Khalsa Diwan Society Vancouver

Founded in 1906, Vancouver's oldest Sikh society built the largest gurdwara in North America, survived a deliberate campaign to drive its community out of Canada, and now worships in an Arthur Erickson building shaped like a lotus.

**Location:** 49.2120, -123.0830

**Categories:** Religious Sites, Sikhism, Vancouver, British Columbia, Immigration History, Modern Architecture

## Introduction

Between 1904 and 1908 the Sikh population of British Columbia climbed to 5,185. By 1911 it was 2,342. By 1918 it had fallen to 1,099. Those numbers are not the result of people changing their minds about Canada. They are the measurable output of a set of federal policies designed to make South Asian settlement in this country impossible - immigration rules built specifically to stop families arriving and to encourage those already here to leave. The Khalsa Diwan Society lived through all of it. Founded on 22 July 1906 and formally registered on 13 March 1909, it is the oldest Sikh organization in Greater Vancouver, and it held a community together through the decades when the country was actively trying to shrink that community out of existence.

## West Second Avenue

The Society's first gurdwara opened at 1866 West 2nd Avenue on 19 January 1908, and for its era it was the largest gurdwara in North America. It also functioned as very much more than a place of worship. The anthropologist Verne Dusenbery described it as "truly a religious, social, political, cultural, and social service center for the entire South-Asian immigrant population of the lower mainland." There was a langar - the free community kitchen that is fundamental to Sikh practice, where anyone who walks in eats - and a homeless shelter. For men working in sawmills and logging camps, often without families because the immigration rules kept their families out, the building on West 2nd was the one address in the province that belonged to them.

## 1914, and Then 1947

The gurdwara stood at the centre of the Komagata Maru affair. In 1914 a ship carrying hundreds of passengers from British India arrived in Burrard Inlet and was refused landing under regulations crafted to exclude exactly those travellers. It sat in the harbour for two months while the Khalsa Diwan Society organized food, water, legal challenges, and money for the people aboard, and then it was forced to turn back. That episode marked the low point and, eventually, the turning point. Through the following decades the Society took a leading role in the campaign for the franchise, working in an explicitly secular capacity on behalf of all Indo-Canadians. South Asians in Canada regained the right to vote in 1947. Every March the Society commemorates the martyrdom of Mewa Singh Lopoke, a figure from those years whose memory still brings Sikhs from as far as California to the gurdwara.

## An Argument Over Who Counts

Not all the Society's hardest moments came from outside. In the early 1950s the Khalsa Diwan Society elected a clean-shaven Sikh to its management committee, and the Canadian Sikh community split over it. Most early Sikh immigrants to Canada had been non-Khalsa, and a majority of the Society's own members were clean-shaven, but a faction held that a non-Khalsa should not sit on the committee. Groups in both Vancouver and Victoria broke away and founded the Akali Singh Society, which opened its own gurdwara in 1952. The dispute was about observance, but underneath it lay a harder question that every diaspora eventually faces: who gets to define the community once it has been living somewhere else for two generations.

## A Lotus on Ross Street

By the late 1950s the congregation was planning Punjabi-language schools for Canadian-born children and raising money for a new centre. Planning began in earnest in 1963, and the Society hired Arthur Erickson - the most significant Canadian architect of his generation - to design it. Erickson travelled to Agra and Amritsar looking for the form, and what he produced at Southwest Marine Drive and Ross Street was not a copy of anything. The building was conceived as a lotus rising out of water, its roof planes lifting in overlapping petals above a concrete base. It was finished in the first week of April 1970. The 500th birthday celebration of Guru Nanak was held there before the formal opening, and on Vaisakhi 1970 the Sri Guru Granth Sahib was carried from West 2nd Avenue to Ross Street.

## Still Open

Through the 1960s the Vancouver gurdwara anchored branch congregations in New Westminster, Abbotsford, Victoria, and Port Alberni, several of which grew into independent societies as their own communities expanded. Membership ran around 5,000 before 1979, when a $12 fee was still charged; removing the fee increased it further. In 2012 the Society opened a historical museum in the Ross Street building, which means the place now holds its own archive - the photographs, documents, and objects of a community that spent its first forty years in this country being counted, restricted, and formally told it did not belong. The langar still runs. Anyone can eat.

## Flight Context

The Khalsa Diwan Society gurdwara stands at 49.212 N, 123.083 W, at Southwest Marine Drive and Ross Street in South Vancouver, British Columbia. A viewing altitude of 1,500-3,000 feet MSL is appropriate - the building's tiered, angular white roof planes make it genuinely identifiable from the air, sitting on the south slope above the Fraser River's North Arm amid an otherwise low-rise residential and industrial grid. The Fraser River itself is the dominant landmark, running east-west just south of the site, with the Knight Street and Oak Street bridges crossing to Richmond. Downtown Vancouver's towers lie about 7 km north. This is congested controlled airspace: Vancouver International (CYVR) is roughly 4 nm southwest across the river, Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) about 6 nm north with heavy floatplane traffic, and Abbotsford (CYXX) 30 nm east. Clearance is required throughout. Expect low ceilings and rain from October through March, with excellent visibility and North Shore mountain backdrops in summer.
