# London Heritage Farm

Four acres of 1880s Richmond preserved beside the Fraser River, where two teenage brothers drained a bog into a farm and a volunteer society later saved the house they built.

**Location:** 49.1144, -123.1514

**Categories:** Historic Sites, Museums, British Columbia, Farming, Historic Houses, Family History

## Introduction

Charles Edwin London was sixteen years old and his brother William was seventeen when they arrived in British Columbia from Ontario in 1877. Three years later the two of them bought 200 acres of Richmond for $2,000 - flat, waterlogged delta ground on the south arm of the Fraser River, the kind of land that is cheap because it is not yet land. They put up a small farmhouse and started clearing and draining. Everything that followed on that property, for the next century and a half, followed from two teenagers deciding a bog could be persuaded into a farm.

## A House Built in Two Halves

In 1888 Charles married Henrietta Dalzeil, who had come from Dalbeattie in Scotland, and he began building the farmhouse that still stands. It went up in stages: the back, northern wing first, then the front, southern portion added through the 1890s and completed in 1898. The Londons were not simply farming. They ran a general store and the local post office, and they built a wharf on the river to land their supplies and ship out what the farm produced - milk, hay, oats, and vegetables bound for New Westminster. In a delta where roads were poor and the river did the heavy work, a private wharf was less a convenience than the whole business model.

## Eight Children

Charles and Henrietta had eight children. Three of them died in infancy, a figure that was not unusual for a rural family in that era and was no easier to bear for being common. Henrietta died in 1916. The family remained at the farm only three more years before moving to the Marpole district of Vancouver, and the place might have passed out of the family entirely. It did not. In 1921 the Londons' eldest daughter, Lucy, and her husband Herbert Howse bought the farm back and raised their own family there, working the land until 1948. After that the farmhouse was rented out to a succession of families - occupied, but no longer anyone's farm - until the City of Richmond purchased it in 1978.

## The Rescue

By then the house was one of the last physical traces of Richmond's agricultural beginnings, in a municipality rapidly becoming something else entirely. The London Heritage Farm Society had spent years working with the city to save it, and the city's purchase secured the house along with four acres of the original London farmland. Once the site was registered as a heritage property under the BC Heritage Act, the Society signed a twenty-five-year operating agreement and set about restoring the building. The arrangement still holds: the City of Richmond owns the farm, the Society runs it, and operations and improvements are paid for by donations together with the tea room, the gift shop, and the events the Society stages through the year.

## Rooms Set for 1900

The farmhouse today is furnished to show rural life in Richmond between roughly 1880 and 1930. Six display rooms hold the furniture, photographs, clothing, quilts, and ordinary household objects of that period - the sort of collection whose power lies in the unremarkable items rather than the treasures. Outside, on 4.06 acres overlooking the south arm of the Fraser, there are heritage and herb gardens, the restored Spragg family barn, antique farm equipment, a small museum of hand tools, chickens, hives of honey bees and blue orchard bees, community garden allotments, wide lawns, and picnic tables. The tea room is the anchor, as tea rooms in places like this tend to be.

## On Screen

The farm has had a second, quieter career in front of cameras. The 1992 thriller Jennifer 8, written and directed by Bruce Robinson and starring Andy Garcia, Uma Thurman, Kathy Baker, and Lance Henriksen, was filmed on location here. The property also served as the setting for the MacGyver episode "Faith, Hope and Charity" in the series' sixth season. Neither is the reason to visit, but both point at something true about the place: with the right camera angle, a working 1890s farmhouse on the Fraser delta can stand in for almost any rural America you like, because for a while that is more or less what it was.

## Flight Context

London Heritage Farm sits at 49.1144°N, 123.1514°W on the south edge of Richmond, British Columbia, overlooking the south arm of the Fraser River. Navigation here is straightforward but the airspace is not: the site lies within a few nautical miles of Vancouver International Airport (CYVR), which occupies Sea Island just to the northwest, so expect controlled airspace, heavy arrival and departure traffic, and noise-abatement routing. Orient on the broad south arm of the Fraser and the long line of Highway 99 running south toward the George Massey Tunnel; the farm's green rectangle stands out clearly against Richmond's dense suburban grid, with Steveston and its fishing harbour a few kilometres west. Recommended viewing altitude, where permitted, is 1,500-2,500 ft MSL for site detail, or 6,000-8,000 ft to see the whole Fraser delta with the North Shore Mountains behind and Mount Baker on the southeastern horizon. Boundary Bay (CZBB) is roughly 8 nm southeast and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 17 nm northeast. Fraser Valley fog is common on autumn and winter mornings.
