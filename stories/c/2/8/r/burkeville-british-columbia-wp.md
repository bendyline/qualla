# Burkeville, British Columbia

A wartime village of 328 identical small houses on Sea Island, built for Boeing's aircraft workers in 1944 and now the only neighbourhood inside Vancouver International Airport.

**Location:** 49.1889, -123.1460

**Categories:** british-columbia, richmond, wartime-housing, aviation, neighbourhood, history

## Introduction

There is a village inside Vancouver International Airport. Sea Island, the flat scrap of Fraser River delta that the airport covers almost entirely, has exactly one residential neighbourhood on it, and about 819 people live there among the runways and the freight sheds and the long-term parking. Burkeville was not built as a curiosity. It was built in a hurry between 1941 and 1943 by a federal crown corporation, because there was a war on, Boeing had an aircraft plant on this island, and rubber and gasoline were both rationed too tightly for workers to commute. The first families moved in during January 1944 to 328 identical small houses, available in three exterior colours.

## Three Colours, Four Rooms

Wartime Housing Limited built to a formula, and Burkeville shows it. Each house ran between 700 and 900 square feet, with either four rooms or six depending on the layout, and the exteriors came in three colours, which was the entire extent of the variety on offer. Simple, fast, adequate: exactly what the moment demanded.

The ground it sits on was taken. The farms on this part of Sea Island were expropriated so the village could go up, which is how wartime land acquisition generally worked and was no less final for the people it happened to. One set of families lost a livelihood built over decades; another set arrived with suitcases into houses that smelled of new paint. Both things were true at the same time, and the second group had nothing to do with the first.

## Named for the Boss

The village takes its name from Stanley Burke, president of Boeing's Canadian operation at the time it was built. It is a company-town naming convention, straightforwardly applied: the workers got houses, and the houses got the name of the man who ran the plant.

The logic behind the whole project was logistical rather than sentimental. Aircraft production needed shifts running around the clock, and with tyres scarce and fuel rationed there was no dependable way to move several hundred workers on and off an island every day. Putting them within walking distance solved it. Governments in wartime build what the production schedule requires, and Burkeville is what that looked like in the Fraser delta in 1943.

## What to Call the School

A neighbourhood becomes a community at the point where it starts arguing about names. Sea Island School opened on 17 October 1947, and getting to that opening involved choosing between three candidates: Princess Elizabeth School, in honour of the young heir to the throne; Frasea School, a portmanteau of Fraser and Sea Island that somebody must have been proud of; and Sea Island School, which is what the Reeve of Richmond, Rudy Grauer, settled on.

He picked the plainest option, and it was the right call. By 1947 the emergency was over and the question facing Burkeville was whether a village thrown up for a single purpose could become a place people simply lived. A school named after the island rather than the war effort or a royal visit was an answer of sorts.

## The Man Who Flew the Mars

Burkeville's most notable resident was Daniel Erskine McIvor, born in 1911, who worked out how to fight forest fires from the air. His instrument of choice was the Martin JRM Mars, an enormous four-engined flying boat built for the US Navy and, in McIvor's hands, converted into a water bomber capable of scooping and dropping thousands of gallons on a burning coastal slope.

The techniques he developed became standard practice, and the Mars aircraft working the British Columbia coast became famous well beyond aviation circles. His Order of Canada citation in 2004 credited him with saving thousands of acres of forest. He was inducted into both the British Columbia and Canadian Aviation Halls of Fame in 2002, and a lake near Campbell River carries his name. He died in 2005. A resident of a wartime housing village on an airport island turned out to have a fair amount to do with how the province protects its forests.

## Holding Steady

What is striking about Burkeville now is how little it moves. The 2021 census counted 819 residents in 304 occupied dwellings out of 315 - a rise of barely half a per cent over five years. In a metropolitan region where neighbourhoods get torn down and rebuilt at six storeys within a decade, this one has held its shape since the Second World War.

It is not preserved in amber either. Just over three quarters of residents claim English as a mother tongue; Cantonese, Mandarin, Spanish, Punjabi, Hindi, Japanese, and half a dozen more languages account for the rest, in the ordinary proportions of the Lower Mainland. Employment sits slightly above the national rate. Median income in 2020 was $48,000. People walk to the dike, watch the jets rotate over the roofline, and go home to a four-room house built in 1943 for somebody who riveted aircraft.

## Flight Context

Burkeville sits at 49.189N, 123.146W on the southeast corner of Sea Island, immediately south of Vancouver International Airport's runway complex. It is impossible to miss on approach or departure from CYVR: a compact grid of small houses and mature street trees, the only residential block on an island otherwise given over to taxiways, cargo terminals, and parking. The Fraser River's middle arm runs along its southern edge toward Richmond; the north arm and downtown Vancouver lie beyond the airport to the northeast, and the Strait of Georgia opens west. YVR (CYVR) is the airport around it; Vancouver Harbour water aerodrome (CYHC) is 8 nm northeast, Boundary Bay (CZBB) 10 nm southeast, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) to the east. This is busy controlled airspace with constant heavy traffic. Expect winter river fog on the delta and strong westerlies off the strait in frontal passages.
