# Steveston Tram Museum

A bright red 1912 interurban car, restored and parked on its own original rails, remembering the line that carried cannery workers to the largest fishing harbour in Canada.

**Location:** 49.1255, -123.1808

**Categories:** Museums, British Columbia, Railway History, Industrial History, Vancouver, Immigration History

## Introduction

They called it the Sockeye Special. For fifty-three years an electric interurban ran from Vancouver down to Steveston, and its nickname said exactly what the line was for: moving people to and from the fish. Car 1220 - built in 1912, painted bright red, steel and wood, sixty-four seats - is one of only seven survivors from that railway, and it now sits inside a museum in Steveston built directly around a surviving section of the original track. The station it once stopped at is gone. The rails under the car are the ones it ran on.

## A Railway That Didn't Pay

The line began as a Canadian Pacific Railway idea. In the 1890s the CPR wanted a company to serve Lulu Island, and in 1902 a light-rail line opened from Vancouver to Steveston. It promptly failed to make money, for a reason nobody seems to have thought through: Steveston's canneries were on the water, and shipping their product straight out by sea cost less than trucking it up to Vancouver to put it on a train. The freight logic collapsed. So the CPR leased the line to the British Columbia Electric Railway in 1905, the BCER electrified it and built a substation at Marpole to feed it, and passenger service began on July 4, 1905. It ran until February 28, 1958.

## What the Sockeye Special Carried

Once the line ran on passengers rather than freight, its purpose became obvious: it carried workers. Steveston in that era was on its way to becoming the largest fishing harbour in the country, with canneries lining the waterfront and a labour force drawn from across the Pacific and across the province. The trams brought people down in the morning and took them home at night, sixty-four seated at a time, through the fields of Lulu Island and into the noise and smell of the packing lines. It is a small piece of industrial machinery that explains the shape of a whole region - where people lived, how far they could live from work, and what a day in a cannery town actually involved.

## 1942

Before the Second World War, Steveston had the second-largest Japanese Canadian population in the country after Vancouver's Powell Street neighbourhood. Japanese Canadian boat builders supplied vessels to canneries up and down the coast; Japanese Canadian women worked the processing lines, some with babies on their backs. In early 1942 that ended by government order. About 2,600 Nikkei residents of Steveston were removed from their homes, their property seized, and sent to internment camps in the British Columbia interior or to farm and road labour further east - part of the uprooting of roughly 22,000 Japanese Canadians, about ninety percent of the country's Japanese population. Their fishing boats were impounded, stored carelessly, damaged, and sold off for a fraction of their worth. The trams kept running on schedule. The community that had filled them did not come back the same.

## One of Twenty-Eight

Car 1220 was built by the St. Louis Car Company in Missouri in 1912 and bought by the BCER the following year, one of twenty-eight cars in the 1200 class ordered for these routes. It worked the Marpole-to-Steveston run. Seven of the twenty-eight still exist in varying condition, and two of its sisters can be seen nearby: car 1225 runs as a heritage streetcar for the Fraser Valley Heritage Railway Society in Surrey, and car 1223 is on display at the Burnaby Village Museum. Out of a fleet designed for a network that no longer exists, a quarter survive - which is a remarkably good outcome for objects this large and this easy to scrap.

## Three Years and $400,000

The museum opened on May 3, 2013, built around the original BCER track and standing across the street from where the Steveston station used to be. Car 1220 is the single largest artifact in the City of Richmond's museum collection, and restoring it took three years and $400,000 of city money before the finished tram went back on display in 2018. That is a serious sum for one streetcar, and it buys something specific: not a replica, not a diagram, but the actual vehicle, in its actual colours, sitting on its actual rails, at the end of the line it used to run. Step aboard and the wooden seats and the hand straps do most of the explaining without any help.

## Flight Context

The Steveston Tram Museum stands at 49.1255°N, 123.1808°W in the village of Steveston, at the southwest corner of Lulu Island in Richmond, British Columbia. Navigate by the river: Steveston sits on the south arm of the Fraser River where it meets the Strait of Georgia, with the historic cannery waterfront, the fishing fleet, and Garry Point Park immediately west of the village grid. Lulu Island's flat farmland and dyke system fill the view north and east, and the Gulf Islands lie across the strait. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,200-2,000 ft MSL to read the village, the docks, and the boardwalk, though note that Vancouver International Airport (CYVR) lies only about 4 nm north on Sea Island - this is busy Class C airspace beneath heavy approach and departure traffic, and transit requires clearance. Boundary Bay (CZBB) is roughly 8 nm southeast and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 20 nm northeast. Expect winter fog over the Fraser delta, low ceilings from October through March, and excellent summer visibility with afternoon westerly sea breeze.
