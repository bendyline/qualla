# Finn Slough

Thirty people live in wooden houses on the marshy edge of the Fraser River in Richmond, some on pilings and some afloat, in a Finnish fishing settlement that has been quietly refusing to disappear since the 1880s.

**Location:** 49.1131, -123.1156

**Categories:** Ethnic enclaves in British Columbia, European-Canadian culture in British Columbia, Neighbourhoods in Richmond, British Columbia, Populated places on the Fraser River, Finnish diaspora in Canada

## Introduction

Half of Finn Slough goes up and down twice a day. On a tidal channel at the south end of No. 4 Road, in the rural Gilmore corner of Richmond, British Columbia, about thirty people live in wooden houses along the marshy bank of the Fraser. Some of those houses stand on pilings driven into the mud. Others simply float, rising with the flood and settling with the ebb, tethered rather than founded. Many were built between the late nineteenth century and the 1950s; some have decayed to the point of grey translucence, others have been carefully brought back. The place is also called Tiffin Slough. It is a few minutes' drive from one of Canada's busiest airports, and it looks like nothing else in the country.

## Why the Finns Came

Finnish settlers arrived in Richmond in the 1880s and found the Fraser River at the height of its generosity. The sockeye runs on this river were among the great salmon fisheries of the world, and the industry built to process them was enormous - around forty-five canneries operated on the Fraser in the 1890s, roughly half of them clustered at Steveston at the island's southwestern tip. A gillnetter with his own boat and a share of the run could make a genuine living. That is exactly what happened at Finn Slough: most of the community's residents did well from fishing, and in time became local landowners. That last detail matters more than it sounds. Owning the ground is the difference between a fish camp and a place people are still living a hundred and forty years later.

## One Metre Above the Sea

Richmond occupies the islands of the Fraser delta, and the land averages about one metre above sea level - which is to say that the city exists at the pleasure of its engineering. Every major island is ringed by dykes, holding back the river's spring freshet on one side and the Strait of Georgia on the other. Behind that line sits a modern city of subdivisions, farmland and the airport. Finn Slough sits on the wrong side of the arrangement, out on the marshy bank where the water still does what it wants. Nobody built a dyke to protect the float houses, because a float house does not need one. The community solved, by accident and a century early, the problem the rest of the delta has been engineering around ever since.

## Grey Wood and Low Light

Photographers found the place a long time ago, and it has been photographed steadily ever since - the images turn up on postcards in tourist shops all over Vancouver. The appeal is not hard to understand. Cedar siding weathered to silver, roofs sagging into curves, a wooden footbridge, boats in various stages of being repaired or abandoned, and behind all of it the flat grey water and the reeds. In winter light it is close to monochrome. The buildings are also a record of how fishing families actually built: not designed, but added to, patched, moved, floated into a new position when the river changed its mind. Some structures have deteriorated severely. Others have been restored with real care. Both conditions are usually visible in the same photograph.

## What the River Took Back

The industry that made Finn Slough possible did not survive its own success. The Fraser's salmon runs peaked around 1913, and the cannery economy that once employed thousands of Japanese, Chinese, First Nations and European workers each summer consolidated and then collapsed, leaving a handful of preserved buildings where there had been dozens. The Gulf of Georgia Cannery, built in 1894 and once the largest plant in British Columbia, is now a national historic site rather than a workplace. Against that, thirty people still live at Finn Slough, in houses their predecessors built, on a channel that still floods and drains on schedule. It is a small remnant, and a deliberately maintained one. The most surprising thing about the place is not that it looks the way it does. It is that anybody is still there.

## Flight Context

Finn Slough lies at 49.1131 N, 123.1156 W on the south bank of Lulu Island in Richmond, British Columbia, at the end of No. 4 Road on the South Arm of the Fraser River. From 1,000 to 2,000 feet AGL, look for the dyke line running along the island's south edge, with the slough as a narrow tidal channel just outside it and a cluster of small structures and boats on the marshy fringe - easy to miss, and much clearer at low tide when the mud flats and the float houses' aspect give it away. The South Arm of the Fraser, its log booms and the Steveston jetty lie to the west; the flat agricultural grid of Richmond fills the island behind. This is congested Class C airspace: Vancouver International (CYVR) is only about 6 nm northwest and its south-runway traffic works this area constantly, while Boundary Bay (CZBB) is roughly 5 nm southeast across the river. Obtain clearance. Expect delta fog on calm autumn mornings, and generally good visibility with a westerly sea breeze on summer afternoons.
