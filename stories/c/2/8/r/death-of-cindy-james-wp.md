# Death of Cindy James

For seven years a Vancouver-area nurse reported a campaign of threats and attacks that police could never corroborate; in 1989 she was found dead behind an abandoned house on Blundell Road in Richmond, and a 40-day inquest could only call it an 'unknown event.'

## Introduction

The coroner's inquest ended on May 25, 1990, exactly one year after Cindy James disappeared, and it ended without an answer. For forty days, jurors in Burnaby had heard from more than eighty witnesses about the life and death of a 44-year-old nurse from Richmond, British Columbia. They were asked a simple question: did she die by suicide, by homicide or by accident? They could not say. Her death was ruled the result of an "unknown event," and the case was formally closed. More than three decades later, it remains unresolved.

## A Nurse for Children

She was born Cynthia Elizabeth Hack on June 12, 1944, in Oliver, British Columbia, one of six children. In 1962 she enrolled in nursing school in Vancouver, and in the summer of 1965 she met Roy Makepeace, a South African psychiatrist 18 years her senior. They married on December 9, 1966, the same year she earned her nursing degree. Her working life was devoted to children. She was a pediatric nurse at Vancouver General Hospital from 1966 to 1975, and then for about twelve years served as a team coordinator at Blenheim House, a facility for children with behavioral disorders, where she earned praise for her competence and professionalism. In 1982 she and Makepeace separated. Four months later, the incidents began.

## Seven Years of Fear

Between 1982 and 1989, Cindy reported about 90 criminal incidents to the RCMP: a prowler, obscene and threatening phone calls, menacing notes made of cut-and-pasted letters, break-ins, strangled cats in her garden, fires in her home. Several times she was found unconscious or dazed, once with a paring knife through her hand, and more than once with a nylon stocking around her neck. She moved house repeatedly, painted her car to avoid being recognized and hired a private investigator, Ozzie Kaban, who stayed on her case for six years. In early 1986 she even changed her surname to James to conceal her identity. Some people saw things too. A neighbor reported a man outside her house on three occasions, a friend staying over in 1986 said he saw a man flee on foot, and in May 1989 scent hounds tracked an unknown person's trail over her backyard fence.

## Doubt and Belief

Over nearly seven years, the RCMP spent an estimated $1 million to $1.5 million investigating her claims, one of the longest and most costly police investigations in British Columbia's history, yet found no evidence to corroborate them. She had a documented history of depression and suicidal thoughts, and authorities came to suspect she was staging the incidents herself. Makepeace, whom she at times suspected, denied any involvement. Not everyone doubted her. Kaban concluded she could not have been the perpetrator, saying, "She'd never have done that if she was the Cindy I knew." A knot expert hired by the RCMP, Robert Chisnall, initially judged it "highly unlikely" that she could have bound herself as she was found in 1988. Cindy herself grew frustrated with the police, singling out only one detective, Jerry Anderson, as someone she felt she could trust.

## Blundell Road

On the afternoon of May 25, 1989, Cindy picked up her paycheck at Richmond General Hospital, where she now worked. A coworker thought she seemed in good spirits. That evening she bought groceries and, at 7:59 p.m., deposited the check at an ATM at the Blundell Shopping Centre. Friends were due that night to play bridge. When they found her house locked and her car gone, they drove to the shopping centre and found her car abandoned in the lot, with blood on the driver's door and her groceries and a wrapped birthday gift inside. On June 8 a municipal paving worker found her body behind an abandoned house at 8111 Blundell Road, a property on a busy street. She had been hogtied, with a nylon stocking tied around her neck. The autopsy found she had died of an overdose of morphine, diazepam and flurazepam, with ten times the lethal dose of morphine in her blood.

## An Unknown Event

The inquest that followed in the spring of 1990 was the longest and most expensive in British Columbia up to that time. Among the witnesses were psychiatrists and psychologists, including those who had treated her. They watched Chisnall, working with the same length of nylon, demonstrate how she could have bound herself within about three minutes, before the drugs took hold. How the morphine had entered her body was never determined. In the end the evidence pointed in too many directions. The abandoned house was demolished in the summer of 1989, and the story passed into books, television and, in 2021, a podcast titled Death by Unknown Event. Behind all of it was a woman who spent her career caring for children, and whose last seven years were consumed by a fear that, whatever its source, was real to her.

## Flight Context

The site on Blundell Road lies at about 49.155°N, 123.135°W in central Richmond, British Columbia, on the flat Fraser River delta just south of Vancouver. The abandoned house is long gone, so from 1,500-2,500 feet AGL there is nothing specific to see; orient on Richmond's grid of arterial roads and the arms of the Fraser River. Vancouver International Airport (CYVR) lies only about 3 nm to the northwest, placing the site inside its Class C airspace, so coordinate with Vancouver terminal.
