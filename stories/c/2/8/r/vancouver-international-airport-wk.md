# Vancouver International Airport

Canada's second-busiest airport, built on a Fraser River sandbar, where a five-tonne Haida canoe sits in the departures hall and floatplanes still carry the mail to villages with no roads.

**Location:** 49.1947, -123.1840

**Categories:** Airports in British Columbia, Richmond, British Columbia, Transport in Metro Vancouver, Public art in Canada, 1931 establishments in British Columbia

## Introduction

Fifty-five thousand people came out to watch the airport open. That was 22 July 1931, and the Sea Island Airport and Seaplane Harbour marked the occasion with an air show and a four-day open house on a runway 2,350 feet long, with two hangars that could hold twelve large aircraft or thirty small ones with their wings folded. The attendance was roughly fifty times the number of passengers the airport would actually handle that year — 1,072 of them. In 2019 the same sandbar in the Fraser River delta processed more than twenty-six million.

## Thirteen Passengers in One Canoe

The most photographed object at Vancouver International is not an aircraft. In the international departures hall sits The Spirit of Haida Gwaii: The Jade Canoe, Bill Reid's bronze of a six-metre Haida canoe carrying thirteen crowded, jostling figures — Raven at the steering paddle, Wolf with his claws in Eagle's back, Frog, Beaver, Bear and his family, Dogfish Woman, Mouse Woman, and among the humans the Chief and a figure Reid called the Ancient Reluctant Conscript. The casting weighs over 4,900 kilograms. Reid modelled it in clay in his Vancouver studio, had it cast at a foundry in Beacon, New York, and showed it on Granville Island before it was installed here on 18 April 1996 with the blessing of the Musqueam First Nation, whose traditional territory the airport occupies. Its subject is the least airport-like thing imaginable and also the most: a crowd of beings who cannot agree, in a vessel that is moving regardless.

## Reading the Building

The canoe is the headline, but the terminal is full of work that rewards a slow walk. Between the domestic and international check-in areas stands a totem pole combining Haida motifs — Creator Raven at the top, a human figure at the bottom — with Celtic and Asian elements, which is about as accurate a portrait of this city as any sculpture could manage. Facing the escalators down to immigration, two Coast Salish welcome figures greet arrivals on behalf of the Musqueam. Large panels woven from sheep's wool hang beside the escalator, and behind it a carved spindle whorl gestures at flight. On the way to the international gates the floor opens into a running stream over a rock bed, crossed by walkways, with salmon and herring and an orca worked into it. Skytrax named this the best airport in North America in 2007 and every year from 2010 to 2022, then again in 2024 and 2026.

## The Other Airport

Behind the jet operation is a second, older one. At the South Terminal, and at floatplane docks on the Fraser River about 300 metres beyond it, aircraft leave for places that have no roads. British Columbia is enormous and much of it is unreachable by car, so small planes here carry mail, freight, supplies, and crews to the north coast and the interior as a matter of routine rather than adventure. Floatplanes run to Nanaimo in twenty minutes, Sechelt in twenty, Salt Spring in twenty-five, Victoria's inner harbour in thirty, Maple Bay in fifty. The road into all of it is called Russ Baker Way, after the bush pilot who flew this coast in the 1930s and 1940s and built the operation that became Pacific Western Airlines. Sea Island has never entirely stopped being a bush strip with a very large terminal attached.

## A Border Inside the Building

One corner of the airport is legally not in Canada. The United States operates a border preclearance facility here, open from 4:30 in the morning to midnight — the longest operating hours of any US preclearance facility — where travellers clear American immigration and customs before boarding — and once through, they are considered to have entered the United States and cannot wander back out into the rest of the terminal. Pier E serves those flights, Piers A, B and C handle domestic departures, and Pier D takes international, with a number of swing gates that can be reassigned as the schedule demands. The arrangement saves hours on the far end and costs you the run of the concourses, which is why experienced travellers eat before they clear.

## Where the Map Points

Geography decided this airport's route network long before any airline did. Vancouver sits closer to Asia than any other major Canadian city, and the metropolitan area's large East Asian population made the connections dense as well as short: daily departures for Japan, South Korea, China and the Philippines, with Hong Kong the most frequent of all. It is the only Canadian city with direct service to Oceania, for the same reason of distance. Air Canada's longest route in the world begins here and ends 12,818 kilometres later in Singapore, about sixteen hours in the air. And for all the intercontinental reach, the Canada Line will take you from the terminal to downtown Vancouver in twenty-five minutes, with travel between the three stations on Sea Island itself entirely free.

## Flight Context

Vancouver International (CYVR) occupies Sea Island at 49.195°N, 123.184°W, in the City of Richmond at the mouth of the Fraser River, about 12 kilometres south of downtown Vancouver. Field elevation is near sea level, which is the first thing to know: this is a flat delta airport with two long parallel east–west runways plus a shorter crosswind strip, and essentially no terrain on the field itself. The visual approach is straightforward — the Fraser's North Arm and Middle Arm bracket the island, the Strait of Georgia opens immediately west, and the city's downtown peninsula with Stanley Park is unmistakable to the north-northeast. The North Shore Mountains rise sharply behind the city and are the terrain that matters; Mount Baker stands out to the southeast in Washington. Floatplane traffic is heavy on the Fraser and into Vancouver Harbour downtown, and Boundary Bay (CZBB) 10 nautical miles southeast is one of Canada's busiest general-aviation fields, so expect a crowded picture. Abbotsford (CYXX) lies about 35 nautical miles east and Bellingham International (KBLI) roughly 30 nautical miles southeast across the border. Expect low stratus and delta fog on calm autumn and winter mornings, strong easterly outflow down the Fraser Valley in cold snaps, and long periods of rain with good visibility beneath the cloud.
