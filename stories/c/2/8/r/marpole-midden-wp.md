# Marpole Midden

c̓əsnaʔəm, an ancient Musqueam village and burial ground near the mouth of the Fraser River, occupied for four thousand years, excavated by outsiders for a century, and finally returned to the Musqueam by 2018.

**Location:** 49.2040, -123.1390

**Categories:** National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Coast Salish art and artifacts, History of Vancouver, First Nations history

## Introduction

The condominium project had a permit. The permit required an archaeological dig, so in January 2012 the shovels went in — and found exactly what the Musqueam had been saying would be found. Intact ancestral remains, in a burial ground their people had used for thousands of years, on a block of south Vancouver zoned for 108 units. What followed over the next nine months was a rally at a cemetery, a blockade of a bridge and a provincial decision — and, in the years after, the land itself coming back to the people whose ancestors had never left it. The place is called c̓əsnaʔəm. On most maps and in most archaeology papers it is called the Marpole Midden, which is not the same thing at all.

## Four Thousand Years at the River Mouth

The site sits near the mouth of the Fraser River in the Marpole neighbourhood of Vancouver — flat, low ground where a great river meets the sea, which is to say one of the most productive places on the entire coast. Coast Salish people lived here from at least four thousand years ago until roughly two hundred years ago, when smallpox arrived on the Northwest Coast and ended what disease ends. For most of that span it was c̓əsnaʔəm, a Musqueam village, and it was also a burial ground. Archaeologists use the site to define an entire period of regional prehistory: the Marpole Culture Type, dated between about 2400 and 1600 years before present. The people who produced that material culture were not a type. They were a community, living continuously in one place across a span that makes most of recorded European history look brief.

## The Trouble With the Word

A midden, in ordinary archaeological usage, is a refuse heap — the compacted shell, bone, ash and discard that accumulates where people live. The term is accurate as far as it goes, and it is why the site was designated a National Historic Site of Canada on May 25, 1933 as one of the largest pre-contact middens in western Canada. But calling c̓əsnaʔəm a midden is a little like calling a cathedral a pile of masonry. It flattens a village and a cemetery into a category of debris, and that framing made it much easier, for a very long time, to treat the ground as material rather than as a place where people were buried by their families. The designation itself has a small absurdity attached: the historic marker stands in nearby Marpole Park, several blocks from the site it commemorates, which lies between Montcalm and Milton streets south of Marine Drive.

## Dug Up for Science

The midden came to settler attention in 1884, exposed during roadwork, and excavation followed for decades. In 1892 Charles Hill-Tout dug extensively on behalf of the Art, Historical, and Scientific Association of Vancouver, and his work prompted interest in other middens across the region. Between 1897 and 1899 Harlan Ingersoll Smith of the American Museum of Natural History, working with the Jesup North Pacific Expedition, mined the site for skeletal remains and shipped them east. In the 1950s and 1960s the University of British Columbia's Charles Edward Borden ran salvage archaeology here, and Borden was the first researcher to draw the explicit link between the remains coming out of the ground and the Musqueam people still living a few kilometres away — a connection the Musqueam had never needed anyone to establish. Meanwhile the ground itself was disappearing. Construction of the Fraser Arms Hotel in the 1950s destroyed much of the site outright.

## January 2012

A development permit for the 108-unit project was issued in December 2011. The dig required under that permit turned up intact remains the following month. In March, Musqueam members halted the digging, saying that no consultation had taken place before the permit was granted and that the silence had continued since. A cooling-off period followed while negotiations were supposed to occur; when nothing meaningful came of them, people returned to the site. Through May, Musqueam members and supporters organized to make the situation impossible to overlook — a rally at Mountain View Cemetery on May 29, chosen for the obvious reason, and a blockade of the Arthur Laing Bridge between Richmond and Vancouver on May 31. It is worth being plain about what was at stake for the families involved: their ancestors had been disinterred, again, for a building.

## The Land Returned

On September 27, 2012, the province delivered its decision on the permits issued under the Heritage Conservation Act. The five-storey development was no longer authorized, and the ancestral remains were to be restored to their original condition — work the Musqueam pressed to begin immediately, before further deterioration, and to carry out according to their own customs. The land itself took longer to settle: it was not until 2018 that the property was returned to Musqueam ownership. After a century and a quarter in which the site had been road-cut, excavated, shipped to New York, built over by a hotel and finally zoned for condominiums, the simplest solution turned out to be the one nobody in authority had proposed for decades: give it back. c̓əsnaʔəm is now held by the community that has been burying its dead there for four thousand years.

## Flight Context

c̓əsnaʔəm — the Marpole Midden — lies at 49.20N, 123.14W in south Vancouver, British Columbia, near the north arm of the Fraser River between Montcalm and Milton streets, just south of Marine Drive. From the air the setting is unmistakable even if the site is not: the broad braided north arm of the Fraser with Sea Island and Vancouver International Airport immediately southwest, the Arthur Laing Bridge crossing the river directly beside the neighbourhood, and the Oak Street and Knight Street bridges upstream to the east. The Musqueam reserve lies about 2nm west along the riverbank. This is the final approach and departure corridor for Vancouver International (CYVR), roughly 2nm southwest — the airspace is controlled, busy, and not a place for sightseeing; any viewing should be from an appropriate transit altitude with a clearance. Pitt Meadows (CYPK) lies 15nm east and Boundary Bay (CZBB) 10nm south. Clearest conditions come with a winter outflow, when the Fraser delta is sharp and the North Shore mountains stand up behind the city.
