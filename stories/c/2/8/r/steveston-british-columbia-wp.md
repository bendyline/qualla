# Steveston, British Columbia

The salmon canning capital of the Fraser River, where Japanese Canadian fishermen built their own hospital because the town's would not treat them, and lost everything in 1942.

**Location:** 49.1275, -123.1814

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in Richmond, British Columbia, Fishing communities in Canada, Populated places on the Fraser River, Japanese Canadian history, Salmon canning, World War II sites in Canada

## Introduction

Around 1897 the Japanese fishermen of Steveston built their own hospital. They did it because the local hospital would not admit them, and the Fraser River Japanese Fishermen's Association raised the money and put up a building rather than keep arguing about it. That single decision tells you most of what you need to know about this village at the mouth of the Fraser: a place that ran on the labour of people it did not treat as citizens, and that those people made into a home anyway. Steveston sits on the southwest tip of Lulu Island in Richmond, and at its peak it was the salmon canning capital of the British Columbia coast, with sailing ships loading tins for export and a summer population drawn from four continents.

## Before It Had That Name

For thousands of years this was home to Halkomelem-speaking peoples, with at least two Musqueam villages here. One of them, qʷeyaʔχʷ, stood east of what is now Garry Point Park, on ground that has since been a cannery site, a boatyard, and a picnic lawn. The name Steveston arrived in the late 1870s with Manoah Steves, who came out from Moncton, New Brunswick, by way of Chatham, Ontario. He had been born Manoah Steeves and dropped one of the e's somewhere along the journey. The townsite began in 1880 as a Crown grant to his son William Herbert Steves, and over the following decade more than a hundred buyers took up lots in a grid of 237 parcels. It became Steveston in 1889, the first subdivision in Richmond.

## Tins, Sails, and Boatyards

Salmon canning started on the Fraser in 1871, and the first major plant, the Phoenix, was established in 1882 by Marshall English and Samuel Martin. What followed was one of the great seasonal migrations on the coast. Every summer, Japanese, Chinese, First Nations and European fishermen and cannery workers arrived to work the run, swelling the year-round village many times over, and the labour was divided along lines that everyone understood and nobody wrote down. Sailing ships tied up at the port to load canned salmon for markets across the Pacific and around the Horn. The fishery supported a serious boatbuilding industry alongside it. The Gulf of Georgia Cannery, built in 1894, was at one time the largest plant in British Columbia; it became a National Historic Site in 1994, as the Britannia Shipyard had in 1991.

## The Fire Truck Broke Down

A town of wooden canneries, wooden hotels, and wooden boardwalks, packed with oil and netting, had exactly the fire history you would expect. The first serious blaze hit the Star Cannery in 1897 and required extensive rebuilding. A 1908 fire in the east end did over $35,000 in damage. Steveston ran its own fire department only from 1912 to 1917; otherwise the nearest hall was at Marpole, half an hour to an hour away depending on the roads. In 1918 fire broke out in a dining and recreation area of the Star Cannery and took more than half a million dollars with it, destroying three canneries, three hotels, and most of Brick Block. The Marpole fire truck broke down on the way.

## Homma Goes to London

Tomekichi Homma settled here in 1883 and became one of the central figures of the Japanese community. In 1900 he tried to register to vote. He was a naturalized British subject, and the registrar, Thomas Cunningham, turned him down under a provincial law barring Asian Canadians and First Nations people from the rolls. Homma took it to the Vancouver County Court that November and won. The province appealed, and in 1902 the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London reversed the decision, holding that British Columbia was entitled to decide who voted in it. That precedent stood for decades, and equal voting rights for all Canadians did not arrive until 1960. Homma Elementary School in Steveston carries his name.

## Twelve Hundred Boats

Before the Second World War, roughly two thousand Japanese Canadians lived in Steveston, about two thirds of the village and the second largest Japanese settlement in Canada after Vancouver's Japantown. The day after Pearl Harbor the federal government ordered every Japanese Canadian fishing boat into port, and by late December 1941 about 1,200 vessels had been seized and towed up the Fraser, stored badly, damaged, and sold off cheap. From 1942 some 22,000 Japanese Canadians were removed from the coast and sent inland, their property auctioned to pay for their own internment. Some came back when they were finally permitted to. In 1954 BC Packers manager Ken Fraser donated a lot for a joint community centre, on the condition that the Japanese Fishermen Benevolent Association have a judo room in it. Canning ceased in the 1990s, but more than six hundred fishing boats still work out of Steveston Harbour, the largest fleet in Canada, and at Garry Point the Fisherman's Memorial stands in the shape of a giant net needle, carrying the names of those who did not come back from the water.

## Flight Context

Steveston lies at 49.128N, 123.181W, on the southwest corner of Lulu Island in Richmond, at the mouth of the South Arm of the Fraser River. From the air the orientation is easy: the Fraser's broad brown outflow plume spreading into the Strait of Georgia, the Steveston jetty running west along the south edge of the channel, and the dense working harbour packed with fishing vessels behind it. Garry Point Park is the green wedge at the westernmost tip. Vancouver International Airport (CYVR) sits about eight kilometres north on Sea Island, directly over this area's approach and departure paths, so Steveston is inside busy controlled airspace and overflight requires coordination. Boundary Bay (CZBB) is about 15 kilometres southeast. Look east to the Coast Mountains behind Vancouver, and south across the delta to Mount Baker in Washington. Best viewed at 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL where airspace permits. Fog forms readily over the delta in autumn and winter.
