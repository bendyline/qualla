# Richmond Olympic Oval

An Olympic speed skating rink built on 300 metres of river silt, roofed with beetle-killed pine, and backed by a contingency plan to fly the whole competition to Calgary if an earthquake moved the floor.

**Location:** 49.1747, -123.1514

**Categories:** olympics, architecture-and-engineering, sports-venues, british-columbia, vancouver

## Introduction

The International Skating Union permits a speed skating surface to deviate by three millimetres over three metres, and by no more than twenty millimetres across the entire four-hundred-metre track. Richmond, British Columbia, agreed to deliver that on ground made of up to three hundred metres of silt, in the delta of the Fraser River, in one of the most seismically active regions in North America. A 2005 geological report said plainly that a significant earthquake before the Games could leave the floor out of tolerance, and the organising committee wrote a contingency accordingly: if the ground moved, speed skating at the 2010 Winter Olympics would be relocated to Calgary. The ground did not move. Three Olympic records were set on that ice instead.

## Two Thousand Columns of Rock

Making delta mud hold still is a specialised problem. Construction began not with foundations but with compressing the ground itself: two thousand columns of rock driven twenty-six metres down, and a hundred thousand cubic metres of sand piled on top to squeeze the water out of the silt beneath. A follow-up report in December 2005 concluded it still was not enough, so franki piles went in as well. Paying for them meant finding $1.9 million in a budget that had already been trimmed, and the money was found by making the building four and a half metres narrower than planned. The finished structure swallowed 335,280 cubic metres of concrete and 5,600 tonnes of steel. Construction ran from 2006 to a 12 December 2008 opening, at a final cost of $178 million - and it was the last of all the 2010 Olympic venues to be completed.

## A Roof Made Out of a Disaster

The most striking thing about the oval is overhead. Its roof is built from wood killed by the mountain pine beetle, whose spread across the interior of British Columbia devastated forests and the towns that depended on them - roughly 305 board-kilometres of it, a million board-feet, deliberately put on display to prove that beetle-killed timber was a material worth using rather than a liability to be written off. CannonDesign shaped the building's elements to echo a heron, the bird that stalks the Fraser delta flats just outside. The lead architect, Bob Johnston, had already built the Olympic ovals in Calgary and Salt Lake City. A feng shui consultant worked on the design as well, in recognition of Richmond's large Chinese Canadian population. The building earned LEED Silver certification, captures rainwater off that roof, and recycles waste heat from the ice plant's refrigeration system. It was not flawless. In April 2008, three months after the roof was completed, fungi and algae turned up in its lower insulation layer, and seventy percent of the roof membrane had to be pulled off and replaced at a cost of $2 million.

## Why Richmond and Not Burnaby Mountain

The bid book had promised the oval to Simon Fraser University, on top of Burnaby Mountain, at a projected $63.7 million - a plan that would have given each of Greater Vancouver's two major universities an Olympic venue. On 17 August 2004 the organising committee chose Richmond instead. The new site cost roughly twice as much, but Richmond offered to absorb everything above $60 million and had something Burnaby Mountain did not: the new Canada Line running past the door. The city assembled its share from a revenue-sharing arrangement with the River Rock Casino Resort worth $50 million, $54 million tied to nearby real estate development, and sponsorships. The real estate part turned out to be the shrewd half. A seven-hectare parcel beside the venue went on the market asking $40 million and sold for $141 million. Luxury condominium towers now stand where the parking lot might have been, in a district planned with no surface parking at all.

## Slow Ice and Cheap Bleachers

Speed skaters prefer thin air, and the Richmond Olympic Oval sits four metres above sea level. Denser air means more drag, which means slower ice, and nobody expected world records here - the venue was ranked only the eleventh-fastest sheet in the world as of 2013. Three Olympic records fell anyway. The oval had already hosted the 2009 World Single Distance Speed Skating Championships before the Games arrived, and afterwards it did what it was always meant to do: in April 2010 it reopened as a community multi-sport park, with two hockey rinks, two running tracks, a climbing wall, a rowing tank, and a floor that converts for basketball, volleyball, indoor soccer, and table tennis. The Olympic furniture was liquidated along the way. A set of bleachers seating 800, worth $300,000 new, was sold to Vancouver Island University for its gymnasium. The price was four thousand dollars.

## Flight Context

The Richmond Olympic Oval stands at 49.175 degrees north, 123.151 degrees west, at 6111 River Road on the south shore of the middle arm of the Fraser River in Richmond, British Columbia, immediately south of Vancouver. It sits essentially next door to Vancouver International Airport (CYVR), roughly three kilometres east of the runway thresholds and directly beneath that airport's controlled airspace - so this is a place to observe from an assigned altitude and routing, not to loiter over. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet the building is unmistakable: a long, low, wave-curved roof beside the river, ringed by the glass towers of the River Green development, with the flat green geometry of Lulu Island stretching south and the North Shore mountains standing behind Vancouver to the north. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CXH) handles downtown floatplanes, Boundary Bay (CZBB) lies southeast, and Abbotsford (CYXX) is 55 kilometres east. Expect winter rain and low ceilings over the delta, frequent morning fog in autumn, and clear northwesterly flow in summer.
