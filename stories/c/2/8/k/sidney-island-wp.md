# Sidney Island

The north end of this Gulf Island is a national park reserve with a famous sandspit; the southern eighty percent is a strata corporation with 111 owners, making Sidney Island one of the stranger legal objects in British Columbia.

**Location:** 48.6106, -123.3008

**Categories:** Islands of the Gulf Islands, National park reserves of Canada, Greater Victoria, Shorebird habitat, Coast of British Columbia

## Introduction

Sidney Island is, for most of its area, a condominium. The southern eighty percent of it is organized as a strata corporation -- the same legal instrument that governs apartment buildings -- divided into 111 lots, each between one and three acres, each with two hundred to four hundred feet of its own oceanfront. The northern end is the opposite: federal land, part of the Gulf Islands National Park Reserve, open to anyone who can get there in a boat. One island, one shoreline, two entirely different theories of who owns a view. Between them runs a line on a survey plan that nobody standing on the beach could possibly detect.

## Named by the Men With the Charts

In 1859 Captain George Henry Richards, running the Royal Navy's survey of these waters, put the name Sidney on this island in honour of Frederick W. Sidney, a fellow officer of the same survey branch. That was how most of the Gulf Islands got their English names: a small group of naval surveyors working from open boats, methodically labelling an archipelago after each other, their superiors, and their ships. The names stuck because the charts stuck, and the charts stuck because everything that came afterward -- land titles, ferry schedules, fishing licences -- was written on top of them. The island is administratively split even now. It belongs to the Capital Regional District for the purposes of government, but its land titles are legally described in the Cowichan Land District, an inheritance from those same nineteenth-century survey lines.

## The Spit

The reason most people come is a long curving tongue of sand reaching north off the island's tip. Sidney Spit is the sort of feature that looks improbable on a chart and obvious in person -- a low bar of pale sand and driftwood, reshaped by every winter, with shallow warm water on the inside and Sidney Channel running past the outside. It was a provincial marine park before it became part of the national park reserve. Boaters use twenty-one mooring buoys and a government dock; in summer a privately operated passenger ferry runs across from the Sidney Pier for people who arrive on foot. Twenty-six walk-in backcountry campsites sit behind the beach, with pit toilets and picnic tables and very little else. There is no potable water -- the groundwater here carries too much sodium -- and no campfires are permitted, so you bring your water in and your rubbish out.

## A Rest Stop on a Longer Flight

The spit matters to more than campers. It is an important resting place for migrating shorebirds, one of the links in a chain of tideflats and sandbars that runs the length of the Pacific coast. Birds arriving here have flown a very long way and will fly a very long way more, and what they need from Sidney Island is undramatic: shallow water, exposed sand at low tide, invertebrates in the mud, and a few hours of not being disturbed. In spring and autumn the flocks work the tideline in tight, wheeling groups, lifting all at once for no reason a human observer can see. The island rises only 77 metres at its highest point. Almost everything that makes it ecologically significant happens in the last few horizontal metres before the water.

## One Hundred and Eleven Owners

The strata arrangement on the southern portion is unusual enough to be worth sitting with. Instead of a single owner, or a subdivision of freehold parcels, or a Crown lease, the bulk of a Gulf Island is held collectively by a corporation whose shareholders are its residents -- with common property, bylaws, meetings, and a shared responsibility for roads, water, and fire. It gives the place a peculiar character: not quite a village, not quite a private estate, and reachable only by private boat or floatplane from Sidney, four kilometres away across the channel. On a clear evening the lights of the Saanich Peninsula are close enough to read, and the ferries to Swartz Bay pass within sight. It is one of the least remote remote places on the coast.

## Flight Context

Sidney Island lies at 48.611N, 123.301W in the southern Gulf Islands, immediately east of the town of Sidney across Sidney Channel. From the air the landmark is unmistakable: the pale hook of Sidney Spit curving north-northwest off the island's tip, bright against dark water, with an enclosed shallow lagoon behind it. James Island, with its distinctive bare bluffs, sits just to the southwest; the Saanich Peninsula and Victoria International Airport are 4-6 km west; Sidney Channel and Haro Strait separate the island from the San Juan Islands and the international boundary to the east. Maximum elevation is only 77 m, so the island reads as flat and heavily forested apart from the spit and its beaches. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 feet at low tide, when the sand flats show their full extent. Nearby airports: Victoria International (CYYJ) about 4nm west, Friday Harbor (KFHR) about 10nm east across the boundary, and Victoria Inner Harbour water aerodrome (CYWH) about 14nm southwest. Expect floatplane and airline traffic on the Victoria approaches immediately west of the island.
