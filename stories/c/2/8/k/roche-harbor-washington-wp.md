# Roche Harbor, Washington

A Coast Salish village site turned Royal Marine outpost turned lime company town on San Juan Island - now a resort harbour whose founder lies uphill in an open-air mausoleum of his own limestone.

**Location:** 48.6097, -123.1490

**Categories:** Company Towns, San Juan Islands, Industrial Heritage, Indigenous Heritage, Washington, National Register of Historic Places

## Introduction

Walk up the hill behind the marina and you come to a table set for a family that is not coming. Six limestone columns stand around an open rotunda; a seventh is deliberately broken off partway up. In the middle is a round limestone table, and pulled up to it are stone chairs with names carved into their backs - John S. McMillin, his wife Louella, four of their children, and Adah Beeny, who worked for the family. Each chair is hollow, and each holds the ashes of the person named on it. McMillin built it six years before he died in 1936, for $30,000, out of the same rock his company had been quarrying and burning at Roche Harbor for half a century. It is the least subtle statement ever made about a man's relationship with a hillside, and it is genuinely moving to stand in.

## Whelaalk

Before it was Roche Harbor it was whelaalk, spoken WH'LEHL-kluh - a Coast Salish community that stretched from this harbour across to Lonesome Cove opposite Speiden Island and, in the mid-1800s, held ten large longhouses. Reid Harbor on nearby Stuart Island was KWUH-nuhs, whale. These were settled places on rich salmon water, fished with reef nets - a Straits Salish method that depended on coordinated crews and on inherited knowledge of exactly where and when the runs would pass. The names have survived better than the buildings. In 2016, representatives of the Lummi Nation, the Saanich First Nation and the National Park Service held a ceremony at Pe'pi'ow'elh - the site the maps call English Camp - to dedicate a Reef Net Captain totem pole and two Salmon Story Boards, putting that history back on the ground where the interpretive signs had mostly told someone else's.

## Two Flags, One Island

The British came to survey and stayed to argue. Lieutenant Richard Roche served under Captain Henry Kellett in 1846 and Captain James Charles Prevost from 1857 to 1860, and it was Roche who scouted this coastline for a suitable Royal Marine encampment - which is how a sheltered harbour on an American island ended up with an English officer's name. In 1860, with Britain and the United States both claiming the San Juans, the two governments settled on a joint military occupation while the boundary question went to arbitration. The Marines who camped here were not idle. They quarried and processed limestone at or near Roche Harbor, establishing the industry that would define the place long after they left. The dispute was resolved in 1872 in favour of the United States, and the Marines sailed.

## Burning the Hill

The land passed through hands quickly at first - homesteaded by Joseph Ruff, sold to the Port Townsend merchant Israel Katz, then to the Scurr brothers and their Ross partners, who worked the quarries through the early 1880s. The operation was simple in principle and punishing in practice: limestone came out of a quarry high on the slope, went down by rail to chutes, dropped into the kilns, and the burned lime was bagged or barrelled a short walk from the warehouse and wharf. In 1886 a Tacoma lawyer named John S. McMillin saw how much better it could be done. With partners including Hugh Campbell Wallace, later United States Ambassador to France, he bought the site and founded the Tacoma and Roche Harbor Lime Company. What he built around it was a company town in the full sense - cottages for employees, a church, a general store, a post office, a school, gardens and a farm, all of it owned by the firm that also owned the jobs. McMillin lived in the Hotel de Haro until 1910. Louella ran the post office and taught the community's children Sunday school. He founded a yacht club.

## Deep Water and a Customs Dock

The lime is long finished, and the town it paid for is now a resort, which is an unusually graceful second act for an industrial site. The harbour itself was always the real asset: one of the best-protected anchorages in the islands, held in by San Juan Island to the east, Pearl Island to the north, and Henry Island around the west and south, with most of the basin lying 35 to 45 feet deep. It faces Haro Strait and, directly across it, Canada. That makes Roche Harbor a designated United States port of entry, with a Customs dock in the marina and Customs and Border Protection agents on duty through the summer; outside those hours, arriving boaters tie up and telephone. There is a small airport used mostly by residents. And in 1966 a film crew came to shoot Namu the Killer Whale here, which was, for a brief moment, how most of America found out the place existed.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.610 N, 123.149 W on the northwest side of San Juan Island, San Juan County, Washington. The harbour is a well-defined pocket of protected water enclosed by Pearl Island to the north and Henry Island to the west and south, opening onto Haro Strait - and the international boundary runs down that strait a few miles offshore, so this is genuine border airspace. From above, the resort reads as white buildings and a dense marina at the head of the bay, with the McMillin Mausoleum on the wooded slope behind it. Vancouver Island and Victoria lie 15-20 km west across Haro Strait; Stuart Island and Speiden Island sit just north; the Olympic Mountains stand south beyond the Strait of Juan de Fuca, and Mount Baker anchors the eastern horizon. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500-3,000 ft MSL. Roche Harbor Airport (WA09) sits immediately inland and is small, uncontrolled and used mostly by locals; Friday Harbor (KFHR) is about 10 km southeast and Victoria International (CYYJ) roughly 25 km northwest across the border. Expect summer marine haze, strong tidal rips visible in Haro Strait, and heavy seasonal boat traffic below.
