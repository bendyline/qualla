# Afterglow Vista

A lime baron built his family a stone dinner table in the woods above Roche Harbor, seated them around it in limestone chairs holding their own ashes, and left one column deliberately broken.

**Location:** 48.6159, -123.1490

**Categories:** memorials, san-juan-islands, washington-state, architecture, local-history

## Introduction

Follow the trail out of the little cemetery above Roche Harbor and the forest opens onto a dinner party that has been going for ninety years. A flight of steps rises to a circle of Roman arches. Inside stands a heavy limestone table, and drawn up around it are six limestone chairs, each with a name carved into its back and each hollow, holding the cremated ashes of the person named. The McMillin family still sits down together on San Juan Island - a father, a mother, three sons, a daughter - in a structure their patriarch ordered six years before his death and never saw finished the way he intended. John S. McMillin called it Afterglow Vista. It is one of the strangest and most affecting memorials in the American West, and it was paid for by lime.

## The Column Broken on Purpose

Everything in the design means something. Six columns ring the rotunda, and a seventh stands deliberately snapped off partway up - the broken column of Masonic symbolism, understood to say that death breaks the column of any man's life, or that every life ends with its work unfinished. The steps climbing to the table are grouped to stand for the stages of life, the classical orders of architecture, the human senses, and the liberal arts and sciences, so that a visitor ascends through an entire curriculum before reaching the chairs. McMillin was a devout Methodist and an ardent Freemason, and the memorial has been called a Masonic landmark outright. The irony is that the broken column turned out to be the least of it: the design called for a dome over the rotunda, and the dome was never built. His son Paul left it open to the sky. Rain falls on the table. Leaves collect in the chairs.

## The Man Who Ordered It

John S. McMillin was born in Indiana in 1855 and educated at DePauw University, where he joined Sigma Chi and became the fraternity's first Grand Consul - its international president. In 1884 he brought his wife and eldest surviving son west to Washington Territory, where he took over the Tacoma and Roche Harbor Lime Company. Lime was the making of him. The northwest corner of San Juan Island holds some of the best limestone on the coast, and McMillin's kilns turned it into the mortar and plaster that built a great deal of Puget Sound. He was a lawyer, a businessman, and a political operator besides. And when he built his family's memorial, he built it out of the same white stone that had made him, at a cost of thirty thousand dollars. Construction finished in 1936, the year he died.

## Who Sits Where

The names on the chairs are a family's whole arithmetic. Louella Hiett, his wife. Fred Hiett McMillin, who died in 1922 at forty-two. Paul Hiett McMillin, who lived until 1961 and who made the decision to leave the dome off. Dorothy Hiett McMillin, born in 1894, who outlived them all and died in 1980, forty-four years after the memorial was completed and presumably knowing exactly which chair was hers. And John Hiett McMillin, born in 1878 and dead the same year - an infant who never had a life for a column to break. Whatever else Afterglow Vista is, it is a father's insistence that a son he barely knew belonged at the table with everyone else.

## The Seventh Person

There is one more set of ashes here, and she was not a McMillin. Adah Beeny - her name also appears as Ada Beane - worked for the family, in some accounts caring for the children and in others serving as John McMillin's personal secretary. She has no chair. Her ashes were placed inside the crypt of the infant John Hiett McMillin, sharing the memorial with the child who died before she ever could have known him. The record does not explain the arrangement, and it is not obvious that any explanation would improve on the fact itself. Afterglow Vista is looked after today by the Monuments and Memorials Commission of Sigma Chi, the fraternity McMillin first led, and anyone who walks up from Roche Harbor can stand at the table and read the chairs.

## Flight Context

Afterglow Vista lies at 48.616 degrees north, 123.149 degrees west, in the forest above Roche Harbor on the northwest end of San Juan Island, Washington. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 3,500 feet frames Roche Harbor's marina, the old lime works and kilns along the shoreline, and Westcott and Garrison Bays to the southeast - the latter being the site of the British camp from the Pig War. The memorial itself sits under tree canopy and is essentially invisible from the air; navigate to the harbour instead, which is unmistakable with its white resort buildings and dense boat moorage. Haro Strait runs west toward Vancouver Island, and Speiden Island lies just north. Roche Harbor has a small private airstrip on site; Friday Harbor (KFHR) is about 15 kilometres southeast on the same island, Orcas Island (KORS) is northeast, Bellingham International (KBLI) is on the mainland, and Victoria International (CYYJ) is west across an international border. Expect summer morning fog in Haro Strait and strong tidal currents visible offshore.
