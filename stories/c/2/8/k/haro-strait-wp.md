# Haro Strait

A German emperor decided in 1872 which of two channels would be the border, and Haro Strait won - making this tide-torn shipping lane the line between Canada and the United States.

**Location:** 48.5681, -123.2414

**Categories:** Coastal Waters, British Columbia, Washington, Borders, Maritime History, Wildlife

## Introduction

On October 21, 1872, Kaiser Wilhelm I of Germany signed off on a two-to-one decision by a three-man commission, and a border settled into place on the far side of the world from him. The question was which channel through these islands counted as the main one: Haro Strait, on the west, or Rosario Strait, on the east. Britain wanted Rosario, which would have kept the San Juan Islands. The Americans wanted Haro. The Americans got it, and to this day the line between Canada and the United States runs down the middle of a strait named by a Spanish captain for his pilot.

## The Road Before It Was a Border

For the Straits Salish peoples - the Lummi, the Saanich, the Klallam among them - these waters were never a boundary. They were a road. The channels flanking the Gulf Islands and the San Juans made this one of the most useful marine corridors on the coast, and it carried everything a road carries: families moving between seasonal camps, trade goods travelling long distances, and periodically raiding parties from the north. That utility is precisely why later powers fought over it. The Spanish arrived in 1790, when Manuel Quimper, commanding the Princesa Real, named the strait for his pilot, Gonzalo López de Haro. Francisco de Eliza sent José María Narváez deep into the Strait of Georgia through here in 1791, and George Vancouver explored and charted the strait in 1792. The historian Edmond S. Meany later suggested a different namesake - Alonso Núñez de Haro y Peralta, Archbishop of Mexico from 1772 to 1800 and, for a few months in 1787, Viceroy of New Spain - though the pilot has kept the credit.

## Which Channel Counts

The trouble began with the Oregon Treaty of 1846, which ran the border along the 49th parallel to the sea and then out through the middle of the main channel - without saying which channel that was. The British read it one way and the Americans the other, and the San Juan Islands sat in the gap. The dispute nearly turned into a shooting war in 1859 over a pig shot in a potato patch, and it stayed unresolved for another thirteen years before going to arbitration. Wilhelm I's commission chose Haro Strait, the San Juans became American, and the international boundary now runs from the western end of the 49th parallel segment down this channel, continuing north through Boundary Pass to reach the Strait of Georgia. On the water, the line is invisible. On the chart, it is the whole point.

## D'Arcy Island

One small island on the Canadian side carries a history that deserves to be stated plainly. In 1891, health officials in Victoria found five Chinese men living with leprosy in the city, and rather than treat them, the province put them on D'Arcy Island in Haro Strait. For the next thirty-three years, Chinese men in Canada diagnosed with leprosy were sent here regardless of where they had been living. Forty-nine people were sent in all - every one of them a man, all but one of them Chinese. There were no nurses, no doctors in residence, no clergy: the men were left to care for one another, with supplies delivered periodically by boat. At least seventeen died on the island and twenty were deported to China. The colony moved to Bentinck Island in 1924. White Canadians with the same illness were not treated this way, and the difference was not medical. D'Arcy Island is a park now, quiet and green, and the silence there is worth understanding rather than enjoying.

## Turn Point and the Rips

Haro Strait is the working channel of this coast. Together with Boundary Pass, it is the westernmost and most heavily used of the three routes linking the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the Strait of Georgia, and it is the main deep-sea approach to the Port of Vancouver. It is also considerably shorter than Rosario Strait, less sheltered, and harder to handle. The two meet at Turn Point on Stuart Island, marked by Turn Point Light, and the tidal rips there can be genuinely dangerous; the same holds at the northern end of Boundary Pass, in the stretch between Patos Island Light and East Point on Saturna. Canadian ships passing through American water here do so under the Oregon Treaty, which promised that commercial shipping south of the 49th parallel in these channels would go unmolested - a nineteenth-century sentence still doing daily work.

## Where the Fraser Salmon Turn

The strait matters as much to fish as to freighters. The bulk of the Fraser River salmon run enters the river system by way of Haro Strait, which makes this water central to the regional commercial fishery and to everything that eats salmon. Orcas hunt here. So do sea lions and seals, and the seabird list runs long. Whale-watching fleets sail out of Greater Victoria and the San Juans because the density and variety of marine life in this channel is reliable in a way few places are. Stand at Turn Point on a summer afternoon and you may watch a container ship, a pod of killer whales, and an international border occupy the same square mile of water.

## Flight Context

Haro Strait centres on roughly 48.5681°N, 123.2414°W, running between Vancouver Island and the Gulf Islands on the Canadian side and San Juan Island on the American side. From the air the geometry is clear: Victoria and the Saanich Peninsula west, San Juan Island east, Discovery Island and Cattle Point marking the southern gate into the Strait of Juan de Fuca, and Stuart Island with Turn Point Light at the northern junction with Boundary Pass. Deep-draft ship traffic threads the channel continuously - the wakes alone will show you the route. D'Arcy Island lies on the Canadian side northeast of Cordova Bay. Recommended viewing altitude is 3,000-5,000 ft MSL to read the island pattern and the shipping lane, or 9,000-10,000 ft to frame the whole archipelago with the Olympics south and Mount Baker east. Victoria International (CYYJ) is about 9 nm northwest and Friday Harbor (KFHR) about 9 nm east; Victoria Inner Harbour Water Aerodrome (CYWH) lies roughly 10 nm southwest. This is an international border - crossing it requires proper flight planning and customs arrangements on both sides. Expect summer afternoon westerlies through the strait, marine fog in late summer mornings, and low ceilings with rain through the winter.
