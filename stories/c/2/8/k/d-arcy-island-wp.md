# D'Arcy Island

For thirty-three years British Columbia exiled Chinese men with leprosy to this small island in Haro Strait, supplied four times a year and left to care for one another.

**Location:** 48.5664, -123.2789

**Categories:** Greater Victoria, Islands of the Gulf Islands, Gulf Islands National Park Reserve, History of Chinese Canadians, History of public health

## Introduction

The supply boat brought food, opium, and coffins. It came four times a year to a small island in Haro Strait where the City of Victoria had put men it did not want inside its boundaries, and the manifest tells you everything about what the city expected of them: enough to stay alive, enough to dull the pain, and a box for when neither worked. Between 1891 and 1924, forty-nine people were sent to D'Arcy Island. All of them were men. All but one were Chinese. There were no doctors on the island, no nurses, no priest, no staff of any kind for the first fifteen years. The men looked after each other, buried each other, and waited.

## Five Men in a Shack

In March 1891, health officers inspecting Victoria's Chinatown found five men with leprosy living together in a shack. The city's response was swift and total. Officials obtained D'Arcy Island, eighty-three hectares of uninhabited rock and fir about thirty kilometres from Victoria, from the provincial government, and by the end of May the five men had been moved there. What began as a response to one shack became a standing policy. For the next three decades, Chinese men diagnosed with leprosy anywhere in Canada could be shipped to this island, regardless of where they had lived or worked or how long they had been in the country. Leprosy, now called Hansen's disease, is in fact difficult to transmit and has been curable since the 1980s. That was not known in 1891. What was known, and acted upon, was who could be removed without political consequence.

## What the Quarterly Visit Looked Like

Every three months a small steamer came out from Victoria carrying a municipal health officer, a sanitary inspector, and an interpreter. They delivered provisions, conducted examinations, and left. That was the entire official contact these men had with the outside world for thirty-three years, four days a year of another human voice from the mainland. Sometimes the boat brought new arrivals. A reporter from a Victoria paper who made one of the trips came back convinced that the isolation was doing as much damage as the illness, which is not a difficult conclusion to reach but was an unusual one to print. The men grew vegetables, kept what routines they could, and managed their own dying. Their names were recorded inconsistently, and most of them are not recoverable now.

## The Federal Government Takes Over

In 1906 responsibility passed from the city to Ottawa, and conditions changed materially. New buildings went up. Caregivers were stationed on the island rather than visiting it. The policy shifted from confining Chinese men to treating anyone in Canada with leprosy, which is the difference between a quarantine and a hospital. It came fifteen years late for the men who had already died there. The station operated until 1924, when the remaining residents were transferred to Bentinck Island, closer to Victoria, and the D'Arcy buildings were left to the weather. Bentinck itself stayed open until 1957. Ruins of the D'Arcy Island structures are still visible in the second growth, foundations and fragments that an archaeological study has used to reconstruct something of how the men actually lived.

## A Convenient Position

The island's other claim on history is its geography. D'Arcy sits close enough to the international boundary that during Prohibition the Seattle bootlegger Roy Olmstead used it as a transfer point. His operation moved Canadian liquor, mostly whisky, out of Victoria to islands in Haro Strait, cached it, and then sent small fast boats to collect it, preferring rough weather because the United States Coast Guard had a harder time picking up small craft in a chop. It is a strange pairing of histories for one small island: an official use that depended on nobody looking, and an unofficial one that depended on exactly the same thing.

## Seven Campsites

D'Arcy Island was declared a marine park in 1961 and folded into Gulf Islands National Park Reserve in 2003. It is the southernmost of the Gulf Islands, reachable only by private boat, with no dock and no services. Seven backcountry campsites sit among the trees, equipped with pit toilets and picnic tables and nothing else. There is no drinking water and campfires are not permitted. People who make the crossing find a quiet forested island with good beaches and a view across Haro Strait to the San Juans, and many of them have no idea what happened here. Interpretation is minimal. The forty-nine men who were sent to this place have no monument, which is its own kind of statement about how long a country can take to look directly at something.

## Flight Context

D'Arcy Island lies at 48.566N, 123.279W, in Haro Strait about 30 kilometres from Victoria, south of Sidney Island and east of the Saanich Peninsula. From the air it is a small wooded island, roughly one kilometre across, with the larger and more obviously developed Sidney Island immediately north and Little D'Arcy Island just off its northeast shore. Look west to the Saanich Peninsula and Victoria International Airport (CYYJ), about 12 kilometres away; look east across Haro Strait to San Juan Island in Washington, about 10 kilometres off, with Mount Baker beyond it. The international boundary runs down Haro Strait just east of the island, so this is border airspace and requires the usual planning. Nearest fields are Victoria International (CYYJ) and Friday Harbor (KFHR) in Washington. Best viewed at 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL. Tidal streams through Haro Strait are strong and often visible as rips from the air.
