# Mount Baker Theatre

A 1927 Moorish-Spanish movie palace in downtown Bellingham whose searchlight once reached Blaine, still the largest theatrical room in Washington north of Seattle.

**Location:** 48.7522, -122.4770

## Introduction

A windstorm in October 2025 tore two letters off the spire, and months later the sign was still short a B and an A. It is a small indignity for a building that has been announcing itself over downtown Bellingham since 1927 — and announcing itself is precisely what this place was built to do. The spire once carried a searchlight with a range of about sixteen miles. On a clear night the beam was visible from Burlington to the south and Blaine to the north, an advertisement thrown across half of Whatcom County to tell people there was something on.

## The Light That Refused to Break

Getting the searchlight up there nearly ended badly. During construction, while the crew was hoisting it into position, the boom gave way under the weight and the light dropped into the framing below. It came through completely undamaged, which in 1927 must have read as either excellent engineering or a good omen, depending on who you asked. The electric sign went onto the front of the building on 25 April 1927, four days before opening. Both light and signage were part of the same calculation — a movie palace in a city of this size had to be visible from a distance, because a substantial share of its audience would be driving in from farms and mill towns across the county.

## Reamer's Spain, in the Rain

The architect was Robert Reamer, who had already given Seattle its 5th Avenue Theatre, and here he worked in a Moorish–Spanish idiom: arches, tilework, plaster ornament, warm colour, all of it deployed against the reality of a wet northern port city. The building takes up half a city block. Construction began in the autumn of 1926 for Pacific Northwest Theaters Inc., a chain then operating thirty-six movie palaces including the brand-new 5th Avenue and the Broadway in Portland. The estimated cost was $300,000, something like $5.6 million in today's money. In April 1927 a Style 215 Wurlitzer theatre organ went in — top of the line at the time, at $20,000, roughly $370,000 now — and it is still there, still playing.

## Opening Night, 29 April 1927

Bellingham treated the opening as a civic event. The surrounding streets were decorated, and a live band played outside the theatre from seven until ten. The first film to run was Slide, Kelly, Slide, a baseball comedy directed by Edward Sedgwick, and it held the screen for four days. George Douglas managed the house; for head usher he hired Doris Winters away from the United Artists Theater in Seattle. The usher corps was entirely women, in satin white dresses with red trim designed by Catherine Grofton — and, by policy, entirely brunette, because Douglas and Winters had decided that dark-haired staff suited the Spanish architecture. It is a detail that sounds absurd now and tells you exactly what these buildings were: not just rooms with screens, but total productions in which even the person showing you to your seat was set dressing. In August 1930 the theatre hosted a free show featuring Leo, MGM's lion.

## Judy

Staff have reported odd things here since at least the 1970s, and the stories have settled around a figure called Judy. In one version her house stood on this ground and was lost when the theatre was built; in another she was the daughter of a construction worker, killed in an accident during the build. The historical record is thinner and sadder than either: two houses and a church were demolished to clear the site, and whatever became of the people who lived in them is not recorded. Judy is said to keep to the corridor running from the balcony down to the mezzanine, and to favour the male staff, calling their names. The supporting cast includes a feral cat in the basement and a man in a pin-striped suit known as Geoffry. In August 2010 a team of paranormal investigators associated with the television series Ghost Hunters spent a night in the building. Old theatres acquire these stories the way old theatres acquire dust, and the staff who tell them are usually the ones most fond of the place.

## The Biggest Room North of Seattle

The main auditorium seats 1,517 between floor and balcony, which makes it the largest theatrical venue in Washington north of Seattle's Paramount and 5th Avenue — a distinction that keeps touring shows coming to a city of ninety thousand. The orchestra pit sits five feet below the stage and holds twenty-five musicians. Two smaller spaces round out the building: the Encore Room, a 1,200-square-foot hall for receptions and meetings, and the Walton Theatre, named for Harold and Irene Walton. The City of Bellingham owns the building and a nonprofit runs it, and it carries a listing on the National Register of Historic Places. Its neighbourhood has kept pace: the fifteen-storey Bellingham Tower next door, tallest building in Whatcom County, went up as a luxury hotel and is now offices over a café, and the 1891 city hall two blocks west has become the Whatcom Museum.

## Flight Context

The Mount Baker Theatre stands at 48.752°N, 122.477°W, in the historic arts district of downtown Bellingham, Washington. Best viewed from 1,500 to 3,000 feet. Downtown Bellingham is a compact cluster of low brick on a slope above Bellingham Bay, and the easiest way to find the theatre from the air is by the Bellingham Tower immediately to its west — fifteen storeys, the tallest structure for many miles in any direction, and impossible to confuse with anything else in the county. The bay opens southwest toward Lummi Island and the San Juans, the Nooksack River delta lies northwest, and Mount Baker itself — the volcano the theatre is named for — dominates the eastern horizon at 10,781 feet, usually snow-covered year round. Bellingham International (KBLI) is about 3 nautical miles northwest and is the controlling field; Skagit Regional (KBVS) lies roughly 20 nautical miles south and Abbotsford (CYXX) about 15 nautical miles north across the Canadian border, so mind the boundary. Expect low ceilings and rain through the winter, morning fog over the bay in autumn, and strong easterly outflow winds funnelling out of the Fraser Valley during cold snaps — the same kind of wind that took the letters off the spire.
