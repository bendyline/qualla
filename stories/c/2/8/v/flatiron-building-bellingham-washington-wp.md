# Flatiron Building (Bellingham, Washington)

Bellingham's first skyscraper was built to sell furniture, nearly killed by the Panic of 1907, gutted by fire in 1924, and gained a seventh floor without getting any taller.

**Location:** 48.7517, -122.4810

**Categories:** historic-architecture, flatiron-buildings, national-register, bellingham, pacific-northwest

## Introduction

For three years in the 1920s, Bellingham bought its sofas under a circus tent. A fire had gutted the top of T.S. Hamilton's furniture emporium on the evening of 28 April 1924, and while the building was rebuilt, Hamilton simply moved the business across the street and carried on trading, partly beneath canvas. The store had a certain right to stubbornness. Its building had already survived a lawsuit, a skating rink, a change of architect, and a national financial panic before it ever opened its doors -- and when it finally reopened after the fire, it had somehow acquired an extra storey without growing a single foot.

## A Rink, a Lawsuit, and a Better Offer

The triangular block bounded by Champion, Prospect and Bay Streets was prime downtown ground, and in late 1906 the Bellingham Bay Improvement Company hired the Seattle architect James C. Teague -- who would go on to design the nearby Exchange and Dahlquist buildings -- to draw up a five-storey concrete-framed block, clad in brick with stone at the base. The scheme imagined a bank or shops at street level with offices and lodge rooms above. Then litigation over the property set in, and somebody put a temporary skating rink on the site, and local opinion settled into the view that nothing would be built there for a good while. In April 1907, the Bellingham Herald reported otherwise: the Bellingham Bay Furniture Company, run by Talifero Simpson Hamilton and trading since 1889, had bought the block outright for $15,000 cash and was rushing plans for the city's first skyscraper. Teague's drawings were dropped in favour of a design by a local man, Frank C. Burns.

## Concrete, and a Panic

Burns produced something austere and practical: seven storeys of reinforced concrete with almost no ornament, topped by false chimneys and a large wooden cornice that is long gone. His cleverest move was in the windows. Large pivoting sashes wrapped every face of the building, flooding the floor plates with enough daylight that the usual central lightwell could be omitted entirely -- which meant more sellable floor space on every level. Two elevators went in, one for passengers, one for freight. The finished structure consumed thirty-five thousand barrels of cement and 200,000 pounds of steel at a cost of $100,000, and it was engineered to carry four more floors should anyone ever want them. Booker & Campbell took the general contract in June 1907, with Baumaster on the woodwork, and the first six storeys were to be done by the first of January 1908. Then the Panic of 1907 hit, construction across Bellingham stalled, and the deadline came and went. The contractors pushed to finish, the extra floors were quietly cancelled, and the building opened to the public in May 1908. For all that effort, it offered only about 4,000 square feet more than the furniture company's old quarters at Holly and Bay.

## What the Fire Proved

The 1924 fire started on the top floor and vented straight up through the roof, and it made the case for the material better than any advertisement could. Concrete and reinforced steel do not burn, and the structure came through intact. Ninety percent of the damage was insured, so rebuilding started almost at once and took three years. The reconstruction was an education in fire engineering. Bellingham's first automatic sprinkler system, fed by a rooftop tank, went into the building. The passenger elevator -- whose shaft had acted as the original fire's flue -- was moved to sit against the freight elevator, so that a future fire could be contained in one place. And the second-floor mezzanine was rebuilt as a full second storey. That is how the building became seven storeys tall without adding an inch to its height, and how a structure engineered to carry eleven floors ended up with an extra one by subdivision.

## Seventy-One Years of Furniture, Then Everything Else

Hamilton's company, later trading as Van's BB Furniture, stayed in the building until it closed in 1979 -- seventy-one years of selling chesterfields from a concrete flatiron. It was standing empty and owned by the City of Bellingham when it was added to the National Register of Historic Places on 27 January 1983. A major remodelling by Christenson Engineering followed in 1990, and the firm made the building its headquarters; Christenson was bought by VECO Corporation in 1993, and VECO by CH2M Hill in 2007, so the same offices changed letterhead twice without anyone moving. Logos Bible Software signed a long-term lease on 1 April 2010, taking over when CH2M Hill's lease ran out that August. In September 2025 the real estate technology company PLACE moved its headquarters in. The building was the tallest thing in Bellingham until 1926, and it has never stopped being the most recognisable.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.75 N, 122.48 W in downtown Bellingham, Washington, near the head of Bellingham Bay. The building fills a triangular block bounded by Champion, Prospect and Bay Streets and reads clearly from the air as a wedge in the street grid -- the same geometry that earned it the flatiron nickname after New York's Fuller Building of 1902. Bellingham Bay and its waterfront industrial flats lie immediately west; Mount Baker stands 30 nm east and dominates the horizon on clear days; the San Juan Islands open to the southwest. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL in late-afternoon light, when the low sun rakes across the concrete faces and the wedge shape throws a long shadow. Bellingham International Airport (KBLI) is about 2 nm northwest and its airspace covers the downtown, so obtain clearance before overflight; Orcas Island (KORS) is roughly 14 nm west across the bay. Expect marine stratus off the bay in the mornings and strong outflow winds from the Fraser Valley in winter.
