# Sehome, Bellingham, Washington

The first town on Bellingham Bay began when two loggers tipped over a tree and found coal in its roots; the mine is long gone, but the street grid, the hill and the name remain.

**Location:** 48.7400, -122.4760

**Categories:** Bellingham, Washington, Neighborhoods in Washington (state), Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Washington (state), National Register of Historic Places in Whatcom County, Washington

## Introduction

In 1852 two men working for Henry Roeder were logging on a claim south of Whatcom Creek when a tree came down and they saw something dark tangled in the root ball. It was coal. Roeder sold the land to the Bellingham Bay Coal Company, which assembled three thousand acres, sank a mine, and built a dock to load ships bound for California. Six years later a town went up around it - the first on Bellingham Bay, and today the neighbourhood of Sehome.

## A Chief's Name on a Company Town

The town was founded in May 1858 by Edmund C. Fitzhugh, the mine's manager, and he named it for his father-in-law, the S'Klallam chief Sehome. The name is the oldest thing in the neighbourhood, and it sits on ground that is the ancestral home of several Coast Salish peoples - the Lummi, the Nooksack, the Nuwhaha and the Samish - who had lived around this bay long before anyone measured it for a coal seam. The townsite itself was owned by the Bellingham Bay and British Columbia Railroad, which is a reminder of how these places actually began: not as communities that later attracted industry, but as industry that later acquired residents.

## Eighty People, Then 258

Growth was steady rather than spectacular. Sehome counted eighty residents in 1860 and 258 by 1870, and three years after that the mine employed a hundred men, white and Chinese - the categories the records of the day insisted on keeping separate, applied to people doing identical and dangerous work underground. The coal did not last. Quality declined, and in 1878 the Bellingham Bay Coal Mine closed. Sehome survived its reason for existing, and in 1891 it was amalgamated with three neighbouring settlements into New Whatcom, which was in turn renamed Bellingham. The town became a neighbourhood, which is a soft landing compared with what happened to most coal towns.

## The Hill

Sehome Hill rises 640 feet above the bay and dominates the neighbourhood's southern half. Most of it is now the Sehome Hill Arboretum, a 175-acre public park and nature preserve that is home to 92 species of birds and more than sixteen species of mammals, deer and coyotes among them - an unusually wild block of ground to find wedged between a downtown and a university campus. Below it, the streets run on the same grid as downtown Bellingham, with the north-south streets named alphabetically ascending from the waterfront and each block laid out with sixteen lots and an alleyway. It is a plan you can still read on foot a century and a half later.

## Streetcars, Then Buses, Then the Freeway

Streetcar lines once connected Sehome to the rest of Bellingham, and they did what streetcars always did - pulled housing out along their routes and created the residential blocks that give the neighbourhood its character. The system was replaced by buses in 1938. Thirty years later the car reshaped the same ground again: in 1969 the Bellingham Mall, the city's first suburban shopping centre, opened beside the freeway interchange at Samish Way in southeastern Sehome. Bellis Fair later eclipsed it, and after a 1991 rebuild it was renamed Sehome Village. North Samish Way is still the busiest street in the neighbourhood, carrying an average of 11,800 vehicles a day in 2023.

## What the Neighbourhood Kept

On 13 February 2001, after a three-year campaign by local residents, the Sehome Hill Historic District was added to the National Register of Historic Places - more than two hundred eligible properties on nine streets, most of them built before 1930. It is a listing that exists because people went looking for it. The neighbourhood's other institution has a longer run: St. Luke's Hospital, opened in 1892 by St. Paul's Episcopal Church in the former Grand Central Hotel with twenty beds, outgrew the building within three years, moved into a purpose-built hospital in January 1895, expanded into a larger one in 1927, and eventually became the sole designated trauma centre for Whatcom County. In 1989 its crosstown rival St. Joseph Hospital acquired it and renamed it the South Campus. Western Washington University, nearly fifteen thousand students strong, sits immediately southwest.

## Flight Context

Sehome lies at 48.74 N, 122.476 W, immediately south of downtown Bellingham. Bellingham International (KBLI) is only about 7 km northwest, so this is Class D airspace and a tower call is required - plan a transit at 1,500-2,500 ft. The visual anchors are excellent: the wooded dome of Sehome Hill at 640 ft between the downtown grid and the Western Washington University campus, Bellingham Bay opening west toward Lummi Island, Interstate 5 running north-south with the Samish Way interchange at the neighbourhood's southeast corner, and Mount Baker standing east above the foothills. Skagit Regional (KBVS) is roughly 35 km south. Expect marine stratus off the bay on summer mornings, mountain obscuration to the east, and strong northeasterly outflow winds through the Fraser Valley gap in winter cold snaps.
