# Fairhaven, Bellingham, Washington

A town founded by a man called Dirty Dan, inflated from 150 people to 8,000 in a single year by railroad speculators, left stranded when the tracks went to Seattle, and now legally required to keep looking like 1890.

**Location:** 48.7181, -122.5069

**Categories:** Bellingham, Washington, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Washington (state), Geography of Whatcom County, Washington, Fisheries in the United States, Immigrant history

## Introduction

Daniel Jefferson Harris was known around Bellingham Bay as Dirty Dan, a nickname he did not acquire for his manners. He arrived in the early 1850s, befriended a settler named John Thomas who had filed a claim along Padden Creek, and helped him start a cabin. Thomas died before it was finished. Harris completed the claim himself, received the patent in 1871, bought up the surrounding ground, and platted a town on it in 1883. Today every new building in the district he laid out is required by Bellingham's municipal code to match the elegant nineteenth-century brick style of its neighbours. It is a fastidious kind of immortality for a man whose contemporaries named him after his hygiene.

## Selling Lots and Cleaning Up

Harris called the place Fairhaven, taking it from a local Indigenous name recorded as see-see-lich-em, understood to mean safe port, and possibly also nodding to a town in Maine that may or may not have been his birthplace. He started selling lots in 1883, and as his fortune improved his appearance and reputation improved with it, to the point that he was able to marry in 1885. In 1888 he sold most of what he owned in Fairhaven to Nelson Bennett and departed for California, which is the classic move of a founder who has correctly read the top of his own market.

## One Hundred Fifty to Eight Thousand

Bennett teamed with Charles Larrabee, who arrived in 1890 and supplied most of the money, and together they formed the Fairhaven Land Company with the frank intention of building a major city out of nothing. Their promotional material promised abundant natural resources, agreeable weather, and limitless prospects, and it worked spectacularly. Fairhaven went from roughly 150 residents in 1889 to 8,000 by the end of 1890. Along the way the land company bought out a tiny neighbouring settlement called Bellingham, tucked between Fairhaven and Sehome, which had been running a post office since 1883. Fairhaven incorporated on 13 May 1890, at the absolute peak of its own story.

## Waiting for a Train That Went to Seattle

All of it was a bet on railroads. Coastal Washington towns spent the late 1870s through the 1880s competing to be named the western terminus of the Great Northern, and Fairhaven built itself as an argument: handsome brick commercial blocks, ornamental detail, streets laid out with a capital city's confidence. The grand Fairhaven Hotel went up during the 1890s speculative frenzy specifically to impress visiting railroad men. The terminus went to Seattle. Remarkably, the building boom continued into the late 1890s anyway, which is why the district looks the way it does. The hotel that was built to catch a railroad it never caught stood until fire and demolition finished it in 1953. The 1888 Terminal Building outlasted everything as Fairhaven's oldest surviving structure, until it was gutted by fire in 2023 and taken down.

## The Cannery and the Mob

What actually paid the bills was salmon. From the late 1800s into the 1940s, canning operations lined this waterfront, employing as many as 4,500 people, and Pacific American Fisheries, headquartered in Fairhaven, grew into what was described as the largest salmon cannery in the world. Its compound included a can factory, a box factory, a shipyard, and housing for more than a thousand Chinese labourers who did the hardest work in the plant and were steadily displaced as machines took over their tasks. The prosperity was not evenly shared, and the resentment it produced turned violent. On the evening of 4 September 1907, a mob of white workingmen in Bellingham attacked several hundred South Asian mill workers, most of them Sikhs from Punjab, driving them from their bunkhouses and out of the city. Within days there were no South Asians left in Bellingham. They had come to work; they were forced out for having done so.

## A Neighbourhood Again

On 27 October 1903, voters in Fairhaven and in neighbouring Whatcom City agreed to merge, and on 28 December the combined city of Bellingham came into being. Fairhaven stopped being a town and became a district, which in the long run preserved it: no one had the money to knock the old brick down and replace it. That same year Fairhaven won a Carnegie grant for the area's first library, built on 12th Street, and it still operates as a branch of the Bellingham system. The district now runs a seasonal farmers market and shows outdoor films every summer weekend at the Pickford Outdoor Cinema. It is also, unexpectedly, a transportation hub: since 1989 Fairhaven has been the southern terminus of the Alaska Marine Highway, meaning you can board a state ferry here and step off in Southeast Alaska, and Fairhaven Station handles Amtrak Cascades trains and intercity buses. A town that spent 1890 begging for a railroad terminal ended up as the place where the ferries to Alaska begin.

## Flight Context

Fairhaven lies at 48.718N, 122.507W, on the south side of Bellingham, Washington, fronting Bellingham Bay with Western Washington University on the ridge to its northeast. From the air, look for the bay's southeastern corner: the cruise terminal and ferry docks jut into the water, the compact grid of red-brick blocks sits just inland, and Padden Creek and the forested slopes of Chuckanut Mountain rise immediately south. Mount Baker's glaciated cone stands about 30nm east and is the dominant landmark in clear weather. Bellingham International Airport (KBLI) is roughly 4nm north-northwest, Orcas Island (KORS) about 20nm west across the water, and Friday Harbor (KFHR) about 25nm southwest. Best viewed at 1,500 to 3,000 feet on a north-south transit of the bay. KBLI's airspace covers the area, so coordinate before overflying; low cloud and marine fog are common over the bay in autumn and winter even when the Cascades are clear.
