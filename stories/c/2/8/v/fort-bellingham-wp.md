# Fort Bellingham

A U.S. Army stockade built on Bellingham Bay in 1856, dismantled by its own garrison and shipped to San Juan Island because of a shot pig - leaving behind one wooden house and a long shadow.

**Location:** 48.7833, -122.5560

**Categories:** Forts, Washington, Military History, Pig War, Historic Houses

## Introduction

The fort came apart because of a pig. In July 1859 an American settler on San Juan Island named Lyman Cutlar shot a Hudson's Bay Company hog that had been getting into his potatoes, and British authorities in Victoria threatened to arrest him. Brigadier General William S. Harney, commanding the Department of Oregon, responded by sending Captain George E. Pickett and his company from Fort Bellingham to the island. The British sent warships and marines. While the two forces glowered at each other across San Juan Island that summer, Pickett's men came back to Bellingham Bay, pulled their own post to pieces - including an entire two-story blockhouse - and hauled the timber to the island's southern shore, where they reassembled it as Camp Pickett. A fort that had stood for three years was disassembled by its own garrison and rebuilt eighteen miles away over a dead pig. Nobody fired a shot at anybody.

## The Only Open Ground on the Bay

The site was chosen for practical reasons. A prairie above Bellingham Bay, several miles north of the settlements and closer to the mouth of the Nooksack River, it was the only open space on the whole bay and it had a spring. It was also occupied. A settler named Maria Roberts had to be evicted so the Army could build, though she and her husband were afterwards permitted to put up a cabin on the beach - a small compensation, and a familiar nineteenth-century sequence in which a claim on paper outranks a person already living on the ground. Construction began on 26 August 1856, carried out by Captain George E. Pickett and Company D of the 9th U.S. Infantry Regiment, sent north from Fort Steilacoom.

## What the Settlers Were Afraid Of

The fort existed because the bayside villages of Fairhaven, Sehome, and Whatcom feared raids by Indigenous peoples arriving by canoe from further north - from what is now coastal British Columbia and Alaska. Those fears were not entirely invented; the mid-nineteenth century saw real violence along this coast, driven in large part by the upheaval that European trade, disease, and settlement had already introduced into Indigenous societies from Puget Sound to the Alaska panhandle. But the fort's official purpose flattened a complicated situation into a simple one, casting whole peoples as a standing threat. The result was an 80-yard square stockade with three gates, two-story blockhouses at opposite corners flanking walls loopholed for rifles and mountain howitzers, and inside it the ordinary apparatus of army life: barracks, storehouses, officers' quarters, mess hall, kitchen, and bakery.

## Taken Apart

After the Pig War crisis, what remained at Bellingham was cannibalized. Units stationed at the new San Juan Island post kept coming back for material to improve or repair their own buildings, and the fort was steadily reduced to a frame and a few sheds. In 1861 the Washington Territorial Legislature petitioned the federal government to station at least one company there and keep it open. Nothing came of it. The fort closed officially in 1863, by which point only the northwest blockhouse and a handful of other structures were still standing. In 1868 the Army returned 320 acres to Mrs. Roberts, who farmed the land for many years afterward - a rare instance of a settler getting her ground back. The last blockhouse burned in 1897. Today essentially nothing of the fort survives on its site.

## The House on Bancroft Street

One building outlasted everything else. The officers' quarters Pickett built in 1856 still stands at 910 Bancroft Street in Bellingham's Lettered Streets neighbourhood - a plain two-story house of undressed planks that is now the oldest building in the city and, by most accounts, the oldest wooden building in Washington State still resting on its original foundation. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1971. Its survival owes something to Hattie Strothers, who lived there from 1889 until her death in 1939 and deeded the house to the Washington State Historical Society in 1936.

## The Family That Lived In It

The house also held a story the Army's paperwork barely records. While stationed here, Pickett married a Native woman - thought to have been Kaigani Haida - whom he met at Semiahmoo Bay. Most accounts do not preserve her name, which is its own kind of statement about whose lives got written down. In 1857 she gave birth to a son, James Tilton Pickett, and died of complications within days. The infant was sent away to be raised by friends in Mason County. Pickett himself left the Northwest, joined the Confederate Army, and in July 1863 led the infantry assault at Gettysburg that carries his name into every American history book. The small plank house on Bancroft Street predates all of it - the charge, the war, the fort's disappearance - and it is the only piece of Fort Bellingham a visitor can still walk up to and touch.

## Flight Context

The Fort Bellingham site lies at 48.783 N, 122.556 W on the north shore of Bellingham Bay in Whatcom County, Washington, several miles north of downtown Bellingham and close to the mouth of the Nooksack River. Nothing of the fort remains visible, so navigate by landscape: the prairie bench above the bay, the Nooksack delta and its braided channels to the northwest, and Lummi Island and the Lummi Peninsula across the water west. A viewing altitude of 1,500-3,000 feet MSL takes in the whole bay with Mount Baker's snow cone dominating the eastern horizon in clear weather. The Pickett House, the one surviving structure, is in the Lettered Streets neighbourhood about 5 km southeast at 910 Bancroft Street. Bellingham International (KBLI) is roughly 3 nm south of the site, so expect approach and departure traffic at low altitude - check the class airspace before transiting. Orcas Island (KORS) is about 15 nm southwest and the Canadian border runs 12 nm north. Marine haze and low stratus are common from autumn to spring.
