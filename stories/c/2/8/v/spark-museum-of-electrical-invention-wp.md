# SPARK Museum of Electrical Invention

One man's radio collection and a retired Microsoft executive's rare-book library collided in Bellingham, Washington, and produced a museum that holds first editions from 1560 and fires a four-million-volt Tesla coil at visitors.

## Introduction

The oldest book in the building was printed in 1560. It sits in a small-city museum in Bellingham, Washington, a few blocks from Bellingham Bay, alongside first editions by Robert Boyle, Benjamin Franklin, Luigi Galvani, Michael Faraday, James Clerk Maxwell, Heinrich Hertz, Georg Ohm, Alessandro Volta, Hans Christian Orsted, Joseph Henry, William Gilbert, Pieter van Musschenbroek and C. F. du Fay - which is to say, most of the people who worked out what electricity actually is, in their own words, in the editions their contemporaries read. Downstairs, on a schedule, an eight-foot Tesla coil throws four million volts across the room. The SPARK Museum of Electrical Invention covers four centuries of invention, and it is one of the least likely institutions in the Pacific Northwest.

## Eight Hundred Radios

It began the way these things usually begin: one person who could not stop. In 1985 a Bellingham resident named Jonathan Winter was accumulating radio sets, spare parts, schematics, recordings and the vintage magazines and manuals that went with them - an informal collection, in the sense that nobody had yet decided it was a collection. It kept growing. By 1998 it had outgrown any private arrangement, and the Bellingham Antique Radio Museum was formally established with more than 800 of Winter's radio sets as its core. That is a very large number of radios for one person to have gathered in thirteen years, and it turned out to be the smaller half of what the museum would eventually become.

## The Executive's Library

The second half arrived in 2001. The museum moved into a 23,000-square-foot building and renamed itself the American Museum of Radio and Electricity, and in the same year John Jenkins - a former sales and marketing executive at Microsoft - retired and became co-curator. He did not arrive empty-handed. Jenkins brought an extensive personal collection of early wireless and electrical apparatus, and with it the rare books: first editions of the foundational literature of electricity, the oldest dating to 1560. A radio hobbyist's receivers and a technology executive's scientific library have very little to do with each other on paper. Put in the same building, they turn out to describe one continuous story, from the first deliberate spark to the living-room set. In 2012 the institution took its present name, SPARK Museum of Electrical Invention.

## Four Million Volts, on the Hour

The museum's signature performance is the Electrical Show, built around demonstration Tesla coils including the MegaZapper - eight feet tall and rated at four million volts. Watching manufactured lightning is a different experience from reading about it, which is the entire pedagogical argument. A static electricity learning centre lets visitors generate charge with their own hands, using and handling apparatus of the same kind used in the early experiments that first made the phenomenon tractable. The museum's education programme carries that method out into the region: assemblies and in-class courses for public, private and home-school students across Western Washington, plus Science Saturdays, summer camps and lectures at home. In 2018 alone it reached nearly two thousand students, teaching static electricity, magnetism, motors, circuits and how to build a crystal radio.

## Cases Full of Firsts

The permanent collection runs from 1580 into the 1950s and includes one of the largest holdings of nineteenth-century electromagnetic apparatus in the country - early telegraph and telephone equipment, electric motors, dynamos and induction coils. There are lamps from Thomas Edison's own laboratory. There is a complete set of Atwater Kent breadboard radios, a working RCA theremin, a 1909 Collins wireless telephone, a functioning RCA CT-100 colour television, a 1930s living room reconstructed down to the furniture, and a rare cabinet that combined a radio, a phonograph and a film projector synchronised to a record - sound-on-disc, the awkward solution that preceded putting the soundtrack on the film itself. A reproduction of the Titanic's radio room houses original Marconi equipment. In a case somewhere is a 1915 telephone used in the first transcontinental telephone call in the United States.

## Flight Context

The SPARK Museum is at 48.7515 N, 122.4800 W in downtown Bellingham, Washington, near the waterfront on Bellingham Bay. From 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, the landmark set is easy: the bay and its working waterfront to the west, the compact downtown grid with Western Washington University on the bluff to the south, Lake Whatcom stretching southeast behind the city, and Mount Baker standing over the whole scene to the east on a clear day. The San Juan Islands and Lummi Island lie to the west and southwest. Bellingham International Airport (KBLI) is roughly 3 nm northwest and is the obvious field; Orcas Island Airport (KORS) sits about 17 nm west across Bellingham Bay and Rosario Strait, and Skagit Regional (KBVS) about 17 nm south. The Canadian border is 20 nm north - mind the airspace. Expect marine stratus and fog off the bay through autumn and winter, and clear, stable air with excellent Mount Baker visibility on summer mornings.
