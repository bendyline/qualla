# Whatcom Falls Park

The creek that built Bellingham still falls through 241 acres of city forest - past a children's fishing pond, a cliff-jumping swimming hole, and a stretch of woods that has never fully recovered from 1999.

**Location:** 48.7521, -122.4330

## Introduction

Bellingham exists because of the sound this water makes. In 1854, Henry Roeder and Russell Peabody looked at Whatcom Creek dropping out of Lake Whatcom and saw a sawmill; the Coast Salish people who had fished the creek for generations already had a name for it, a North Straits Salish word usually rendered as "noisy waters." The mill became a settlement, the settlement became a city, and the creek - improbably, for four miles of urban waterway - kept its forest. Today 241 acres of that forest are a public park, and the falls that once turned saw blades draw children with fishing rods and teenagers with more nerve than judgment.

## Four Falls and Two Parking Lots

The park holds four separate sets of waterfalls strung along the creek's descent toward Bellingham Bay, connected by several miles of trail that switch between second-growth conifer and ferny, dripping ravine. An arched stone bridge crosses the upper falls, mossed over and photographed so often it has become the park's unofficial signature. Most of what happens here is gloriously ordinary. Bellingham Technical College runs a trout hatchery inside the park on behalf of the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife, giving students a working fishery and the city a stocked pond reserved for anglers fourteen and under. There are two tennis courts and athletic fields near the upper lot, playgrounds at both entrances, picnic shelters the parks department rents by the afternoon, and a pump track for bikes. A historic railroad trestle stood over the creek until the city removed it in 2023, documented first in a virtual tour so the structure would outlive its timbers.

## The Cliffs at Whirlpool

Whirlpool Falls is not the tallest drop in the park - barely ten feet - but it is the one everyone means when they say they went to the falls. The water lands in a deep punchbowl pool, and the rock walls beside it rise to roughly thirty feet. On hot afternoons a queue forms along the top. Someone counts, someone jumps, and the sound carries up the Whirlpool Loop Trail long before you reach the water. The city tried, for years, to stop it. After the disaster of 1999 swimming here was formally outlawed; warning signs went up, then came down, then went up again. Fences were cut. Eventually Bellingham conceded the point, pulled the barriers, and let the swimming hole be a swimming hole. Confusingly, people often call this spot Middle or Lower Whatcom Falls. It is neither. The actual middle falls sit downstream near the park's western edge with no trail to reach them, and the lower falls are a couple of miles further on, just above the estuary at Maritime Heritage Park.

## June 10, 1999

The reason the middle falls have no trail is the reason this park appears in federal safety law. On a June afternoon in 1999, a sixteen-inch gasoline pipeline running beneath the park failed. An automatic valve had closed for reasons never fully determined, sending a pressure surge down the line; a relief valve that should have absorbed it had been improperly configured and did not open. The pipe split. More than two hundred thousand gallons of gasoline poured into Whatcom Creek and ran downstream, and the vapor found an ignition source. Liam Wood, eighteen, was fly fishing below the rupture and was overcome by fumes before the fire came. Wade King and Stephen Tsiorvas, both ten years old, were playing along the creek upstream. They died the following day. A mile and a half of forest burned. The three of them were not statistics to this city; they were a recent high school graduate with a fishing rod and two fifth-graders on an ordinary summer afternoon.

## What the Creek Carries Now

The legal consequences reshaped an industry. The Olympic Pipe Line Company and its operators faced a seven-count federal indictment, pleaded guilty, and paid a settlement of $112 million - at the time the largest in the history of American pipeline enforcement, and the first criminal conviction of a pipeline company under the Hazardous Liquid Pipeline Safety Act of 1979. In 2007, Bellingham named a new elementary school for Wade King after his parents donated $400,000 to the school district. Walk the trails today and the recovery is legible in the trees: a band of uniformly young alder and conifer where the mature forest burned, the ground beneath still designated too sensitive for public access. Upstream, none of that is visible. The stone bridge holds its moss, the hatchery raises its trout, the children fish the pond, and somebody is almost certainly about to jump off a thirty-foot rock into cold green water. A park can hold both things at once. This one has had to.

## Flight Context

Whatcom Falls Park lies at 48.7521 N, 122.4331 W, on the eastern edge of Bellingham, Washington, roughly four nautical miles southeast of Bellingham International Airport (KBLI). From 2,500 to 4,000 feet AGL the park reads as a dark green wedge of timber pushing into an otherwise gridded residential city - follow Whatcom Creek's wooded corridor west from the broad blue of Lake Whatcom toward Bellingham Bay and the park is the widest section of green along it. Lake Whatcom itself, a long finger of water running southeast into the foothills, is the unmistakable navigational anchor. Bellingham Bay and the San Juan Islands sit to the west, Mount Baker's glaciated cone roughly 30 nautical miles east. Nearby fields include Bellingham International (KBLI), Skagit Regional (KBVS) 25 nautical miles south, and across the border Abbotsford International (CYXX) and Vancouver International (CYVR). Marine layer and low stratus are common on summer mornings and through the wet season from October to April; the clearest viewing is on late-summer afternoons, when low sun rakes across the creek ravine and separates the park's canopy from the city around it.
