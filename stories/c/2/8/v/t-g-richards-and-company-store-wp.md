# T. G. Richards and Company Store

Washington's oldest brick building arrived by ship from San Francisco - 100,000 bricks and 73 barrels of lime, delivered to a gold-rush boomtown in 1858.

**Location:** 48.7547, -122.4860

**Categories:** Commercial buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Washington (state), Buildings and structures in Bellingham, Washington, National Register of Historic Places in Whatcom County, Washington, Museums, History

## Introduction

On 16 July 1858 the bark Ann Parry came into Bellingham Bay carrying a building. Not assembled, obviously - the Daily Alta California's shipping column listed her cargo as 100,000 bricks and 73 barrels of lime, loaded in San Francisco, and a second vessel, the D M Hall, may have brought the windows and doors. Thomas G. Richards and his brother Charles put it all together into a two-storey store at a cost of eight thousand dollars, and it still stands. It is the first and oldest brick building in the state of Washington, and it was built to sell supplies to men on their way to a gold rush in somebody else's country.

## The Fraser Canyon Outfitters

The town was Whatcom, in Washington Territory, and in 1858 it had a reason to exist: gold had been found on the Fraser River, north of the border, and thousands of prospectors were moving up the coast. Whatcom positioned itself as the place to stop, buy what you needed, and continue. The T. G. Richards Company store was the durable expression of that ambition - brick in a town of green lumber, which in a settlement built overnight out of whatever was to hand was an extraordinary statement of confidence. A note in the files of the local historian Howard Buswell, now held at the Center for Pacific Northwest Studies, credits John Alexander, a prominent settler from Whidbey Island, with running the construction. In the spring of 2012 the building's bricks were scientifically matched to a fragment from Nagle, an early brickmaker, confirming the San Francisco origin.

## A Courthouse Where Nobody Was Tried

The rush passed. In 1863 Whatcom County bought the building for two thousand dollars - a quarter of what it had cost to build five years earlier - and made it the county's third courthouse. It is still commonly called the Territorial Courthouse, which it was, in the sense that it held the county's government before statehood. It was not, however, where anyone stood trial: court matters were handled at Port Townsend. What the building did handle was everything else. It served as the county jail. In 1873 it became home to the Bellingham Bay Mail newspaper, and by 1877 a pharmacy was operating on the first floor. In 1883 the county added a vault and five more jail cells.

## Declared Unsafe

The sea was the first problem. The building originally stood at the edge of the tide flats, and in 1874 a breakwater had to be built to keep the water off its foundation. By 1889 it was deemed unsafe for use altogether; the county offices and the jail decamped to temporary quarters, and in 1891 Whatcom County moved into a new courthouse on G Street built of Chuckanut sandstone. The old store did not empty. In 1890 and 1891 the Women's Relief Corps, an organisation supporting veterans of the Civil War, began renting it, and by 1906 the county had deeded it into private hands - sold to members of the Grand Army of the Republic, J. B. Steadman Post No. 24, together with the Relief Corps.

## The Floor That Became a Basement

Then the city buried it. As the tide flats were filled in, the grade of E Street rose around the building until the original first floor was below ground and the second floor had become the entrance. What had been a waterfront store was now a basement with a shop on top. The occupants that followed read like a century of American small-city life compressed into one address: the Junior Order of United American Mechanics, a Jehovah's Witness congregation, Aker's Taxidermy, The Northwest Passage - an alternative newspaper of the 1960s and 70s - an outfit called Base Camp, and finally a series of artists and potters.

## Helen Loggie's Museum

It was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 2003, sold in 2019, and given a complete period restoration finished in 2020. Since 2021 it has housed the Helen Loggie Museum of Art. Loggie, who lived from 1895 to 1976, was a Bellingham artist who trained at the Art Students League of New York under Robert Henri and George Luks and later studied privately with the etcher John Taylor Arms, and she spent her career making small, exceptionally detailed drawings and etchings of Pacific Northwest trees and coastline. Putting her work inside the oldest brick walls in Washington is a good match. Both are records of this landscape made with more patience than the moment required.

## Flight Context

The T. G. Richards and Company Store stands at 48.7547 N, 122.486 W, on E Street in downtown Bellingham, Washington, a block or two back from the Bellingham Bay waterfront. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500-3,000 ft AGL. The building is small and will not be picked out individually from the air, so orient on the waterfront: the old industrial shoreline of Bellingham Bay with its log ponds and marine terminals, the Alaska Ferry terminal at Fairhaven to the south, and the compact downtown grid between them. Lummi Island and Portage Island sit across the bay to the west, with the San Juans beyond; to the east, Lake Whatcom stretches back into the hills, and Mount Baker's glaciated cone dominates the horizon roughly 50 km east-southeast. Nearest airports: KBLI (Bellingham International) about 5 km northwest, KORS (Orcas Island) about 35 km west across the water, and KAWO (Arlington Municipal) about 55 km south. Low cloud and rain are the default from October to April; Mount Baker generates its own weather and can be clear when the lowland is not.
