# Olympic Pipeline Explosion

On 10 June 1999, a ruptured gasoline pipeline poured a quarter of a million gallons into Whatcom Creek in Bellingham, Washington; ninety-seven minutes later it ignited, killing an eighteen-year-old fisherman and two ten-year-old boys.

**Location:** 48.7521, -122.4330

**Categories:** Disasters in Washington (state), Industrial fires and explosions in the United States, Bellingham, Washington, Pipeline safety, 1999 in Washington (state)

## Introduction

For ninety-seven minutes, Whatcom Creek ran pink. A sixteen-inch pipeline carrying gasoline from the Cherry Point refinery south toward Seattle and Portland ruptured at 3:25 on the afternoon of 10 June 1999, beneath Whatcom Falls Park in Bellingham, Washington. Roughly 277,000 gallons went into the creek bed. People all over the neighbourhood smelled it and called 911. The Bellingham Fire Department arrived to find the water discoloured and the fumes overwhelming, and began clearing the area. Everyone involved understood, in a general way, what a creek full of gasoline could do. What nobody could do was make it stop, or make it evaporate, or find every person already inside the park.

## 5:02

The vapour found its ignition source at 5:02 p.m., and the creek became a fuse. A fireball ran downstream along the water, consuming the gasoline in the channel and setting the forest on either side alight. The fire burned at over 2,000 degrees Fahrenheit, hot enough to gut buildings it never touched directly, and the smoke column climbed to thirty thousand feet -- visible from Vancouver to Anacortes, a black pillar over a mid-sized city on a clear June evening. Interstate 5 was closed. The Coast Guard halted marine traffic in Bellingham Bay, because nobody could be certain the burn would not follow the creek all the way to salt water. It stopped short of the freeway. Windows shattered across the neighbourhood, one house was destroyed outright, and inside the city's water treatment plant -- which stood close enough that the building survived but its contents did not -- everything was ruined. Bellingham's water was kept safe for weeks afterward by staff adding treatment chemicals by hand.

## Liam, Wade, and Stephen

Three people died. Liam Wood was eighteen and fly fishing in Whatcom Creek that afternoon, upstream of everything, doing the most ordinary thing a person can do on a June day in Bellingham. The fumes rendered him unconscious and he fell into the water and drowned, before the explosion, before anyone knew he was there. Wade King and Stephen Tsiorvas were both ten years old and playing near the confluence when the vapour ignited. Both survived the blast itself. Both died the following day at Harborview Medical Center in Seattle, of burns. Eight other people were injured. In 2007 the Bellingham School District named a new elementary school for Wade King, after his parents gave $400,000 toward it -- a decision that put a ten-year-old's name on a building full of children roughly his age, which is one of the few things a community can do with that kind of grief.

## A Chain of Small Failures

No single mistake caused this. A pressure relief valve had been improperly configured and did not open when it was needed. An automatic valve on the line closed for reasons that were never established, and with the relief valve inoperative, the pressure surge had nowhere to go. The sixteen-inch pipe failed. Compounding everything, a section of the line had been damaged earlier by excavation work whose operator had not called in to have the pipeline located and the damage reported. Each of these is a routine industrial lapse of the sort that happens constantly and harms nobody. Stacked in the right order, on the right afternoon, under a park where children played and a young man fished, they produced a $58 million disaster and three funerals.

## The Reckoning

In 1999 the Olympic pipeline was owned and operated by Equilon, a joint venture of Shell Oil and Texaco; by 2006 it was 65 percent Enbridge and 35 percent BP. Investigators spent three years on the case. In 2002 Olympic Pipe Line Company and Equilon faced a seven-count indictment, pleaded guilty to several charges, and settled for $112 million -- a record at the time, and the first criminal conviction of a pipeline company under the Hazardous Liquid Pipeline Safety Act of 1979. Regulators and pipeline operators across the country were watching, and the case became a reference point for what happens when a fuel line is run without adequate safeguards through a populated area.

## Whatcom Falls Park Today

The park is open, busy, and green again. Families walk the trails; the falls still draw photographers; children play near water that no longer smells of anything but water. Look carefully, though, and the 1999 fire is legible in the forest -- areas still posted as sensitive, stands of young trees where mature ones burned, hazard trees that never fully recovered. A generation is not very long in the life of a Pacific Northwest forest. A creek that was, for ninety-seven minutes, the most dangerous place in Washington state has gone back to being an ordinary urban park, which is both a relief and the reason the three names matter. The pipeline is still there. It is still carrying fuel.

## Flight Context

The rupture and fire occurred in Whatcom Falls Park at approximately 48.752N, 122.433W, in eastern Bellingham, Washington, where Hannah Creek joins Whatcom Creek. From the air the site reads as a green wooded corridor running west through the city street grid toward Bellingham Bay, with Lake Whatcom immediately east and Interstate 5 crossing the creek about two kilometres downstream -- the freeway marks the line the 1999 fire did not pass. The Bellingham waterfront and bay lie west, the Cherry Point refinery complex about 15 km northwest along the shoreline, and Mount Baker fills the eastern horizon. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 feet, low enough to distinguish the younger regrowth along the creek corridor from the mature canopy around it. Nearest airports: Bellingham International (KBLI) about 3nm west, with its approach paths over the city, and Skagit Regional (KBVS) about 20nm south. Note terrain rising sharply east toward Lake Whatcom and the Cascade foothills.
