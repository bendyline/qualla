# Bellingham Cruise Terminal

In October 1989 Alaska moved its ferry system's southern gate out of Seattle and put it here — a single Fairhaven building where a train, a bus and a three-day voyage to Skagway all meet.

**Location:** 48.7217, -122.5125

**Categories:** Ferry terminals in the United States, Bellingham, Washington, Transport infrastructure, Alaska Marine Highway

## Introduction

In October 1989, the state of Alaska moved its front door. For years the Alaska Marine Highway System had run its mainline ferries out of Seattle; that autumn, under a twenty-year lease, the southern terminus shifted ninety miles north to Bellingham, Washington, and the newly completed Bellingham Cruise Terminal became the place where the Lower 48 hands travellers over to the Inside Passage. It is an unassuming building in the Fairhaven neighbourhood, at the end of a road off Interstate 5. What happens there is not unassuming at all. Walk aboard on a Friday and you will not touch land again until Ketchikan, and you will not reach the end of the line for nearly three days.

## Just Under Three Days to Skagway

The mainline run north is one of the great scheduled voyages left in North America. The MV Columbia and the MV Kennicott work the route from Bellingham, threading the Inside Passage — the sheltered channel behind the islands where the water stays flat while the open Pacific rages a few ridges west — and calling at Ketchikan, Wrangell, Petersburg, Sitka, Juneau, and Haines before terminating at Skagway. End to end, the trip takes just under three days. These are not cruise ships. They are working state ferries carrying cars, freight, residents, and travellers who want the Inside Passage without a cruise fare. Alaska-bound sailings also call at Prince Rupert, British Columbia, which gives northern BC a direct link to the contiguous United States that exists nowhere else.

## Everything in One Building

What makes the terminal genuinely unusual is how many modes of travel converge on it. Immediately adjacent stands Fairhaven station, served by Amtrak's Cascades trains — the northernmost stop in the United States, with the line continuing north to Vancouver, British Columbia, and south through Seattle to Portland and Eugene, Oregon. The terminal building itself, owned by the Port of Bellingham, doubles as the Greyhound bus station. Whatcom Transportation Authority local buses connect it to the rest of Bellingham, and private shuttles run to Bellingham International Airport. The whole complex is reached from Interstate 5 by way of State Route 11. Arrive by train, cross a parking lot, board a ferry to Alaska: very few places in the United States let you do that without a car.

## The Smaller Boats

For all the drama of the Alaska run — weekly Friday departures and arrivals, with an additional summer sailing on alternating Saturdays — most of the terminal's traffic is local. Private operators run passenger ferries out to the San Juan Islands. Whale watching boats and charter vessels work out of here, along with small cruise ships that use Bellingham as a home port. Altogether the facility handles more than 200,000 passengers a year. That number is the real story of the place: it is not primarily a gateway to a distant frontier but a working waterfront for a city of roughly 95,000 people, whose weekends, fishing trips, and island errands all pass through the same doors.

## The Point Roberts Run

In September 2020 the terminal took on a job nobody had planned for. Point Roberts, Washington, occupies the tip of a peninsula that dangles below the 49th parallel, cut off from the rest of the United States by the Canadian mainland — which means its residents normally reach their own country by driving through British Columbia. When the Canada-United States border closed during the COVID-19 pandemic, that ordinary commute became impossible, and an entire American community found itself severed from its own country by a line on a map. A temporary ferry began running from Bellingham to give them a way home. It is a good illustration of what a terminal like this is for. Most of the time it is a place to catch a boat. Occasionally it is the only way in or out.

## Flight Context

The Bellingham Cruise Terminal sits at 48.7217 degrees north, 122.5125 degrees west, on the Bellingham Bay waterfront beside the Fairhaven neighbourhood in Bellingham, Washington. Best viewed from 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL on a north-south track along the bay — look for the terminal pier and its adjacent rail yard at the southern end of the Bellingham waterfront, with the Fairhaven passenger station alongside and a large ferry frequently alongside the berth on Fridays. Bellingham Bay opens west toward Lummi Island and the San Juans; Mount Baker stands up prominently to the east on a clear day, and Chuckanut Drive (State Route 11) traces the shoreline south. Nearest airports: Bellingham International (KBLI) about 8 km north, Friday Harbor (KFHR) across the water in the San Juan Islands, and Vancouver International (CYVR) roughly 70 km northwest in Canada. NOTE: the international border lies about 30 km north — verify airspace and crossing requirements. Morning fog and low stratus over the bay are common in autumn and winter.
