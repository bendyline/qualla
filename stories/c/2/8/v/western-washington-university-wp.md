# Western Washington University

The northernmost university in the contiguous United States keeps Noguchis and Serras out on the lawn, and opened the country's first college devoted to environmental science in 1969.

**Location:** 48.7340, -122.4860

**Categories:** Universities and colleges in Bellingham, Washington, Public universities and colleges in Washington (state), Universities and colleges established in 1893, Public art collections, Western Washington University

## Introduction

There is a Richard Serra on the lawn, and nobody is guarding it. Western Washington University began collecting outdoor sculpture in 1960, and the works that have accumulated on its Bellingham campus — more than thirty of them, by Isamu Noguchi, Donald Judd, Mark di Suvero, Nancy Holt, Robert Morris, Beverly Pepper, Anthony Caro, Bruce Nauman, and others — sit out in the weather where students walk past them, sit on them, and arrange to meet beside them. Funded by the Washington State Arts Commission, the National Endowment for the Arts, and private donors, the collection has turned a public university on a hillside above Bellingham Bay into an outdoor museum that charges nothing and closes never. It is the most visible sign of an institution that has spent its whole existence being less conventional than its category suggests.

## Phoebe Judson's School

The lineage starts with a woman and a small town. In 1886, Phoebe Judson founded the Northwest Normal School in Lynden, Washington — a teachers' school chiefly for women, though men also enrolled. The school later moved to the settlement then called New Whatcom, and on 24 February 1893 Governor John McGraw signed the legislation establishing the state-funded New Whatcom Normal School in its place. Construction of a permanent building began in 1895 and finished in 1896, but it could not open: the original contract had not covered heating, lighting, or grounds work, and students had to wait until the money was found. That building, designed by the Seattle architects Warren Skillings and James Corner, is Old Main, still the administration building. The first official class entered in 1899 with 88 students and had grown to 264 by the end of the year.

## Five Names in Eighty-Four Years

Few institutions have been renamed as often or as revealingly. It became the State Normal School at Whatcom in 1901 when the town changed its own name; the Washington State Normal School at Bellingham in 1904, when Whatcom and Fairhaven merged into one city; Western Washington College of Education in 1937, on becoming a four-year college; Western Washington State College in 1961; and finally, in 1977, Western Washington University. Each name records a promotion. The 1960s were the decisive decade — enrollment went from 3,000 students to more than 10,000 in ten years, and a teachers' college turned into something considerably larger and stranger.

## 1969

Three things happened at Western at the end of that decade, and together they explain the place. In 1967 the Fairhaven College of Interdisciplinary Studies opened, built around the kind of non-traditional, interdisciplinary teaching that Evergreen State College, in Olympia, would adopt four years later. In 1969 Western founded the College of the Environment, the first college in the United States dedicated to environmental science, at a moment when the field barely had a name. That same spring, students made the news by blocking Interstate 5 in protest against the Vietnam War. And also in 1969, the university established a College of Ethnic Studies. It met significant resistance, and it was dismantled in 1978. Western's institutional history holds both of those facts at once: an early and genuine willingness to build new kinds of colleges, and a demonstration that building one is not the same as keeping it.

## A Wind Tunnel and Two Electron Microscopes

The Bellingham campus occupies 215 acres on a slope above Bellingham Bay with the San Juan Islands laid out to the west, 90 miles north of Seattle and 55 miles south of Vancouver, British Columbia, with Mount Baker about an hour's drive east. Thirty-eight of those acres are the Sehome Hill Arboretum, run jointly with the city. The facilities list reads like a catalogue of a university that kept saying yes: an electronic music studio, an air pollution laboratory, a motor vehicle research lab, a marine research lab, a wind tunnel, and two electron microscopes. Automobile Magazine once called Western very possibly the best school in the country for total car design — an unexpected verdict on a campus better known for its trees. There is more of it elsewhere: the Shannon Point Marine Center at Anacortes, a fifteen-acre waterfront facility at Lake Whatcom, and county land used for environmental and aquatic research.

## Vikings and Volunteers

Roughly 14,700 students attend, about 86 percent of them Washington residents, making Western the state's third-largest university. The athletic teams are the Vikings, competing in NCAA Division II since September 1998, with national championships in women's rowing, men's basketball in 2012, and women's soccer in 2016. Football was played here from 1903 until 2008, when the university folded the program to keep its other teams competitive. The statistic that says the most about the student body, though, came from the Peace Corps: in 2024 Western was ranked the top all-time producer of Peace Corps volunteers among medium-sized colleges and universities in the United States. Alumni have gone in every direction — the sociologist Douglas Massey, the painter Agnes Martin, the musicians Carrie Brownstein and Ben Gibbard, the civil rights activist Elizabeth Peratrovich, and, improbably, the man who invented Pictionary.

## Flight Context

Western Washington University occupies 48.7340 degrees north, 122.4860 degrees west, on the south side of Bellingham, Washington. Best viewed from 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL on a north-south track along the Interstate 5 corridor — the campus reads clearly from the air as a dense cluster of institutional buildings on a hillside, backed immediately to the east by the dark wooded dome of Sehome Hill Arboretum and facing west over Bellingham Bay. The bay, Lummi Island, and the San Juan Islands fill the western view; Mount Baker stands up unmistakably to the east; the Bellingham Cruise Terminal and Fairhaven lie a few kilometres southwest along the shore. Nearest airports: Bellingham International (KBLI) about 8 km northwest, Friday Harbor (KFHR) across the water in the San Juans, and Vancouver International (CYVR) roughly 70 km northwest in Canada.
