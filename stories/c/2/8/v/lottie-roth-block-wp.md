# Lottie Roth Block

A Bellingham sandstone building put up as an advertisement for the quarry that made it, named for the woman whose family owned the stone.

**Location:** 48.7561, -122.4872

**Categories:** Commercial buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Washington (state), Buildings and structures in Bellingham, Washington, National Register of Historic Places in Whatcom County, Washington, Romanesque Revival architecture in Washington (state), Quarrying

## Introduction

Charles Roth put his wife's name on the building, and for once that was the right way round. Lottie Roeder Roth was the daughter of Henry Roeder, one of the first settlers on Bellingham Bay, who opened a sandstone quarry near Teddy Bear Cove in 1856 and shipped blocks out by schooner across Chuckanut Bay. Charles married into the family and took over the operation, which became the Roth Stone Quarry. Lottie became an early historian of Whatcom County. The three-storey block that carries her name stands at the corner of Holly and G Streets in Bellingham, and every square foot of its stone facing was, in the most literal sense, a sales pitch.

## A Sample Card Three Storeys High

Roth was a quarry manager and a Washington state legislator, and in 1890 he commissioned a commercial and office building for one of his properties at the northeast corner of Holly and G, on the northern fringe of the Whatcom business district. He specified that the west and south elevations, the two faces that people on the street would actually see, be clad in Chuckanut sandstone from his own Bellingham Bay quarry. This was not sentiment. It was advertising, a full-scale demonstration of what his stone looked like when a good architect used it. Construction began in the summer of 1890 and finished in 1891. Several other substantial buildings around town were going up in the same material at the same time, including the Pike Block at Holly and Elk and the Whatcom County Courthouse.

## The Architect Seattle Made

The man Roth hired was Elmer H. Fisher, and the reason he was available at all was a fire. When downtown Seattle burned in June 1889, Fisher became the central figure in rebuilding it, producing a run of Romanesque Revival designs with heavy round-arched stone facades that gave Pioneer Square the character it still has. Roth had seen that work and wanted it. He had also supplied stone for it, since the Roth quarry shipped Chuckanut sandstone south for Seattle's reconstruction and later for San Francisco's after 1906. The Lottie Roth Block was one of Fisher's last large commissions and the only building he ever designed in Whatcom County, which makes it a piece of Seattle architectural history that happens to stand ninety miles north of Seattle.

## The Town Walked Away

In February 1891, with the block essentially finished, Whatcom merged with the adjacent settlement of New Whatcom. Commercial development promptly moved toward the newer townsite, which sat higher and flatter and was easier to build on, and the Roth Block found itself stranded on the wrong end of the district it had been built to anchor. The surrounding blocks began shifting toward industrial use. In 1903 the merged municipalities became the city of Bellingham, whose downtown developed in the other direction entirely. A building conceived as the vanguard of a commercial expansion ended up as its high-water mark, an office block with a shrinking reason to contain offices.

## Eighteen Apartments

By 1912 the answer was obvious, and a young local architect named F. Stanley Piper carried it out. The interior was gutted and rebuilt as eighteen apartments. Piper gained a floor by splitting the tall retail level in two, then filled in the old storefronts with smaller windows and more sandstone, preserving the material logic of the facade while quietly converting a commercial building into a residential one. A single shop was kept at the lower corner. The building has been housing people ever since, which is now well over a century of continuous residential use and a considerably longer career than it had as an office block.

## What the Stone Outlasted

Its contemporaries did not fare as well. The Pike Block and the Whatcom County Courthouse, both built of the same Chuckanut sandstone in the same boom, were demolished in the middle of the twentieth century, when American cities were confident that old stone buildings were a problem rather than an asset. The Lottie Roth Block survived partly by being useful and partly by being overlooked, on a corner nobody wanted to redevelop. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 12 December 1978. The quarry is long closed and the boom it advertised never fully arrived, but the advertisement is still standing, still doing exactly what Charles Roth built it to do: showing anybody who walks past what his stone could look like a hundred and thirty years on.

## Flight Context

The Lottie Roth Block stands at 48.756N, 122.487W, on West Holly Street near downtown Bellingham, Washington, a short distance inland from the Bellingham Bay waterfront. From the air, orient on the bay itself and the working waterfront at its head, with the compact downtown grid rising on the slope behind; the building is a small three-storey masonry block at the northwest edge of that grid and is easy to lose among its neighbours. Look south along Chuckanut Drive to Chuckanut Bay and Teddy Bear Cove, where the sandstone in its walls was quarried. Mount Baker rises 10,781 feet to the east, about 50 kilometres away and the dominant landmark of the region. The San Juan Islands lie west across the bay. Bellingham International Airport (KBLI) is about five kilometres northwest, which puts the downtown inside its airspace. Best viewed at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL. Low overcast is the default here for much of the winter.
