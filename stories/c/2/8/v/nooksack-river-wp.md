# Nooksack River

A glacier-fed river off Mount Baker whose two forks are named in the Nooksack language for murky water and clear water - and which, when it floods, does most of its damage in another country.

**Location:** 48.7748, -122.5991

**Categories:** Rivers, North Cascades, Flooding, Salmon Habitat, Indigenous Heritage, Washington

## Introduction

Two of the forks are named for what the water looks like. In the Nooksack language the Middle Fork is Nuxwt'iqw'em, always-murky water, because it comes off the glaciers of Mount Baker carrying rock flour and never entirely clears. The South Fork is Nuxw7iyem, always-clear water. They meet near Deming within a few miles of each other and become one river, and anyone standing at that confluence after a warm spell can watch the distinction dissolve. It is a precise piece of observation encoded into a name, from people who had been reading this drainage for a very long time before anyone charted it - and it is still, after everything, accurate.

## Three Forks Off One Volcano

The Nooksack drains the north side of the North Cascades: the valleys around Mount Shuksan, Mount Baker and the Twin Sisters, plus a slice of the Fraser Lowland that reaches up across the Canadian border. It has no single source. Three main forks - North, Middle and South - all rise in the Mount Baker Wilderness and converge near Deming to make the river proper. The North Fork is the longest, and is sometimes treated as the main stem for that reason. The Middle Fork runs about twenty miles from the southern slopes of Mount Baker near Baker Pass, threading northwest through the gap between Baker and the Twin Sisters, briefly ducking south into Skagit County before turning back north past Acme to join the others near the State Route 9 bridge, southeast of Deming and the Nooksack Indian Reservation. From there it runs west across the Whatcom County lowland, through farm country, to salt water.

## Dams In, Dams Out

Like most accessible rivers in the region, the Nooksack was put to work. A dam on the North Fork near Nooksack Falls generates hydroelectric power for the small town of Glacier. In the late 1950s the City of Bellingham built a diversion dam on the Middle Fork to send water over into Lake Whatcom, which is the city's drinking supply - a piece of engineering that solved a municipal problem and blocked a salmon river in the same stroke. Seventy years of changing priorities followed. By July 2020 the old diversion had been modified, the dam removed, and the channel restored specifically to reopen critical spawning habitat. It is a small project by the standards of Northwest river restoration, and a fairly clean illustration of what the region now values differently than it did when the concrete went in.

## The Log Jam That Moved a River

Rivers on a flat delta do not stay where you left them. Through most of the nineteenth century the Nooksack delivered the bulk of its flow near the mouth through what is now the short Lummi River, out into Lummi Bay northwest of Bellingham Bay. Around the turn of the twentieth century a log jam plugged that channel, and the river simply went somewhere else - carving the course it occupies today. The old delta at Lummi Bay now receives water only when the river is running high. The new one has been building ever since, and the wetlands forming on it are an active subject of research: a chance to watch an estuary assemble itself from the beginning, on a timescale short enough for people to measure.

## A River That Floods Another Country

The Nooksack floods. Heavy coastal rain, some of the deepest snowpack in the country, and the atmospheric-river weather pattern known as the Pineapple Express combine to overwhelm the lowland channel with real regularity - Everson alone flooded fourteen times between 1936 and 2003. In November 1990 a flood did more than $20 million in damage and closed the Trans-Canada Highway for twenty-six hours. In 1995 another cost Whatcom County $4 million. Then in November and December 2021 the river spilled north across the boundary and refilled the Sumas Prairie in British Columbia's Fraser Valley, forcing evacuations, triggering landslide emergencies, and inflicting roughly a billion dollars of damage on Abbotsford. December 2025 brought worse again at home: around 1,248 houses damaged along the river, 159 of them severely damaged or destroyed, Sumas, Nooksack and Everson hit hardest, four levees compromised, and county damages past $23 million - more than 2021 had cost Whatcom County. What makes the problem so durable is that the water and the money are in different jurisdictions. Washington has been reluctant to fund works that mainly protect Canadians; British Columbia has been reluctant to invoke the International Joint Commission. The river, which has no opinion about the boundary, keeps going where the ground is lowest.

## Flight Context

The lower river runs near 48.775 N, 122.599 W in western Whatcom County, Washington. From the air the Nooksack is easy to follow: a pale, braided, sediment-heavy channel winding west across the flat green farm country between Bellingham and the Canadian border, past Lynden, Everson, Nooksack and Ferndale, with obvious levee lines and oxbow scars marking where it has been before. Trace it upstream and it forks near Deming into three valleys climbing into the North Cascades; Mount Baker (3,286 m) and Mount Shuksan (2,783 m) dominate the eastern horizon and are the anchors for the whole drainage. The Lummi River branch and Lummi Bay lie southwest of the present mouth. Recommended viewing altitude is 3,000-6,000 ft MSL for the lowland and channel, higher for the volcanoes. Bellingham International (KBLI) is roughly 15 km south of the lower river, Abbotsford (CYXX) about 25 km north across the border, and Skagit Regional (KBVS) some 45 km south. Expect low cloud and valley fog over the floodplain in autumn and winter, strong outflow winds down the Fraser Valley in cold snaps, and rapidly deteriorating conditions during atmospheric-river events.
