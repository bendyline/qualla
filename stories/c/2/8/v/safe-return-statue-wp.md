# Safe Return (statue)

A bronze fisherman throws a mooring line at the edge of Bellingham's Squalicum Harbor, memorialising the crews who never got to make the throw.

**Location:** 48.7540, -122.5009

**Categories:** Monuments, Bellingham, Washington State, Maritime History, Public Art

## Introduction

Some of the hands that helped build this statue belonged to the children of the men it commemorates. Safe Return stands at Zuanich Point Park in Bellingham, Washington, at the edge of Squalicum Harbor, and it shows a fisherman in the act of casting a mooring line: the exact gesture of a boat coming in, a rope thrown toward a dock, a crew home. That is the whole idea. The memorial is for local fishermen lost at sea, and the moment it chose to freeze in bronze is the one they did not get.

## An Anchor Bought for the Price of a Net

The first memorial here was modest: a plaque set at Zuanich Point in 1975. What joined it was stranger and better. Local histories tell of a fisherman named Jay Gould who snagged an anchor near Port Gamble and destroyed his net doing it, a small catastrophe of the sort that ends a season's profit in an afternoon. Tom Glenn, then manager of the Port of Bellingham, bought the anchor from him for $2,500, which happened to be the cost of replacing the netting, and installed it at Squalicum Harbor. A ruined net became a rusted monument. It stood there as the harbour's first real acknowledgement that the sea takes things, and sometimes people, and gives nothing back.

## Eight Hundred Boats

Whatcom County had been a centre of the regional fishing industry since the nineteenth century, and Puget Sound's first fish cannery opened up the coast at Blaine. The work was never confined to home waters. Fishermen out of Bellingham ran the entire Pacific seaboard: cod and pollock off Alaska, herring down in California, crab in the channels right outside the breakwater. Washington's commercial fishery entered a long decline from the 1970s onward, and today the local scene leans heavily on recreational fishing with limited commercial catches. Even so, by 1996 Squalicum Harbor held the largest fishing fleet in northwestern Washington, some eight hundred boats, which gives a sense of how many families in this town had someone on the water.

## How Do You Sculpt a Winch?

Following the March 1993 sinking of the fishing vessel Lady of Good Voyage, which killed four Bellingham fishermen, the Squalicum Harbor Fisher's Memorial Committee formed to replace the plaque and the anchor with something larger, and together with the Whatcom Maritime Historical Society it began raising money the following year against an initial target of $200,000. The commission went to Eugene Fairbanks, a retired Bellingham doctor who as a boy had assisted his father, Avard Fairbanks, a prolific sculptor of American public monuments. Then came the problem. Modern commercial fishing is mechanised almost end to end, and Fairbanks struggled to find an image of it that would work as sculpture. Hydraulics do not make good bronze. The solution came from his son John, a local fisherman, who suggested the one motion that has not changed and never will: a man throwing a line to the dock.

## Trucked from South Dakota

A scale model existed by 1996. The finished statue was built largely by Eugene Fairbanks and his brother Justin, helped by a local college student and by the children of fishermen who had died, which is the kind of detail that changes how you look at the finished object. It was shown publicly, without its pedestal, at Zuanich Point Park on 6 September 1998, and then went into storage at a seafood restaurant at the harbour while the base was prepared. The granite plinth came by truck from South Dakota and was set in place on 26 May 1999, after which the bronze was lifted onto it by crane. The dedication took place at a public ceremony on Memorial Day 1999. Statue and plinth together stand sixteen feet tall and weigh more than thirty-eight thousand pounds; the bronze figure alone, cast slightly larger than life, is eight feet and eighteen hundred pounds.

## The Names, Still Being Added

The plinth carries the names of local fishermen lost at sea, running from the early 1900s to the present. The Bellingham Herald's working list held around eighty-six names, and twenty-five were nominated for the monument at the outset; more have been added since it was erected, because the sea has not stopped. Around the base, relief panels show the working world these people came from: a purse seiner, Squalicum Harbor itself, a ship's wheelhouse, and a Native American fisherman, a reminder that the industry commemorated here is recent compared with the fishing that preceded it on this coast. Stand at the monument and the harbour is right there behind it, boats moving, lines being thrown for real. That is the point of putting a memorial where the work still happens.

## Flight Context

Safe Return stands at 48.7540 N, 122.5009 W in Zuanich Point Park, on the spit of land enclosing Squalicum Harbor in Bellingham, Washington. Best viewing altitude is 1,000 to 2,000 feet; the statue itself is a sixteen-foot monument and is located by the park and harbour around it rather than picked out individually. Key visual landmarks: the enclosed basins and breakwaters of Squalicum Harbor with its dense rows of moored fishing and pleasure craft, Bellingham Bay opening southwest, the city and its waterfront immediately southeast, Lummi Island and the San Juans across the water to the west, and Mount Baker standing snow-covered on the eastern horizon. Bellingham International Airport (KBLI) lies roughly 2 nm north of the harbour, so this is Class D airspace with the harbour close under the pattern. Orcas Island (KORS) is about 18 nm west and Skagit Regional (KBVS) some 20 nm south. Expect marine stratus off the bay in the mornings and strong easterly outflow from the Fraser Valley in winter.
