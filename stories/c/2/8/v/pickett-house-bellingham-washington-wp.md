# Pickett House (Bellingham, Washington)

Bellingham's oldest house, built in 1856 by the army captain who would later lead the most famous doomed charge in American history - and the birthplace of his Haida son.

**Location:** 48.7561, -122.4860

**Categories:** washington, bellingham, historic-house, national-register, civil-war, indigenous-history

## Introduction

For the first years of its life, you reached the upstairs bedrooms of this house by ladder. It is a small two-storey box of milled fir on a rise above Whatcom Creek, put together in 1856 from boards cut at the Roeder-Peabody mill a short walk away, and it is the oldest house still standing in Bellingham. The man who built it was a captain in the United States Army, sent north to oversee the construction of Fort Bellingham on a frontier where the American claim was new and not universally accepted. Seven years later, on a hot afternoon in Pennsylvania, he would send his division across three quarters of a mile of open ground toward a stone wall on Cemetery Ridge, and his name would attach itself permanently to the most famous failed attack in American history.

## Boards from the Creek

George Pickett arrived in what was then the town of Whatcom with orders to build a fort, and picked out a spot on Peabody Hill for a house of his own. The lumber came from the Roeder-Peabody mill on Whatcom Creek - the same industry that had drawn settlers to this shoreline in the first place, and the reason a milled-lumber house was possible here at all in 1856.

The result is plain to the point of severity: two storeys, a lean-to at the back, and an interior arranged for a small household with no pretensions. What is remarkable is how little has changed. A narrow staircase eventually replaced the ladder to the upper bedrooms, and a kitchen was added to the lean-to. Beyond that, the building is very close to what was nailed together in the year it went up.

## Morning Mist

While stationed at Fort Bellingham, Pickett married a Haida woman. The name that comes down to us is Morning Mist, an English rendering of a name recorded by other people, in another language, for purposes of their own - which is most of what we can say about her, and far less than she deserves.

In 1857 she gave birth in this house to a son, James Tilton Pickett. A few months later she died. The child stayed on this coast. Marriages between army officers and Indigenous women were common enough on the frontier and almost never recorded as marriages in the way the officers' later biographies would prefer. Morning Mist has no portrait and no monument. She has a house where her son was born, still standing on Bancroft Street, open to visitors.

## The Captain Goes East

In 1861 Pickett left for the war. He resigned his United States commission and went to fight for the Confederacy, and in July 1863 he led the assault at Gettysburg that history calls Pickett's Charge - a frontal attack across open ground into concentrated artillery and rifle fire that lost roughly half the men who made it.

He did not take his son with him. James, four years old, stayed in Washington Territory while his father went east to fight for the Confederacy. Father and son never lived together again. It is worth pausing on the geometry of that: a boy growing up on Puget Sound, the child of a Haida mother, while three thousand miles away his father's name was becoming a byword for sacrificial courage in a cause that had nothing to offer either of them.

## The Artist

James Tilton Pickett grew up to be good at drawing. He made his living as a newspaper artist in the Pacific Northwest, in the era before photographic reproduction, when getting an image into print meant somebody had to sit down and render the scene by hand for an engraver - courtrooms, fires, shipwrecks, portraits of the newly notorious, produced fast and to deadline.

He died of tuberculosis near Portland, Oregon, in 1889, aged thirty-two. Between his mother, who died months after his birth, and his own death well before middle age, the family that began in this house had a very short run of it. The house outlived them all.

## What the House Became

After Pickett went east the property passed through several owners, and its survival owes less to reverence than to ordinary continuity - somebody was always living in it. The chain ends with Hattie Strothers, who left the house to the Washington State Historical Society when she died in 1936.

In 1941 it opened as a museum, and it later became home to the Daughters of Pioneers as well; both still occupy the site. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1971. A city has grown up around it, and the house sits on its corner of Bancroft Street doing what old houses do - being smaller than you expect, and considerably more complicated than the plaque out front has room to say.

## Flight Context

The Pickett House stands at 48.756N, 122.486W at 910 Bancroft Street in Bellingham, on the rise above Whatcom Creek just north of the downtown core. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet the landmarks are Bellingham Bay to the west, Whatcom Creek running down into it, and the downtown street grid climbing the slope - the house itself is a single small roof among them, best located by finding the creek and the old mill district at its mouth. Mount Baker stands 30 nm east and dominates the horizon in clear weather; Lummi Island and the San Juans lie west across the bay. Bellingham International (KBLI) is 3 nm northwest, Orcas Island (KORS) 15 nm west, Skagit Regional (KBVS) south, and Abbotsford (CYXX) northeast across the Canadian border - mind the boundary, which runs a few miles north of the city. Expect marine layer off the bay in summer mornings and strong easterly outflow from the Fraser gap in winter.
