# Bellingham waterfront

A 137-acre pulp and chemical complex on Bellingham Bay changed hands for ten dollars - and the buyer took on a century of mercury with it.

**Location:** 48.7505, -122.4848

**Categories:** Geography of Bellingham, Washington, Geography of Whatcom County, Washington, Economy of Bellingham, Washington, Redeveloped ports and waterfronts in the United States

## Introduction

In June 2006 a property assessed at 37 million dollars changed hands in Bellingham, Washington, for exactly ten dollars. Georgia-Pacific sold its waterfront complex to the Port of Bellingham, and in return the port accepted responsibility for cleaning the place up. That is the entire arithmetic of the American industrial waterfront in one transaction: the land is valuable, the contamination beneath it is worth more than the land, and the number where those two meet is ten dollars and a signature. Everything that has happened on Bellingham Bay since has been the working out of that trade.

## What the Bay Was For

Bellingham Bay has been industrial since the late nineteenth century, and the sequence of industries reads like a compressed history of the Pacific Northwest. Lumber processing came first. Salmon canning followed, packing the runs that came down these rivers into tins bound for distant markets. Early in the twentieth century pulp and paper replaced the canneries on the ground now slated for redevelopment. In 1963 Georgia-Pacific bought the Puget Sound Pulp and Timber Company and ran a pulp mill on the central downtown waterfront until 2001. A tissue mill had stood on the same site since 1926; it finally stopped on 21 December 2007. Whatever else is true about the complex, it paid family wages to a great many Bellingham households for a very long time, and the city knew it.

## The Chemistry Problem

In 1965 Georgia-Pacific added a chlor-alkali facility to make chlorine and related chemicals, and operated it until 1999. Chlor-alkali production in that era used mercury, and over thirty-four years the plant released many tons of it into the Whatcom Creek waterway. Soil beneath the former facility has been measured with mercury concentrations as high as 12,500 parts per million. Other stretches of the waterfront are contaminated for a different reason entirely: they were built as a municipal waste landfill, a common mid-century practice that had the convenient side effect of manufacturing dry land where there had been tideflats. The whole 137-acre site sits on the consequences of both.

## The Lagoon Nobody Agrees About

One feature dominates the argument. The Aerated Stabilization Basin - the ASB - is a large lagoon enclosed by an earthen breakwater built across former tidelands, on the far side of the Whatcom waterway from the plant site, and it was part of the mill's wastewater treatment. It is also, by a considerable margin, the cleanest ground on the property. The Port has long wanted to turn it into a pleasure-boat marina. Environmental advocates have wanted to use it instead to enable a more thorough removal of the contaminated sediments in the waterway. Both positions are coherent. They are also mutually exclusive, and that has made the ASB the place where every larger disagreement about the waterfront comes to a head.

## Six Thousand Four Hundred Signatures

Bellingham did not leave this to the agencies. The Bellingham Bay Foundation formed in 2005. ReSources has campaigned for the full removal of mercury from the nearshore areas of the bay. In the summer of 2006, concerned about the mercury levels under the old chlor-alkali site, the Foundation launched People for a Healthy Bay and an initiative that would have required the city to push for removal to the highest practical level. Organisers gathered 6,400 signatures in twenty days. The City of Bellingham then sued successfully to keep the measure off the ballot. Polling by the Foundation had found that what residents cared about most was a waterfront that was clean and safe - a finding that the signature drive made difficult to dispute and the lawsuit made moot.

## The Waterfront District, In Progress

A consent decree covering the Whatcom Waterway and the ASB has been signed by the Washington State Department of Ecology, the Department of Natural Resources, the city, the port and numerous other agencies. The city and port entered a partnership to clean and redevelop the land under the name The Waterfront District, guided first by a Waterfront Futures Group plan and later by a Waterfront Advisory Group working at finer grain. More than 100 acres of former industrial ground are in play, with a hotel, conference centre, condominiums, retirement housing and retail all part of the intended mix, and a container village of food and retail planned at WayPoint Park. Progress has been uneven - the two public bodies have disagreed over street grids and planning ordinances and have at times stepped back from each other. A waterfront like this is not repaired in a decade. It is repaired in a generation, argument by argument.

## Flight Context

The Bellingham waterfront lies at 48.7505°N, 122.4848°W along the eastern shore of Bellingham Bay in Whatcom County, Washington. From 2,000-3,500 ft the site is easy to read: the large rectangular lagoon of the Aerated Stabilization Basin sits enclosed by its earthen breakwater on the bay side of the Whatcom waterway, with the cleared and partly redeveloped 137-acre mill site and downtown Bellingham immediately inland, and the Fairhaven district south along the shore. Lummi Bay and the Lummi Peninsula lie west, Samish, Padilla and Skagit Bays run south, and Boundary Bay is north near the Canadian line. Mount Baker stands 10,786 ft (3,288 m) about 30 miles due east and is visible from most of the bay in clear weather. Bellingham International Airport (KBLI) is three miles northwest of the city, 23 miles from the Peace Arch border crossing and 54 miles from downtown Vancouver, which makes it an unusually busy field for a city this size. Expect frequent low cloud off the strait and strong northeasterly outflow from the Fraser Valley in winter.
