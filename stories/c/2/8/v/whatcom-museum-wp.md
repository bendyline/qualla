# Whatcom Museum

A Victorian city hall copied from a Michigan courthouse, a logging photographer's glass plates, and a wall built to hold daylight - three buildings that carry Bellingham's memory.

**Location:** 48.7528, -122.4800

**Categories:** Museums, Architecture, History, Photography, Washington State

## Introduction

The original is gone. Fred W. Hollister's second Saginaw County Courthouse rose in Michigan in 1884 and was destroyed in 1971, and by then an almost exact copy of it had been standing above Bellingham Bay for nearly eighty years. A local architect named Alfred Lee built that copy in 1892, in red brick and pale Chuckanut sandstone, for a town called New Whatcom - a town that no longer exists either, having folded itself into Bellingham. The replica outlasted both its model and the city that commissioned it. Today it anchors the Whatcom Museum, whose holdings run to more than 30,000 objects and whose campus spans 117 years of architecture.

## The Bluff That Isn't There Anymore

When Lee's building opened, saltwater lay directly below it. This was a civic hall on a bluff, looking out over the working edge of Bellingham Bay, and the view was the point. Then the water moved. Decade by decade the waterfront was filled in to make land, so the drop that once ended at the tideline now ends at Maritime Heritage Park, and the bay has retreated to the middle distance. The building served as city hall until 1936 and became part of the museum in 1941. In 1962 fire tore through it. What put it back together was not a municipal line item but a fundraising campaign - a town deciding, dollar by dollar, that a hundred-year-old imitation courthouse was worth saving.

## Two Names on Every Print

The museum's most-consulted holding is the work of two people, only one of whom got the credit at the time. Darius Kinsey photographed western Washington's logging industry from 1890 to 1940, carrying an 11-by-14-inch Empire State view camera into the cut on a custom tripod that could be run up twelve feet. He shot loggers, locomotives, mills and the landscape coming down around them, working on glass plates until 1914 and film thereafter. Back home, Tabitha Kinsey - he married her in 1896 - developed the negatives and made the prints. Those prints were carried back out to the camps and sold to the loggers themselves. The men in the pictures bought the pictures. The Whatcom Museum holds more than 4,000 items from the Kinsey archive, and both names are on it.

## A Room of Stopped Wings

One floor belongs to an older way of knowing the natural world entirely. The John M. Edson Hall of Birds is exactly what its name promises: hundreds of taxidermied birds from Edson's collection, case after case of them, still. A modern visitor can find the room unsettling, and many do. It helps to remember what it was for. Before cheap optics and field guides, before anyone could carry a camera or a recording of a birdsong in a pocket, a mounted specimen behind glass was how most people got close enough to count the feathers. The hall now does two jobs at once. It shows you the birds, and it shows you how an earlier generation went about looking at them.

## A Wall Built to Hold Daylight

In 2009 the museum opened a building that argues with its oldest one. Where Old City Hall is brick, mass and symmetry, the Lightcatcher is a translucent curve - a wall 37 feet high and 180 feet long, designed by the Seattle firm Olson Kundig Architects to do real thermal work, diffusing northern daylight into the galleries while trimming what the building demands in energy. The name is not decoration; it is a description of the mechanism. Inside sits the Family Interactive Gallery, an art and creative play space where children are expected to handle things rather than read labels about them, which makes the Lightcatcher the noisiest building on the campus and, on a wet Bellingham afternoon, the busiest.

## Two Landlords, One Collection

The Whatcom Museum became a non-profit in 1982 and has been run since by an unusual pairing: the City of Bellingham and the Whatcom Museum Foundation share management, a civic-private arrangement that has kept a small-city museum accredited by the American Alliance of Museums. The campus has long included Old Fire Station No. 1 alongside its two better-known halls, and today visitors are welcomed at two addresses - the Lightcatcher on Flora Street and Old City Hall on Prospect. Galleries open Wednesday through Sunday. On the first Friday of each month admission is free and the lights stay on into the evening, which is the best time to stand on Prospect Street and look up at a copy of a vanished Michigan courthouse, glowing above a park that used to be a bay.

## Flight Context

Bellingham, Washington sits at 48.7528 N, 122.48 W, at the northeast corner of Bellingham Bay and about twenty miles south of the Canadian border. Old City Hall is the easiest visual fix: a red-brick Victorian block with a tower, standing above the green of Maritime Heritage Park at the edge of the downtown grid, with the Lightcatcher's pale curved wall two blocks northwest. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet AGL the bay, the port's log booms and the museum campus read clearly. Bellingham International Airport (KBLI) lies three miles northwest of downtown and is the obvious arrival. Abbotsford International (CYXX) sits across the border to the northeast and Vancouver International (CYVR) farther north. Mount Baker rises to the east and is your all-weather bearing on a clear day; in winter, expect low marine stratus off the Strait of Georgia to hide the whole waterfront until mid-morning.
