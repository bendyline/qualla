# Fairhaven College

Nobody at Fairhaven gets a B. The experimental college on the wooded south edge of Western Washington University has issued narrative evaluations instead of letter grades since 1967.

**Location:** 48.7303, -122.4858

**Categories:** Universities and colleges in Bellingham, Washington, Western Washington University, Educational institutions established in 1967, 1967 establishments in Washington (state), Public liberal arts colleges in the United States

## Introduction

Nobody at Fairhaven gets a B. There are no letter grades at all — every class ends with a written narrative evaluation from the instructor, and the student writes an evaluation of their own work to sit beside it. That arrangement takes longer, produces a transcript that reads like correspondence rather than a spreadsheet, and forces both parties to say something specific about what actually happened over ten weeks. Fairhaven College of Interdisciplinary Studies has worked this way since 1967, when it opened on the wooded southern edge of Western Washington University with thirteen students, five professors, and no buildings of its own.

## Thirteen Students in a Borrowed House

The first year was improvisation. Fairhaven had no campus, so it took over the former Latter-day Saints house and held classes there; its students lived in Western's dormitories and took courses on both sides of the arrangement, all while sitting in on the planning of the institution they had just enrolled in. That is an unusual thing to ask of eighteen-year-olds and it shaped the culture permanently. Meanwhile, construction went up just south of Western's main campus. The 1968 academic year brought Fairhaven's first full-size cohort — two hundred students, an order-of-magnitude jump — and they lived, held their seminars, and shared a roof with the faculty and administration in Edens Hall until the new campus was ready in 1969. Something about a college where the dean's office is down the hall from your bedroom sets an expectation that never quite goes away.

## The Year the Students Won

Fairhaven was conceived as a residential college in the strong sense: live on campus for all four years, because the living was supposed to be part of the education. The students disagreed. By 1970 a restive student body had pushed hard enough that the requirement was cut from four years to two, and a substantial share of juniors and seniors promptly moved off campus. Read one way, that is a founding ideal getting diluted within three years. Read another, it is the first real test of whether a college that told students to help design it actually meant it — and Fairhaven, to its credit, blinked. Today there is no residential requirement at all, and the Fairhaven dorms are open to any Western student who wants them.

## Seminars, Not Lecture Halls

The mechanics are simple to state and hard to sustain. Almost every Fairhaven class is a seminar rather than a lecture. Enrollment is rarely above twenty, and some courses are team-taught, two professors in the room arguing productively with each other in front of the students — which is, when it works, the single most instructive thing an undergraduate can watch. Rather than checking off Western's general education requirements, Fairhaven students take interdisciplinary core courses meant to cover the same range of subjects by cutting across them instead of sorting them into columns. It is a bet that breadth is better built by connection than by checklist.

## Design Your Own Degree

When Fairhaven students reach what the college calls concentrated studies, they can simply choose any major or minor Western offers and proceed like anyone else. Or they can build something that does not exist yet — an interdisciplinary concentration of their own design, assembled from Fairhaven seminars, Western courses, independent study, and internships, with a written rationale for why those pieces belong together. The freedom is real and so is the difficulty: a student who cannot articulate what they are doing will flounder, and a self-designed degree offers nowhere to hide. Fairhaven was founded in the same year the Washington legislature chartered The Evergreen State College, which would also abandon letter grades for narrative evaluations. Both were products of a moment when American higher education was genuinely willing to question its own furniture. What is notable about Fairhaven is not that it started that way. It is that, nearly sixty years of educational fashions later, it still does.

## The Neighbourhood It Is Named For

The name comes from just downhill. Fairhaven was one of the separate towns that consolidated into the city of Bellingham in 1903, and its brick commercial district survives — a compact grid of 1890s buildings, bookshops, and cafés a short walk from the water at the southern end of town. The Sehome Hill Arboretum rises in forest directly behind the campus, and downhill to the west Bellingham Bay opens toward the San Juan Islands. It is a good setting for a place built on the idea that learning should not be partitioned: forest on one side, working waterfront on the other, and a campus in between where the classes are small enough that everyone has to speak.

## Flight Context

Fairhaven College occupies the south end of the Western Washington University campus in Bellingham, Washington, at approximately 48.730°N, 122.486°W. Fly it at 1,500 to 3,000 feet on a pass along Bellingham Bay. The campus sits on a hillside above the water and is easy to identify: look for the wooded dome of Sehome Hill immediately east, the bay and its ferry terminal to the west, and the compact brick grid of the historic Fairhaven district on the shoreline just south of the campus. Lummi Island and the San Juans lie out across the water to the west and southwest, Lake Whatcom fills the valley behind the hill to the east, and Mount Baker stands at 10,781 feet on the eastern horizon, with the jagged Twin Sisters Range south of it. Bellingham International Airport (KBLI) is roughly 4 nautical miles northwest, with Class D airspace to respect over the city. Orcas Island (KORS) and Friday Harbor (KFHR) lie west across Rosario Strait, and Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 45 nautical miles northwest. Low cloud and drizzle are the default from November into spring; July through September usually delivers clear air and long views up the Cascades.
