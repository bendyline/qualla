# Leopold Hotel

A Bellingham hotel where a broke Alaskan painter settled his bill in canvases, where a brewer died in his own guest rooms, and which has been rebuilt, renamed, and repurposed for more than a century.

**Location:** 48.7492, -122.4790

**Categories:** historic-hotels, bellingham, washington-state, architecture, art-history

## Introduction

A painter named Sydney Laurence used to stay at this hotel in Bellingham, and there were stretches when he could not pay for the room. Laurence had spent years in Alaska, and from 1911 onward he built a career on a single subject - Denali, seen from the hills above the Tokositna rapids, painted again and again in changing light. What he had was canvases, not cash. So he offered them to the hotel's owner, Leopold F. Schmidt, in place of rent, and Schmidt took the deal. The Schmidt family still holds one of the largest private collections of Laurence's work anywhere. It is the single best thing that ever happened in a building that has otherwise been demolished in pieces, renamed four times, and converted into something else about once a generation.

## Captain Byron's House

The first hotel on the corner went up in 1899 and took its builder's name: Byron House, after Captain Josiah B. Byron, ninety-three rooms in a city that was still deciding what it was going to be. Byron held it until 1910, by which point it was known simply as the Hotel Byron. His buyer was Leopold F. Schmidt, who had founded the Olympia Brewing Company and owned the Bellingham Bay Brewery, and who paid a hundred thousand dollars for the place. A brewer buying a hotel in 1910 was not eccentric - it was vertical integration. Schmidt immediately began building, adding a two-hundred-room wing that opened in May 1913 and more than tripled the house. He did not get long to enjoy it. On 24 September 1914 he died inside the hotel, and his investors renamed it the Hotel Leopold shortly afterward as a mark of regard.

## Nine Stories in Sandstone Country

In 1929 the original 1899 building came down and a nine-storey tower went up in its place, designed by architects from H. L. Stevens & Company of San Francisco and finished in the Mission Revival idiom that was moving up the coast from California. The new tower pushed the hotel to three hundred rooms, which in Bellingham was an act of some confidence in the months before the stock market did what it did that October. The Leopold survived anyway, and in 1930 it became one of the first properties to sign on with a brand-new chain called Western Hotels - an outfit of independent Pacific Northwest hoteliers pooling their reservations, which grew into Western International and is known today as Westin. For four decades the Leopold was part of it.

## Demolished by Instalments

The hotel's twentieth century was a slow subtraction. In 1960 a small motel wing with an outdoor pool went in behind the 1929 tower, and the sign changed to the Leopold Hotel and Motor Inn - a hotel adapting to guests who now arrived by car and wanted to park beside their door. In 1967 the Schmidt addition of 1913 was pulled down entirely and replaced by a four-storey, forty-eight-room motel wing, and the name shortened again to the Leopold Inn. Two years later it left Western International Hotels. The 1960 wing has since been demolished as well. What survives is the 1929 tower and the 1967 wing: an eighty-year gap in construction dates standing shoulder to shoulder on the same lot.

## Two Lives at Once

In the 1980s the Leopold stopped being a hotel altogether and became a retirement home, with a substantial renovation to suit. On 19 February 1982 it was added to the National Register of Historic Places, which is the sort of recognition that often arrives precisely when a building's original purpose has run out. Then in November 2019 the hotel came partly back. The Hotel Leo opened with thirty-one rooms in the motel wing and nine in the historic tower, sharing the original lobby and ballroom, plus a gym, a rec room, and a library. The rest of the building remains apartments. On any given night the Leopold is simultaneously somebody's home and somebody's one-night stop, which is roughly what a downtown hotel was always supposed to be.

## Flight Context

The Leopold Hotel stands at 48.749 degrees north, 122.479 degrees west, at 1224 Cornwall Avenue in downtown Bellingham, Washington. At 2,000 to 3,000 feet the nine-storey tower is one of the tallest things in the historic core, a pale block just inland of the waterfront. Navigate by Bellingham Bay, with the Squalicum Harbour breakwater north of downtown and the old industrial waterfront and Whatcom Creek immediately west of the building; Mount Baker stands 50 kilometres east, and Lummi Island and the San Juans fill the view to the west and southwest. Bellingham International (KBLI) is about 5 kilometres northwest, so downtown sits close under that airport's traffic; Skagit Regional at Burlington (KBVS) is 40 kilometres south, and Abbotsford (CYXX) lies 25 kilometres north across the Canadian border. Expect frequent winter low cloud and rain, autumn fog off the bay, and strong Fraser Valley northeasters funnelling down over the city during cold outbreaks.
