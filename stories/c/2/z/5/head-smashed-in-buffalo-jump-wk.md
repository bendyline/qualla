# Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump

The Alberta cliff where First Nations peoples drove buffalo to their deaths for nearly 6,000 years - named for a young man who stood beneath the cliff to watch and was buried by falling carcasses.

**Location:** 49.7487, -113.6282

**Categories:** alberta, canada, unesco, first-nations, archaeology

## Introduction

For nearly 6,000 years, Blackfoot peoples and their ancestors drove buffalo herds over a cliff in what is now southern Alberta. The animals fell 10 meters to their deaths on the rocks below, where hunters waited to butcher them. The bone bed at the base of the cliff is over 10 meters deep - the accumulated remains of thousands of hunts, perhaps millions of buffalo. The site is named, according to Blackfoot tradition, for a young man who wanted to watch the spectacle from below. As the buffalo fell, he was trapped against the cliff base and crushed. His people found him later, his head smashed in by the falling bodies. Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, one of the oldest, largest, and best-preserved buffalo jumps in the Americas.

## The Hunt

Buffalo jump hunting was not simple cliff-driving. It required sophisticated planning, intimate knowledge of buffalo behavior, and coordinated community effort. Hunters spent days or weeks positioning herds, using drive lanes marked by stone cairns to funnel animals toward the precipice. Young men dressed in wolf and coyote skins crept behind the herd, slowly pressuring them forward. At the crucial moment, the lead animals were stampeded; the herd behind couldn't stop. The cliff at Head-Smashed-In offered perfect conditions: drive lanes that channeled buffalo toward a cliff that was invisible until they were upon it. The fall was 10 meters - usually fatal.

## The Processing

Below the cliff, the real work began. Women and children joined the butchering, working quickly to process meat before it spoiled. Every part of the buffalo was used: meat was dried into pemmican; hides became tipis and clothing; sinew became thread and bowstrings; bones became tools; horns became spoons and containers. Nothing was wasted. The bone bed at the base accumulated over millennia - layer upon layer of processed remains, mixed with ash from fires and broken tools. Archaeologists have excavated portions of this bed, finding the stratified history of 6,000 years of hunting.

## The Name

According to Blackfoot oral tradition, a young man wanted to see the buffalo fall from directly below. He stood at the cliff base as the hunt commenced. That hunt was unusually successful - so many buffalo fell that he was trapped against the rock face. By the time the falling stopped and hunters reached him, he was dead, his head crushed by the weight of the carcasses. His people named the place 'where he got his head smashed in.' The Blackfoot name is Estipah-skikikini-kots. The English translation became the official site name - accurate if inelegant.

## The Site Today

Head-Smashed-In became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1981, recognized for both its archaeological significance and its cultural importance to the Blackfoot peoples. The interpretive center is built into the cliff itself - you enter at the top (hunter level) and descend through exhibits to emerge at the kill site below. The center is designed and operated in partnership with the Blackfoot Confederacy. Outside, drive lanes marked by stone cairns still trace the routes hunters used to funnel buffalo toward the cliff. The bone bed is protected; the cliff edge looks much as it did 6,000 years ago.

## Visiting Head-Smashed-In

Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump is located 18 km northwest of Fort Macleod, Alberta, on Highway 785. The interpretive center is open year-round with varied hours; check ahead. Exhibits trace the history of the buffalo jump from ancient times through European contact and the near-extinction of the buffalo. The architecture is striking - built into the sandstone cliff. Walking trails lead to the drive lanes and cliff edge. Summer programs include Blackfoot cultural presentations. Calgary International Airport is 165 km north; Lethbridge Airport is closer but smaller. The site is a 2-hour drive south of Calgary via Highway 2.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.75°N, 113.63°W in southern Alberta, along the Porcupine Hills. From altitude, the site appears as a sandstone escarpment overlooking the prairie to the east. The interpretive center is visible built into the cliff face. The drive lanes - stone cairns leading to the cliff - are visible on the plateau above if conditions allow. The terrain transitions from foothills to prairie; the Rocky Mountains rise to the west. Calgary lies 165 km north. The landscape is open ranching country, little changed from the buffalo hunting era except for the absence of the great herds.
