# Large Zenith Telescope

For thirteen years a six-metre pool of spinning mercury in a British Columbia rainforest was one of the largest telescope mirrors on Earth - and it was never polished, because gravity did that part.

**Location:** 49.2881, -122.5730

**Categories:** Liquid mirror telescopes, Astronomical observatories in Canada, University of British Columbia, Optics, Science and technology in British Columbia

## Introduction

The mirror was a puddle. Six metres across, a few millimetres deep, made of liquid mercury and turning at eight and a half revolutions a minute in a research forest north of Maple Ridge, about 70 km east of Vancouver. Nobody ground it, nobody polished it, nobody spent years figuring its surface to a fraction of a wavelength - the usual, agonising, ruinously expensive business of making a big telescope mirror. The Large Zenith Telescope simply spun a pan of metal and let physics take care of the optics.

## Gravity Does the Polishing

Rotate a liquid around a vertical axis at a constant speed and its surface settles into a paraboloid. Not approximately - exactly, because the balance between gravity and centripetal acceleration has only one stable solution, and that solution happens to be the precise shape a reflecting telescope needs. The relationship is almost insultingly simple: twice the focal length times the square of the angular velocity equals the acceleration of gravity. Density does not appear in it, which means the liquid's identity is irrelevant to the figure; mercury is used because it reflects well and stays liquid at room temperature. Spin the LZT's pan at 8.5 rpm and a six-metre parabola appeared in it, self-correcting, free. The whole instrument cost roughly a fiftieth of what a conventional glass-mirror telescope of comparable aperture would have.

## The Catch, Which Is a Large One

You can point it straight up. That is the entire list. Tilt a liquid mirror and the parabola is destroyed instantly, so a telescope like this sees only the narrow band of sky that the Earth's rotation carries directly overhead. The LZT worked by transit imaging, turning that constraint into a method: as stars drift across the field, the accumulating charge in the sensor is shifted electronically at exactly the same rate, so the image stays sharp and is read out at the trailing edge. There was a second constraint, less elegant. Mercury vapour is poisonous, and so the mirror and the people operating it lived in separately ventilated rooms, the astronomers and their optic sharing a building but never the same air.

## An Idea Older Than the Machinery

None of the physics was new. Isaac Newton understood that a rotating liquid would take a useful shape; what he lacked was any way to spin something that smoothly. Ernesto Capocci set the concept out properly in 1850, and in 1872 Henry Skey, working in Dunedin, New Zealand, built the first liquid mirror telescope that actually functioned in a laboratory. Then the idea waited more than a century for bearings, motors and control electronics good enough to hold a mercury surface steady against vibration. When the LZT was assembled, it borrowed hardware from a predecessor: parts of NASA's three-metre Orbital Debris Observatory telescope, which had been running a mercury mirror for several years.

## The Wrong Sky

The site was the University of British Columbia's Malcolm Knapp Research Forest, at about 395 metres elevation in the foothills north of Maple Ridge. Completed in the spring of 2003, the six-metre LZT was among the largest optical telescopes in the world - as late as 2016 it was reckoned the third largest in North America. It was also, for astronomy, in almost exactly the wrong place. This is coastal temperate rainforest, where the clouds come in off the Pacific and stay; the weather at the site made it unsuitable for serious observing programs. But that was never really the point. Built for about a million dollars, the telescope was a demonstration that the technique scaled to six metres and held its figure. In the summer of 2016 it was decommissioned, and the mercury was drained and stored for use elsewhere.

## What It Proved, and Where It Went

Proof of concept is a modest-sounding phrase for the only thing this telescope was ever asked to deliver. It delivered. The four-metre International Liquid Mirror Telescope in India, under test from 2011, opened in 2022 as the first liquid-mirror instrument built for routine astronomical work at a site chosen for its sky rather than its convenience. The more ambitious proposals of the mid-2000s have not followed: ALPACA, an eight-metre design, and LAMA, an array of sixty-six 6.15-metre mirrors that together would collect light like a 55-metre telescope, both remain on paper. The LZT's legacy is not a catalogue of discoveries. It is a spinning dish in a rainy forest that showed a mirror does not have to be solid, and that the most expensive step in building one can be handed to gravity for nothing.

## Flight Context

The Large Zenith Telescope stood at 49.2881°N, 122.5730°W, at roughly 395 m elevation in UBC's Malcolm Knapp Research Forest, in the foothills north of Maple Ridge and about 70 km east of Vancouver. Fly it at 3,500-5,500 ft AGL and treat the terrain seriously - the Golden Ears peaks rise well above 1,700 m immediately to the north, and this is the edge of the Coast Mountains, not the valley floor. Navigate by water: the long trench of Pitt Lake lies to the west, Alouette Lake fills the valley just northeast of the site inside Golden Ears Provincial Park, and the Alouette River drains south past Maple Ridge into the Fraser. The forest itself is a solid dark canopy broken by research cutblocks and gravel roads, so the clearing is the visual cue rather than any structure. The Fraser's braided channels and the flat farmland of Pitt Meadows lie to the south. Nearby airports: CYPK Pitt Meadows roughly 13 km southwest, CYXX Abbotsford International about 30 km southeast, CYCW Chilliwack some 55 km east, and CYVR Vancouver International around 45 km west. Mountain weather changes fast here; low cloud sits on these foothills for much of the autumn and winter, which is precisely why the telescope was never a serious observatory.
