# Stave Falls Dam and Powerhouse

British Columbia's largest power station for decades, drowned the waterfall it was named for, kept the name, and left five silent Francis turbines standing in a hall in the woods.

**Location:** 49.2297, -122.3560

**Categories:** Dams in British Columbia, Gravity dams, Hydroelectric power stations in British Columbia, Dams completed in 1912, Museums in British Columbia, Science museums in Canada, Mission, British Columbia, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, BC Hydro

## Introduction

There is no waterfall at Stave Falls. There was one — a twenty-four-metre drop on the Stave River, the whole reason anybody came up this valley in the first place — but in 1925 engineers raised the dam and threw a second structure across an adjacent watercourse called Blind Slough, at precisely the spot where the falls had been. The reservoir rose, the falls went under, and the name stayed on the map. What stands in the woods north of Mission now is a concrete complex, a long artificial lake, and inside one of the buildings, five enormous horizontal turbines lined up in a row in complete silence, connected to nothing at all.

## Thirty Years of Wanting It

The idea arrived in the 1890s, when hydroelectric development was spreading fast and every good drop of water on the coast was being appraised by somebody. In 1895 the Stave Lake Electric and Power Co. Ltd was granted permission to study the falls with a view to generating electricity and selling it. Studying, as it turned out, was as far as that company got.

In 1909 the Western Canada Power Company bought Stave Lake Electric and Power outright and started building. The first generator came online in December 1911 and the second in January 1912. The third followed in 1916, the fourth in 1922, and the fifth on 19 September 1925, after the dam had been raised to hold more of Stave Lake behind it. For a long stretch afterward, this powerhouse in the bush was the largest source of hydroelectric power in British Columbia.

## Two Dams and a Spillway

The complex works as a pair. The Stave Falls Dam itself is a comparatively modest thing — 122 metres long, concrete gravity and rock fill, with a crest just five metres wide. It holds the water. It does not have to move it.

That job belongs to the Blind Slough Dam a few hundred metres north, which is both longer and heavier: 190 metres of concrete gravity with an 8.5-metre crest, functioning as the complex's spillway. Ten tainter gates and four sluice gates are set into it, and at full cry the structure can discharge 3,500 cubic metres of water per second. That capacity is not decorative. The Stave drains a steep coastal watershed that can take enormous rain in a single system, and a dam that cannot get rid of water fast enough is a dam with a much shorter story.

## The Silent Hall

Walk into the original powerhouse and the scale is the first thing that registers. Five horizontal double-Francis turbine-generators stand in a line, machines from an era when generating equipment was built to be looked at as well as run. Three exciters are installed alongside them; two were once spun by smaller Francis turbines of their own. The penstocks that fed units one through four were 4.2 metres in diameter and 45 metres long, which is to say that the pipes alone are the size of a road tunnel.

Decommissioning began in 1995, and by 2000 the hall had stopped producing power for good. Rather than strip it, BC Hydro kept it whole. It is a National Historic Site of Canada and operates as a visitor centre, the Powerhouse at Stave Falls, where the public can walk among machinery that ran for most of a century and then simply stopped.

## Two Kaplans in the New Building

The replacement was a complete rebuild rather than a refit. Between 1995 and its completion in January 2000, the project delivered a new intake, a new powerhouse, a new tailrace channel and new penstocks — essentially a second power station built alongside the first while the first was still finishing its shift.

Inside are two 45-megawatt Kaplan turbines. Unit one draws through a tunnel 183 metres long; unit two's tunnel runs 202 metres. Together they took the installed capacity from 52.5 megawatts to 90 — nearly double the output of the old hall, from machinery that occupies a fraction of the space and requires a fraction of the attention. The valley looks much as it did. The numbers do not.

## One River, Metered Three Times

Stave Falls is one stage of the Alouette–Stave Falls–Ruskin Hydroelectric Complex, and the interesting thing about it is how little of the water is allowed to pass unexploited.

Alouette Lake, held back by the Alouette Dam 11.5 kilometres to the northwest, is tapped through a tunnel 1,067 metres long that carries its water across into the Stave system. At the tunnel's far end a penstock drops it through the 8-megawatt Alouette Powerhouse on the shore of Stave Lake — generation number one. The water then waits in Stave Lake until it is released through the Stave Falls plant — generation number two. From there it falls into Hayward Lake, and 5.6 kilometres downstream the powerhouse beside Ruskin Dam takes a third pass at it before the Stave finally reaches the Fraser.

Three turns at the same water, across a watershed boundary it would never have crossed on its own.

## Flight Context

Stave Falls Dam sits at 49.230°N, 122.356°W, at the south end of Stave Lake in the hills north of Mission, British Columbia. The reservoir is the navigational feature that matters: a long, narrow, dark body of water running roughly 20 nm north into the Coast Mountains, unmistakable from 3,000-5,000 ft and one of the easiest waypoints in the eastern Fraser Valley. From that altitude the dam, the Blind Slough spillway and the two powerhouses read clearly at the lake's southern lip, with Hayward Lake as a second, smaller pool immediately below and Ruskin Dam at its far end. Abbotsford International (CYXX) lies about 13 nm south-southeast, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) roughly 14 nm west, and Chilliwack (CYCW) about 20 nm east. Terrain rises quickly north of the lake, so treat any northbound run up the reservoir as mountain flying and keep an escape turn available. Low cloud regularly fills this valley when the Fraser floor is clear, and winter inflow winds off the lake can be sharp.
