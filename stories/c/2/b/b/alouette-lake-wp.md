# Alouette Lake

A two-kilometre lake was dammed into a sixteen-kilometre reservoir whose entire purpose is to feed a one-kilometre hole bored through a mountain - and it was renamed after a lark.

**Location:** 49.3333, -122.4170

**Categories:** Lakes of the Lower Mainland, Reservoirs in British Columbia, Maple Ridge, British Columbia, Provincial parks of British Columbia

## Introduction

In 1914 someone in a British Columbia land office had to solve a naming problem, and solved it with unexpected grace. There were two Lillooet Lakes and two Lillooet Rivers in the province, and the pair down in the Lower Mainland kept getting confused with the larger pair to the north. The southern set needed a new name. What they chose was Alouette - French for lark - picked not for any connection to larks or to France but because it was melodious and because it sounded, to the ear, like the word it was replacing. It is one of the few instances of a government renaming something and improving it.

## Two Kilometres Becomes Sixteen

The lake that carries the name today is largely an artificial object. Before 1928 it was about two kilometres long, a modest body of water tucked at the southeastern foot of the Golden Ears. The Alouette Dam changed the scale entirely. Built that year on the upper South Alouette River, eleven and a half kilometres up from the valley floor, it backed the water up into a reservoir roughly sixteen kilometres long on a northeast-southwest axis, dropping away to 159 metres at its deepest. From the air the shape gives it away: a long, narrow, steep-sided trench of green water pressed between mountain walls, far too straight and far too deep for its width to be anything but a drowned valley.

## A Reservoir That Feeds a Hole

Here is the strange part. There is no powerhouse at the Alouette Dam. The reservoir does not exist to generate electricity where it stands; it exists to supply a tunnel. A bore 1,067 metres long runs east through the flank of the ridge between Mount Crickmer and Mount Robie Reid, carrying Alouette water clean out of its own watershed and into Stave Lake. At the far end a penstock drops it into the Alouette Powerhouse, an 8-megawatt BC Hydro station sitting midway along Stave Lake's western shore, on the wrong side of a mountain from the lake it is named for. The old railway grade that once ran up the river to the dam site had served as a logging railway first - one industry's roadbed inherited by the next.

## The Forest That Wasn't Cut

Most of the Alouette basin has never been logged, which in this part of the province makes it a genuine rarity. The slopes around it were not so lucky: the Lougheed and Abernathy Logging Company worked the area hard through the 1920s, and in 1929 a forest fire took out roughly 60,000 hectares, most of the old growth going with it. What survived on the lake's north flank is now protected inside Golden Ears Provincial Park - 555.9 square kilometres of it, named for the twin summits of Mount Blanshard, 1,716 metres, which catch the last light and give the range its nickname. The area was absorbed into Garibaldi Provincial Park in 1933 and split off as a park in its own right in 1967.

## Where the Boundary Wanders

Alouette Lake also happens to be a small monument to the arbitrariness of drawing municipal boundaries with a ruler. The lake is generally described as being in Maple Ridge, and most of it is. But the District of Mission's boundary was laid out as a rectangle, and that rectangle clips a portion of the lake along with its largely inaccessible northwestern shore near the narrows - so a stretch of wilderness shoreline nobody can easily reach belongs, administratively, to a town on the far side of a mountain range. North of the Mission slice, the upper end of the lake sits in no municipality at all. Nothing about this is visible from the water, which is rather the point.

## The Beach at the End of the Road

Road access comes in at the southwestern corner and stops there, which concentrates nearly everything human into one stretch of shoreline. That is where the provincial park put its large public beach, its picnic ground and the bulk of its camping: 206 vehicle sites at Alouette, 148 at Gold Creek and 55 at North Beach, with backcountry sites and marine sites for anyone willing to paddle up the lake and sleep where the road cannot follow. Golden Ears is one of the busiest parks in British Columbia - it drew more than 950,000 visits in 2017-18 - and almost all of that pressure lands on a few hundred metres of gravel. Push a canoe two kilometres north and the crowd is simply gone, replaced by cold green water, the sound of creeks, and slopes of uncut forest going straight up out of the lake.

## Flight Context

Alouette Lake lies at 49.3333 N, 122.4170 W, filling a steep northeast-southwest trench about 16 km long at the southeastern foot of the Golden Ears, roughly 50 km east of Vancouver. It is an excellent visual reference: a long, narrow ribbon of deep green water with almost no development along it except the park facilities clustered at the southwest end, and the twin summits of Mount Blanshard (1,716 m) standing immediately northwest. Fly it at 3,000-5,000 ft or higher - the valley walls rise steeply on both sides and this is genuine mountain terrain, with strong downdrafts on the lee slopes, rapid cloud build-up on summer afternoons, and no good landing options along the lake itself. Pitt Meadows (CYPK) is about 14 nm southwest and the usual departure field; Abbotsford (CYXX) lies roughly 19 nm south, Chilliwack (CYCW) 22 nm southeast, and Vancouver International (CYVR) 31 nm southwest. Stave Lake, the reservoir the Alouette tunnel feeds, is the next large body of water east and makes the pair easy to identify together.
