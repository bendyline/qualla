# Widgeon Valley National Wildlife Area

A 125-hectare marsh at the foot of one of the world's few tidal lakes, holding twenty-one species at risk and open to the public only from a canoe.

**Location:** 49.3694, -122.6333

**Categories:** Protected areas of British Columbia, National Wildlife Areas of Canada, Wetlands of Canada, Pitt Lake

## Introduction

Paddle into the channels at the south end of Pitt Lake and the water does something it should not. It rises and falls, twice a day, on the tide — and it is fresh. Pitt Lake is one of the very few tidal lakes on Earth and among the largest of them, with an average range of about three feet, and the marsh at its southern end breathes in and out with an ocean it was cut off from thousands of years ago. That marsh is the Widgeon Valley National Wildlife Area: 125 hectares of hardhack, bog and slough, almost all of it closed to you.

## A Fjord That Got Landlocked

The shape of this place was cut by ice. A major glacial advance buried the landscape more than ten thousand years ago, and when it withdrew it left the classic signature of the coast: U-shaped valleys, long narrow lakes, and inlets gouged below sea level. The trough that holds Pitt Lake bottoms out more than 140 metres below the present ocean surface. This was a fjord, open to the sea.

Then the Fraser River did what big rivers do. Its delta grew steadily westward, dumping sediment across the mouth of the inlet until the connection narrowed and sealed. The fjord became a lake — but not a normal one. The Fraser still runs to the sea, the tide still runs up the Fraser, and the tide still reaches Pitt Lake. The water turned fresh. The rhythm never left.

## A Hundred and Twenty-Five Hectares

The Nature Trust of British Columbia bought the property in 1973, and the Canadian Wildlife Service declared it a National Wildlife Area in October of the same year; formal gazetting followed in 1978. It is a small reserve by any measure, and it packs four distinct habitat types into that area.

The central lowland is a dense stand of hardhack — a shrubby spirea that grows in thickets you do not walk through — threaded with grasses, forbs and skunk cabbage. The western upland rises into mixed deciduous and conifer woodland, some of it old growth. In the wet ground, lodgepole pine stands scattered through Labrador tea and horsetail, and along the margins the stream banks and riverine marsh carry their own communities. Four ecosystems, none of them large, sitting shoulder to shoulder.

## Twenty-One Species at Risk

Environment and Climate Change Canada counts twenty-one species listed on Schedule 1 of the Species at Risk Act as occurring here. The roster runs from the water upward: white sturgeon in the channels, a fish whose lineage outlasted the dinosaurs and which now hangs on in a handful of Fraser basin populations. The Oregon spotted frog, listed as endangered in Canada. Western painted turtle, western toad, barn owl. Grizzly bear, which on 125 hectares of Lower Mainland marsh reads like a clerical error and is not.

The ordinary amphibians matter just as much, because they are the reason the marsh exists as habitat at all. Northwestern salamanders, rough-skinned newts and red-legged frogs breed in the standing water here — animals that need shallow, undrained wetland and have almost nowhere else in this valley to find it.

## Paddle Only

You can go in, but barely. The channels are open to non-motorized boats, and wildlife viewing and sport fishing from a canoe or kayak are permitted under the applicable permits and provincial rules. No engines. Everything off the water is closed to the public.

This is not gatekeeping for its own sake. Marsh-nesting birds and breeding amphibians are acutely sensitive to foot traffic at the wrong week of the year, and a reserve this small has no interior for animals to retreat into. The neighbours help: the Pitt Polder Ecological Reserve, a fen, and the Pitt Addington Wildlife Management Area, a swamp, lie adjacent, so the protected wetland at the south end of the lake adds up to considerably more than Widgeon's own hundred and twenty-five hectares.

## The Eighty-Five Percent

Here is the figure that puts the paddling restrictions in proportion. Roughly 85 percent of British Columbia's freshwater wetland ecosystems have been disturbed or destroyed outright. Draining and filling, invasive species, land development, resource extraction, forestry practice and recreational pressure have all worked on them, and wetlands do not come back the way a forest does.

The province's wetlands are also concentrated in exactly the wrong place: most lie in and around the Lower Mainland, the fastest-growing and most heavily built corner of British Columbia, where flat wet ground has always been the cheapest land to fill. Widgeon Valley survived because somebody bought it in 1973 and then persuaded the federal government to lock the door behind them. Drifting through the channels on a tide that has no business being there, it is easy to forget how narrow that escape was.

## Flight Context

Widgeon Valley National Wildlife Area lies at 49.3694°N, 122.6333°W, on the west side of the south end of Pitt Lake in British Columbia, about 6 nm north of Pitt Meadows Airport (CYPK). From 2,500–4,500 ft AGL the geography is emphatic: Pitt Lake is a long, narrow blade of water running roughly 25 km north into the Coast Mountains, walled by steep forested slopes, and the ground at its southern end flattens abruptly into the diked farmland of the Pitt Polder and the Pitt River, which runs south to join the Fraser. Widgeon Slough and its channels form the braided green-and-brown marsh on the lake's southwest shore, opposite the Pitt Addington marsh on the east side. Nearby fields: CYPK (Pitt Meadows) immediately south, CYXX (Abbotsford International) about 20 nm southeast, CYVR (Vancouver International) 20 nm southwest, CZBB (Boundary Bay) to the south. Low cloud sits in the lake's mountain walls most of the winter and the valley funnels outflow winds; terrain rises steeply on both shores, so treat the lake as a canyon rather than open water. Best light for the marsh channels is early morning, when the tidal flats and open water separate clearly from the hardhack.
