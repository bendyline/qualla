# Malcolm Knapp Research Forest

Fifty-one square kilometres of Coast Mountains rainforest that looks like wilderness and works like a laboratory — and once held a telescope whose mirror was spinning mercury.

**Location:** 49.3100, -122.5700

**Categories:** Forest research institutes, Forests of British Columbia, Forestry education, Maple Ridge, British Columbia, Research institutes affiliated with the University of British Columbia

## Introduction

For thirteen years there was a six-metre telescope in these woods whose mirror was a spinning pan of liquid mercury. The Large Zenith Telescope could only ever look at one thing — straight up — because mercury takes a perfect parabola only when gravity is left to shape it, and tilting the pan would destroy the surface. So it stared at the zenith and let the Earth's rotation carry the sky across its sensor. First light came in the spring of 2003; it was decommissioned in the summer of 2016 and the mercury drained off for other projects. That a working observatory could sit unremarked in a rainforest an hour from Vancouver tells you something about the Malcolm Knapp Research Forest: 5,157 hectares of Coast Mountains foothills that look like wilderness and operate like a laboratory.

## One Professor's Idea

In 1922 the forestry programme at the University of British Columbia was two years old and owned a small patch of trees at the edge of the Point Grey campus. That was the entire research estate. Malcolm Knapp, who taught logging, wood technology and forest products, thought the discipline needed something on a wholly different scale — not a demonstration plot but a working forest large enough to run real silviculture over real decades. He would become the first Registered Professional Forester in British Columbia. His idea took twenty-seven years to arrive: in 1949 UBC took over management of a large tract in Maple Ridge, roughly 60 kilometres from Vancouver, and the research forest was established. Knapp's service to the university ran forty-one years and ended in 1963. The forest was formally renamed for him in 1988.

## What the Loggers Left

Nothing about this landscape is untouched, and that is part of why it is scientifically useful. Major logging ran through here from the 1800s until 1931, which means most of what stands today is a single even-aged generation — the vast majority of the trees are between seventy and a hundred and twenty years old, all of them growing back from the same interruption. Scattered through that regrowth are patches of intact old growth where trees exceed four hundred years, survivors of the era the rest of the valley lost. A forest with that structure, under one management, with a known history, is a rare experimental object. More than a thousand research projects have been run here, most of them on riparian management, silviculture, wildlife, hydrology and ecology.

## Every Terrain on the Coast

The forest contains, in effect, a cross-section of coastal British Columbia. It touches sea level at Pitt Lake and climbs to roughly 1,000 metres in its northeastern corner, which means a hiker can pass through most of the region's ecology in an afternoon's climb. The whole of it sits in the Coastal Western Hemlock biogeoclimatic zone, its lower ground in the dry maritime subzone and its upper reaches in the very wet maritime — and the rain bears that out, somewhere between 2,200 and 3,000 millimetres a year depending on where you stand, with snow at the top through the winter. Douglas fir, western red cedar, western hemlock and amabilis fir dominate. Beneath them grow bigleaf and vine maple, Oregon grape, salmonberry, skunk cabbage in the wet hollows, and sword and spiny wood ferns in almost every shaded metre.

## Red, Green, Yellow, Blue

The southern end is open for public day use, and the trail system there is colour-coded with pleasing bluntness: red is the easiest, blue the longest and roughest, with green and yellow in between. The maximum route runs about eight kilometres and takes an average walker something like two and a half hours. Overall the trails are rated intermediate, which in this terrain mostly means roots, grade and mud rather than exposure. A separate six-kilometre trail runs around Loon Lake, but that one is reserved for guests of the lodge and closed to the general public — a reminder that this is a working forest with research plots in it, not a park, and that access is a privilege the university grants rather than a right.

## Loon Lake

Deep in the forest, on q̓ic̓əy̓ (Katzie) First Nation territory, the Loon Lake Lodge and Retreat Centre operates under UBC's Faculty of Forestry. It runs corporate retreats and wellness weekends, and takes school groups out for canoeing, climbing, rappelling and ropes-course work — the kind of programme that turns a research station into a place children remember. One use stands apart. Each summer Loon Lake hosts Camp Goodtimes, the Canadian Cancer Society's camp for children and teenagers living with cancer and for their families, run over five weeks at no cost to any participant. It holds accreditation from the British Columbia Camping Association and the Canadian Association of Pediatric Oncology Camps. A forest established to study how trees grow back has turned out to be good for other kinds of recovery as well.

## Flight Context

The Malcolm Knapp Research Forest lies at roughly 49.3100°N, 122.5700°W, north of Maple Ridge in the Coast Mountains foothills about 60 km from Vancouver. Terrain rises from sea level to about 1,000 metres in the northeast corner, so plan on 4,000 to 6,000 feet MSL for a safe and legible look at the whole block. Navigation landmarks are unusually clear here: the long finger of Pitt Lake defines the western edge, Alouette Lake lies immediately east, the peaks of Golden Ears Provincial Park rise to the northeast, and the flat Fraser Valley opens to the south. The forest itself reads as a dense, uniform canopy broken by the geometric edges of research cutblocks — a pattern you will not see in the wild terrain around it. Pitt Meadows (CYPK) is about 8 nm southwest and is the natural base for this area; Langley Regional (CYNJ) is roughly 13 nm south-southwest, Abbotsford (CYXX) about 19 nm southeast, and Vancouver International (CYVR) some 25 nm west-southwest. Mountain weather applies: low cloud can sit on the upper forest while the valley floor is clear, and rain is the default for most of the year.
