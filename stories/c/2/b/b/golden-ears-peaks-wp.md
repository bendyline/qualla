# Golden Ears

The twin summit that dominates the Lower Mainland skyline owes its name to a mishearing, its official name to a governor who lasted a year, and its forest to a fire that burned 60,000 hectares.

**Location:** 49.3628, -122.5070

**Categories:** Mountains of the Lower Mainland, Maple Ridge, British Columbia, Garibaldi Ranges, One-thousanders of British Columbia, Provincial parks of British Columbia, Hiking in British Columbia

## Introduction

Nobody is entirely certain what this mountain was meant to be called. The double summit north of Maple Ridge was first known as The Golden Eyries — an eyrie being an eagle's nest, and golden eagles having been reported near the top. Somewhere in the retelling, Eyries slurred into Ears. The error stuck, largely because it works: two rock points rise from the massif like a cat's ears, and on a clear evening the west-facing stone turns the colour of the name. Half the Lower Mainland can see them. Almost nobody there uses the mountain's official name.

## Three Names for One Mountain

Lieutenant Richard Charles Mayne christened the massif Mount Blanshard in 1859, honouring Richard Blanshard, the first governor of the Colony of Vancouver Island. Officialdom later sorted the group into parts: Mount Blanshard for the whole, Golden Ears for the 1,716-metre twin summit, Edge Peak at 1,680 metres, and Blanshard Peak — sometimes called Blanshard Needle — at 1,550 metres. Edge Peak carries the name of Sam Edge, who climbed it in 1876. Because the group sits exactly on the seam between national topographic sheets 92G/07 and 92G/08, current editions of both still print Mount Blanshard across the whole thing. It is a label almost nobody local recognises. Ask anyone in Maple Ridge, Langley or Vancouver what they are looking at, and the answer is Golden Ears — the peak, the park and the region, all of it.

## Fire and Rail

The valleys below the peaks were not always quiet. Through the 1920s the Abernethy and Lougheed Logging Company ran rail lines into this country and took timber out at industrial scale. Then, in 1929, fire swept roughly 60,000 hectares of it, consuming most of the remaining old-growth cedar and hemlock. Almost everything green on these slopes today grew back afterward — a second-growth forest standing on the stumps of a much older one. The water changed too. BC Hydro built the Alouette Dam in 1926, raising Alouette Lake into a reservoir and tying it to Stave Lake through a diversion tunnel. What looks from the air like untouched wilderness is in fact a landscape rebuilt twice over in a single century, first by saws and then by flame.

## A Park Split in Two

The land came under protection early, as part of Garibaldi Provincial Park when that park was created in 1927. In 1967 the southern portion was carved off into a park of its own, and Golden Ears Provincial Park now covers 555.9 square kilometres of mountain, lake and regrown forest. Proximity does the rest. Sitting at the edge of Metro Vancouver, it has become one of the largest and most popular parks in the province, drawing 956,515 visitors in 2017–18 alone. It is also ecologically serious ground, with more than 85 species at risk recorded inside its boundaries. Eight First Nations — among them the Katzie, the Kwantlen and the Matsqui — hold traditional territory within the park and retain rights to gather resources there.

## The Long Way Up

At 1,716 metres Golden Ears is not a tall mountain by British Columbia standards, and that number badly understates the work involved. The trailhead sits far below on the valley floor, which means a summit attempt swallows almost the entire height of the peak in a single push — second-growth forest first, then subalpine, then open rock. It is among the most demanding day hikes in the Lower Mainland, and people misjudge it regularly. The payoff is a horizon that makes sense of the whole region: the Fraser delta spread out below, the city on its peninsula, the Strait of Georgia beyond, Mount Baker off to the southeast. From down there, the view runs the other way. Golden Ears is the shape people in Vancouver look north-east toward without knowing its name — the one that goes gold at the end of the day.

## Flight Context

Golden Ears rises to 1,716 metres (5,630 feet) at 49.3628° N, 122.507° W, in the Garibaldi Ranges of the Coast Mountains, about 10 NM north of Maple Ridge. The twin summit is the identifying feature — two rock points close together, with Edge Peak (1,680 m) just east and Blanshard Peak (1,550 m) to the south, the three forming a distinct wall above the valley. Alouette Lake lies immediately east as a long narrow reservoir, Pitt Lake to the west, Stave Lake further east, and the Fraser River runs along the southern edge of the frame past the Golden Ears Bridge at Maple Ridge. A safe sightseeing altitude is 8,000 to 10,000 feet, well above the ridgeline and clear of the rotor and downdraft that build on the lee side in wind. Nearby airports are CYPK (Pitt Meadows) roughly 10 NM south, CYNJ (Langley Regional) southwest, CYXX (Abbotsford International) southeast, and CYVR (Vancouver International) about 25 NM southwest. Coast Mountain weather changes fast: cloud can fill the valleys while the summits stay clear, and vice versa. Summer mornings are the most reliable window.
