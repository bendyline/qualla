# Dr. Sun Yat-Sen Classical Chinese Garden

A Ming-style scholar's garden built in Vancouver's Chinatown for Expo 86 - the first Chinese garden constructed outside Asia, and a National Historic Site of Canada.

**Location:** 49.2797, -123.1040

**Categories:** Chinese-Canadian culture in Vancouver, Parks in Vancouver, Monuments and memorials to Sun Yat-sen, Tourist attractions in Vancouver, Gardens in Canada, Chinese gardens, National Historic Sites in British Columbia

## Introduction

Sun Yat-sen came to Vancouver three times, and each time he stayed a while. He was not sightseeing. The man later called the father of modern China spent those months working the rooming houses and association halls of Chinatown, raising money from laundry workers and shopkeepers for a revolution on the other side of the Pacific. Eight decades later, a few blocks from where that fundraising happened, the city built him a garden - and not a memorial garden in the Western sense, with a plaque and a bench, but a walled Ming-dynasty scholar's retreat of the kind that took shape in Suzhou four hundred years ago.

## Built in Two Years, Learned Over Four Centuries

Construction ran through 1985 and 1986, and the timing was not accidental: the garden opened on April 24, 1986, just as Expo 86 was about to pour the world into Vancouver. Wang Zu-Xin served as chief architect of the inner garden, working with specialists from the Landscape Architecture Company of Suzhou, the city whose gardens are the form's canonical examples. The outer park was handled by the Vancouver architects Joe Wai and Donald Vaughan. Money came from an unusually broad coalition - the Chinese and Canadian governments, Chinatown's own community organisations, and a mix of public and private donors - which is one reason the place has always been understood as belonging to the neighbourhood rather than to any one funder. It was the first Chinese garden built outside Asia.

## Two Places, One Pond

Visitors often miss that there are actually two sites here, joined at the water. The Dr. Sun Yat-Sen Park is a public park in Chinese style, free to enter and built largely out of North American materials. The Dr. Sun Yat-Sen Classical Chinese Garden is the walled garden proper, charging admission, and it is the one built to classical rules. The artificial pond links them, which means the water is shared and the boundary between the two is not where most visitors assume it is. Looking across the pond from inside the walls, the free park reads as part of the composition - a small enclosure made to feel as though it opens onto something much larger.

## The Grammar of a Scholar's Garden

Nothing in here is decorative in the way a Western garden is decorative. Craggy limestone stands in for mountains. The pond supplies three things at once - stillness, sound, and a reflection of the sky - and water ties the separate courtyards into a single composition. Fish move under the surface and turtles haul out on the rocks, both carrying meanings a literate Chinese visitor of the Ming period would have read instantly. The Hall of One Hundred Rivers takes its name from the same tradition. Plants are used sparingly and chosen twice over: once for what they mean in poetry and history, and once for when they bloom, so the garden stages the turning of the seasons and particularly the spring awakening. Vancouver's winters happen to resemble Suzhou's closely enough that many of the same species thrive here that grow in the originals.

## What It Is For

The garden's stated mandate is to 'maintain and enhance the bridge of understanding between Chinese and western cultures, promote Chinese culture generally and be an integral part of the local community.' That third clause does a lot of work. Carrall Street sits at the seam between Chinatown and the Downtown Eastside, a block with as much hardship on it as any in Canada, and the garden's walls are both a retreat from that street and a deliberate anchor within it. On January 13, 2016, the site was designated a National Historic Site of Canada - formal recognition that a garden completed in living memory could carry that much history.

## The Camera Finds It

Location scouts worked out long ago what the garden offers: an enclosed, unmistakably non-Western space forty minutes from a full production crew. Jet Li and Aaliyah moved through it in the 2000 film Romeo Must Die. It stood in repeatedly through the fourth season of Falling Skies. Psych used it for the fifth-season opener, 'Romeo and Juliet and Juliet,' and The Amazing Race Canada set a calligraphy challenge here in the second episode of its first season. There is an irony in it. A garden designed around the idea that you should never see the whole of it at once has become one of Vancouver's most photographed enclosures.

## Flight Context

The garden occupies a compact walled block at 49.2797 N, 123.1040 W, at 578 Carrall Street in Vancouver's Chinatown, roughly a kilometre southeast of the downtown core. At street level it is invisible behind its whitewashed walls; from the air, at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, it reads as an unexpected patch of dark water and grey tile roofs punched into a dense grid of low brick blocks, immediately south of the Georgia and Dunsmuir viaducts and a few hundred metres west of the rail yards at False Creek Flats. Use the downtown towers, BC Place's white roof and the curl of False Creek as the approach references. This lies beneath the Vancouver International (CYVR) terminal area, about 12 km northeast of that airport; Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is 2 km north on Burrard Inlet with heavy floatplane traffic, and Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 25 km south. Expect controlled airspace, harbour helicopter and seaplane movements, and frequent low cloud between November and March.
