# Denman Arena

A lumber fortune spent on refrigeration built the biggest indoor arena in Canada at the edge of Stanley Park - and the only building where Vancouver has ever won the Stanley Cup.

**Location:** 49.2931, -123.1330

**Categories:** Sports venues, Ice hockey, History, Vancouver, British Columbia, Lost buildings

## Introduction

In January 1911, Joe Patrick sold his lumber business in Nelson, British Columbia for $440,000, and his family spent much of it on ice. Not on a mine, not on land, not on anything a sensible man in the Kootenays would have bought: on machinery capable of freezing a hockey rink in a city where the winters are green. His sons Frank and Lester had seen mechanical ice plants working at New York's St. Nicholas Arena, and they understood the implication. If you could manufacture winter indoors, you could play professional hockey on the Pacific coast. They built two arenas and a league to fill them, and the larger of the two rose at the corner of Georgia and Denman, with Stanley Park's trees at its back.

## The Gamble at Georgia and Denman

The Patricks bought thirteen lots running from the water's edge of Coal Harbour up to Georgia Street, between Denman and Chilco, with a streetcar line connecting the site to downtown. They incorporated the Vancouver Arena Company with $200,000 in capital and put shares on the market. The market declined: by September 1911, the offering had raised $1,400. Two more investors came in and the family covered the rest themselves. Denman Arena opened on December 20, 1911, and 1,500 people turned up simply to skate on it. Finished, it cost $226,382 and seated 10,500 - the largest indoor arena in Canada, among the largest in the world, and second in North America only to the second Madison Square Garden. In Victoria the Patricks simultaneously built a 4,000-seat rink for the other half of their new Pacific Coast Hockey Association.

## What Stood Here First

Before it was thirteen lots, this ground was Kanaka Ranch, settled in the 1860s by Hawaiian families who grew fruit and vegetables and burned charcoal on the shore of Coal Harbour. Kanaka - the Hawaiian word for a person, and the name British Columbians of the era used for Hawaiian workers - covered a community that had come north on trading ships and in fur-brigade crews, and that in many cases married into local Indigenous families. They were among the earliest non-Indigenous settlers on this peninsula, farming the waterfront a full generation before Vancouver had a name. Their descendants live in British Columbia still. The ranch does not appear in the arena's construction accounts as anything other than a purchase price, which is how most such places disappear.

## Twenty-Six Goals in Five Days

In March 1915 the Stanley Cup came west of Winnipeg for the first time, and the Vancouver Millionaires took Ottawa's Senators apart on their own manufactured ice. Game one, March 22: 6-2. Game two, March 24, played under eastern rules: 8-3. Game three, March 26: 12-3. Twenty-six goals to eight, a sweep in three, and the first Cup ever won by a PCHA club. Cyclone Taylor scored six across the series. Barney Stanley put up five, three of them in the rout, including the goal that ended it at 5:30 of the second period, with Mickey MacKay at centre and Frank Nighbor on the right wing. More than a century later it is still the only Stanley Cup a Vancouver team has won. Every subsequent Canucks playoff run has been measured, silently, against five days in this building.

## A Room the Whole City Used

Hockey was only the excuse. In 1914 the Arena billeted more than a thousand men assembling as the 23rd Infantry Brigade, who shipped out that August as the first Canadian troops of the war - young men sleeping in a hockey rink on their last nights in the country. On October 21, 1924, Prime Minister William Lyon Mackenzie King spoke here on a western tour, in what may have been Canada's first political radio broadcast; the telephone line installed for it carried the first radio broadcast of a hockey game in British Columbia later that year. On April 28, 1935, a CCF rally packed in a crowd reported at over 16,000 - well past the seated capacity, and the largest indoor gathering Vancouver had yet seen. The Connaught Skating Club, founded the same month the Arena opened, held its carnivals on this ice through the 1920s.

## The Night of August 19

Four thousand people watched Max Baer box here that evening. Later the same night, fire broke out in the Coal Harbour waterfront next door and jumped to the Arena despite the brick cladding added to protect it. Two people were killed and three firefighters injured; the loss came to $500,000, and the largest arena in Canada was simply gone. The 2,500-seat Denman Auditorium the Patricks had built alongside it in 1927 survived - taken over by the navy in the next war, then used by Boeing for storage, then bought in 1945 by H. M. Singer and reopened on September 10, 1952 as the Georgia Auditorium. It got a second life: the Vancouver Symphony through the 1950s, and in 1957 the touring Biggest Show of Stars, with Buddy Holly, Chuck Berry, Fats Domino, Eddie Cochran, the Everly Brothers and Paul Anka on one bill. Its last night was June 19, 1959. Devonian Harbour Park covers the ground now, with a marker at the foot of Denman Street to say what stood here.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 49.2931 N, 123.133 W, at the northwest corner of Vancouver's West End where Georgia Street meets Denman, now inside Devonian Harbour Park. Fly it low - 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL - since the subject is a single city block. The orientation cues are unmistakable from any direction: the dark forested mass of Stanley Park immediately west, the green steel of the Lions Gate Bridge crossing First Narrows just beyond it, and Coal Harbour's marinas and floatplane docks curling east toward Canada Place. The former arena footprint lies between the park's edge and the wall of West End towers. Nearby airports: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC), a water aerodrome barely a nautical mile east, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 6 nm south-southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm southeast, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 17 nm east. This is busy, controlled, low-level airspace with constant floatplane traffic in and out of the harbour, so expect clearances and hold the published routes. Clear winter mornings after a front passes give the sharpest light on the North Shore mountains behind the site.
