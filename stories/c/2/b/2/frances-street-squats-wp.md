# Frances Street Squats

Six condemned houses in East Vancouver held thirty-six people for nine months in 1990, and ended with a police operation that brought a helicopter and a bomb squad to a street of wooden bungalows.

**Location:** 49.2794, -123.0680

**Categories:** Evicted squats, Squatting in Canada, Protests in Canada, 1990 in British Columbia, History of Vancouver, Housing

## Introduction

On 27 November 1990 the Vancouver Police Department sent more than eighty officers to six wooden houses in East Vancouver. Twenty-five of them came from the Emergency Response Team. A helicopter worked overhead. A bomb squad stood by, along with police vans, fire engines, dumpster trucks and ambulances. Inside the houses were thirty-six people who had been living there rent-free since February, tending a communal garden and running a free shop out of one of the rooms. Police said they had reliable information about weapons on the property. They searched for two days and found nothing.

## Six Empty Houses

The sequence began in 1989, when a property developer bought six houses on Frances Street and evicted the tenants living in them. The buildings then sat empty. Four months later, in February 1990, people who had learned the houses were slated for demolition simply moved in — not secretly, and not one at a time, but as an organised occupation. They called themselves SAVE, the Squatters Alliance of Vancouver East, and they named the houses as they took them: the Big House, Didley Squat, the Eco Squat, the Stein House, the Token Squat, and Bush Wimmins, which was for women only. Thirty-six people ended up living across the six. The argument they were making fit on a single line of a press release: habitable houses should not be left unoccupied.

## A Neighbourhood That Said Yes

What made Frances Street unusual was not that people squatted — it was that the neighbourhood around them largely approved. The squatters put in a communal garden. They opened a free shop. They took the argument to city council, where the question of what Vancouver was doing about its housing shortage was not a comfortable one. The Downtown Eastside Residents Association backed them. So did the Grandview-Woodland Area Council. For nine months a set of condemned bungalows on an ordinary residential street functioned as both housing and a standing public argument, and the surrounding blocks, by most accounts, did not mind.

## Barricades in August

In August the developer asked the squatters to leave. They answered by barricading two of the houses, on the assumption that a request would be followed eventually by a police line. Their spokesperson, Penny Singh, put it directly: "We will not be bullied out by uniforms, black boots and badges." Three more months passed. Two more buildings on nearby McLean Street were occupied during this period and cleared almost immediately, which gave everyone on Frances Street a fair preview of how the ending was likely to go. What nobody seems to have anticipated was the scale of it.

## The Morning of 27 November

Twelve people were charged with mischief and obstructing a police officer. Nothing else came of it; the occupation itself produced no criminal charges. The Vancouver Sun confirmed that no weapons had been found, which left the helicopter and the bomb squad looking less like precautions than like a statement. Mayor Gordon Campbell defended the police operation. The Committee of Progressive Electors called it inappropriate and vulgar, asked for a public inquiry, and drew a comparison to the Oka Crisis, which had ended only weeks earlier — a comparison that tells you how the politics of the moment felt to the people making it. Squatters, for their part, went and occupied the mayor's offices.

## What's Left

The houses did not survive their eviction by long. The developer sold the properties, and by April 1991 the site had been demolished — the outcome the squatters had moved in to prevent, arriving about fourteen months later than it otherwise would have. There is nothing on Frances Street now to tell you any of this happened. What does survive is a film. In December 1990, within weeks of the eviction, Eleven Foot Productions released The Beat of Frances Street, a documentary shot largely inside the occupation itself; it circulates today in five parts online. It is a strange artefact — a record of a housing argument Vancouver has never stopped having, made by the people who lost that particular round of it.

## Flight Context

Frances Street runs through East Vancouver at roughly 49.2794°N, 123.0680°W, a few blocks south of the Burrard Inlet waterfront. At 1,500 to 2,500 feet the street grid of the east side reads clearly: the port's container terminals and grain elevators line the inlet to the north, Hastings Park and the PNE grounds sit just to the northeast, the Ironworkers Memorial Second Narrows Crossing spans the inlet beyond that, and downtown's towers cluster to the west with the North Shore mountains filling the horizon behind them. This is busy controlled airspace. Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 7 nm southwest, and Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) — a water aerodrome with heavy floatplane traffic — is barely 2 nm west-northwest in Coal Harbour. Pitt Meadows (CYPK) is about 15 nm east-southeast and Boundary Bay (CZBB) roughly 14 nm south. Expect low ceilings and rain from October through April; the site is unremarkable from the air in any weather, which is rather the point.
