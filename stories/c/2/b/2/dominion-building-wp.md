# Dominion Building

Vancouver's first steel-framed high-rise, financed by a German count who had arrived in the city eight years earlier with four dollars - and who lost everything when the war made his accent a crime.

**Location:** 49.2829, -123.1097

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Vancouver, Skyscraper office buildings in Vancouver, Buildings and structures completed in 1910, Second Empire architecture in Canada, Heritage buildings

## Introduction

Alvo von Alvensleben got off at Vancouver in the summer of 1904 with four dollars to his name. He worked as a labourer, fished, sold poultry, scraped together about $1,500 on the Fraser River, and turned it into a finance company in 1907. He helped found the Vancouver Stock Exchange the same year. By 1912 he employed fifty people directly and had money in fisheries, timber, and coal. Somewhere in the middle of that ascent he financed a thirteen-storey tower on West Hastings Street - the first steel-framed high-rise in a city still mostly built of wood. It is still standing, still full of tenants, and still the strangest-looking building on its block.

## The Usefulness of a Rumour

Von Alvensleben was a genuine German count, and his father was a friend of Kaiser Wilhelm II. From that thin thread Vancouver spun something far more exciting: that the young man moving so much money through the city was the Kaiser's godson, an agent placed here to invest imperial funds. He did not go out of his way to correct it. A rumour that makes investors assume you have the German emperor behind you is worth more than most assets, and it fed the story that the tallest building in the British Empire had been paid for by the Empire's chief rival. It made a fine tale. It was also wrong on the facts - Toronto's Trader's Bank Building, finished in 1906 at 55.39 metres, had the Empire beat. The Kaiser's actual money, as it happened, sat in German municipal securities.

## Thirteen Floors and a Mansard

Completed in 1910 to designs by J.S. Helyer and Son, the Dominion Building rises 44.8 metres - about 147 feet - across thirteen storeys at 207 West Hastings, on the edge of Gastown. The style is Second Empire, which in practice means a steep mansard roof, a lot of ornament, and a silhouette that belongs on a Paris boulevard rather than a raw Pacific port. The steel frame was the real novelty; Vancouver had never built that way before. A century of glass towers has since grown up around it, and the effect is that of a dowager in full Edwardian dress at a party where everyone else is in a grey suit. You can pick it out of the skyline in a second.

## The Staircase That Didn't Kill Anyone

Ask around downtown and someone will tell you that the architect died on the building's front staircase - tumbled down it, or collapsed on it, depending on who is telling the story - either at the opening or shortly after, done in by his own creation. The story is false. John S. Helyer did not die on those stairs. But the legend has proved remarkably durable, the way building legends do when a structure is grand enough to seem like it ought to have a curse. Perhaps it survives because the alternative is admitting that the most interesting thing about the staircase is that it is a very good staircase.

## What the War Took

In August 1914 the thing that had made von Alvensleben rich became the thing that ruined him. Canadian authorities seized his holdings as enemy property. He moved to Seattle and tried to rebuild, but suspicion followed - in 1917 he was accused of spying, and from August of that year until March 1920 he was interned at Fort Douglas, Utah. He was eventually cleared. He never got the fortune back. He became an American citizen in 1939 and died in Seattle in October 1965, a very long way from the boom he had helped create. The building he financed outlasted him by decades and is now a provincially designated Class A heritage structure, meaning the city has decided it will never lose it.

## Across from Victory Square

The Dominion sits opposite Victory Square, which in its day held the provincial courthouse, and while that courthouse stood the corner was the hub of Vancouver's legal and financial life. In 1913 the courts decamped to Georgia Street and took the centre of gravity with them. What the neighbourhood got instead, eventually, was a second career in front of cameras. The building appears in the closing sequence of The NeverEnding Story, when Bastian sails over the city on Falkor. A rooftop chase opens Blade: Trinity here. Battlestar Galactica used it and the square for scenes of an abandoned city, and the 2012 series Alcatraz borrowed it while pretending to be San Francisco. In 2023 it turned up at the end of The Mother, looking down into the square. Inside, meanwhile, it does ordinary work: film production offices, designers, booksellers, a dance company, nonprofits, a dentist, a restaurant at street level. A hundred and sixteen years on, it is still just a good building full of people.

## Flight Context

The Dominion Building stands at 49.2829°N, 123.1097°W, at 207 West Hastings Street on the Gastown edge of downtown Vancouver. At 44.8 m it no longer reads as a tower, so hunt for its shape rather than its height: the dark mansard roof and ornate crown sit alone in a field of flat-topped glass, immediately south of the green rectangle of Victory Square. Fly the downtown peninsula at 2,000-3,000 ft AGL. Orientation is easy here - Burrard Inlet and the cruise-ship sails of Canada Place to the north, the saucer of Harbour Centre a few blocks northwest, Lions Gate Bridge spanning First Narrows, and the North Shore Mountains rising steeply behind. Airspace is busy and tightly controlled: CYHC Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre, the downtown water aerodrome, lies barely 1.5 km north with constant seaplane traffic on the inlet, and CYVR Vancouver International sits about 12 km south. CZBB Boundary Bay is roughly 25 km southeast and CYPK Pitt Meadows about 30 km east. Get clearance before approaching the peninsula. Autumn and winter deliver low overcast and rain; the crisp northeast outflow days of January give the sharpest views of the building against the mountains.
