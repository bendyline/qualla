# Strathcona, Vancouver

Vancouver's oldest residential neighbourhood was condemned, partly bulldozed, and then saved by the people who lived in it - a rescue the city has been quietly undoing ever since.

**Location:** 49.2792, -123.0880

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in Vancouver, History of Vancouver, Urban renewal, Gentrification in Canada, Community activism

## Introduction

In 1971, a group of mothers from the Raymur housing project sat down on a railway line. Their children walked to Admiral Seymour Elementary across tracks that had cut through the neighbourhood since 1909, and freight trains blocked the crossing often enough that the walk to school had become a daily negotiation with a moving wall of boxcars. The women occupied the right-of-way and would not move. They became known as the Militant Mothers of Raymur, and what they got was a pedestrian and cycling overpass at Keefer Street. It is still there, though a February 2026 safety inspection closed it indefinitely. So, remarkably, is the neighbourhood - which was scheduled for demolition at the time.

## The East End

Strathcona is the oldest residential quarter in Vancouver, grown out of the settlement that formed around Hastings Mill on the Burrard Inlet shore. When the city incorporated in 1886 and was chosen as the transcontinental railway terminus, the boom that followed filled these blocks between Chinatown and Clark Drive with small wooden houses and the people who worked the mills, the docks and the yards. It was called simply the East End for most of its life; the name Strathcona only arrived in the 1960s, borrowed from Lord Strathcona Elementary, which opened in 1891 and remains the oldest school in the city. From the beginning the neighbourhood held many communities at once - Chinese, Japanese, Italian, Black, Eastern European - which was precisely the quality that later planners would treat as evidence of decay.

## Fifteen Blocks

In the 1950s the city earmarked Strathcona for clearance, to be rebuilt as block upon block of identical apartment buildings and townhouses. The program was not hypothetical. MacLean Park went up between Union, Keefer, Gore and Jackson; Stamp's Place rose on Campbell between Hastings, Union and Raymur; and some fifteen blocks of the old neighbourhood were bulldozed outright - including Hogan's Alley, the only Black neighbourhood Vancouver had. Then it stopped, because the residents stopped it. Bessie Lee, Mary Lee Chan, her husband Walter Chan and their daughter Shirley Chan organised their neighbours into the Strathcona Property Owners and Tenants Association, and SPOTA fought the plan block by block until the city abandoned it. The movement changed municipal politics permanently: the TEAM and later COPE parties and figures including the future mayor Mike Harcourt came out of it.

## The Freeway That Wasn't

Saving the houses was not the end of it. Even as SPOTA gained ground, city engineers proposed a freeway through southern Strathcona, to connect with a planned waterfront route, with connector ramps running up Gore and Carrall Streets. Those two streets are the spine of Chinatown and the edge of Gastown, so the proposal would have taken a substantial bite out of both. Vancouver is one of the very few large North American cities with no freeway through its downtown, and this is where that fact was decided - not by planning philosophy but by residents of a condemned neighbourhood who had already learned how to fight. Elsewhere on the continent, the equivalent communities lost.

## Rebuilding on the Cleared Ground

What remained after the bulldozers was a set of vacant lots and a debt. In 1972, following local protest, the city dropped a plan to put a municipal fire hall on one such site and reserved it for family housing instead. The Strathcona Area Housing Society had been set up to rehouse people whose homes were expropriated and demolished in the 1965 clearances, and the last of its five projects, the Mau Dan Gardens Co-operative, was established in October 1981 on that city-owned land. Its founding membership was predominantly Chinese, with Vietnamese, Cambodian, Japanese and other Canadian families among them - a deliberate reassembly, on the ground where a neighbourhood had been cleared, of the kind of neighbourhood that had been cleared.

## Half the Deed

One building here records an older injustice with unusual precision. The Vancouver Japanese Language School, established in 1906 and the oldest Japanese school in Canada, stands on Alexander Street. Property owned by Japanese Canadians was seized during the Second World War and, as a rule, never given back. This building is the exception - the only property in Canada where half the ownership was returned to the Japanese Canadian community after the internment. Half. Strathcona today holds some 12,585 people, by the 2016 count, and around forty percent of residents spoke a Chinese language as their mother tongue at the last detailed census. Its late-Victorian and Edwardian houses are a genuine rarity in a city that demolished most of its own, and many are now heritage-designated, renovated and expensive. The stock that survived because working people fought for it is being bought by people who did not.

## Flight Context

Strathcona occupies the blocks around 49.2792°N, 123.0880°W, immediately east of Vancouver's downtown core and Chinatown. Fly it at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL. The neighbourhood reads clearly from the air as a low, fine-grained grid of two-storey wooden houses with mature street trees, wedged between three much larger textures: downtown's tower cluster to the west, the container terminals and grain elevators along Burrard Inlet to the north, and the broad rail lands of the False Creek Flats - the CN and BNSF classification yards - filling the ground to the south. The twin Georgia Viaducts curve in from downtown at the southwest corner. Clark Drive marks the eastern edge, Hastings Street the northern. Look for the Keefer Street overpass crossing the north-south rail line, and for Lord Strathcona Elementary's block with its adjoining community centre. Nearby airports: CYVR Vancouver International about 12 km southwest, CYHC Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre water aerodrome roughly 3 km west in Coal Harbour, CZBB Boundary Bay some 25 km south, and CYPK Pitt Meadows around 27 km east. Dense controlled airspace over a major port city with low-level floatplane traffic - clearance required. Cold, clear post-frontal days in winter give the best definition; morning marine stratus is common through spring and autumn.
