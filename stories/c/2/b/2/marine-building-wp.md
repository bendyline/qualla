# Marine Building

Vancouver's 1930 Art Deco masterpiece was built to look like a sea crag encrusted with marine life — and sold four years later for less than half what it cost.

**Location:** 49.2875, -123.1169

**Categories:** Art Deco architecture in Canada, Skyscrapers in Vancouver, Buildings and structures completed in 1930, Heritage buildings in Vancouver, Coal Harbour

## Introduction

The architects wanted it to look like "some great crag rising from the sea, clinging with sea flora and fauna, tinted in sea-green, touched with gold." That is an unusual brief for a twenty-two-storey office block, and McCarter & Nairne — a Vancouver firm that had never designed a skyscraper before — delivered on it with a literalism that still stops people on the pavement. Crabs. Skate. Sea horses, scallops, carp, seaweed, sea snails. Ships and locomotives, and on one exterior panel an airship, because in 1930 that was how the future was expected to cross a continent. The Marine Building stands 97.8 metres above 355 Burrard Street in downtown Vancouver, and it is routinely named among the finest Art Deco buildings in the world.

## A Bet on the Panama Canal

The building exists because one man did some arithmetic about a canal four thousand miles away. Lieutenant Commander J. W. Hobbs, an entrepreneur from Toronto, understood that the opening of the Panama Canal in 1914 had permanently changed what a Pacific port was worth — prairie grain and Asian trade could now reach the Atlantic world through Vancouver, and the city was going to grow into that. What it lacked, in his view, was a building that said so. He had the newly completed Chrysler Building in New York in mind as the standard. The commission went to McCarter Nairne and Partners, who responded to the total absence of skyscraper experience by ignoring the problem entirely and designing an object rather than a tower. It opened on 7 October 1930 as the tallest building in Vancouver, and held that title until 1939.

## Built for $2.3 Million, Sold for $900,000

The timing could hardly have been worse. Construction ran to $2.3 million, some $1.1 million over budget, and the building was finished just as the Great Depression closed in on the city. Offices went unrented. The debt did not care. Within a few years the Marine Building was sold to the Guinness family of Ireland for $900,000 — well under half what it had cost to put up, a price that would be remembered in Vancouver for decades as the bargain of the century. The building's 2023 property assessment was $153 million. It is worth pausing on the scale of that reversal: the same walls, the same brass, the same carved crabs, revalued by roughly a factor of a hundred and seventy simply by outlasting the decade that nearly killed them.

## Twelve Woods and a Zodiac

Step into the lobby and the ocean theme stops being decorative and becomes total. The elevator doors are polished brass, worked with marine life; inside the cars, the walls are inlaid with twelve varieties of local hardwood. Sea creatures and the transport machinery of the age run across the surfaces in every direction. The floor carries the signs of the zodiac. For its first fifty years the lobby was surfaced in battleship linoleum imported from Scotland — a wonderfully unglamorous material for such a room — and the renovation that ran from 1982 to 1989, modernising the electrical, mechanical and air-conditioning systems, replaced it with marble. The same work gutted the old Merchant Exchange, the trading room where the port's business had been done. A restaurant occupies that space now.

## The Deck Nobody Could Afford

There was an observation deck once, near the top, with an admission price of twenty-five cents. In 1930 that was a reasonable ask. By 1932 it was not, and through the Depression years the deck sat largely unvisited because a quarter had become real money to most of the people who might have wanted the view. The deck is gone, and there are no public galleries in the building today. This is the thing visitors most often fail to register about the Marine Building: it is not a monument or a museum but a working office tower, and always has been. You can walk into the lobby and look at one of the great interiors in Canada, and then the lifts will take other people up to their desks.

## Vancouver Playing New York

A building this specific turns out to be useful precisely because it looks like somewhere else. Film and television crews have used it constantly. It closed out Timecop. It served as the Daily Planet in Smallville and as the Freelancers' headquarters in Continuum, appeared in Blade: Trinity, and stood in for Marvel's Baxter Building in the 2005 Fantastic Four and its sequel, Rise of the Silver Surfer. Netflix's Altered Carbon put a main character's home in the lobby in 2018. Real business has happened here too: from its founding in May 2000 until June 2011, when the operation moved to New York City, the Marine Building housed the management centre of Oneworld, one of the three great global airline alliances. A tower built on a bet about shipping ended up running an air network.

## Flight Context

The Marine Building sits at 49.2875°N, 123.1169°W, at Burrard and West Hastings on the northwestern edge of downtown Vancouver, terminating the vista down Hastings Street. At 97.8 metres it is now surrounded by taller neighbours, so pick it out by its stepped, tapering crown and green-and-gold colouring rather than by height; 1,500 to 2,500 feet gives the best angle on the downtown peninsula. Landmarks: the white sails of Canada Place and the cruise terminal lie just northeast on Burrard Inlet, Coal Harbour is immediately north, Stanley Park fills the western tip of the peninsula, and Lions Gate Bridge crosses to the North Shore beyond it. Airspace here is congested and controlled. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC), a water aerodrome, is less than a nautical mile north in Coal Harbour, with floatplanes operating continuously in good weather; Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 6 nm southwest; Boundary Bay (CZBB) is roughly 13 nm south. Low cloud and rain dominate from October to April, and the building's gold accents show best in low late-afternoon sun.
