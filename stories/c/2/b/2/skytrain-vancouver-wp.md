# SkyTrain (Vancouver)

Vancouver's driverless elevated railway was built to show off at a world's fair in 1986 and became the spine the whole region grew along.

**Location:** 49.2608, -123.1139

**Categories:** SkyTrain (Vancouver), Rapid transit in Canada, Automated guideway transit, History of Vancouver, Transportation history

## Introduction

The best seat on the SkyTrain is the one no driver is using. There is no cab at the front of an Expo Line train, just a window and whatever is coming - the Fraser River sliding underneath, the towers of New Westminster swinging past, a long curve of guideway running out ahead into the rain. Children claim that window instantly. Adults pretend not to want it. The system runs on computers that have been voting among themselves since the mid-1980s, and on rare snowy mornings crews walk the platforms knocking ice out of the door tracks with hockey sticks. Both of those facts are true at once, and together they explain the thing fairly well.

## Legacy of a World's Fair

Vancouver had wanted rapid transit since the 1950s, when a monorail was sketched by the modernist architect Wells Coates and then abandoned. By the 1970s the traffic was bad and the city could not pay for a fix. The answer came from Ontario, where a crown corporation was developing an automated technology then called the Intermediate Capacity Transit System. British Columbia selected it in 1980 for the corridor between Vancouver and New Westminster, timed to open alongside Expo 86 - a fair whose theme was, conveniently, transportation and communication. Construction began in March 1982 and threaded the line through the Dunsmuir Tunnel under downtown, a bore the Canadian Pacific Railway had dug decades earlier, which saved a fortune. The line opened on 11 December 1985 and began revenue service on 3 January 1986. It carried more than 30 million passengers in its first year.

## Growing Outward

Expansion followed the region's growth, and occasionally argued with it. In March 1990 the SkyBridge carried the line across the Fraser to Surrey - a 616-metre cable-stayed span with 123-metre towers, built for trains alone. Three more Surrey stations opened in 1994. The Millennium Line arrived in 2002 and was promptly nicknamed the "SkyTrain to Nowhere" by critics who noted that its eastern terminus sat in a vacant field; VCC-Clark station did not open until 2006. The Canada Line, built as a public-private partnership and running south to Richmond and Vancouver International Airport, opened in time for the 2010 Winter Olympics and beat its ridership forecasts by three years. The Evergreen Extension reached Coquitlam on 2 December 2016, and for a while afterwards SkyTrain was the longest fully automated driverless system in the world.

## Nobody at the Controls

The Expo and Millennium Lines use linear induction motors - no traction through the wheels, just a flat aluminium reaction rail between them and a magnetic shove. Control is handled by vehicle control computers, each a cluster of three processors that must agree before a command reaches a train. Two out of three, or nothing happens. Lose communication entirely and the train times out and brakes itself. Trains run up to 90 km/h and average about 45 km/h end to end, roughly three times the pace of a bus. Attendants ride the system rather than drive it, handling first aid, fare checks and the override switch when snow or an obstruction defeats the automation. The Canada Line, built later and separately, uses conventional motors and wider cars that cannot run on the other two lines at all.

## Three Notes and Two Voices

Every regular rider knows the chime - three rising tones before the doors close - without ever having thought about where it came from. It was recorded in 1984 and 1985 at Little Mountain Sound Studios in Vancouver, a room better known for rock records than for transit infrastructure. The announcements have had two voices in forty years. Karen Kelm spoke the system from 1985 to 2001. Laureen Regan has spoken it since the Millennium Line opened in 2002. In a region where almost everything visible has been rebuilt twice over, those sounds are among the few things a returning Vancouverite can count on being exactly as they were.

## The Next Twenty Kilometres

Three lines, 54 stations, 79.6 kilometres of track, and 146,789,800 trips taken in 2025 - roughly 450,000 on an average weekday. Another 21.7 kilometres are under construction. The Millennium Line's Broadway extension is tunnelling west beneath one of North America's busiest bus corridors toward Arbutus Street, now expected to open in 2027 after concrete strikes and slow boring pushed it back twice. The Expo Line's 16-kilometre run out to Langley City follows, with eight stations planned for 2029. New Alstom Mark V trainsets - five cars long, the longest on the system, their interiors carrying Indigenous artwork - began testing in March 2025 and entered revenue service that July, replacing cars that have been running since Expo 86.

## Flight Context

SkyTrain's downtown core is at 49.2608 N, 123.1139 W, at Waterfront station on Burrard Inlet. Follow the network from 3,000 to 5,000 feet AGL: the Expo Line runs southeast from downtown through Burnaby to New Westminster, where the SkyBridge's cable-stayed towers cross the Fraser to Surrey - the single clearest landmark on the system. The Canada Line runs due south along Cambie Street, crosses the North Arm of the Fraser on its own extradosed bridge, and splits at Bridgeport, one branch reaching Vancouver International. Elevated guideway reads as a pale ribbon threading between buildings, with clusters of towers marking each station. Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 8 nm southwest; Boundary Bay (CZBB) roughly 15 nm south; Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) 1 nm north on the inlet. The downtown peninsula lies within CYVR's control zone beneath busy floatplane and helicopter routes.
