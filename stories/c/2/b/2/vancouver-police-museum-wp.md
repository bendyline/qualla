# Vancouver Police Museum

For forty years the city's police history lived inside its old morgue on East Cordova; in late 2026 the collection finally moves out of the autopsy room.

**Location:** 49.2822, -123.0982

**Categories:** Museums, Vancouver, British Columbia, Heritage buildings, Labour history, Law enforcement

## Introduction

For forty years, the way to learn about policing in Vancouver has been to walk into the room where the city's dead were opened up. The Vancouver Police Museum and Archives occupies the top floor of the old Coroner's Court at 240 East Cordova Street, and its most memorable permanent exhibit is not an exhibit at all but the building itself: the original morgue, the tiled autopsy suite, the drains, the cold rooms. Visitors arrive expecting badges and vintage patrol cars and find themselves standing where hundreds of post-mortems were carried out. That arrangement is about to end. The museum's last day in the building is October 31, 2026, after which it reopens a few blocks away on Abbott Street, in rooms that have never held a body.

## Purpose-Built for the Dead

The building went up in 1932 to a design by the architect Arthur J. Bird, commissioned specifically for the City Coroner's Services. Everything about it was intended for the handling of death as municipal business: a Coroner's Court where inquests were held, morgue and autopsy facilities that stayed in use until 1980, and the City Analyst's Laboratory, which ran until 1996 and did the chemical work that inquests depended on. It is a municipally designated class A heritage building, the top category the city awards, and it is an unusually intact example of a type of civic architecture that most cities have long since demolished or gutted. Very few buildings are designed from the foundation up around the specific question of what to do with the recently deceased.

## June 18, 1935

Once, the court served an entirely different purpose. That spring, Vancouver's waterfront employers had locked out roughly nine hundred dockers in a dispute over who controlled the dispatching of work gangs. On June 18, somewhere between nine hundred and eleven hundred longshoremen and their supporters marched through the city toward Ballantyne Pier. Vancouver police, British Columbia Provincial Police and the RCMP met them with clubs, tear gas and firearms, and the fighting ran for about three hours through the streets of the east end. Sixty people were injured, twenty-eight of them badly enough to be hospitalized, and twenty-four men were arrested; one bystander was shot in the legs. The Coroner's Court, two blocks from the trouble, was pressed into use that day as a makeshift hospital. The strike dragged on until December but never recovered its momentum. Two years later the men on those docks organized themselves into the union that represents them still.

## A Sergeant with a Hobby

The museum exists largely because of one person's persistence. Joe Swan, a former Vancouver police sergeant and an amateur historian of the serious kind, pushed for years to see the department's accumulated records and objects properly housed. In 1983 the Vancouver Police Historical Society was incorporated as a non-profit with two stated aims: to foster interest in the department's history, and to open a museum. Three years later both the Vancouver Police Department and the City of Vancouver reached their hundredth birthdays, and the museum opened as part of the celebrations. Swan became its first curator and wrote the department's official history, A Century of Service: The Vancouver Police, 1886-1986, published that same year.

## Thirty Thousand Objects

The collection now runs to roughly 30,000 items, of which perhaps forty percent is on display at any given time. Some of it is what you would expect from a police archive: case files, photographs, departmental publications. The rest is stranger and more revealing, because a police museum inherits whatever the police once seized. There are confiscated firearms and other weapons, counterfeit currency, the physical traces of a century of criminal ingenuity in a port city. The museum runs forensic investigation programs for children and teenagers, which is a genuinely odd and very effective use of an autopsy room, and keeps a small bookshop stocked with local authors. It receives no direct funding from either the police department or the city; admissions, memberships, donations and grants pay for it.

## Leaving the Morgue

Forty years in a heritage building has costs that a non-profit eventually cannot absorb. The Coroner's Court has no elevator, which quietly excludes a portion of every audience that ever wanted to visit. So the museum is going: last day at 240 East Cordova on October 31, reopening in mid-November on the second level of 351 Abbott Street, with elevator access and a theatre it has never had. Something will be lost in the move, and everybody involved knows it, because the building was the argument. Standing in the tiled room where the city's unexplained deaths were examined tells you more about a century of urban policing than any display case can. What comes next will be more comfortable, more accessible, and easier to get a school group into. It will also, for the first time, be only a museum.

## Flight Context

The Vancouver Police Museum and Archives sits at 49.2822 N, 123.0982 W, at 240 East Cordova Street on the eastern edge of Gastown, a few blocks inland from the Burrard Inlet waterfront; from mid-November 2026 the collection moves to 351 Abbott Street, roughly 700 metres west. Best viewed from 1,000 to 2,500 feet over the downtown peninsula. Orient on the working port along the inlet immediately north, with Ballantyne Pier, site of the 1935 clash, about 1.5 km northeast along the same shoreline; Gastown's low brick blocks and Chinatown lie just south and west. Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) is barely 1 nm northwest on the inlet with constant floatplane movements; Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 8 nm southwest, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) some 13 nm east and Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 13 nm southeast. Expect dense controlled airspace, harbour helicopter traffic, and low cloud over the city through the winter months.
