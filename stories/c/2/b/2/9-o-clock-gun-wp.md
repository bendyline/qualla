# 9 O'Clock Gun

A naval cannon cast at Woolwich in 1816 has been Vancouver's official time signal from Brockton Point for more than a century - and it has the ransom note, the paint job and the powder shortages to prove it.

**Location:** 49.2980, -123.1175

**Categories:** Naval artillery, Stanley Park, Time guns, Individual cannons, Vancouver landmarks

## Introduction

Before there was a cannon, there was a man with a stick of dynamite. William D. Jones kept the light at Brockton Point, and when Vancouver wanted a sound that would carry the length of Burrard Inlet, he obliged by detonating explosives out over the water. His replacement is, for the job, magnificently over-qualified: a 12-pound muzzle-loading naval gun cast at Woolwich, England in 1816, the year after Waterloo, with the monograms of King George III and Henry Phipps, 1st Earl of Mulgrave — Master-General of the Ordnance when it was made — still legible on the barrel. It has been going off in Stanley Park ever since, and a good part of the city still sets its evening by it.

## A Gun for the Fishermen

The cannon did not arrive as a civic ornament. Around 1894 the Department of Marine and Fisheries brought it to Stanley Park for an entirely practical reason: the Sunday close of fishing fell at 18:00, boats on the inlet had no reliable way to know when that moment came, and a cannon is very hard to misinterpret. On 15 October 1898 it was fired in the park for the first time, at noon.

The nine o'clock slot came later, and it served two audiences at once. Ordinary Vancouverites got a free time check every evening. More consequentially, ships lying in port could set their chronometers by it — a matter of navigational life and death in an era when an error of seconds translated into miles of longitude somewhere off the coast.

## Ten Seconds of Darkness

The gun is still loaded every day with a black powder charge, but nobody holds a lanyard any more. The Parks Board electrical department fitted an automatic electronic trigger, and the firing now happens on a schedule rather than a command.

There is a tell, if you know to watch for it. The fluorescent lights that illuminate the gun from overhead switch off exactly ten seconds before it goes, and come back on a few seconds afterward — a countdown for anyone standing close enough to notice. What the light show cannot fix is the clock, which runs fast. By late December the gun has drifted as much as fifty seconds early, and then on New Year's Day it is apparently reset and the year starts over, punctual again.

Since 1986 the cannon has stood under a pavilion designed by architect Gregory Henriquez, a centennial gift to the city from Ebco Industries, Chester Millar, First Generation Capital and the Hudson's Bay Company.

## Held for Ransom by Engineers

In 1969 the gun disappeared. University of British Columbia engineering students had taken it, and they kept it until a ransom was paid — not to them, but to BC Children's Hospital, in the form of a donation. The cannon came home and was promptly surrounded by a stone and metal enclosure, which is the kind of thing that happens after your artillery goes missing.

The enclosure did not entirely solve the problem. In 2008 UBC engineers got at it again and painted the whole thing red, silencing it for a spell. The university's engineering faculty has a long relationship with this cannon, and every episode of it has cost the Parks Board a cleaning bill.

## The Nights It Didn't Fire

For a daily ritual, the record is refreshingly spotty. The gun went quiet through the Second World War. A work stoppage silenced it in 2007. Scattered evenings simply passed without a bang and without an explanation — 20 May 2011, two nights in July 2017, four more across 2019, New Year's Day 2021.

The pandemic rearranged it entirely. From 30 March 2020 the gun fired at 19:00 instead, joining the nightly noise Vancouverites were making for health care workers; it stayed on the seven o'clock schedule until 31 July, well past the month it was meant to last. Then on 4 October 2020 it began firing at ten — almost certainly because somebody moved its clock back an hour a month before daylight time actually ended.

Damage to the structure kept it quiet for several days in October 2021. And twice the most basic problem of all: no powder to be had, from 11 to 27 February 2022 and again from 3 to 10 January 2023.

## A Sound You Stop Hearing

Vancouver no longer needs a cannon to know what time it is. Nobody has set a chronometer by it in generations, and the Sunday fishing deadline it was bought to announce long ago stopped needing it. It fires anyway, every evening, across water that carries sound extraordinarily well — over the Coal Harbour marinas, the West End towers, the container terminals on the north shore.

Live within earshot and the sound turns into furniture. The first few weeks you flinch; after that it registers only as the marker that the evening has turned over. It is the nights it fails that get noticed — a strange kind of monument, one that works chiefly by its absence.

## Flight Context

The 9 O'Clock Gun sits at 49.298°N, 123.118°W on Brockton Point, the eastern tip of Stanley Park where the peninsula hooks into Burrard Inlet. This is among the busiest low-level corridors in Canada: Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) lies about 1.3 nm southeast in Coal Harbour, with floatplane traffic threading the inlet at low altitude all day, and Vancouver International (CYVR) is roughly 9 nm south-southwest. Do not freelance here - the harbour operates under published VFR procedures and mandatory frequency requirements, and the Lions Gate Bridge 1.5 nm west marks the western constraint. From a legitimate transit at 1,000-1,500 ft the point reads clearly: the Brockton Point lighthouse, the seawall arc, the totem poles just west, and the gun's small pavilion. Bowen Island and the Sunshine Coast open to the northwest; the North Shore mountains rise sharply behind. Summer evenings give spectacular light down the inlet; autumn and winter bring low cloud that can close the harbour route entirely.
