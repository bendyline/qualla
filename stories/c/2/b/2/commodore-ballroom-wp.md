# Commodore Ballroom

A 1929 cabaret on Granville Street with a floor built to move under dancers' feet, still booking shows nearly a century later - and still the room Vancouver measures every other room against.

**Location:** 49.2806, -123.1210

**Categories:** Music venues in Vancouver, Nightclubs in Vancouver, Art Deco architecture in Canada, Concert halls, Canadian music history

## Introduction

You feel it before you notice it. Somewhere in the second song, when most of nine hundred and ninety people find the beat at the same instant, the floor begins to move with them - not alarmingly, just enough that the room seems to breathe. The Commodore Ballroom's dance floor is sprung, lined underneath with horsehair that absorbs the impact of feet rather than bouncing it back, and when the place opened in December 1929 only a handful of venues in the world had anything comparable. Everything else about the Commodore has been renovated, rebranded, closed, and reopened. The floor is the reason musicians talk about this room the way they do.

## A Cabaret With Terrible Timing

George Conrad Reifel built it and H. H. Gillingham designed it: an Art Deco room at 868 Granville Street, opened in December 1929 as the Commodore Cabaret. Four months is how long that lasted. The Depression closed the doors, and the Commodore stayed dark until November 1930, when it reopened under the first of many owners. The building was always a stack of unrelated businesses. Street level was fitted for retail, some of the shops notable in their own day. Below street level sits Commodore Lanes, a vintage bowling alley and poolroom, which means that for most of a century people have been rolling five-pin balls directly beneath the feet of dancers who had no idea they were there.

## Before the Amplifiers

The Commodore's first several decades belonged to swing and to the touring circuit that fed it. Tommy Dorsey came through. So did Duke Ellington. In 1948, Sammy Davis Jr. played the room and sat for an interview afterward with Wilf Ray of CKMO radio - an ordinary night's work, in a Vancouver that was still a modest port city at the far edge of the continent's entertainment map. What the Commodore offered then is exactly what it offers now: a room small enough that the performer can see faces, and a floor engineered on the assumption that the audience will not be sitting down.

## Drew Burns Turns It Up

In 1969, Drew Burns took over management, and the ballroom became something else entirely. Under Burns the Commodore turned into the rock room for western Canada - the venue where a band too big for a club and not yet big enough for an arena stopped on the way up, or came back to on the way through. The roll call is absurd for a space this size: Tina Turner, The Police, Nirvana, Red Hot Chili Peppers, Pearl Jam, Kiss, the Beastie Boys, Oasis, Dr. Dre, Lady Gaga, Katy Perry, Ice-T, Johnny Winter. Nine hundred and ninety people is a capacity that punishes a promoter's arithmetic and rewards everyone standing in the room. Billboard later named the Commodore one of North America's ten most influential clubs - the only Canadian venue on that list, and the oldest.

## Dark, Then Louder

It closed in 1996, and for three years Granville Street had a hole in it. The renovation ran $3.5 million and included a new hardwood surface laid over the original sprung structure, and the Commodore reopened on November 12, 1999 under the House of Blues banner. The seventy-fifth anniversary season began early, on October 16, 2004, with Tom Waits - a booking that reads like a statement of intent - and ran through to December 2005. Ownership has changed again since; Live Nation runs the room today, and the calendar for autumn 2026 is as full as it ever was. That continuity is the actual achievement. Grand old venues in North American downtowns have a habit of becoming condominiums, and this one has instead spent ninety-seven years doing the one thing it was built to do.

## Home for a Rest

For years the Commodore's St. Patrick's Day show belonged to Spirit of the West, the Vancouver Celtic rock band whose song "Home for a Rest" became something close to a national drinking anthem in Canada. In September 2014 the band's singer, John Mann, made public that he had been diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's disease. He was in his early fifties. The band announced a retrospective tour, then a retirement, and the final concerts took place at the Commodore in April 2016 - the room where the tradition had lived, with an audience that knew exactly what it was witnessing and sang anyway. Mann died on November 20, 2019, at fifty-seven. On the right night, when the floor starts moving, it is not difficult to believe the room remembers.

## Flight Context

The Commodore sits at 49.2806 N, 123.121 W, mid-block on Granville Street in downtown Vancouver. There is no approach here - the building is a narrow Art Deco facade wedged into a dense commercial street, invisible from the air except as part of the downtown block grid. Navigate by the peninsula instead: from 2,000 to 3,000 feet the downtown core reads as a bright cluster of towers between Coal Harbour and False Creek, with Stanley Park's dark green wedge to the northwest, the white sails of Canada Place on the north shore, and the domed roof of BC Place just southeast of Granville. Nearby airports: Vancouver Harbour (CYHC), a water aerodrome, lies about 1 nm north in Coal Harbour; Vancouver International (CYVR) is roughly 8 nm southwest; Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm southeast; Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 17 nm east. Downtown Vancouver sits inside busy controlled airspace shared by CYVR arrivals and floatplane traffic - get a clearance before sightseeing. Low cloud and rain dominate from November through March.
