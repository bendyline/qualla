# The Canadian

Four days, 4,466 kilometres and a fleet of 1950s stainless steel cars - North America's longest passenger train, on a schedule its own railway advises you not to trust.

**Location:** 49.2737, -123.0979

**Categories:** Rail travel, Itineraries, Transport in Canada, VIA Rail, Canadian Rockies

## Introduction

VIA Rail's official advice about The Canadian is not to plan anything for the day you arrive. The train is frequently late, and the railway says so plainly, offering to rebook missed connections rather than pretend otherwise. That is what happens when a passenger train shares 4,466 kilometres of freight railway with the companies that own it. Toronto to Vancouver takes something over four days - about 97 hours westbound, 92 eastbound - across the longest scheduled passenger route in North America, 542 km longer than the California Zephyr, though still less than half the run from Moscow to Vladivostok. It leaves twice a week in each direction. Nobody rides it to save time.

## A Name That Changed Trains

The Canadian began as a Canadian Pacific train on a southern line through Thunder Bay, Calgary and Banff. When both railways handed their passenger services to VIA in the 1970s, VIA ran it alongside Canadian National's rival Super Continental for a while, then in 1990 moved the Canadian onto the CN route through Saskatoon and Edmonton and let the southern schedule go. The name crossed to a different railway's tracks and stayed. The southern line is freight now, apart from the Vancouver-Banff stretch Rocky Mountaineer uses. The cars are the same ones: stainless steel Budd equipment from a 155-car order CP placed in 1953, rebuilt more times than anyone has bothered to count. Between Capreol and Winnipeg the train still does unglamorous work, stopping anywhere a passenger holds a ticket, because for some communities out there it is the only scheduled transport.

## Through the Canyons in the Dark

Eastbound trains leave Vancouver's neoclassical Pacific Central Station on BNSF rails, run through Burnaby to New Westminster, cross onto CN there, and at Gifford swing over the Fraser to Mission. From there the two railways share the Fraser and Thompson canyons by direction: eastbound trains take the old Canadian Pacific line, westbound trains the CN. The CN side runs through Painted Canyon, where the rails sit barely 60 metres above the Thompson, and crosses the Fraser at Cisco on a 240-metre steel arch directly above the CPR main line. It is some of the finest railway in the country, and on the regular schedule both directions pass through it at night. Beyond Basque the eastbound joins the CN main and runs into Kamloops, 428 km and nine hours from the coast.

## Two Words Missing From an Order

North of Kamloops the train climbs for six hours up the North Thompson, crossing the river repeatedly as dry plateau gives way to evergreen. It clings to the flanks of Groundhog Mountain and Mount Cheadle at 40 to 50 km/h behind slide fences, past the icefields of the Albreda Glacier. Pyramid Falls drops 100 metres down Mount Cheadle beside the track, and the train slows so people can see it. Just south of Valemount there is a memorial. On the morning of 21 November 1950, a troop train carrying the 2nd Regiment, Royal Canadian Horse Artillery toward Fort Lewis and the Korean War met the eastbound Continental Limited head-on. Seventeen soldiers died, along with both two-man locomotive crews; Gunner David Owens died in an Edmonton hospital on 9 December. The cause was two words - "at Cedarside" - omitted from a written order, so the troop train ran on instead of taking the siding. The telegraph operator, Jack Atherton, was charged with manslaughter. A Saskatchewan lawyer joined the British Columbia bar specifically to defend him and won an acquittal. His name was John Diefenbaker, and he became prime minister seven years later.

## Tête Jaune's Pass

Past Valemount the eastbound takes CN's Albreda Subdivision, which climbs and then runs level through Mount Robson Provincial Park beneath the highest point in the Canadian Rockies - Mount Robson, 3,954 metres, not the highest mountain in Canada though it looks like it ought to be. At Redpass Junction the line joins the lower westbound route and follows the north shore of Moose Lake, then Yellowhead Lake with Mount Fitzwilliam standing 2,909 metres above the far side. At Yellowhead Pass, 1,131 metres, the train crosses the Continental Divide on the gentlest grade in the range. The name belongs to Pierre Bostonais, an Iroquois-Métis trapper who guided for the Hudson's Bay Company and was called Tête Jaune for his fair hair. The pass is the Alberta-British Columbia border, the Pacific-Mountain time-zone line, and the edge of Jasper National Park all at once. The train stops ninety minutes at Jasper station, a 1926 CNR building designated a heritage railway station in 1992, with a 4-8-2 steam locomotive and the Jasper Raven Totem Pole outside. The station came through the July 2024 wildfire; 358 of the townsite's 1,113 structures did not.

## Twenty-Four Seats Under Glass

The reason to endure four days on a train is a glass dome with twenty-four seats. Each consist carries at least two Skyline cars, a lounge at either end and the raised dome between; the Park car brings up the rear with its own dome and lounges. Sleepers come in two flavours, Manor and Château: berths, single cabins, doubles and the occasional triple. The berths are the old way of travelling: couches by day, beds by night, converted while you are at dinner, the upper bunk with no window and no power outlet. Plenty of people ride the whole distance for the sake of it, but plenty more use it as a train - getting off at Winnipeg, Saskatoon, Edmonton and Jasper, which is what it was built to let them do.

## Flight Context

The Canadian's western terminus, Pacific Central Station, stands at 49.2737°N, 123.0979°W, on the north side of Thornton Park just east of downtown Vancouver and False Creek. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL the station is easy to pick out: a low neoclassical building with a broad forecourt, facing the geodesic sphere of Science World across the water, with the SkyTrain guideway and a fan of freight yards and container track running east behind it. The route itself is the better landmark - follow the rails southeast through Burnaby to the Fraser at New Westminster, then east up the valley past Mission toward the Fraser Canyon, where the two transcontinental main lines run on opposite banks all the way to Lytton and Cisco. Vancouver airspace is congested and controlled: CYHC Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre lies about 3 km northwest on Burrard Inlet with continuous seaplane traffic, CYVR Vancouver International roughly 11 km southwest, CZBB Boundary Bay about 22 km south and CYPK Pitt Meadows some 28 km east. Get clearance before overflying the downtown peninsula. Inland along the route, CYKA Kamloops is the principal airport on the mid-route corridor. Summer gives the clearest air over the canyons; from October to March expect low cloud and rain on the coast and rapidly changing mountain weather inland.
