# Little Sisters Book and Art Emporium v Canada

A Davie Street bookstore spent a decade proving that Canada Customs was deciding what was obscene by looking at who had ordered it.

**Location:** 49.2823, -123.1343

**Categories:** Supreme Court of Canada cases, Canadian freedom of expression case law, Canadian LGBTQ rights case law, Censorship in Canada, History of Vancouver

## Introduction

The same photographs could be legal or illegal depending on the name on the shipping label. When the Vancouver art collective Kiss & Tell sent its series Drawing the Line across the border, copies bound for mainstream retailers went through without comment. Copies bound for Little Sister's Book and Art Emporium were detained as immoral. Every bookseller importing from the United States deals with customs; one bookseller on Davie Street found that customs dealt back, consistently, for years. Proving that in court took a decade and ended at the Supreme Court of Canada.

## A Store on Davie Street

Jim Deva, Bruce Smyth and Barbara Thomas opened Little Sister's in 1983, and in June 1996 it moved to 1238 Davie Street, in the heart of Vancouver's West End. What it sold - gay and lesbian literature, art, and adult merchandise - is only half of what it was. In a neighbourhood that was becoming Davie Village, the store functioned as a public room: a place to find writing that existed nowhere else in the city, and to find other people looking for the same thing. Not everyone approved. A bomb exploded in the stairwell in December 1987, and another beneath the store in February 1988. Nobody was seriously hurt either time, and the store opened the next morning both times.

## The Border as a Censor

Because most of the stock came from American publishers and distributors, every order passed through Canada Customs. The Customs Tariff prohibited the import of obscene material, and the Customs Act put the burden on the importer to demonstrate that a detained book or magazine was not obscene - a reverse onus, which meant a bookseller had to prove a negative about material an officer had already flagged. In practice the decisions were made at the counter by individual inspectors with no particular training in the case law. Shipments to Little Sister's were detained again and again. The Drawing the Line photographs, made by Vancouver artists, were stopped over depictions of bondage while the identical images went to other shops unmolested. A small business cannot outspend a border agency, and that is roughly the point of a reverse onus.

## Ten Years to the Top Court

The store filed suit in 1990. When the British Columbia court ruled in 1996, it made a finding that mattered enormously: Canada Customs had targeted shipments to this bookstore and tried to keep them out. That was a breach of section 2 of the Charter - the guarantee of freedom of expression. But the trial judge also held that the obscenity provisions themselves were a justified limit under section 1, the clause that permits reasonable limits on rights in a free and democratic society. So the store had proved it was being singled out and still lost the larger argument. The appeal went up through the British Columbia Court of Appeal and was heard by the Supreme Court of Canada on 16 March 2000.

## 15 December 2000

The judgment split six to three. Justice Binnie wrote for the majority, joined by Chief Justice McLachlin and Justices L'Heureux-Dubé, Gonthier, Major and Bastarache; Justice Iacobucci wrote separately, with Justices Arbour and LeBel. The law survived. Prohibiting the import of obscene material does infringe freedom of expression, the majority held, but the infringement is justified. What did not survive was the reverse onus: the Court struck it down, so that the state must prove a work obscene rather than the importer prove it is not. The Court also found that the way customs officials had actually administered the law was discriminatory and needed remedying - language it explicitly suggested would assist the bookstore in any later fight. The practical line drawn was this: customs could continue to stop material the courts had already ruled obscene, but could no longer detain, pre-emptively or punitively, material that no court had ever adjudicated.

## What Outlasted Them

Legal victories of this kind are rarely tidy. The obscenity provisions stayed on the books; the store kept fighting over particular shipments for years afterward; the men who carried it did not live to see the whole arc. Jim Deva died on 21 September 2014, aged 64. Bruce Smyth died on 23 December 2019. In 2019 the BC and Yukon Book Prizes established the Jim Deva Prize for Writing that Provokes, an award named for a bookseller who spent a decade insisting that provocation is not a crime. During Pride Month in 2026, Canada Post put him on a commemorative stamp honouring historic LGBTQ sites. And the store itself is still there on Davie Street, still trading, now under the ownership of Parmjot Gill - which, given the decade it took and the two bombs it absorbed, is the part of the story most worth noticing.
