# Vancouver Maritime Museum

A Kitsilano museum built around the RCMP schooner St. Roch - the first ship to cross the Northwest Passage west to east, and the first to sail around North America.

**Location:** 49.2775, -123.1470

**Categories:** Museums, Maritime history, Arctic exploration, Ships, British Columbia

## Introduction

She was built across the water from where she now sits. In 1928 the Burrard Dry Dock yard in North Vancouver laid down a stubby RCMP schooner of Douglas fir, thick-planked and braced inside with heavy beams, built on the assumption that sooner or later the ice would try to crush her. It did, repeatedly, for a quarter of a century. The St. Roch outlived the attempt, and today she sits on dry land at Kitsilano Point with a museum constructed around her - the reason the Vancouver Maritime Museum exists at all, and the first ship to cross the Northwest Passage in both directions, and the first to sail all the way around North America.

## The Passage, Both Ways

Henry Larsen captained her for most of the voyages that matter. Between 1940 and 1942 the St. Roch became the first vessel to complete the Northwest Passage from west to east, a crossing measured in seasons rather than weeks, spent frozen in for the winters when the ice refused to open. That would have been enough. In 1944 she went back the other way, becoming the first ship to make the return trip through the passage and the first to transit it in a single season. On 29 May 1950 she reached Vancouver from Halifax by way of the Panama Canal, and in doing so became the first vessel ever to circumnavigate North America. She was a police boat. The record belongs to a working patrol vessel and the men who wintered aboard her.

## The Museum Built Around the Boat

Decommissioned in Halifax, the St. Roch was acquired by Vancouver in 1954 and brought home. In 1958 she was set into a dry berth at Kitsilano Point for restoration, and the following year a museum opened around her as one of the city's centennial projects, in the green flats of Vanier Park just west of the entrance to False Creek. In 1962 she was designated a National Historic Site of Canada, which is an unusual status for an object you can walk through. The museum is currently revitalising the St. Roch exhibit, with the work due to finish in the winter of 2026-27; the ship is not going anywhere, since the building would have to come off first.

## Carved from Bone

The model galleries are where the museum turns strange in the best way. Its finest miniature is a model of the French warship Vengeur du Peuple, made around 1800 out of bone by French prisoners of war - men with years to fill and almost nothing to fill them with, who produced rigging and gun ports of astonishing delicacy from the scraps of their own rations. Another gallery holds a fleet built entirely from paper and cardboard. Elsewhere there is a full recreation of the forecastle of HMS Discovery, the ship that carried George Vancouver's survey of this coast, and a workshop where visitors can watch model builders at work. Patience is the real subject of this floor.

## The Charts and the Chung Collection

The museum's archives run deeper than its galleries suggest. The Chung collection covers the Canadian Pacific steamship empire that once tied Vancouver to Asia, and it includes original hand-drawn charts from Captain Cook's Pacific voyages - working documents from the first European mapping of waters that Indigenous navigators had known for millennia. It is a distinct holding from the Wally Chung material at the University of British Columbia's Rare Books and Special Collections, a point that trips up researchers regularly. Alongside it sits a large maritime art collection and a library that draws people who never look at the ship at all.

## What Sits Outside

Out on the grass are two objects that belong to different centuries. One is the boiler of the Beaver, the first steamship on the Pacific Northwest coast - a lump of riveted iron that once made the difference between arriving and waiting for wind. The other is the Ben Franklin, a research submersible used by NASA, a white cylinder that looks more like a spacecraft than a submarine. Below the lawn, the museum keeps a small heritage harbour of historic working vessels. Inside, the current exhibit is called Iced In. The doors are open Tuesday through Sunday, at 1905 Ogden Avenue, a short walk along the seawall from the Burrard Bridge.

## Flight Context

The museum stands at 49.2775 N, 123.147 W, on Kitsilano Point in Vanier Park, on the south shore of English Bay immediately west of the entrance to False Creek and the Burrard Street Bridge. From 1,200 to 2,500 feet AGL the site is easy to pick out: a broad green park interrupting Vancouver's dense west-side grid, a steep A-frame roof at its waterfront edge, and the heritage harbour's small craft moored below. The English Bay freighter anchorage lies offshore to the west, usually holding a scatter of bulk carriers waiting for berths. The Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) at Coal Harbour is barely 3 km northeast around the point, and Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 9 km south on Sea Island; both sit under busy controlled airspace, so treat the park as scenery rather than a waypoint. Clear winter afternoons give the sharpest view, with the North Shore mountains standing directly behind the museum's roofline.
