# Sylvia Hotel

An ivy-covered 1912 apartment building on English Bay outlasted the neighbourhood around it, opened Vancouver's first cocktail bar, and was named after a girl with a better story than the building.

**Location:** 49.2887, -123.1420

**Categories:** Hotels in Vancouver, Heritage buildings in Vancouver, History of Vancouver, Architecture

## Introduction

Vancouver waited until 1954 for its first cocktail bar, and when it finally got one, it was in a red brick apartment building from 1912 named after somebody's daughter. The Sylvia Hotel has stood on Gilford Street at the edge of English Bay for more than a century, half-swallowed by Boston ivy that goes scarlet across the Gilford Street face every autumn, watching a West End of wooden houses turn into a forest of glass towers around it. It is still a hotel - a hundred and twenty rooms, a restaurant looking straight out at the water. And the daughter, as it turns out, had a more interesting life than the building did.

## Sylvia Court Apartments

The Seattle architect W. P. White drew it, Booker, Campbell and Whipple built it, and a Mr. Goldstein paid for it. When it went up in 1912 it was not a hotel at all but apartments, and Goldstein named the place for his daughter Sylvia. Brick and terracotta, rising above a neighbourhood of frame houses and beachfront bathhouses, it was the tallest thing in the West End and would remain so until 1958 - a span of forty-six years, which tells you how slowly Vancouver's densest neighbourhood actually grew before the high-rise boom hit it. The hotel restaurant traded on that height for decades under a slogan that reads now as pure optimism: Dine in the Sky. The towers eventually stole the view, the slogan retired, and the building settled into being the low old thing at the beach end of the street.

## Rooms for the Merchant Marine

The Depression did to Sylvia Court what it did to a great many buildings full of tenants who could no longer pay, and in 1936 the owners gave up on apartments and converted the place into an apartment hotel - long-stay residents and short-stay guests under the same roof. The war changed the arithmetic again. Suites were chopped into single rooms to house crews of the merchant marine, the civilian sailors who kept cargo and fuel moving across the Atlantic and Pacific. Afterwards the permanent residents thinned out year by year, and by the 1960s the Sylvia had become what it is now: a full-service hotel for people passing through. The 1954 cocktail bar - the first licensed in the city - arrived in the middle of that transition, and it is still the thing the hotel is proudest of.

## The Woman in the Name

Sylvia Goldstein grew up in the West End, a few blocks from her father's building, and she was a serious swimmer - strong enough to train in the cold open water of English Bay and to win a race across to Kitsilano Beach. She got the attention of the man she would marry, Harry Ablowitz, by diving into False Creek off a boat carrying Jewish singles on an outing. After she finished a degree at the University of British Columbia her family moved to Los Angeles, where she worked for a labour union; she came back to Vancouver in 1928, met Harry, married him inside a year and settled in North Vancouver, where the two of them founded a realty company. Much of the rest of her life went into Jewish seniors' care - community organizing, and and helping to establish a rest home and hospital. In her nineties she was still volunteering on a telephone check-in programme for older Jewish Vancouverites. She died on 12 April 2002.

## Mr. Got To Go

In 1975 the City of Vancouver designated the Sylvia a heritage building, which is why it is still here to be photographed rather than replaced by something with more floors. Its other form of protection has been affection. A stray cat turned up at the hotel at some point, decided the premises suited him and declined to leave, and the writer Lois Simmie turned him into Mr. Got To Go - three children's books, illustrated by Cynthia Nugent, about a cat who checks himself into a hotel and stays. The American folk singer Cheryl Wheeler wrote a song about the hotel that mentions a cat, possibly the same one. Nothing about any of this is grand. The Sylvia has never been the best hotel in Vancouver, or the most expensive, or the tallest since 1958. It is simply the one that has been standing at the beach the longest, going red in October, and letting the cat in.

## Flight Context

The Sylvia Hotel sits at 49.2887 N, 123.142 W, at 1154 Gilford Street where the West End meets English Bay Beach, immediately southeast of Stanley Park. From the air, look for the point where the dense downtown peninsula's tower cluster gives way to the dark green mass of Stanley Park, then follow the beach line southeast: the Sylvia is the low, dark, ivy-covered rectangle among much taller neighbours, one block back from the sand at the northwest end of the English Bay crescent. View it at 1,500 to 2,500 feet with the seawall as your guide - it rings the whole park and continues past the hotel toward Sunset Beach and the Burrard Street Bridge. Lions Gate Bridge spans the First Narrows on the far side of the park to the north, and Kitsilano lies across the bay to the south. This is controlled and heavily used airspace: Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) works constant floatplane traffic off Coal Harbour just a mile northeast, and Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 8 nm south, so clearance is mandatory. Boundary Bay (CZBB) is 15 nm south and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) 17 nm east. English Bay is often the clearest part of the city in summer; from November to March expect rain, low ceilings and a southeasterly that puts real surf on the beach.
