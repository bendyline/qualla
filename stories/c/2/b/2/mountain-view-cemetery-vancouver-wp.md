# Mountain View Cemetery (Vancouver)

Vancouver's only cemetery holds more people than most Canadian cities do, and its property lines are a map of how the city grew.

**Location:** 49.2355, -123.0930

**Categories:** Geography of Vancouver, Culture of Vancouver, Cemeteries in British Columbia, History of Vancouver, Tourist attractions in Vancouver

## Introduction

The first occupant had to wait outside. In January 1887 a body was carried south out of the new city of Vancouver toward its new burial ground, and everything that could go wrong did: foul weather, a raw unfinished road, and finally a broken wagon wheel. The man was buried in temporary ground outside the cemetery fence and moved inside only months later, which is why the first interment at Mountain View Cemetery is dated 26 February 1887 instead. The place has been filling steadily ever since. It covers 0.43 square kilometres, holds more than 100,000 grave sites and over 150,000 people, and it remains the only cemetery within the City of Vancouver.

## Property Lines as a Timeline

Mountain View opened in 1886, the year Vancouver itself was incorporated, and it replaced older burial grounds on the peninsula that the young city had just designated as its first park - the ground now called Stanley Park. Trace the cemetery's boundaries and you are reading the city's growth in land purchases. The Old Section, bounded by Fraser Street and 33rd and 37th Avenues, is marked on its western edge by three surviving trees from what was once a row of tall Lawson cypress. Land to the north became the Horne I Addition in 1901, named for the developer who sold it. The Jones farm between 37th and 41st came in 1910, another parcel in 1922. The tract between 41st and 43rd is called the 1919 Addition, and it holds many of the city's dead from the influenza pandemic - a whole strip of ground bought because a virus had outrun the existing graves. The last piece, Abray Park Addition, arrived in 1941 through a swap with the Park Board and carries the name of Nonus Abray, a squatter who had grazed cattle and tended an orchard there.

## Sorted in Death as in Life

For most of the cemetery's history, people were buried among their own - Jewish sections, Chinese and Japanese sections, plots for Freemasons and Oddfellows, separate ground for those buried at public expense. The divisions were not sentimental; they reflected exactly how the city organized itself above ground. The military presence is enormous. More than 12,000 Canadian service graves lie here, and the Commonwealth War Graves Commission maintains 579 of them: 319 from the First World War, largely gathered in a Soldiers' Plot, and 260 from the Second, some in a Veterans' Plot and the rest scattered through the grounds. Most of the airmen died while serving at the Royal Canadian Air Force station on Sea Island, now the site of Vancouver International Airport. Many of the sailors came from HMCS Discovery, the naval reserve division. In 1983 three granite stelai were installed, dedicated to all Canadian military members who have died.

## Three Bad Days

Some days arrive at a cemetery all at once. In 1918 the steamship Princess Sophia was lost near Juneau, Alaska, taking 353 people with her - the worst maritime disaster by loss of life on the Pacific coast of North America. Sixty-six of them are buried here. In 1910 an avalanche in Rogers Pass killed sixty-two men; thirty of them came to Mountain View. In 1909 a British Columbia Electric Railway streetcar wrecked at Lakeview, killing fifteen in what remains the worst transit accident in the city's history, and twelve were buried here. Walk those sections and the dates repeat down the rows, which is the plainest record a city keeps of the days it would rather not remember.

## The Stone That Says Only JOE

Among the markers is a flat stone bearing one word: JOE. It belongs to Joseph Seraphim Fortes, born in Port of Spain in 1863, who learned to swim off Liverpool and stepped ashore in Burrard Inlet on 30 September 1885, before Vancouver had a name. He began teaching children to swim at English Bay as a volunteer and by about 1897 the city had put him on the payroll as its first paid lifeguard. His own generation called him Old Black Joe and English Bay Joe, and the affection in the nickname did not cancel out what such a name meant in 1900. He is officially credited with twenty-nine rescues; the real figure was almost certainly far higher. When he died on 4 February 1922, his funeral at Holy Rosary Cathedral drew the largest procession the city had seen. Nearby lie Malcolm Alexander MacLean, Vancouver's first mayor; Yip Sang, who founded the Wing Sang Company in 1888; the sprinter Harry Jerome; Sara Anne McLagan, the first woman newspaper publisher in Canada; and Robert McBeath, a Victoria Cross recipient killed as a Vancouver police constable in 1922, aged twenty-three.

## Room for Three

A city that cannot expand its only cemetery has to get inventive about the ground it has. Between 2005 and 2009 Mountain View was substantially rebuilt: new administration and maintenance buildings, reworked landscape, and a memorial-service building named Celebration Hall that has since become a working cultural venue. The cemetery was rededicated in July 2009. The bigger change came on 1 January 2020, when a City by-law allowed graves to be shared not only between family members - already unusual in North America - but between people who had never met, up to three to a plot, interred one above another in a column nine feet deep. It applies only to new graves. The reasoning is partly environmental, partly financial, since the cost divides among the occupants. It is also, quietly, a very Vancouver solution to a real estate problem.

## Flight Context

Mountain View Cemetery lies at 49.2355 N, 123.093 W, a green rectangle set into the street grid of south Vancouver west of Fraser Street between 31st and 43rd Avenues - roughly 110 acres of open, tree-lined ground with no buildings to speak of, which makes it stand out cleanly against the surrounding rooftops. View it from 1,500 to 3,000 feet. Two useful references sit close by: Queen Elizabeth Park on Little Mountain, the highest ground in the city, is about a mile northwest at 33rd and Cambie and carries the domed Bloedel Conservatory, and the Fraser River's north arm runs about 2 nm south. Downtown Vancouver and Stanley Park are 4 nm north, and the North Shore mountains fill the horizon beyond them. This is controlled airspace under the arrival and departure flow for Vancouver International (CYVR), about 6 nm west-southwest, so clearance and precise altitude keeping are required; Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) works floatplanes off the downtown waterfront, Boundary Bay (CZBB) is 12 nm south and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) 14 nm east. Clear summer air gives long views to Mount Baker in the southeast; from autumn to spring expect low cloud, rain and morning fog over the delta.
