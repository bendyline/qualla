# Georgia Viaduct

Vancouver's twin elevated roadways are the only completed piece of a freeway network the city refused to build - and they were laid across the neighbourhood of Hogan's Alley to get there.

**Location:** 49.2772, -123.1060

**Categories:** Bridges in Greater Vancouver, Viaducts in Canada, Bridges completed in 1972, Road bridges in British Columbia, Buildings and structures in Vancouver, Black Canadian history, Urban planning

## Introduction

The viaducts are an on-ramp to a highway that does not exist. Two elevated roadways lift out of downtown Vancouver, sail over the old rail yards and the False Creek flats, and set you back down on Prior Street. That is the whole journey. They were designed as the first finished segment of a freeway system meant to drive through Strathcona, Chinatown and the Downtown Eastside — a system those neighbourhoods organized against and killed. The freeway died. The ramps went up regardless, and for more than fifty years Vancouver has commuted across a piece of infrastructure whose purpose was cancelled before it opened, over ground where a community used to live.

## Every Second Lamppost

There was an earlier one. The first Georgia Street Viaduct was built between 1913 and 1915, thrown across the swelling CPR rail yards and a corner of False Creek — a narrow structure with streetcar tracks laid into it that were never once used.

One detail says everything about how that bridge was regarded by the people responsible for it: at some point, every second lamppost was removed to reduce the weight it carried. Not reinforced. Not rebuilt. Lightened, by taking away half its lights. It served for more than half a century in that condition before the city finally replaced it, between 1971 and 1972, with the twinned structure standing today, in which the two directions of traffic are carried on physically separate decks.

## Hogan's Alley

Park Lane was an alley in southwestern Strathcona, running between Union and Prior from roughly Main Street east to Jackson Avenue. Everyone called it Hogan's Alley, and through the first six decades of the twentieth century it was the closest thing Vancouver had to a Black neighbourhood — a population of roughly 300 by 1914, in a city where Black families had very few other places to put down roots.

It had its own institutions. Community members established the African Methodist Episcopal Fountain Chapel at 823 Jackson Avenue between 1918 and 1923; one of its founders was Nora Hendrix, who had immigrated in 1911 and whose grandson Jimi would become one of the century's most famous musicians. Vie Moore opened Vie's Chicken and Steak House at 209 Union Street in 1948, and Nora Hendrix worked there; the restaurant stayed open until 1980, outliving the neighbourhood around it.

Most of Hogan's Alley did not survive the viaduct. The construction that cleared the way for it bulldozed the heart of the community — homes, businesses, the streets that held it together — and the people who lived there were scattered across the city.

## The Freeway That Wasn't

The replacement viaduct was conceived as a component, not a destination. Vancouver's planners of the era intended it to feed an extensive urban freeway network, and building that network would have meant demolishing swathes of Strathcona, the Downtown Eastside and Chinatown.

Those communities fought it, and they won — an outcome unusual enough in North American city planning that Vancouver is still, decades later, the city famous for having no freeway through its core. But the fight came too late for the viaduct itself, which was already committed. The Georgia deck opened to traffic on 28 June 1971. The Dunsmuir deck followed on 9 January 1972, and on that day protesters tried to block Mayor Tom Campbell's limousine from reaching the western end. The ribbon was cut on a bridge to a cancelled idea.

## Two Decks, Two Directions

In daily use the arrangement is simple enough. Eastbound traffic comes off Georgia Street, passes south of Rogers Arena and delivers you to Prior and Main. Westbound traffic — everyone calls that deck the Dunsmuir Viaduct, because that is the street it lands on — runs north of the arena from Prior and Main into downtown, feeding Melville and eventually Pender. BC Place sits on the other side.

The structures have a second career as a film location, helped considerably by how easy they are to close. On 5 April 2015 the viaduct was shut for two weeks so that Deadpool could be filmed on it; the movie was released on 12 February 2016, and its freeway pile-up sequence is, fittingly, set on a road to nowhere.

## The Long Goodbye

On 27 October 2015 Vancouver City Council voted to tear the viaducts down, replacing them with a six-lane surface street merging Expo and Pacific boulevards. That was more than a decade ago and the decks are still up. By January 2025 the demolition had been delayed indefinitely, with no redevelopment of the area contemplated until after the 2026 FIFA World Cup had come and gone from BC Place.

What happens to the ground underneath matters more than the ramps. The Hogan's Alley Society, a Black-led non-profit, has pressed for the Hogan's Alley Block to be developed through one of Canada's few Black-led community land trusts — an attempt to make the return permanent rather than commemorative, and to ensure that whatever replaces the viaducts does not displace anyone a second time.

## Flight Context

The viaducts run at 49.277°N, 123.106°W, along the northeastern edge of False Creek where downtown Vancouver meets the Strathcona flats. From 1,500-2,500 ft they are unmistakable: two parallel pale ribbons threading between the white retractable roof of BC Place to the south and the dark bulk of Rogers Arena to the north, with Science World's geodesic dome 0.7 nm southeast marking the head of False Creek. Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) lies about 1.5 nm northwest and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 8 nm southwest; Boundary Bay (CZBB) is roughly 13 nm south. This is dense controlled airspace over a major downtown - transit only on published VFR routes with the appropriate clearance, and expect heavy floatplane and helicopter movement along the inlet and the creek. Clearest views come on winter high-pressure mornings, when the North Shore mountains stand up sharply behind the towers; summer afternoons often deliver haze off the delta.
