# Deadman's Island

A 3.8-hectare islet in Coal Harbour that has been a Squamish burial ground, a smallpox cemetery, the cause of Vancouver's first riot act, and — since 1944 — a naval base.

**Location:** 49.2953, -123.1231

**Categories:** Coast of British Columbia, History of Vancouver, Islands of British Columbia, Stanley Park, Military history

## Introduction

He looked up, and the trees were full of boxes. In 1862 John Morton, one of Vancouver's earliest white settlers, rowed out to a small wooded islet in Coal Harbour and found hundreds of red cedar boxes lashed high into the upper boughs. One had come down and split open, showing bones and a tassel of black hair. Morton had wandered into a Squamish cemetery, where the dead were raised into the branches rather than buried. He decided he would like to own it. Chief Capilano told him what he was standing on: dead ground, the site of a battle in which, by the account Morton was given, some two hundred warriors had fallen, and where fireweed was said to have sprung up on the spot and driven the attackers off. Morton dropped the idea. Almost nobody since has.

## Two Names

The island covers 3.8 hectares — about nine and a half acres, a two-minute walk end to end. The Squamish name is skwtsa7s, and it is refreshingly plain: it means "island." The English name is the opposite of plain, and is generally understood to come from the burials and the battle tradition, though the Geographical Names Board of Canada did not make it official until 1937, and then chose the singular, Deadman Island, over the Deadman's Island everyone in Vancouver actually says. The gap between the two names is more or less the gap between two ways of seeing the place: one as a piece of ordinary geography in an inhabited world, and one as a curiosity with a ghost story attached.

## Quarantine

Settlers kept using the island as a burial ground in the city's early decades, until Mountain View Cemetery opened in 1887 and gave Vancouver somewhere official to put its dead. The island's grimmest use came immediately afterward. From 1888 to 1892 it served as a quarantine station during a smallpox epidemic — a place to send the infected, well away from the town — and as the burial ground for those who did not recover. People were carried out there to be isolated from a city that could not treat them, and a good many never came back. There is no monument. The layered use of this ground is the thing to hold onto: Squamish ancestors in the trees, smallpox victims in the soil, and later a community of squatters living on top of both.

## The First Riot Act

In 1899 a businessman named Theodore Ludgate leased the island from the federal government, intending to log it and build a sawmill. Vancouver's civic officials were astonished; they had assumed the island came with the Stanley Park grant. Mayor James Garden went out with police to stop the clearing, and when Ludgate's men and the constables faced off, Garden read the riot act — the first time it had been read in Vancouver. Ludgate slipped away without being arrested. Police were posted on the island, but the legal fight dragged on long enough for him to get his crews working anyway. By 1911 he had won in court, felled the trees, and cleared out the people living there. Then he failed to meet the terms of his own lease, and the island reverted to Ottawa: stripped bare, emptied of its residents, and owned by the government that had started the whole argument.

## Stone Frigate

In 1930 the federal government offered the island to the city for a park. The park was never built. Instead, in 1944, the navy moved in. HMCS Discovery — commissioned on 1 November 1941, with roots going back to the Vancouver Half Company of 1924, and named for the ship George Vancouver used to chart this coast — settled onto Deadman's Island as a stone frigate, the navy's term for a shore establishment run as though it were a vessel. Roughly 250 reservists train there today. The Naval Museum of Vancouver is on site, along with two cadet corps that parade weekly. When Vancouver hosted the APEC summit in 1997 and the Winter Olympics in 2010, the Joint Maritime Operations Centre ran out of this island. You reach it by a short timber bridge, or at low tide simply by walking across.

## Still Contested

The city's lease from the federal government expired in 2007 and was renewed the following year, which settled less than it sounds. Sam Sullivan, mayor at the time, sketched out a future with a downtown ferry shuttle, a maritime museum, and a building honouring the island's Indigenous history. None of it was built. The Musqueam First Nation includes the island in its land claim in the area. The Department of National Defence still holds it. For an islet you could cross in the time it takes to read this paragraph, Deadman's Island has generated an improbable amount of argument — over who owns it, what it is for, and whose dead are underneath — and none of those questions has been closed.

## Flight Context

Deadman's Island sits at 49.2953° N, 123.1231° W, tucked into Coal Harbour off the southeast shore of Stanley Park, joined to the park by a short timber bridge and by a tidal spit exposed at low water. This is intensely busy low-level airspace: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC), a water aerodrome, lies about 1.5 km east with floatplanes operating at low altitude directly over the harbour, and the whole area is inside Vancouver's controlled terminal airspace. Consult the VFR terminal chart and published harbour routes before going anywhere near it, and stay on frequency. Practical viewing runs low, around 1,000 to 1,500 feet, which is where the harbour routes put you anyway. From the air the island reads as a small dark-green oval with grey naval buildings, set against the marina forest of masts in Coal Harbour, with downtown's towers to the south and the Lions Gate Bridge crossing First Narrows 2.5 km northwest. Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 9 nm south, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 15 nm southeast, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 16 nm east. Expect low ceilings, rain and marginal VFR through the winter; summer afternoons are usually clear with the Coast Mountains sharp to the north.
