# Janet Smith case

A 22-year-old Scottish nursemaid died in a Shaughnessy mansion in 1924; the investigation that followed destroyed the evidence, produced a racist bill, and sent an innocent man into a torture cellar.

**Location:** 49.2328, -123.0970

**Categories:** History of Vancouver, 1920s in British Columbia, Anti-Chinese sentiment in Canada, Unsolved cases, Legal history

## Introduction

Before anyone had properly examined the basement where Janet Smith died, the undertaker was told to embalm her. The order came from both the coroner and the police. It was the first time in that undertaker's career that he had prepared a victim of a violent death without a post-mortem, and he noted, for whatever it was worth, unexplained burns on the young woman's right side. Everything else - whether she had been assaulted, what had actually happened in that house on 26 July 1924 - went into the ground with her at Mountain View Cemetery. The Janet Smith case is remembered in Vancouver not because it was solved. It never was. It is remembered for what the city did while pretending to try.

## A Long Way from Perth

Janet Kennedy Smith was born in Perth, Scotland, on 25 June 1902, the daughter of a railway fireman, Arthur Mitchell Tooner Smith, and Joanna Benzies. The family moved to London when she was eleven. In January 1923 she took a position looking after the newborn daughter of Frederick and Doreen Baker, and when the Bakers moved - to Paris in April, then to Vancouver that October - she went with them. The Bakers belonged to the city's social elite. By May 1924 the household had settled at 3851 Osler Avenue in Shaughnessy Heights, in a house belonging to Frederick's brother, Richard Plunkett Baker. Janet Smith was twenty-two, five thousand miles from home, and employed.

## The Verdict That Came First

On 26 July 1924, Point Grey Police Constable James Green was called to the house. The only other adult present was the family's Chinese servant, Wong Foon Sing, who said he had heard a bang like a car backfiring and then found her. A revolver lay beside the body. Green picked it up, which ended any chance of usable fingerprints. There was no blood or tissue on the walls, no powder burns, and injuries plainly inconsistent with the story - and Green concluded she had shot herself. The coroner's inquest split the difference with a verdict of "self-inflicted, but accidental death." Then came the embalming order, and with it the destruction of anything a competent examination might have found.

## Her Friends Would Not Let It Go

Janet Smith had friends, and they did not accept it. They took the case to the newly formed United Council of Scottish Societies, which pressed the provincial government and Attorney General Alexander Malcolm Manson until he reopened it. Her body was exhumed on 28 August 1924. A second inquest jury found that she had been murdered, and Manson appointed a special prosecutor, Malcolm Bruce Jackson. By then the case had acquired a second engine. The Vancouver Star, a scandal sheet run by Victor Odlum, had seized on it - helped along by the fact that a personal enemy of Odlum's was related by marriage to the Bakers - and the Star had already decided who was guilty.

## The Janet Smith Bill

Odlum had run for Parliament in 1921 on an anti-Asian platform and believed Asian immigrants could never assimilate. On 8 August 1924 he published an editorial headlined "Should Chinese Work with White Girls?", calling for a law to protect "white girls of impressionable youth" from what he described as the wiles of "low caste yellow men" - the language of that era's press, and a fair measure of the atmosphere around the case. In November 1924 the bill arrived in the legislature, sponsored by Mary Ellen Smith, the first woman elected to the BC Legislature and, in 1921, the first woman cabinet minister anywhere in the British Empire. The Janet Smith Bill would have made it illegal to employ Asian men and white women in the same household. The Vancouver Province pointed out that it breached the Anglo-Japanese Treaty of 1911 and exceeded provincial authority. It died after second reading.

## Six Weeks in a Cellar

Wong Foon Sing had come to Canada in 1913, leaving his family in Hong Kong. He was in his mid-twenties, and he was the obvious target. Abducted once in late 1924 by private detectives working with police knowledge, he was seized again on 20 March 1925 by men wearing Ku Klux Klan robes and held for six weeks while they tortured him for a confession he never gave. He was released on 1 May 1925. The kidnappers had been paid by the province and promised immunity; Manson knew where Wong was and let it continue, hoping the torture would produce a result. When it came to court, one man pleaded guilty and served nine months, another got a year, and a third was released into his mother's custody with the jury's recommendation of mercy. Wong himself was then tried for the murder in September 1925. In October the case was thrown out for lack of evidence. He kept working for the Bakers, and in 1926 he left Canada for good.

From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, Shaughnessy is unmistakable: an oval of curving, tree-heavy streets laid over Vancouver's otherwise rigid grid, with large lots and mature canopy.
