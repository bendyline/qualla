# 2011 Vancouver Stanley Cup riot

A hockey game ended at 8 p.m. and downtown Vancouver burned; by ten the next morning strangers with brooms had swept it clean, and a million photographs began doing the work of the courts.

**Location:** 49.2842, -123.1211

**Categories:** 2010–11 in Canadian ice hockey, 2011 in Vancouver, 2011 in British Columbia, 2011 riots, 2011 Stanley Cup, History of the Vancouver Canucks, Ice hockey in Vancouver

## Introduction

By ten o'clock the next morning the streets were clean. People had turned up downtown with brooms and dustpans and swept up the glass themselves, and the plywood going over the smashed storefronts filled with handwritten apologies - to the city, to the shopkeepers, to anyone reading. The Hudson's Bay store, whose windows had gone in the night before, thanked them with a free pancake breakfast. An Angus Reid poll taken shortly afterward found that 90 per cent of British Columbians were disgusted by what had happened. None of it undid the night before, when roughly 150 people were hurt, four of them stabbed, and a city that had hosted the Winter Olympics sixteen months earlier watched its downtown burn on live television.

## Six Games That Went Fine

Vancouver had done this before, badly. After the Canucks lost the 1994 final, a riot left more than 190 injured, $1.1 million in damage and charges against over a hundred people. For 2011, planners built a downtown fan zone with fencing and checkpoints where police controlled access and poured out any alcohol they found, and liquor stores in the area closed early. The template had worked beautifully during the 2010 Olympics, and it worked again through the first six games of the series, each drawing around 70,000 people who behaved themselves. Then game seven pulled an estimated 100,000 into the same space. People found ways past the alcohol checks. The corridors left open for ambulances and fire trucks filled solid with bodies and stopped being corridors at all.

## Four to Nothing

The puck dropped just after five on Wednesday 15 June 2011 at Rogers Arena, the Canucks having earned home ice with the league's best regular-season record. Patrice Bergeron scored at 14:37 of the first period and added a shorthanded goal at 17:35 of the second. Brad Marchand scored between them at 12:13, and again into an empty net late in the third. At the other end, Tim Thomas stopped all 37 shots he faced. Boston won 4-0 and took the Stanley Cup, having outscored Vancouver 23-8 across the series. It was the Canucks’ third final and their third loss. Outside, in a crowd the length of several city blocks, the last horn was the starting gun.

## The Night the City Became a Stadium

A car went over on its roof; before eight o'clock it was burning. Portable toilets collapsed under the people standing on them. Cars parked near the giant screens became fuel; the newspaper boxes left nearby became ammunition. Critics flagged both immediately. The independent review commissioned by the province and the city - a 396-page report released on 31 August 2011 as The Night the City Became a Stadium - put alcohol at the centre of its findings and concluded that police reinforcements arrived too late. It also concluded something more uncomfortable: with roughly 155,000 people downtown, no police force of any size could have prevented what happened. Mayor Gregor Robertson's first instinct was to blame "a small group of troublemakers." By 20 June, Chief Jim Chu was already correcting the picture - most of those being charged, he said, were "a wider spectrum of young people, many of whom do not have criminal records." UBC political scientist Glen Coulthard rejected the idea that anarchists had driven it, calling the label "just more of an assumption or bias that has been around for a long time." Another commentator called anarchists "a convenient scapegoat for the police to deflect responsibility for what happened."

## A Million Photographs

What made this riot unlike any before it was that nearly everyone present was filming. More than a million photographs and over a thousand hours of video reached the Vancouver Police Department. Seventy officers from eight agencies formed an Integrated Riot Investigation Team and spent years inside that footage. Several people turned themselves in after seeing their faces on the news, including the man who set the first car alight. The Insurance Corporation of British Columbia offered its facial-recognition software; in 2012 the province's Information and Privacy Commissioner, Elizabeth Denham, ruled that police could not use it without a warrant. The crowd was not so restrained. Online identification campaigns swept up minors whose names are protected by law, and police warned of "a growing danger that the tools of social media will be used to mete out vigilante justice." Sociologist Christopher J. Schneider called it lateral surveillance - citizens policing citizens - and named the gap it exposed: "There is a profound disconnect between who we are online and in life."

## The Couple on the Pavement

The investigation took four years. Police had recommended 1,204 charges against 352 people by July 2013; the last two suspects were charged in July 2015, for a final tally of 887 charges against 301 people, 274 of whom pleaded guilty. Crown prosecutors later put the total near $9 million - about $4 million in damage, $5 million in prosecution staffing. And out of all that, the image the world kept was of two people lying in the street, kissing, while riot police moved past. Vancouver sports photographer Richard Lam shot it almost by accident. Sports Illustrated called it the most compelling sports image of the year, and viewers immediately assumed it was staged. Lam had a simple answer: "What you don't see in the frame is that twenty feet beyond is the mounted squad, on horseback. I don't think they were in any mood for people to be playing around." Video later proved him right. The English band Placebo put it on a compilation cover in 2016. It remains the strangest thing about that night: the picture everyone remembers is one of tenderness.

## Flight Context

The riot centred on the downtown Vancouver core around 49.2842°N, 123.1211°W - the fan zone filled Georgia and Hamilton Streets between Rogers Arena and the Vancouver Art Gallery, with the worst damage along Granville and West Georgia. Fly the downtown peninsula at 2,000-3,000 ft AGL, southeast to northwest, and the geography lays itself out: False Creek and the white domed roof of BC Place on the south side, Rogers Arena immediately east of it, the glass towers of the West End ahead, and Burrard Inlet with the sails of Canada Place on the north shore of the peninsula. The Dunsmuir and Georgia Viaducts run in from the east over the old rail lands. The Lions Gate Bridge crosses First Narrows to the northwest with the North Shore Mountains behind it. Airspace here is tight and busy: CYHC Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre is a water aerodrome barely 1 km north of the site with constant seaplane movements over the inlet, and CYVR Vancouver International lies about 12 km southwest. CZBB Boundary Bay is roughly 25 km southeast and CYPK Pitt Meadows about 30 km east. Clearance is required before approaching the peninsula. June evenings - the season of the riot - give long light until nearly ten o'clock and the clearest views of the year.
