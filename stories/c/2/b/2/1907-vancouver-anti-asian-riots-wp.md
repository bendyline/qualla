# 1907 Vancouver Anti-Asian Riots

Ten thousand people marched behind a band to Vancouver's city hall in September 1907; by three in the morning, Chinatown's windows were gone and Powell Street had fought the mob to a standstill.

**Location:** 49.2806, -123.0997

**Categories:** History of Vancouver, Anti-Asian sentiment in Canada, History of Chinese Canadians, Indian diaspora in Canada, 1907 in British Columbia

## Introduction

The band was the point. When the Asiatic Exclusion League planned its Saturday parade for 7 September 1907, the organisers specifically arranged for music, because music draws a crowd - and a crowd was what the demonstration needed in order to make its argument to Ottawa. The route ended at Vancouver's city hall, then at the corner of Hastings and Westminster Avenue, a block from Chinatown. Around 10,000 Canadians and Americans came. Speakers were still describing the anti-Asian riot that had swept Bellingham, Washington days earlier when a teenager threw a rock through a window. The sound of breaking glass was the whole thing turning over.

## A Policy Imported From Australia

The League had a model in mind and said so. Australia had passed its Immigration Restriction Act in 1901, the legislative core of the White Australia policy, shutting down Asian immigration almost as soon as the country federated. Exclusion leagues along the North American Pacific coast wanted the same, and the Vancouver branch spent the summer of 1907 pressing for it. On 12 August its members adopted a resolution demanding that Ottawa enforce an immigration act passed by the provincial legislature that spring. At a later meeting they settled on the parade. None of this was coordinated between cities - Bellingham, San Francisco and Vancouver all erupted within weeks of one another without any central direction - which is in some ways the more disturbing fact. The same arguments were simply in the air along the whole coast at the same time.

## Who Was Already Here

British Columbia at the 1901 census held roughly 16,000 Chinese residents, 8,000 Japanese and about 5,000 South Asians - people who had come, broadly, to build the railways, to fish, and to farm and log. Ships kept arriving: the Suian Maru with 83 Japanese passengers in 1906, the SS Kumeric with 1,177 in 1907, the SS Tartar with 514 from India, the SS Monteagle with 901 more. By October 1907 that year's arrivals totalled 11,440 - 8,125 Japanese, 2,049 Punjabi Sikhs, 1,266 Chinese. Meanwhile the Grand Trunk Pacific Railway was lobbying Ottawa for permission to import 10,000 Japanese labourers to build its northern British Columbia line. Then the Panic of 1907 crossed the continent from a failed New York bank, jobs tightened, and a familiar transaction took place: economic fear looking for a face to attach itself to.

## Two Nights

From city hall the crowd went into Chinatown - four blocks bounded by Canton Alley, Hastings, Keefer and Westminster, with Dupont Street its commercial heart - and worked through it with rocks and beer bottles, breaking the windows of shops and homes where families lived above their businesses. Then the mob moved east to Powell Street, into the Japanese quarter, and there it did not go the same way. Residents met the rioters in the street and turned them back, repelling four separate waves through the night. More than fifty stores and businesses were damaged before the violence finally exhausted itself at three in the morning on 8 September. Police closed streets; the city went quiet and stayed frightened. In Steveston, down on the Richmond flats where Japanese fishing families worked the Fraser, there were attacks of the same kind.

## Twenty-Six Thousand Dollars

Ottawa sent William Lyon Mackenzie King, then Labour Minister and two decades from the prime ministership, to run a royal commission into what had happened. He assessed the damage and arranged compensation: $26,000 to the Chinese community of Vancouver, $9,000 to the Japanese. As a response to a mob that had spent two nights trying to destroy people's livelihoods, a repair bill is a curious sort of justice - it addressed the broken glass and left the reason for it entirely intact. What followed made the direction of official thinking clear. The riot had been staged to convince federal authorities that anti-Asian feeling in British Columbia was serious. On that count, the organisers succeeded completely.

## The Door That Closed

On 25 January 1908 Canada entered the gentlemen's agreement with Japan, under which Japan itself would limit passports for labourers and domestic servants to 400 a year. South Asians were handled less diplomatically. A $200 head tax was imposed on immigrants from India, and a continuous journey regulation required arrivals to travel without interruption from their country of origin - a requirement that was, in practice, impossible, because no vessel sailed directly from India to Canada. British Columbia then stripped South Asian men of the provincial vote, which removed them from the federal franchise as well. The arithmetic afterward is stark: annual South Asian immigration to Canada did not exceed eighty people until the 1940s. The continuous journey rule was still on the books in 1914, when the Komagata Maru reached Burrard Inlet and its passengers were refused landing - the riots' longest shadow, cast from two nights of broken windows.

## Flight Context

The ground where the riot ran lies at 49.2806°N, 123.0997°W, in the Powell Street and Chinatown districts on the eastern edge of downtown Vancouver, a few blocks inland from the Burrard Inlet waterfront. Fly it at 2,000-3,000 ft AGL. The downtown peninsula is unmistakable from the air: a dense cluster of towers on a point, with Stanley Park's forested wedge at its tip, False Creek cutting in from the south, and the port's container terminals and grain elevators lining the inlet to the north and east. The historic district sits just east of the tower cluster, where the buildings drop to two and three storeys - Chinatown around Pender and Keefer, Powell Street running east toward the port, with Oppenheimer Park as a green square in the middle of it. The North Shore Mountains wall the far side of the inlet and make an instant heading check. Nearby airports: CYVR Vancouver International about 12 km southwest, CYHC Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre water aerodrome roughly 2 km west in Coal Harbour, CZBB Boundary Bay some 25 km south, and CYPK Pitt Meadows around 30 km east. This is intensely busy controlled airspace over a major city, with floatplane traffic in the inlet at low level - clearance and vigilance both required. Clear winter days after a cold front give the sharpest light; marine stratus can cover the whole peninsula in spring and autumn mornings.
