# St. Paul's Indian Residential School

For six generations, Squamish, Tsleil-Waututh and Musqueam children were taken into a school on Keith Road in North Vancouver. The building is gone; a carved cedar wave stands where it was.

**Location:** 49.3196, -123.0875

**Categories:** Residential schools in British Columbia, North Vancouver (city), Educational institutions established in 1899, 1899 establishments in British Columbia, Former schools in British Columbia, Demolished buildings and structures in British Columbia, Indigenous history of Canada

## Introduction

Jason Nahanee was three years old when he entered St. Paul's Indian Residential School. Decades later, the Squamish Nation asked him to carve the memorial that now stands on the ground where it operated - a wave in concrete and red cedar, cresting high for the time before contact, dropping into a trough for the residential school era, and rising again with a canoe and two children in it. He carved the cedar himself. In March 2022 the memorial was vandalised, as it had been in 2020, and the Nation announced it would be taken away temporarily so that Nahanee could repair his own work.

## How It Began

A chapel came first. Father Léon Fouquet built St. Paul's Indian Church on the Mission Reserve, opening it in 1866. When Bishop Paul Durieu took up work among the Squamish, he determined that schooling should follow, and in 1898 he wrote to the motherhouse of the Sisters of the Child Jesus in Le Puy-en-Velay, in France. Three sisters stepped off a ship on 5 October 1898 - the first school for First Nations people anywhere on the North Shore. In its early years the Squamish themselves kept it going with food and donations. Then, in 1900, the Department of Indian Affairs took over its administration, and the school became part of a national system. The Oblates of Mary Immaculate ran it from 1899 until 1958.

## Who Was Sent There

Children came from the Squamish reserves along Burrard Inlet, Howe Sound and the Squamish River, from the Tsleil-Waututh and Musqueam nations, and from further off - Lil'wat families at Mount Currie near Pemberton, Shishalh at Sechelt, Stó:lo from up the Fraser. More than two thousand children passed through in six generations. They arrived at four, five, six years old and stayed until grade eight or until they turned sixteen; very few reached a grade twelve graduation. Inside, they were sorted by age and by sex, and often forbidden to see brothers and sisters held in the same building. They were punished for speaking their own languages and for practising their own traditions. In the early 1930s, government officials reported that the children were underfed and that the building itself was a 'death trap' and a 'fire trap.' Survivors who testified before the Truth and Reconciliation Commission described physical, sexual and emotional abuse. Squamish Nation spokesperson Khelsilem has called it 'a horrific place of abuse for many Squamish and other Indigenous peoples.'

## What the Country Did

St. Paul's was not exceptional, and that is the hardest part of it. Roughly 150,000 Indigenous children passed through Canada's residential schools across more than a century; the last federally funded one did not close until 1997. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission, which sat from 2008 to 2015, documented at least 4,118 children who died in these institutions, and its chair, Justice Murray Sinclair, believed the real number exceeded six thousand - most of them killed by tuberculosis and other diseases that spread through overcrowded, underfunded buildings. The Commission's conclusion was blunt: the system amounted to cultural genocide.

## Xwechtáal

Andy Paull - Xwechtáal, the Serpent Slayer - got the first six years of his education at St. Paul's, beginning the year it opened. Born in 1892, he went on to study law on the job between 1907 and 1911, and then declined to practise, because a Status Indian who became a lawyer had to surrender his status to do it. He served as secretary of the Allied Tribes of British Columbia and testified before Parliament in 1927 on Indigenous land rights; he managed the Native Brotherhood of British Columbia from 1942 and founded the North American Indian Brotherhood in 1945. He organised bands, orchestras, ball teams and labour groups. The political scientist Paul Tennant observed that by gathering children from many different peoples and holding them together, the schools inadvertently built a shared identity across nations and handed the next generation of leaders the tools to fight back. Paull kept up friendships with Oblates and nuns for the rest of his life, and he never stopped working against the system they served.

## The Ground Today

The school was pulled down in 1959. The archdiocese took the land and built St. Thomas Aquinas Regional Secondary School on it, a school Indigenous students were permitted to attend, and where some Squamish students still enrol each year. The residential school's actual footprint, in the 500 block of West Keith Road, is now a parking lot. Nahanee's memorial stands on the high school grounds, alongside a four-by-eight-foot plaque carrying at least four hundred names of children who attended. It was completed in June 2014 and paid for by an unlikely coalition: the Ustlahn Social Society, the Indigenous Women's Studies Institute founded by Kultsia Barbara Wyss, the Squamish Nation, the City of North Vancouver - and the Sisters of the Child Jesus and the Oblates of Mary Immaculate, the two orders that ran the school.

## Flight Context

The site lies at 49.3196 N, 123.0875 W, in the 500 block of West Keith Road in the City of North Vancouver, about 500 metres north of Burrard Inlet and roughly 3 km northeast of downtown Vancouver across the water. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look for the low green benchland between the inlet's shipping terminals and the rising wall of the North Shore mountains - Grouse, Fromme and Seymour behind it - with the school grounds and their playing field sitting among residential streets just west of the Lonsdale corridor. The Lions Gate Bridge is 3 km west, the Ironworkers Memorial Bridge 3 km east, and the yellow sulphur piles of the North Vancouver terminals are on the shoreline immediately south. This is dense controlled airspace: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is 2 km southwest across the inlet with continuous floatplane traffic, Vancouver International (CYVR) is 17 km southwest, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) 25 km east. Mountain wave and downslope turbulence off the North Shore ridges are common in strong northerly flow, and low cloud clings to the slopes for much of the winter. This is an active high school; keep clear and treat the memorial and grounds with respect.
