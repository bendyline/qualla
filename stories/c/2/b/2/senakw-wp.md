# Sen̓áḵw

A Squamish village at the head of False Creek, sold out from under its residents in 1913 and burned - and now, on the 11.7 acres the courts gave back, the site of one of the largest Indigenous-led developments in Canada.

**Location:** 49.2734, -123.1430

**Categories:** Indigenous history, History, Urban development, Vancouver, British Columbia

## Introduction

The Vancouver papers of 1913 called it Indians Moving Day. That was their phrase, printed without apparent discomfort, for what happened at the head of False Creek when the province's attorney general bought a reserve that was not legally his to buy: twenty Squamish men handed $11,250 apiece and told the alternative was nothing at all, families packing what they could carry, and settlers moving in behind them to loot and burn the emptied houses. Sen̓áḵw - the place inside the head of False Creek - stopped being a village that day. It took eighty-eight years of litigation to get a fraction of it back, and it is now covered in construction cranes.

## Cedar, Salmon, Shellfish

Long before the bridges, this shoreline was a seasonal village of the Squamish, chosen for what the inlet supplied: cedar on the slopes, salmon running the narrows, shellfish in the tidal flats at the creek's head. Other Coast Salish peoples, the Musqueam among them, came here to harvest, to trade, and to hold potlatches. Nobody built to stay until the mid-nineteenth century, when families from the upper Squamish River valley moved down and made permanent homes on the site. One of the children born here was August Jack Khatsahlano, who grew up to be a siy̓ám̓ of his people and who spent much of his life telling Vancouver what its own ground had been before it was Vancouver. A great many Squamish families today trace their descent to the households that stood on this shore.

## Forty-Two Names

In 1868, forty-two residents - fourteen men, sixteen women, twelve children - petitioned the colonial government about their land. The count matters. It is a list of a community, not an abstraction, and it worked: a survey the following year recognized the village on the south shore of False Creek, and in 1877 the Joint Indian Reserve Commission formally established Kitsilano Indian Reserve No. 6 at eighty acres. Censuses through the following decades counted forty to fifty people, occasionally fifty-seven, living by fishing and harvesting. Meanwhile a city was being built around them, and eighty waterfront acres began to look intolerable to the people building it. By 1903 Vancouver's mayor was publicly proposing that the reserve be repurposed for courthouses and fairgrounds.

## An Illegal Sale

What followed in 1913 was not a grey area. The Indian Act forbade the private sale of reserve land without federal authorization, and the federal government never endorsed this one. The attorney general of British Columbia arranged it anyway, paying twenty Squamish men $11,250 each - $225,000 in total for eighty acres of what is now some of the most valuable land in Canada - under conditions that left them little room to refuse. Women, who had signed the 1868 petition alongside the men, were excluded from the decision entirely. The residents scattered to other Squamish communities. Their homes were looted and burned. In 1916 the Vancouver Harbour Commission expropriated what remained, under the interim report of the McKenna-McBride Royal Commission, clearing the last families and structures from the shore.

## Mathias v. Canada

The Squamish never accepted it, and the legal case they eventually built rested on three points: that Canada owed a fiduciary duty to protect their reserve and had failed to discharge it; that the 1913 transaction violated the Indian Act outright; and that the remedy had to include land, not only money. Parts of the case reached the Federal Court before a negotiated settlement arrived in 2001, ahead of any final judgment. The Nation received compensation, and 11.7 acres of the original eighty - the portion at the south foot of the Burrard Street Bridge - came back under Squamish control. Eleven point seven acres out of eighty is not restitution. It is, however, something no amount of compensation could substitute for: ground.

## Six Thousand Homes on Eleven Acres

In 2019 the Squamish Nation announced what it intended to do with that ground, and the ambition startled the city: first 3,000 rental apartments, then 6,000, in towers rising as high as 58 storeys and 560 feet. Because the land is reserve, Vancouver's height and density limits do not apply to it - a detail that reads as poetic justice given how the reserve was taken. Ottawa committed a CA$1.4 billion loan on September 6, 2022 for the first two phases. Phase One's three towers hold 1,408 rental homes across roughly a million square feet, nearly 300 of them affordable, warmed in part by a district energy plant that recovers heat from Metro Vancouver's sewer trunk. In August 2025 the developer Westbank sold its stake in the first two phases to the pension fund OPTrust; the Nation holds the later phases outright. Leasing is under way. Hereditary Chief Janice George, a descendant of the people removed in 1913, has described what it means to watch the village come back.

## Flight Context

Sen̓áḵw occupies 49.2734 N, 123.143 W, on the south shore of False Creek at the foot of the Burrard Street Bridge, immediately east of Vanier Park. Fly it at 1,500-2,500 feet AGL. The site is now the most conspicuous new landmark on this shoreline: a tight cluster of slender high-rises rising abruptly from low-scale Kitsilano, unmistakable from any approach because nothing else on the south side of False Creek comes close to that height. Use the Burrard and Granville bridges as the frame - Granville Island sits beneath the latter, a few hundred metres east - with Vanier Park's open lawns and the conical roof of the Space Centre just west, Kitsilano Beach beyond that, and English Bay opening to the northwest. Nearby airports: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC), a water aerodrome about 2 nm northeast, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 5 nm southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 13 nm southeast, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 17 nm east. This is controlled airspace with heavy floatplane and helicopter traffic over the harbour and False Creek, and tower cranes on the site add obstacles well above the rooflines. Clear winter afternoons put the North Shore mountains directly behind the towers.
