# TRIUMF

Under the forest at the edge of the UBC campus sits the world's largest normal-conducting cyclotron - a 520 MeV machine that has spent half a century making medical isotopes, rare nuclei and Canada's seat at CERN.

## Introduction

"I don't really know what a cyclotron is," Pierre Trudeau said on 9 February 1976, "but I am certainly very happy Canada has one." He was standing at the south end of the University of British Columbia's campus, dedicating a machine that had already been producing beam for two years and would still be producing it fifty years later. Prime ministers are not required to understand particle accelerators. What the country got that day was a 520 MeV proton source with a vacuum tank 28 feet in radius - the largest normal-conducting cyclotron in the world, then and now - buried under enough concrete and earth that from the air the site looks mostly like parking lots and a quiet stand of second-growth forest.

## Three Universities That Could Not Afford It Alone

TRIUMF exists because in 1968 no single Canadian university could build what its physicists wanted. Simon Fraser, the University of British Columbia and the University of Victoria pooled resources, which is what the name encodes: TRI University Meson Facility. The acronym is now a fossil. The consortium has grown to twenty-one member universities from coast to coast - fourteen full members, seven associate - and the science long ago outgrew mesons, spreading into particle physics, molecular and materials science, nuclear medicine and accelerator development. The cyclotron produced its first beam in 1974. For decades it was the largest cyclotron in the world by any measure; Japan's Riken superconducting ring cyclotron is now the heavier machine, but TRIUMF still holds the largest beam radius and the largest vacuum tank, a consequence of running a deliberately low magnetic field to keep its negative hydrogen ions from being stripped apart in flight. In 2010 the IEEE designated it a Milestone in electrical engineering.

## The Isotope Pipeline

The most consequential thing a particle accelerator can do for an ordinary person is make medicine. TRIUMF started producing iodine-123 on Beamline 4A in 1978 for distribution across Canada, and by 1982 there was a literal isotope pipeline running to the UBC hospital. Queen Elizabeth II dedicated the laboratory's positron emission tomography facility in 1983 - only the second PET camera in the country. A pion cancer therapy programme ran from 1979. Proton treatment for ocular melanoma began in 1995 and reached its hundredth patient a decade later; for a cancer inside the eye, a beam that can be stopped at a precise depth is the difference between saving sight and removing the eye. The largest of these achievements arrived in 2015, when a TRIUMF-led consortium won the NSERC Brockhouse Prize for working out how to make technetium-99m - the most-used medical isotope in the world - on ordinary cyclotrons rather than in ageing nuclear reactors.

## Weighing the Unstable

The other half of the laboratory's life is rare isotopes: nuclei so short-lived they must be created, captured and measured in the same breath. ISAC delivered its first beam in 1998, and by 1999 it was the world's highest-current isotope separator on-line facility. ISAC-II added a superconducting linear accelerator that pushes radioactive ions past the Coulomb barrier, feeding instruments with names that read like a bestiary - TIGRESS, DRAGON, EMMA, TITAN, HERACLES. In 2007 physicists here measured the mass of lithium-11, at the time the shortest-lived and lightest ion ever weighed in a Penning trap; the following year they did it for helium-6. These are not abstractions. Lithium-11 has a halo, two loosely bound neutrons ranging so far from the core that this featherweight nucleus has a mass radius close to that of lead-208, and measuring it tells you where the limits of nuclear matter actually lie. The ARIEL project, begun in 2010, is building the capacity to run several such beams at once.

## Canada's Hand at CERN

From 1994 TRIUMF became the route by which Canadian physics reached the Large Hadron Collider. The laboratory built and delivered LHC magnets to Geneva in 2003, contributed to the ATLAS detector, and hosts the ATLAS Canadian Tier-1 Data Centre, one of a small number of facilities worldwide that take raw collision data from CERN and hold it for analysis. Earlier there had been the HERA beamline at DESY in Hamburg, built with Canadian funding in the mid-1980s. Later came T2K, the long-baseline neutrino experiment with J-PARC in Japan, joined in 2004. More than fifty international agreements now run through a site on the edge of a Vancouver campus.

## Fifty Years and Counting

TRIUMF marked its semicentennial in 2018 and incorporated in 2020, becoming TRIUMF Inc., a not-for-profit registered charity, under director Nigel J. T. Smith since 2021. Roughly five hundred people work there - physicists, engineers, machinists, welders, students - and more than a thousand visiting researchers pass through in a year, with the laboratory crediting itself with over a billion dollars of economic activity across the past decade. In July 2026 its ultracold neutron source was announced as a new world leader in its class. There is also, somewhere between Mars and Jupiter, an asteroid called 14959 TRIUMF. Trudeau was right about one thing: whether or not you know what a cyclotron is, it is a good thing to have one.

## Flight Context

TRIUMF occupies the south campus of the University of British Columbia at 49.2478 N, 123.231 W, near the western tip of the Point Grey peninsula. From 2,000 to 4,000 feet AGL the peninsula itself is the landmark - a broad forested headland jutting west into the Strait of Georgia, with the Pacific Spirit Regional Park forest filling most of it and the university's buildings clustered at the seaward end. TRIUMF sits inland of the main campus among low industrial-looking structures and parking areas; the cyclotron is underground, so there is no dramatic building to spot. Wreck Beach and the sandstone cliffs on the peninsula's western edge, the mouth of the North Arm of the Fraser River to the south, and English Bay with downtown Vancouver's towers to the northeast are the useful fixes. Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 5 nautical miles southeast and its control zone covers this area, so coordinate before transiting; Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is about 8 nautical miles northeast, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 13 nautical miles southeast. Sea fog can sit on the peninsula in spring and early summer mornings and low overcast dominates from November to March; clear summer afternoons give sharp views west to Vancouver Island.
