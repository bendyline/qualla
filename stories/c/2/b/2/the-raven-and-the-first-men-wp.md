# The Raven and the First Men

Bill Reid spent two years carving the instant the first Haida climbed out of a clamshell - and the yellow cedar he carved it from had to be glued together first.

**Location:** 49.2694, -123.2596

**Categories:** Haida mythology, Sculptures of mythology, First Nations culture in Canada, Indigenous art in Canada, Northwest Coast art, Indigenous sculpture of the Americas, University of British Columbia, Museums in British Columbia

## Introduction

Raven was bored. That, in the Haida telling, is the reason there are people at all. The trickster had come ashore at Rose Spit, the long sandy hook at the northeastern corner of Haida Gwaii, when a sound stopped him: small voices inside a large clamshell, belonging to creatures who had no intention of coming out. So he coaxed. He worked at the shell with his beak until curiosity did the rest, and out into the world climbed the first Haida. Bill Reid spent two years carving that single instant out of yellow cedar. The result sits in a skylit rotunda at the University of British Columbia, lit from directly overhead, looking like something still deciding whether to emerge.

## The Story Raven Tells on Himself

The account does not end with the shell. The beings who came out were all men, and Raven — who had created them chiefly to be entertained — grew bored a second time watching them play and wander.

So he went looking. He found a chiton, prised it open, and discovered small women living inside it. He carried them to the men, then settled in to watch what happened next. What happened was that the people paired off, spread out across the islands, and began having families. Raven, the story notes with some satisfaction, was never bored again.

It is worth registering how unsolemn this is. The engine of the Haida creation account is not divine purpose but a restless, self-interested bird who wanted something to look at — a creator who is funny, greedy, and easily distracted, and whose species survives because he could not sit still.

## A Boxwood Miniature in Montreal

Bill Reid was living in Montreal in 1970 when he carved the idea small, in boxwood, and called it The Raven Discovering Mankind in a Clamshell. He was working in a line he did not invent: the piece drew directly on the work of Charles Edenshaw, the Haida master whose treatments of the same subject Reid had studied closely.

The miniature was seen by the businessman Walter Koerner, who commissioned Reid to make it big — a monumental version for the Museum of Anthropology at the University of British Columbia. Reid travelled west from Montreal in 1972 to begin. The carving itself would not start for another six years.

## Not a Single Log

The delay was a material problem, and an instructive one. Reid wanted a monolith: one clean block of red cedar, more than two metres across, with no rot and no flaws. Trees that size exist on this coast. Trees that size without defects at their heart, at a moment when the oldest and soundest of them had already been logged, largely did not.

The solution came from industry. Rayonier Canada donated a quantity of yellow cedar beams, and with technical help from Koppers International they were laminated into a single working block. The finished sculpture reads as though it were cut from one tree. It is instead many pieces of wood persuaded to behave as one — which is either an embarrassment or the most honest thing about it, depending on how you feel about the century that produced it.

## The Hands on It

Carving began in the autumn of 1978, and Reid did not do it alone. Gidansda Guujaaw and George A. Norris roughed out the initial form. George Rammell carved the human figures struggling out of the shell. Reg Davidson and Jim Hart did the final tool finishing — the surface a viewer actually sees.

The work was declared complete on 1 April 1980, and unveiled that day by the Prince of Wales, now King Charles III. The ceremony that mattered more came two months later: on 5 June 1980 the Haida people and their guests celebrated it on their own terms.

A collaborative object, in other words, made by a group of named carvers under a single artist's direction. That is not a departure from Northwest Coast tradition. It is how monumental work on this coast has always been done.

## Into Every Wallet in Canada

From 2004 to 2012 the sculpture appeared on the back of the Canadian twenty-dollar bill, in the Canadian Journey series — which meant that for eight years the most widely circulated banknote in the country carried a Haida creation account on its reverse, handled daily by people who had no idea what they were looking at.

The bill has since been retired. The sculpture has not. It remains one of the Museum of Anthropology's most visited works, and its reception has stayed warm; critics have noted how deliberately Reid fused European sculptural technique with Haida form, which is a reasonable description of the object and an even better one of the man who made it.

## Flight Context

The Museum of Anthropology stands at 49.269°N, 123.260°W, on the northwest shoulder of the Point Grey headland at UBC, where the bluff drops to Wreck Beach and the Strait of Georgia. From 1,500-2,000 ft the campus is one of the clearest landmarks on the Lower Mainland: a dense block of buildings and playing fields at the tip of a wooded promontory, with the museum's glass-and-concrete pavilion set right at the seaward edge above the cliff. Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 6 nm southeast and its control zone reaches this shoreline, so plan the transit and get a clearance; Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) is roughly 7 nm east across the peninsula. Howe Sound and the Coast Mountains fill the view north, Vancouver Island lies 20 nm west across the strait. Winter mornings bring low stratus off the water that can obscure the bluff entirely; clear afternoons in late summer give the sharpest light on the headland.
