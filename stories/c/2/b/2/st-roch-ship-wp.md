# St. Roch (ship)

A 104-foot RCMP schooner built in North Vancouver crossed the Northwest Passage in both directions, became the first vessel to circumnavigate North America, and now sits in dry dock a few kilometres from the yard that built her.

**Location:** 49.2775, -123.1472

**Categories:** maritime-history, vancouver, british-columbia, exploration, museum-ship, national-historic-site

## Introduction

The Northwest Passage was not opened by a navy. It was crossed, twice and in both directions, by a 104-foot police boat with a 150-horsepower diesel and a Norwegian sergeant at the wheel. The St. Roch was launched in North Vancouver in 1928 to carry mail, supplies and Mounties to Arctic detachments - unglamorous work at the top of the world. Within twenty years she had done things Franklin died attempting, and she is still here: hauled out of the water, roofed over, sitting in dry dock at Kitsilano Point a short distance from the yard that built her.

## Built for Being Squeezed

Tom Halliday designed her, and he did not start from scratch. He took his lines from Roald Amundsen's Maud, the ship built to survive being locked in polar ice, and the result was less a vessel than a floating fortification. The hull went up in thick Douglas fir. Over that came a sheathing of Australian ironbark eucalyptus, a timber chosen purely for its extreme hardness. Inside, heavy beams braced the whole structure against the pressure that arrives when a million tonnes of pack ice decides to close.

She measured 104 feet, displaced 323 long tons, and carried a 150-horsepower diesel. Burrard Dry Dock in North Vancouver finished her in 1928. From 1929 to 1939 she ran supply and patrol work through the Canadian Arctic - a decade of ordinary voyages that taught her crew the ice better than any expedition could have.

## The Norwegian in Red Serge

Henry Asbjorn Larsen was born on 30 September 1899 on Herfol, a small island near Fredrikstad on the Norwegian coast - which is to say he grew up in the same seafaring tradition that produced Amundsen. He came to Canada and became a British subject in 1927, joined the Royal Canadian Mounted Police the following year, and was made captain of the St. Roch for her very first Arctic voyage.

He held that command for roughly two decades, rising from constable to sergeant and eventually to inspector. He did not take Canadian citizenship until 1947, by which point he had done more to demonstrate Canadian presence in the Arctic than almost anyone alive. The Royal Geographical Society gave him its Patron's Medal in 1946; the Royal Canadian Geographical Society made him the first recipient of its Massey Medal in 1959. He died on 29 October 1964.

## Twenty-Eight Months

In June 1940, with a war on, the St. Roch left Vancouver heading north and east. Amundsen had gone through the passage the other way on the Gjoa thirty-eight years earlier; nobody had managed it west to east. The first winter stopped them at Walker Bay, on the west coast of Victoria Island - months frozen in place, the ship a cold wooden house for a handful of men.

They broke free in July 1941 and worked their way to Gjoa Haven by August, only to meet impassable ice near King William Island and take shelter on the Boothia Peninsula for a second winter. In August 1942 Larsen forced her out. The St. Roch reached Halifax on 11 October 1942, twenty-eight months after leaving the Pacific coast. It was the second transit of the Northwest Passage by any ship, and the first in that direction.

## Eighty-Six Days, and Then All the Way Around

Having proved it could be done slowly, Larsen went back and did it fast. On 25 July 1944 the St. Roch cleared Halifax, took the more northerly channel - the route geographers regard as the true Northwest Passage - and tied up in Vancouver on 16 October. That is 7,295 miles in 86 days: the first return trip through the passage, and the first transit ever completed in a single season, without freezing in.

Six years later she went further. On 29 May 1950 the St. Roch arrived at Vancouver from Halifax by way of the Panama Canal, becoming the first vessel to circumnavigate North America. A ship built to deliver mail to police posts had, over two decades, closed a loop around an entire continent.

## Under the A-Frame

She was decommissioned at Halifax in 1954 and brought home to Vancouver. In 1958 she was placed in dry dock at Kitsilano Point, and her presence there helped determine where the Vancouver Maritime Museum would be built; the museum opened the following year as a centennial project. In 1962 the St. Roch was designated a national historic site of Canada - not a place, but a ship, with an address.

Parks Canada kept formal control even after she was enclosed in the A-frame hall beside the museum, and only handed it over in 1995. She sits there now with her hull under a roof, the ironbark still on her, in Vanier Park at the mouth of False Creek. You can stand on her deck in the middle of a mild coastal city and look at the bunks where men waited out two Arctic winters in a hull 104 feet long.

## Flight Context

The St. Roch is preserved at 49.28N, 123.15W in Vanier Park, at Kitsilano Point on the south shore of English Bay where False Creek opens into Burrard Inlet, Vancouver, British Columbia. From the air, look for the distinctive steep A-frame roof of the ship's hall beside the domed, crab-shaped roof of the H.R. MacMillan Space Centre - together they are the only unusual rooflines on an otherwise green waterfront point. The Burrard Street Bridge crosses just east, Kitsilano Beach runs west, and the downtown peninsula with Stanley Park stands across the water to the north. The North Shore mountains fill the horizon beyond. Nearby airports: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) 4 km northeast on Burrard Inlet, Vancouver International (CYVR) 10 km south, Boundary Bay (CZBB) 27 km south, and Abbotsford International (CYXX) 65 km east. Best viewing altitude is 1,000-2,500 feet, but note Vancouver's Class C airspace and heavy floatplane traffic on the inlet and English Bay. Rain and low ceilings dominate from November through March.
