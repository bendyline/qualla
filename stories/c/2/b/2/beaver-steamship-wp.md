# Beaver (steamship)

The first steamship to work the North Pacific coast spent fifty years serving fur posts and charting fjords, ended as a Burrard Inlet tugboat, and was slowly carried away by souvenir hunters after running aground below Stanley Park.

**Location:** 49.3140, -123.1422

**Categories:** Victorian-era merchant ships of Canada, Shipwrecks of the British Columbia coast, Stanley Park, Paddle steamers of British Columbia, Hudson's Bay Company, Maritime incidents in July 1888

## Introduction

For four years she sat on the rocks at Prospect Point, listing and visible from shore, and Vancouver took her apart. People rowed out and came back with planking, railings, fittings — anything two men could lift. By July 1892, when the wake of the passing steamer Yosemite finally shoved the hull off into deep water, there was not a great deal of ship left to sink. The Beaver had been the first steamship to work the North Pacific coast. She ended her working life as a tugboat and her afterlife as a souvenir stand.

## Built for a Coast Nobody Had Charted

Wigram & Green launched her at Blackwall Yard on the Thames on 9 May 1835 — 109 tons, 101 feet 9 inches long, 33 feet in the beam, drawing eight and a half feet. Two Boulton & Watt engines of 35 horsepower apiece turned a pair of thirteen-foot paddlewheels. She carried a brigantine rig, because no one sane trusted a steam engine alone on a voyage of that length, and four brass cannons, because the Hudson's Bay Company knew exactly what kind of coast it was sending her to.

The combination was the point. Sail depends on wind, and the inlets of the Northwest Coast are a maze of narrow, steep-walled channels where the wind does whatever the mountains tell it to. A ship that could ignore the wind could go where sailing vessels could not — up the fjords, into the river mouths, on a schedule.

## A Fur Company With an Engine

For decades the Beaver worked the Hudson's Bay Company's line of posts between the Columbia River and the edge of Russian America, carrying trade goods north and furs south. William Henry McNeill took her as her second captain in 1837 and held the command until 1851 — fourteen years of the same man reading the same tides.

Her strategic value showed in 1858, when news of gold on the Fraser brought some thirty thousand men up to Victoria within a month, most of them arriving from California, into territory Britain claimed but barely occupied. A vessel that could move officials, mail and cargo up the coast on demand, in any weather, was worth more than her cargo. The Beaver helped keep British authority credible during the Fraser Canyon rush at the exact moment it was most open to question.

## Charts, and Something Harder

In 1862 the Royal Navy chartered her to survey and chart the coastline of the Colony of British Columbia — a job for which she was almost purpose-built, since charting a fjord requires going into it and coming back out.

Two years later she was at Bute Inlet, assisting the Navy during the Chilcotin War. In the spring of 1864 Tsilhqot'in men attacked crews cutting a wagon road through their territory, work undertaken without their consent and during a smallpox epidemic that had devastated their communities. Five chiefs were hanged that autumn after coming in for what they understood to be talks; a sixth, ʔAhan, was executed at New Westminster on 18 July 1865. British Columbia exonerated all six in 2014, and Canada did the same in 2018.

## Towing for a Living

In 1874 a consortium that became the British Columbia Towing and Transportation Company bought her, and the first steamship in the North Pacific went to work pulling log booms and sailing ships around Burrard Inlet. It was an unsentimental second career, and it lasted fourteen years.

It ended in July 1888 on the rocks off Prospect Point, at the entrance to the harbour, with a drunk crew at the helm. Vancouver was two years old. The city that had just incorporated itself on that shoreline got to watch the oldest working thing on the coast pile up in plain sight of its front door, and then got to help itself to the pieces.

## What Is Left

Divers surveyed the wreck in the 1960s and raised one of the paddlewheel drive shafts. When the Underwater Archaeological Society of British Columbia went down in the 1990s, they found that rot and the currents of the First Narrows had finished the job — she had largely disintegrated.

The Vancouver Maritime Museum holds what survives: the boiler, and both drive shafts, the second of them repatriated from a collection in Tacoma. A plaque in Stanley Park marks where she went aground. It is a strange set of relics — heavy iron, the parts nobody could carry off in a rowboat — and an honest one. What is left of the Beaver is precisely the portion of her that was too awkward to steal.

## Flight Context

The wreck site lies off Prospect Point, Stanley Park, at 49.3140°N, 123.1422°W, on the south side of the First Narrows where Burrard Inlet meets English Bay. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL the geography is self-explaining: the dark forested wedge of Stanley Park juts north from downtown Vancouver, the Lions Gate Bridge crosses the narrows directly overhead, and the tidal race through the gap is often visible as a rippled seam on the water. Prospect Point is the park's northernmost tip, immediately east of the bridge's south tower. Nearby fields: CYVR (Vancouver International) about 8 nm south, CXH (Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre water aerodrome) 2 nm east in the inner harbour, CYPK (Pitt Meadows) to the east, and CZBB (Boundary Bay) to the southeast.
