# Chinatown, Vancouver

Canada's largest Chinatown, built by people the country taxed for arriving and then barred outright, and now reckoning with quieter streets and a museum that reclaimed the date of the exclusion.

**Location:** 49.2800, -123.0994

**Categories:** Chinatowns in Canada, Chinese-Canadian culture in Vancouver, History of Vancouver, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Neighbourhoods in Vancouver

## Introduction

For a generation of Chinese Canadians, the first of July was not Dominion Day. They called it Humiliation Day, and they refused to celebrate it, because on July 1, 1923, the Chinese Immigration Act came into force and shut the door on almost all Chinese immigration to Canada. Exactly one hundred years later, on July 1, 2023, the Chinese Canadian Museum opened its doors at 51 East Pender Street in Vancouver's Chinatown, in a brick building put up in 1889 by a merchant who had come to the goldfields with nothing. The choice of date was not an accident. Neither was the choice of building.

## Yip Sang's Bricks

Yip Sang was born in Taishan, Guangdong, in 1845, and went to California as a labourer at nineteen. He reached the Canadian goldfields in 1881, sold coal, kept books, and rose to superintendent, and between 1881 and 1885 he handled the labour contracting for roughly seven thousand Chinese men working on the Canadian Pacific Railway. Language was his asset; so was the trust of people on both sides of a divide neither side crossed easily. In 1889 he built the Wing Sang Building on East Pender Street, and it still stands as one of the oldest buildings in Vancouver. He had four wives and twenty-three children. His son K. Dock Yip became one of the first Chinese Canadian lawyers; his daughter Susanne studied at the University of British Columbia and became a school principal in Guangzhou. Yip died in 1927, four years into the exclusion.

## The Price of Being Here

Canada charged admission. The Chinese Immigration Act of 1885 imposed a head tax of fifty dollars on Chinese arrivals, raised to a hundred in 1900 and to five hundred in 1903, a sum meant to be unaffordable. Contempt ran alongside the tax. In 1896 Vancouver's health officer reported that the city had destroyed houses in Chinatown owing to what he called their filthy condition, in language that says a great deal more about the writer than about the people whose homes were demolished. In September 1907, a rally organized by the Asiatic Exclusion League turned into a mob of ten thousand. Around two thousand Chinese residents were driven out of their homes, and the damage to the neighbourhood was put at fifteen thousand dollars. Mackenzie King, then Deputy Minister of Labour, investigated and recommended thirty-six thousand dollars in compensation. The 1923 Act replaced the tax with a prohibition. It was not repealed until May 14, 1947, and the federal apology did not come until June 22, 2006.

## Gates, Gardens and Neon

What the neighbourhood built for itself is easier to look at. The Dr. Sun Yat-Sen Classical Chinese Garden opened in 1986 on Carrall Street, designed by Joe Wai, Donald Vaughan and Wang Zu-Xin. The China Gate on Pender Street arrived as a gift from the government of the People's Republic of China after Expo 86 and was rebuilt in stone and steel two decades later. The Millennium Gate, approved in September 2001 and raised in 2002 to Joe Y. Wai's design, straddles Pender at Taylor Street on the spot where a temporary wooden arch once welcomed the Duke and Duchess of Cornwall and York in 1901. Then there is the neon. Chinatown once glowed with it, until a 1974 sign bylaw thinned the signs out one by one. The Ho Ho sign, a rice bowl and chopsticks, was the last to go, in 1997. In 2017 a green and yellow rooster returned to Pender Street above the Sai Woo, rebuilt from a single second of film shot at a 1958 parade after the original could not be found.

## Quieter Streets

Prosperity moved. As Chinese Vancouver spread across the metropolitan area, particularly to Richmond, the restaurants and groceries that once had Chinatown to themselves found competitors everywhere. When city planners surveyed seventy-seven businesses for a revitalization plan in 2011, sixty-four percent reported falling revenue since 2008, and tourists accounted for only about twelve percent of customers. Lease rates here run a fraction of what Robson Street commands, which is both the problem and the opening. Council raised height limits in 2011 to bring residents back, and the same year the neighbourhood was designated a National Historic Site of Canada. Newcomers have arrived, some of them with no connection to the community that built these blocks, and the question of who Chinatown is being revived for is argued on the street rather than settled. That argument is itself a sign of life. The alternative is a neighbourhood nobody bothers to fight over.

## Flight Context

Chinatown occupies a compact grid centred near 49.280 N, 123.099 W, immediately east of downtown Vancouver, running roughly along Pender and Keefer Streets between Taylor Street and Gore Avenue. From the air at 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL it reads as a distinct low-rise island: a patch of two- and three-storey brick and cornice lines pressed between the towers of the downtown peninsula to the west and the residential blocks of Strathcona to the southeast. The best orientation cues are large ones. Follow the Burrard Inlet waterfront and the sawtooth roofs of the port, find the domed roof of the stadium complex and the Georgia Viaduct approaches to the south, and Chinatown sits in the wedge between them. The green square of the Dr. Sun Yat-Sen Classical Chinese Garden is visible as a dark, planted courtyard in an otherwise hard-surfaced block. Note that this is class C airspace directly beneath the Vancouver terminal area and adjacent to a busy harbour water aerodrome; clearance and current charts are mandatory. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is roughly 1 nm northwest, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 8 nm southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm south-southeast, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 15 nm east. Winter brings persistent low cloud and rain; the clearest light falls in late summer, when the North Shore Mountains stand up sharply behind the city.
