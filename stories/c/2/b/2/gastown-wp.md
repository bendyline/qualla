# Gastown

Vancouver began as a saloon that sawmill workers built in exchange for all the whisky they could drink in one sitting - and the city is still arguing about the man who bought the round.

**Location:** 49.2847, -123.1110

**Categories:** National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Neighbourhoods in Vancouver, Downtown Vancouver, Historic districts in Canada, Tourist attractions in Vancouver, History of Vancouver

## Introduction

The deal was simple. John Deighton arrived on Burrard Inlet in 1867 with a barrel of whisky and a proposition for the off-shift men at the sawmill: build me a bar, and drink whatever you can hold in one sitting. They built it. The Globe Saloon went up fast, and it worked, because the company town of Moodyville directly across the water was dry and this side of the inlet was not. Deighton talked - constantly, at length, about everything - and the men called it gassing, and they called him Gassy Jack, and they called the cluster of buildings around his bar Gassy's town. The survey of 1870 gave the place a proper postal name, Granville, B.I., and nobody used it. A city of three-quarters of a million people grew out of that saloon.

## Granville, B.I.

The 1870 townsite ran from the waterfront - now Water Street and the CPR tracks - south to Hastings, and between Carrall and Cambie, which is still roughly what people mean by Gastown today. Hastings Mill gave it a reason to exist: sawn timber, a working seaport, and a steady traffic of sailing ships come to load lumber for anywhere. Around that trade grew the rest of it, a rough resort town for loggers and fishermen ashore with money and for the crews and captains of every vessel in the inlet. This was not a genteel place and never pretended to be. It was a mill town with a good bar, at the exact spot where a continent stopped and the Pacific began.

## Quahail-ya

Deighton's first wife was a Squamish woman whose name no record preserved. She died around 1870, and he married again - to Quahail-ya, known also as Madeline. In 1940, seventy years after the fact, she told the Vancouver city archivist Major J.S. Matthews that she had been about twelve years old when that marriage took place. Deighton was in his late thirties. He died on 23 May 1875 at forty-four, and for decades a bronze figure of him stood on a barrel in Maple Tree Square, on the ground where his saloon had been. On 14 February 2022, during the 31st annual Memorial March for Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls, demonstrators covered the statue in paint and pulled it down. The neighbourhood carries his nickname. It is not possible to tell his story honestly without also telling hers.

## The Thirteenth of June

Almost nothing here is older than 1886, for a straightforward reason: on 13 June of that year, fire took the entire city. Between 600 and 1,000 buildings burned, with few surviving - the Regina Hotel at the north end and the Bridge Hotel in the southeast among the handful. At least 21 people died, and the true figure was never established, because Vancouver's population at that moment was so transient that no one could say with confidence who had been in town. What happened next is the more remarkable half. Within two weeks, Cordova Street from Carrall to Abbott had businesses trading again out of rough sheds. Within six months, 500 buildings stood where the ash had been, and a great many of them were brick this time. The Victorian streetscape visitors photograph today is the second Gastown, built by people in a hurry who had just learned a lesson about wood.

## The Freeway That Wasn't

By the 1960s the plan was to knock it all down. A major freeway was to be driven into downtown Vancouver, and Gastown, Chinatown and Strathcona lay in its path. The coalition that stopped it was an unlikely one - property owners and businesspeople on one side, the counterculture and political protestors on the other, agreeing on almost nothing except that the buildings should stay. Henk Vanderhorst, a Dutch immigrant who became a Canadian citizen, opened the Exposition Gallery on Water Street and drew a run of small startups in behind him, which did more for the case than any argument could. Mayor Art Phillips gave him a key to the city in 1976 and named him the first Pioneer Citizen of Gastown. The governments eventually did what the campaigners wanted and protected the heritage buildings. Ottawa made the whole district a National Historic Site of Canada in 2009.

## A Very Modern Antique

The most photographed object in Gastown is a fake. The steam clock at Cambie and Water was built in 1977, and it was built for two reasons: to cap a grate venting the downtown steam-heating network, and to stop people without housing from sleeping on the warm spot in winter. Inside, a miniature steam engine drives a chain lift that carries steel balls upward; they roll onto a descending chain, and their weight drives an ordinary pendulum escapement geared to four faces. Whistles, not bells, sound the Westminster chime. The original design failed and had to run on electricity until local businesses paid to restore the steam properly; it went away for major surgery in October 2014 and came back in January 2015. Everything around it now is the same argument in slower motion - boutiques, design studios, law offices and tech firms on streets where poverty never left. The Gastown Gazette has been covering the fight over gentrification since February 2013. In the summer of 2024, the city closed Water Street to most cars as a pilot, and for a season the cobbles belonged to people again.

## Flight Context

Gastown occupies 49.2847°N, 123.111°W, on the Burrard Inlet shoreline at the northeast corner of the downtown Vancouver peninsula, immediately east of the central business district and west of the Downtown Eastside. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL the district reads as a small wedge of low brick between the glass towers downtown and the rail yards along the waterfront - look for the white sails of Canada Place just west of it, the long line of port cranes and container stacks running east, and the SeaBus terminal at Waterfront Station on the shore. The Second Empire mansard of the Dominion Building marks its western edge. Beyond the inlet, the North Shore Mountains rise steeply, with the Lions Gate Bridge crossing First Narrows to the northwest. This is congested airspace: CYHC Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre is a water aerodrome directly offshore, with floatplanes working the inlet continuously, and CYVR Vancouver International lies about 13 km southwest. CZBB Boundary Bay is roughly 25 km southeast, CYPK Pitt Meadows about 28 km east. Obtain clearance before entering the harbour area. July and August give reliable visibility and long evening light down the east-west street grid; from November to March expect low cloud, rain and frequent marine stratus sitting on the inlet.
