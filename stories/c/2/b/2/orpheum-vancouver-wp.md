# Orpheum (Vancouver)

Canada's largest theatre when it opened in 1927, saved from becoming a multiplex by a public campaign and a city cheque in 1974, and restored by the same decorator who had chosen its colours fifty years earlier.

**Location:** 49.2799, -123.1206

**Categories:** Music venues in Vancouver, Theatres in Vancouver, Heritage buildings in Vancouver, Theatres completed in 1927, B. Marcus Priteca buildings, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Vancouver Symphony Orchestra

## Introduction

In 1973 Famous Players did the arithmetic on the Orpheum and concluded that one three-thousand-seat movie palace was worth less than several small ones. The plan was to gut the interior and carve it up into a multiplex. What followed was a public campaign called, without much subtlety, Save the Orpheum, and it drew in Jack Benny, who flew to Vancouver to lend the fight his name. It worked. On 19 March 1974 the City of Vancouver bought the building for $7.1 million, with the provincial and federal governments contributing. Vancouver had just purchased itself a theatre.

## Priteca's Room

The building they saved opened on 7 November 1927 with its first shows and officially the following night, as a vaudeville house. It was designed by B. Marcus Priteca, the Glasgow-born architect who built theatres across the west, and when it opened it was the largest in Canada: three thousand seats under a domed auditorium with a painted ceiling, a crystal chandelier of frankly intimidating scale, and staircases that exist mainly so that an audience can be seen using them.

There had been an earlier Orpheum a few doors away at 761 Granville Street. Once the new house took the name, the old one became the Vancouver Theatre, then the Lyric, then the International Cinema, then the Lyric again — a building running through aliases — before it came down in 1969 to make room for the first phase of Pacific Centre. The new Orpheum outlived it by a wide margin, though not without effort.

## Thirty-Four Years of One Man

William A. Barnes was the first manager. The name that stuck to the place, though, belongs to Ivan Ackery, who ran the Orpheum from 1935 until he retired in 1969 — thirty-four years spanning the death of vaudeville and the long reign of the movies.

A single manager for a third of a century does something to a building. Staff learn the room, audiences learn the staff, and the theatre accumulates the kind of institutional habit that cannot be installed later. When Famous Players decided four years after Ackery's retirement that the Orpheum was a liability, the city that rose to defend it was in large part an audience he had spent three decades cultivating.

## Painted Twice by the Same Man

Restoration began, and the city made one inspired decision: it tracked down Tony Heinsbergen, the American decorator who had chosen the interior's original palette of ivory, moss green, gold and burgundy, and brought him back. Fifty years after he first specified those colours, he supervised their return to the same plaster.

The Orpheum reopened on 2 April 1977 and has been the permanent home of the Vancouver Symphony Orchestra ever since. Canada designated it a National Historic Site in 1979. A second entrance opened onto Smithe Street in 1983. Somewhere in the middle of all this the present neon sign went up on Granville — a gift from Jim Pattison, and still one of the landmarks of a street long famous for its neon.

## What It Holds Now

Two thousand six hundred and seventy-two seats, a little short of the original count and considerably more comfortable. The Vancouver Symphony Orchestra fills them most often, joined by the Vancouver Bach Choir, the Vancouver Chamber Choir, and touring artists who arrive expecting a concert hall and find a vaudeville palace instead.

The room's one enduring weakness is what lies behind the curtain: a 1927 vaudeville stage was never designed for a modern symphony's storage and staging needs. In 2006 the City of Vancouver traded extra height and density on the neighbouring Capitol 6 cinema site to the Capitol Residences developer in exchange for a long-wanted backstage expansion — described at the time as the largest amenities trade in the city's history.

## The Room That Plays Other Rooms

Film crews found the Orpheum a long time ago, and it has spent decades pretending to be somewhere else. In the 2004 reboot of Battlestar Galactica, the auditorium is dressed as a heavenly opera house — a vision of a place that may or may not exist, which is a fair description of what Priteca was aiming at in the first place. The theatre and its neon have also turned up in Fringe and Highlander: The Series, and Brent Hodge shot his documentary on the musician Dan Mangan, What Happens Next?, here.

There is a neat symmetry in it. A building nearly lost because it was too big and too ornate for 1973 now earns part of its keep precisely because nothing built since looks anything like it.

## Flight Context

The Orpheum stands at 49.2799°N, 123.1206°W on Granville Street near Smithe, with its second entrance at 601 Smithe Street in downtown Vancouver. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL the theatre itself is a low roof lost among towers — pick it out by geometry rather than by the building. Granville Street runs a clear diagonal southeast across the downtown grid from the harbour to the Granville Street Bridge, and the Orpheum sits on its west side about two-thirds of the way down, roughly three blocks northwest of the bridge ramps and four blocks southeast of Robson Street. False Creek and Granville Island lie just beyond the bridge; Burrard Inlet and the cruise terminal sit behind.
