# Insite

North America's first supervised injection site, opened in Vancouver's Downtown Eastside in 2003 - and kept open by a unanimous Supreme Court of Canada that told the federal health minister he had no choice.

**Location:** 49.2811, -123.1011

**Categories:** Harm reduction, Drug policy of Canada, Public health, Downtown Eastside, 2003 establishments in British Columbia

## Introduction

In 2017 alone, 2,151 overdoses happened inside a single room in Vancouver's Downtown Eastside. Nobody died in any of them. That is the entire argument for Insite compressed into two numbers: the overdoses were going to happen somewhere, and the difference between somewhere and here was a nurse standing eight feet away. Across that year the room logged 175,464 visits - roughly 480 a day - from 7,301 different people. It is a modest storefront operation on a hard stretch of East Hastings, and it has been at the centre of a national argument that went all the way to the Supreme Court of Canada.

## What Is Actually in the Room

Insite does not give anyone drugs. People bring their own, and what the facility supplies is everything around the act: clean equipment, a booth with a mirror, nurses watching, oxygen and naloxone within reach, and a free service that will test a sample for fentanyl and carfentanil before anyone uses it. There is addiction treatment on offer, mental health support, and wound care - infections are a relentless problem for people injecting on the street. Vancouver Coastal Health and the Portland Hotel Society run it jointly. The neighbourhood it serves had an estimated 4,700 chronic drug users by 2000, a concentration severe enough that public health officials called it an epidemic - a medical word, deliberately chosen, for a population the city had long preferred to describe in other terms.

## An Experiment With Conditions

When Insite opened in 2003 - the first supervised consumption site in North America - it did so under a Section 56 exemption from the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act, granted by Health Canada under the Liberal government of the day. The exemption carried a string: the site had to be rigorously studied. It was, to an unusual degree. More than thirty studies appeared in fifteen peer-reviewed journals. The Lancet reported substantial reductions in syringe sharing. Work in the New England Journal of Medicine and in Addiction found that clients took up detoxification and long-term treatment at higher rates. A 2008 cost-benefit analysis in the Canadian Medical Association Journal projected net savings of $18 million and 1,175 additional life-years over a decade; a 2010 analysis in the International Journal of Drug Policy estimated the site prevented about 35 HIV infections and three deaths a year. Researchers looking for harms did not find them.

## The Minister Says No

Evidence and politics are different currencies. In 2008 federal Health Minister Tony Clement declined to renew the exemption past July of that year, and Insite's operators went to court. The Supreme Court of British Columbia sided with them: Justice Ian Pitfield found that applying the drug possession and trafficking laws here cut people off from health services in a way the Charter could not tolerate, and he gave Ottawa until 30 June 2009 to fix the statute. Parliament did not. The federal government appealed instead, and kept appealing. For three years the staff held the doors open and the counters running while lawyers argued about whether the doors should exist at all.

## Unanimous

On 30 September 2011 the Supreme Court of Canada decided Canada (AG) v. PHS Community Services Society. It was unanimous, written by Chief Justice Beverley McLachlin, and it was blunt. Refusing the exemption engaged Section 7 of the Charter - life, liberty, and security of the person - and the refusal was both arbitrary and grossly disproportionate, given that eight years of operation had produced no discernible negative effect on public health or safety. The Court did not send the question back for reconsideration. It issued an order compelling the minister to grant the exemption. It went further, setting out the standard for future applications: where a facility would decrease the risk of death and disease with little or no evidence of harm to public safety, an exemption should generally be granted.

## What Followed

Ottawa's response was legislative - a framework requiring, among other things, letters of provincial support before a site could be licensed, which slowed new applications considerably. The approach nevertheless spread: by July 2019 Canada had 39 authorized supervised consumption services across four provinces, with nineteen in Ontario, nine in British Columbia, seven in Alberta, and four in Quebec. The consensus has never been settled. Ontario moved to close ten of its sites by the end of March 2025 and barred new ones. Delegations still arrive in Vancouver to walk through the facility - from American states, from Colombia, from Brazil - because it remains the longest-running experiment of its kind on the continent and the most heavily documented. The numbers are easy to cite. The room itself is harder: fluorescent light, a row of booths, and somebody whose job is to notice, immediately, when a person stops breathing.

## Flight Context

Insite sits at 49.2811°N, 123.1011°W on East Hastings Street in Vancouver's Downtown Eastside, a few blocks east of Gastown. There is nothing to see from the air - it is a storefront - so treat this as a neighbourhood overflight rather than a landmark: fly the downtown peninsula at 2,000-3,000 ft AGL and find the strip of low prewar brick running east from the Dominion Building's mansard roof and Victory Square toward Chinatown, with the illuminated Woodward's 'W' beacon marking the block. North of it lie the Port of Vancouver's container cranes along Burrard Inlet; the Ironworkers Memorial Second Narrows Crossing spans the inlet to the east, and the North Shore Mountains wall the horizon. CYHC Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre, the downtown water aerodrome, is roughly 1 km north with steady seaplane traffic, and CYVR Vancouver International lies about 12 km southwest; CZBB Boundary Bay is around 25 km southeast and CYPK Pitt Meadows about 28 km east. This is controlled airspace over a dense urban core - obtain clearance. Expect low ceilings and rain from October through March, with the cleanest air on cold outflow days.
