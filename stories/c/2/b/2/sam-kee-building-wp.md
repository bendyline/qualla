# Sam Kee Building

Vancouver took all but six feet of Chang Toy's Chinatown lot in order to ruin it. He built on the six feet, and the result holds a world record.

**Location:** 49.2804, -123.1048

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Chinatown, Vancouver, Chinese-Canadian culture in Vancouver, Commercial buildings completed in 1913, History of Vancouver, Spite houses, Tourist attractions in Vancouver

## Introduction

Step through the door at the corner of Carrall and Pender and you have already arrived at the back wall. The ground floor of the Sam Kee Building is four feet deep. It runs ninety-six feet along the sidewalk, so there is plenty of building - just none of it in the direction you are walking. Guinness World Records lists it as the narrowest commercial building in the world, and Ripley's Believe It or Not! calls it the thinnest. What neither record mentions is why it is shaped this way: in 1912, a lobby group organized to drive Asian residents out of Vancouver persuaded city council to take most of the land it stands on.

## The Lot Before

Chang Toy (陳才) was born in 1857 and became one of the most substantial businessmen in Vancouver's Chinatown. Europeans in the city knew him by the name of his firm - Sam Kee (三記), the Sam Kee Company - and in 1903 he bought a standard commercial lot at the corner of Carrall and Pender Streets for it. The parcel was an irregular trapezoid, running roughly 30 by 88.71 by 30.94 by 96.04 feet. Nothing about it was remarkable. Pender was Chinatown's main street, the commercial spine of a community that had built itself in the face of head taxes, exclusion campaigns and a riot, and a thirty-foot lot on that corner was simply a good place to do business.

## A Street Widened on Purpose

The Vancouver Asiatic Exclusion League was founded in 1907, an all-European organization whose stated goal was expelling Asians from the city. Its most effective weapon turned out to be a municipal works project. In 1912 the League convinced Vancouver City Council to widen Pender Street - not for traffic, but because widening it would eat the frontage of Chinese-owned lots and render them unsuitable for commercial use. The city expropriated the land without compensation. Chang's parcel went from roughly thirty feet deep to six. On paper, the Sam Kee Company's corner had been erased as a going concern, which was precisely the point of the exercise.

## Built on a Bet

Chang bet a business associate that he could put a building on what was left. On March 27, 1913, the city issued a permit for a structure "6.19 feet in width, 96.04 feet in length." It was finished that same year at a reported cost of CA$8,000: a steel-framed, two-storey slab of a thing with retail shops at street level, residential and organizational units above, and public baths in a basement that reaches ten feet out under the Pender Street sidewalk. The upper floor is the wider one, at six feet, because its bay windows overhang the street. That single design move - pushing the second storey out over public air - is what makes the building habitable, and it is also what got Chang and every owner after him into a long argument with the city.

## Eighty-Five Years of Encroachment Fees

Having taken most of the lot, Vancouver then began charging annual encroachment fees on the fragments that reached beyond it - rent, in effect, for the airspace occupied by the bay windows and for the basement under the sidewalk. The bills went to the Sam Kee Company and later to Jack Chow Insurance. In November 1996 council voted to reduce the fees retroactively; the company's response was to stop paying and apply to have them waived entirely, citing the building's heritage value, its tourist draw, and its world record. Council waived the CA$2,500 basement fee in November 1998 and the CA$260 window fee that December. Rod Chow, son of the owner, described the vote as "the moral support of council" and the hoped-for end of what he called an eighty-five-year feud.

## Skinny and Mini

Jack Wing Chow (周永職), who lived from 1930 to 2021, bought the building in 1985 and hired the architect Soren Rasmussen to restore it, a CA$250,000 job completed the following year. Vancouver designated it a cultural heritage site on January 14, 2003, and it appears on the Canadian Register of Historic Places; a city heritage award followed in 2017. A glass wicket installed in 2016 lets staff serve customers standing on the sidewalk, which effectively doubled the usable business capacity of a four-foot room. Insurance is still sold on the ground floor. Upstairs and in the basement, the furniture and fittings are deliberately, comically undersized to suit the premises. Owners of the Skinny Building in Pittsburgh dispute the record on the grounds that theirs is a consistent five feet deep on every floor, while the Sam Kee is four on one and six on the other. It is a good-natured quarrel over a building that began as an act of municipal spite and answered it with carpentry.

## Flight Context

The Sam Kee Building stands at 49.2804 N, 123.1048 W, at Carrall and West Pender in Vancouver's Chinatown, just east of the downtown core. From the air it is effectively invisible - a two-storey sliver in a dense block - so navigate by Chinatown itself: from 2,000 to 3,000 feet look for the Millennium Gate over Pender Street and the walled green square of the Dr. Sun Yat-Sen Classical Chinese Garden immediately west of Carrall. The rail yards and the curve of False Creek lie south, the cruise-ship berths of Canada Place north, and the downtown towers rise immediately to the west. Nearby airports: Vancouver Harbour (CYHC) water aerodrome is about 1 nm north in Coal Harbour; Vancouver International (CYVR) roughly 9 nm southwest; Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 16 nm east; Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm southeast. This is congested controlled airspace beneath CYVR arrivals and busy floatplane routes - coordinate before descending, and expect low ceilings and rain from November through March.
