# Museum of Anthropology at UBC

A concrete building on a wartime gun battery at the western edge of Vancouver, holding 50,000 belongings from every continent and the sculpture that Canadians carried in their wallets for eight years.

**Location:** 49.2692, -123.2597

**Categories:** Anthropology museums, University museums in Canada, Museums in Vancouver, First Nations museums in Canada, University of British Columbia, Arthur Erickson buildings, Modernist architecture in Canada, Museums established in 1947

## Introduction

Bill Reid's The Raven and the First Men stands on a gun emplacement. When Arthur Erickson laid out the Museum of Anthropology on the cliffs of Point Grey in the 1970s, he built into the concrete batteries that had been sunk there to defend the approach to Vancouver during the Second World War, and the yellow cedar sculpture - Raven coaxing the first humans out of a clamshell - sits on one of them. From 2004 to 2012 an image of that carving was printed on the Canadian twenty-dollar bill, which means most people in the country handled a picture of this room's contents for years without ever knowing where the original lived. It lives at 6393 NW Marine Drive, on university land that is not technically part of the City of Vancouver at all.

## Out of a Library Basement

The collection started as a storage problem. UBC bought the Frank Burnett Collection in 1927; the graduating class of that same year acquired two Musqueam house posts and gave them to the university; salvaged totem poles came from the anthropologist Marius Barbeau, and the Buttimer collection brought First Nations basketry. All of it went into the basement of the Main Library, where it sat for two decades. The museum was formally founded in 1947, with Harry Hawthorn as its first director and Audrey Hawthorn as its first curator - a partnership that ran the institution through the years when it was less a museum than a very large cupboard with ambitions.

## Concrete, Post and Beam

Money arrived in 1971 from the federal government and the university, and in 1976 Erickson's building opened under a new director, Michael M. Ames, who would hold the post until 1997. The design borrows the post-and-beam framing of northern Northwest Coast houses and renders it in Erickson's favourite material, which is to say concrete - massive, grey, unapologetic, and rising as the visitor walks inward until the space is tall enough for a standing pole. Walter and Marianne Koerner had donated their Northwest Coast collection the previous year, and it furnished much of what opened. Erickson and the landscape architect Cornelia Oberlander wanted a reflecting pool out front from the beginning. They did not get one; pools were installed temporarily three times - for a film shoot in 1993, for the APEC leaders' summit in 1997, and for Erickson's eightieth birthday in June 2004 - before a permanent pool funded by Yosef Wosk finally arrived in September 2010, thirty-four years late.

## Belongings

MOA tends to call the things in its care belongings rather than artifacts, and the distinction is doing real work. There are close to 50,000 ethnographic belongings from every continent, plus roughly 535,000 archaeological pieces held by UBC's Laboratory of Archaeology. The Multiversity Galleries, opened as part of a $55.5-million renewal completed in January 2010, put more than 9,000 objects on view that would otherwise sit in drawers. That same project built the Reciprocal Research Network, which links Northwest Coast collections held in institutions around the world so that originating communities can find their own material without crossing an ocean to do it. The museum is also plain about how some of it arrived: of roughly 2,800 African belongings, the earlier ones came through missionaries, travellers and ex-colonial officers. Elsewhere there are 6,000 textiles, half of them Asian, including Cantonese opera costumes rated among the finest anywhere, and 90,000 photographs reaching back to the 1890s.

## The Great Hall

The poles are the reason most people come. Fragments and full figures from Haida and other coastal villages stand in the Great Hall, alongside large Musqueam works from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and contemporary commissions from Musqueam artists including Susan Point, Joe Becker, and Robyn and Debra Sparrow. Outside, the Haida houses were built under Bill Reid's direction, and many of the poles around them were carved by Reid with Doug Cranmer. The original frontal pole on the big house came down in 2000 - cedar loses to Pacific rain eventually - and Haida artist Jim Hart carved its replacement, the Respect to Bill Reid Pole. In November 2020 the museum began rebuilding the Great Hall to survive a major earthquake, which meant closing altogether in 2022 and moving the collection out. It reopened on 13 June 2024.

## Six Students Every Summer

Every summer the museum hires six urban Indigenous high school students, with two UBC students working as program manager and research assistant, for the Native Youth Program - the longest-running training program of its kind in British Columbia, co-run with UBC's First Nations House of Learning. MOA also helps deliver Indigitization alongside the Irving K. Barber Learning Centre, which funds and trains Indigenous communities to digitize their own heritage recordings rather than surrendering them to someone else's archive. These are small programs beside a building that holds half a million objects. They are also the clearest statement of what the place is trying to become: a collection that works for the people it came from, rather than merely about them.

## Flight Context

The Museum of Anthropology sits at 49.2692°N, 123.2597°W, at 6393 NW Marine Drive on UBC's Point Grey campus - the blunt forested headland that forms the southern jaw of English Bay, roughly 10 km west of downtown Vancouver. Fly the coast at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL and Point Grey is unmistakable: a dark green wedge of campus and endowment forest ending in cliffs, with the long pale arc of Spanish Banks and Wreck Beach below. The museum is on the northwest shoulder of the point, close to the cliff edge, with its reflecting pool visible as a bright rectangle on clear days. Look north across English Bay to West Vancouver and the North Shore Mountains, east down the bay to the downtown towers and Stanley Park, and south across the Fraser delta. Airspace is tightly managed: CYVR Vancouver International lies about 9 km southeast and its arrival and departure paths cross the mouth of the Fraser nearby, CYHC Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre is roughly 12 km east with constant seaplane traffic, and CZBB Boundary Bay is around 25 km southeast. Clearance is required. Late spring through early autumn offers the best conditions; winter brings persistent rain and low cloud, and onshore flow can put fog on the point while the city is clear.
