# Great Vancouver Fire

On 13 June 1886 two land-clearing fires met in a gale and erased a city that had elected its first mayor six weeks earlier - leaving two buildings standing and a rebuilt downtown within half a year.

**Location:** 49.2832, -123.1113

## Introduction

Vancouver's fire brigade was sixteen days old. The Vancouver Volunteer Hose Company No. 1 had held its inaugural meeting on 28 May 1886; the city had elected its first mayor, Malcolm MacLean, on 3 May. On 13 June the place they were all supposed to be organizing burned to the ground, and what stopped it was not the brigade but the simple exhaustion of things left to burn. Between 600 and 1,000 buildings went. Two survived. The settlement had been growing since the mid-1860s between two sawmills on Burrard Inlet, a rough company town called Granville that had only recently acquired a grander name, and on that Sunday it ceased to exist as a physical object.

## Two Fires and a Wind

Neither fire was an accident in the usual sense. Both were deliberate - the kind of slash burning that clears a forest for building. One, southwest of the city, was opening ground for the Canadian Pacific Railway's roundhouse. The other, near Cambie and Cordova, was making room for the city to expand into. The conditions were terrible and nobody seems to have thought so at the time: three weeks of abnormal heat, a landscape of dense deadfall left over from logging, and on that particular day an offshore breeze coming in off the Pacific. Men fought both fires the way men fought fires then, with buckets and shovels and wet blankets - one crew drawing water from False Creek, another working a hand pump at the Regina Hotel. Then the breeze became a gale. The crew at the southwestern fire had to abandon their tools and run for the False Creek shore. Pickaxe firebreaks failed. The two fronts found each other, and after that there was nothing to fight.

## The Water's Edge

People ran for water, because there was nowhere else to run. They went to the shores of Burrard Inlet and False Creek and into the water itself. Roughly two hundred reached the Robert Kerr, a sailing ship anchored offshore, and got aboard. Others were in the inlet, out of their depth or exhausted or both, and it was the Squamish - whose villages had been on these shores long before either sawmill - who put canoes in the water and went out to get them. They paddled survivors to safety while the town they had watched being built burned behind them. It is the detail most often left out of the founding story, and it belongs near the front of it.

## Counting

How many died is genuinely unknown, and the reason is itself a portrait of the place. Vancouver in 1886 was a transient city of mill hands and railway labourers, many of them recent arrivals with no fixed address and nobody local to report them missing. Modern accounts put the toll at at least twenty-one. Contemporary newspapers ranged from five to fifty. One Michigan paper, the Manistee Weekly Times, told its readers on 15 June that twelve had died, blamed the Canadian Pacific Railway for the blaze, and printed a promise of immediate aid from the mayor of Toronto - none of which any other source confirms. Two buildings came through: the Regina Hotel to the north and the Bridge Hotel to the southeast. The Bridge Hotel became the morgue.

## Our City Is Ashes

MacLean's telegram to Prime Minister John A. Macdonald is the whole disaster compressed into one breathless line: "Our city is ashes three thousand people homeless can you send us any government aid?" The answer came faster from close by than from Ottawa. Relief supplies from New Westminster and Port Moody reached the survivors by midnight. Richard Alexander, who managed the Hastings Mill, announced free lumber to anyone rebuilding a home or a business - an offer of consequence, given that the mill was one of the two reasons the settlement existed. MacLean swore in three special constables to stop looting, which is how Vancouver acquired its first police force, and set up a city hall in a tent because there was no alternative.

## Brick

Cordova Street had structures on it again within two weeks. Within six months five hundred buildings stood where the town had been, and this time a great many of them were brick - the lesson of June having been absorbed at some expense. The rebuilt city was also, immediately, a bigger one. The Canadian Pacific Railway was Vancouver's largest landowner and the engine of everything that followed; the transcontinental line arriving at tidewater turned a mill village into a terminus. From several hundred people, Vancouver reached 2,000 within a year of the fire and 13,000 within six. The downtown grid that burned in a single afternoon in 1886 is, in its street lines, still the downtown grid today.

## Flight Context

The heart of the burned district lies around 49.2832 N, 123.1113 W, in what is now Vancouver's Gastown and Downtown Eastside, between Burrard Inlet to the north and the head of False Creek to the south. From 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL the geography that shaped the fire is still perfectly legible: the city sat on a narrow neck of land with water on two sides, which is why survivors had nowhere to go but into it. Look for the Gastown steam clock district and the sawtooth cruise-ship berths of Canada Place on the inlet shore, with the CPR's historic Waterfront Station beside them; False Creek, Science World's geodesic dome and the Cambie and Georgia viaducts mark the southern limit. Stanley Park is the forested peninsula northwest, the North Shore mountains rise directly across the inlet. Airspace here is busy and controlled - Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) sits directly on the inlet with continuous floatplane traffic, Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 7 nautical miles southwest, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 15 nautical miles east. Expect low cloud and rain from November to March; clear summer afternoons with a westerly inflow give the best light on the old street grid.
