# Kuomintang Building (Vancouver)

A four-storey brick headquarters at Gore and Pender, opened in 1921 and still flying three flags, marks the corner where Vancouver's Chinatown built itself a public face two years before Canada shut the door.

**Location:** 49.2802, -123.0976

**Categories:** heritage, vancouver, british-columbia, architecture, chinese-canadian-history, history

## Introduction

Three flags fly from a rooftop at the corner of Gore Avenue and Pender Street. One is Canada's. One is the white sun on blue that the Kuomintang carried long before it governed anything. The third is the flag of the Republic of China. They stand over a four-storey brick building that opened on 4 May 1921, in a country that at the time charged a Chinese immigrant five hundred dollars for the right to step off the boat - and the people who put up that building chose, deliberately, to make it the most conspicuous thing on the block.

## Built to Be Seen

The Chinese Nationalist League of Canada, the Kuomintang's affiliate in this country, commissioned the building as the party's headquarters for Western Canada and hired W. E. Sproat, an architect with a long list of Vancouver work behind him. The brief was not modest. Sproat was asked for something that would stand out in Chinatown and announce the neighbourhood's importance, and he spent all of 1920 building it.

The site mattered as much as the design. Gore and Pender marked the historical eastern boundary between Chinatown and Vancouver proper, which made it a threshold rather than an interior. Sproat answered with a facade that mixes Chinese and Western elements - fitting, for a building standing on the seam between them. Large traditional characters run below the fourth-floor windows, naming the League that built it. The ground floor, for most of the building's life, was let to wholesale grocers. Political headquarters upstairs, sacks of rice downstairs.

## Two Years Before the Door Closed

To understand what a four-storey headquarters meant in 1921, you have to know what the previous forty years had cost. Canada began taxing Chinese arrivals in 1885 at fifty dollars a head; the figure rose to one hundred in 1900 and five hundred in 1903, a sum that could take years of labour to assemble and was charged to no other group of immigrants. In September 1907 a mob of around ten thousand moved through the neighbourhood, driving some two thousand Chinese residents from their homes.

Then, in 1923, Parliament passed a new Chinese Immigration Act. It dropped the head tax and replaced it with something worse: an almost total ban on Chinese immigration. Husbands in Vancouver could not bring wives. Fathers could not bring children. The building at Gore and Pender had been open barely two years, and the community that raised it spent the next generation cut off from its families by statute.

## Grocers, Weddings, and a School

For all the politics in its name, the building's daily life was ordinary in the best sense. The Chinese Public School operated inside it until 1953 - children who had already sat through a full day in an English-language classroom coming here in the late afternoon to learn to read and write Chinese, so that the language would survive a generation raised in Canada.

The same rooms hosted wedding receptions. A community with limited access to the city's halls and hotels needed somewhere large enough to seat everyone, and this was it. So the building accumulated the layers that make a place matter to people: the party meeting, the wedding banquet, the long afternoon of character drills, the grocer's delivery at the curb. Wayson Choy caught that texture in his 1995 novel The Jade Peony, set in the Chinatown of the 1930s, where a fictional business called American Steam Cleaners operates out of the Kuomintang Building.

## What the Corner Holds Now

The building was granted municipal heritage recognition on 14 January 2003 and is listed as number 2744 in the Canadian Register of Historic Places. Eight years later, on 19 July 2011, the whole of Vancouver's Chinatown was designated a national historic site of Canada: not a single building, but the neighbourhood entire. The province had already named it a historic area in 1971, and the Millennium Gate went up on Pender in 2002.

Recognition has not been the same thing as health. Chinatown has contended with vacant storefronts - around one unit in ten - even as new businesses and housing arrive and residents argue, sometimes fiercely, over what revitalization here should mean. Against that, the flags on the roof are a stubborn kind of statement. They mark a building that has outlasted the exclusion act, the party's loss of the mainland, and most of the wholesale grocers - still standing on the corner it was designed to be noticed from.

## Flight Context

The building stands at 49.28N, 123.10W, at Gore Avenue and East Pender Street on the eastern edge of Vancouver's Chinatown, British Columbia. It is a single four-storey structure and cannot be picked out from altitude, so navigate by the district: Chinatown sits immediately southeast of the downtown peninsula's towers, with the Georgia and Dunsmuir viaducts arcing in from the east and False Creek, Rogers Arena and the white roof of BC Place just to the south. The geodesic dome of Science World is about one kilometre southeast, the clearest single landmark in the area. Burrard Inlet and the port's container cranes lie roughly one kilometre north, with the North Shore mountains beyond. Nearby airports: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) 2 km northwest on the inlet, Vancouver International (CYVR) 13 km southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) 28 km south, and Abbotsford International (CYXX) 60 km east. Best viewing altitude is 1,500-3,000 feet, though Vancouver's Class C airspace and the CYVR and CYHC approach paths restrict low flight over the downtown core. Expect low cloud and rain from late autumn into spring.
