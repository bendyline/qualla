# Fountain Chapel

A small frame church on Jackson Avenue was the institutional centre of Black Vancouver for 67 years - and it outlived the neighbourhood that built it by more than a decade.

**Location:** 49.2770, -123.0939

**Categories:** History of Black people in Canada, African Methodist Episcopal churches, History of Vancouver, Churches in Vancouver, Black Canadian organizations

## Introduction

Somebody lives there now. The building at 823 Jackson Avenue in Vancouver's Strathcona is a private residence, unmarked and undesignated, one modest structure in a street of modest structures. For 67 years it was the Fountain Chapel - the African Methodist Episcopal congregation that served this city's Black community, that organised when the courts and the police needed watching, and that stood at the eastern edge of the neighbourhood everyone called Hogan's Alley. The neighbourhood is gone. The building is still there, doing something else entirely, which is a very Vancouver kind of survival.

## A Congregation That Bought Its Own Roof

Before 1918 there was no building, only rented halls around town and a congregation that moved between them. A small group decided that had gone on long enough, raised money locally, and persuaded the AME to match whatever they brought in. What the money bought was a church built in 1910 that had served German and Scandinavian Lutherans - one immigrant congregation handing its pews to another. The denomination they were joining had its own history of exactly this kind of decision. African Americans founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church in 1816 precisely because the churches that admitted them treated them badly, and from the antebellum decades onward the AME was an organising centre against slavery and against racism generally. Nora Hendrix, who had arrived in Vancouver from Knoxville, Tennessee in 1911, was among the chapel's founders.

## Park Lane

Hogan's Alley was never the street's real name. Park Lane ran through southwestern Strathcona between Union and Prior, from Main Street east to Jackson Avenue, and it acquired its nickname the way such places do. Black Californians had begun arriving in British Columbia in 1858, many settling first on Salt Spring Island before moving to Vancouver around 1900; Black Albertans whose families had come north from Oklahoma followed, and so did porters working the Great Northern Railway, who settled near the rail yards because that was where the work was. By 1914 roughly 300 people lived in and around the alley. Vie's Chicken and Steak House opened at 209 Union Street in 1948 and stayed open until 1980; Nora Hendrix worked there, and the late-night clientele over the years included Louis Armstrong and Nat King Cole. The neighbourhood also produced the dancer Leonard Gibson, the performer Thelma Gibson-Towns, and Barbara Howard, the sprinter who in 1938 became the first Black woman to represent Canada internationally.

## What a Church Is For

The AME's activist inheritance did not stay theoretical on Jackson Avenue. In 1922 a railway porter named Fred Deal was charged with the murder of Vancouver constable Robert McBeath, a Victoria Cross recipient, and sentenced to death. The Fountain Chapel congregation organised to make sure the trial accounted for the possibility that Deal had been racially targeted by the police. The case was retried and the sentence reduced to life imprisonment - an outcome that, in 1923, required a small congregation to insist on something the system had not thought to consider. Three decades later the chapel became the place where the community demanded an inquiry into the police beating of Clarence Clemons, a longshoreman who died shortly after the incident. A church is a building where people gather. What they gather to do is a separate question, and here the answer was frequently civic.

## Eight Lanes Through a Neighbourhood

In 1967 the Vancouver Transportation Study proposed an eight-lane freeway cut through this part of the city. It was stopped - the Strathcona Property Owners and Tenants Association, with organisers including Bessie Lee and Mary Lee Chan, fought the full demolition and won. But the viaducts that were to feed the freeway went ahead anyway, built between 1967 and 1971 and opened in 1972, and they took most of Hogan's Alley with them. The slum-clearance programs of the 1950s and 1960s had already scattered the community that had gathered here; the concrete finished the job. The chapel outlasted its own neighbourhood by more than a decade. In 1985, not long after Nora Hendrix died, the AME sold the building. The Basel Hakka Lutheran Church used it until 2008, when it was decommissioned as a church altogether.

## The Name on the Street Sign

Recognition arrived late and in pieces. On 30 January 2014, Canada Post issued a stamp commemorating Hogan's Alley; the official first-day cover was illustrated with the Fountain Chapel, which by then had not been a church for six years. In 2015 the city announced it would remove the Georgia Viaducts and build a 27,000-square-foot cultural centre devoted to Black Canadian history, working with a Hogan's Alley Working Group of 25 community members on long-term leases and a community land trust. Nora Hendrix Place, modular housing on Union Street for people without homes, opened in 2019. In 2021 the city approved Nora Hendrix Way - the first Vancouver street named for a Black woman. The chapel that she helped found still has no heritage designation at all.

## Flight Context

The former Fountain Chapel stands at 49.2770°N, 123.0939°W, on Jackson Avenue in Strathcona, immediately east of Vancouver's downtown core. Fly it at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL - this is a small residential building, so low and slow is the only way to pick it out, and the neighbourhood matters more than the structure. Navigate by the contrast: downtown's tower cluster and the white dome of BC Place sit to the west, and where the towers stop, Strathcona's grid of two-storey wooden houses begins. The twin Georgia Viaducts curve out of downtown toward this district and are the single clearest landmark - the ground beneath and around their eastern end is where Hogan's Alley ran. Chinatown lies a few blocks northwest, the port's container cranes line Burrard Inlet to the north, and False Creek cuts in to the southwest.
