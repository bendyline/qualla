# Point Atkinson Lighthouse

A ribbed concrete tower at the mouth of Burrard Inlet, guarded for a century by keepers with hand pumps, and surrounded by old-growth forest that survived because the light needed darkness behind it.

**Location:** 49.3304, -123.2646

**Categories:** Lighthouses, Maritime history, British Columbia, West Vancouver, National Historic Sites of Canada, Old-growth forest

## Introduction

West Vancouver still has thousand-year-old Douglas firs because a lighthouse needed something dark to stand against. In 1881 the Dominion of Canada set aside 182 acres of forest around Point Atkinson and left it uncut, partly to fuel the steam foghorn and partly for a reason that sounds almost poetic until you realize it is pure navigation: a white tower is invisible against a pale sky or a cleared slope, but against a wall of unbroken old growth it reads instantly. The trees were spared because they made the light easier to see. A century and a half later they are among the last of their kind anywhere near the city, and people walk among them without knowing they owe their existence to a piece of maritime bookkeeping.

## The Wrong Light

Arthur Finney of Nanaimo put up the first lighthouse here in 1874, a wooden tower with the keeper's house attached. It should have gone into service immediately. Instead it stood dark for months, because the lighting apparatus shipped from England turned out to be the wrong one, and nothing could be done until the right one crossed an ocean and a continent. The beacon was finally lit on 17 March 1875. The lamp burned 95 feet above the sea and could be picked up 14 miles out, marking the headland that Captain George Vancouver had named in 1792 while working his way through these waters aboard the Discovery, at precisely the point where a vessel must decide between Burrard Inlet and Howe Sound.

## Three Blasts and a Hand Pump

Fog is the real hazard on this coast, and the first arrangement for dealing with it was almost unbelievably manual. A captain groping through the murk would sound his own horn three times. The keeper, hearing it, would begin working a horn by hand, pumping steadily, and would keep pumping until the ship signalled that it had found its bearings and he could stop. One man, one set of lungs and arms, standing between a steamship and a rock. In 1889 Canadian Pacific Steamships lost patience and demanded a proper fog alarm, and a separate building went up west of the tower housing a steam-driven rotating drum. In 1902 that gave way to a diaphone, a slotted piston driving inside a slotted cylinder to produce the low two-tone groan that generations of coastal sailors would come to know.

## Concrete with Ribs

The wooden tower was replaced in 1914 by something entirely different in spirit: a reinforced concrete structure designed by William Anderson, whose towers went up all along the Canadian coasts in those years. It is an octagonal truncated shaft braced by six flying ribs that flare out at the base, a form that looks less like a traditional lighthouse than like an early experiment in engineered concrete, which is exactly what it was. The tower rises 18 metres, and because it stands on the rock of the headland the light itself sits 33 metres above the water. On 18 May 1974 it was designated a National Historic Site of Canada.

## The Names on the Roster

For 121 years this was a job somebody lived at. Edward Woodward took it first in 1874, followed by Robert G. Wellwood, and then by Walter Erwin, who stayed for thirty years, from 1880 to 1910. Thomas David Grafton held it for the next twenty-three, and a Grafton followed him. Ernest Charles Dawe served from 1935 to 1961, through a war during which this light marked the approach to a working port. Gordon Odlum, James Barr, Oscar Edwards, and finally Gerald D. Watson and Donald Graham, who were there at the end. In 1996 the light was automated and the keepers went home. The rock still has a lit tower on it; it no longer has anybody watching the weather from a window above the water.

## Walking Out to It

The forest the Dominion reserved has outlived its original purpose and become the point of the place. Now 75 hectares of West Vancouver parkland, it holds a stand of Douglas fir that the trail system calls the Valley of the Giants, trees hundreds of years old on ground that has never been logged, a scrap of what the entire inlet looked like before the mills. Trails drop through it from the parking area to the headland, where the granite runs out into the strait and the concrete tower sits at the edge of it. Come on a grey afternoon with the cloud down on Bowen Island and you get some sense of the old job: the water invisible, the horn sounding, and somewhere out there a ship listening for it.

## Flight Context

Point Atkinson Lighthouse stands at 49.3304 N, 123.2646 W on the headland marking the northern side of the entrance to Burrard Inlet, in West Vancouver, British Columbia. Best viewed from 1,500 to 3,000 feet. It is one of the easiest landmarks on this coast to find: the dark wedge of old-growth forest at the tip of the peninsula, with a white ribbed tower at its seaward point, sits where English Bay, Howe Sound and the Strait of Georgia meet. The Lions Gate Bridge lies about 4 nm east up the inlet, Bowen Island about 3 nm west across Queen Charlotte Channel, and the Point Grey cliffs and UBC campus face it across English Bay to the south. Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) is roughly 7 nm east, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 10 nm south-southeast, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) some 22 nm east, and Nanaimo (CYCD) roughly 29 nm west across the strait. This is controlled airspace with heavy floatplane traffic transiting to Howe Sound and the Sunshine Coast. Fog and low stratus are common here in any season, which is rather the point of the place; clear winter days give the sharpest contrast between the forest and the water.
