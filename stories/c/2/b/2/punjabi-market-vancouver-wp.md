# Punjabi Market

Six blocks of Main Street that a single sari shop started in 1970, built by a community Canada had once turned away at the harbour mouth.

**Location:** 49.2257, -123.1017

**Categories:** neighbourhoods, cultural-history, immigration, british-columbia, pacific-northwest

## Introduction

On 31 May 1970, Sucha Singh and Harbans Kaur Claire unlocked the door of a shop on Main Street and began selling saris and drapery. Shan Sharees and Drapery was the only business of its kind on the block. Within a few years dozens more had opened around it, filling the stretch between 48th and 50th Avenues with fabric, gold, spices and sweets, and Vancouver had a Punjabi Market - six blocks that the city would eventually recognise formally as a cultural and business district. It is worth knowing what came before that door opened, because the distance between 1914 and 1970 in this city is not measured in years.

## Turned Away at the Harbour Mouth

Punjabis had been arriving in Vancouver since the 1890s, working the lumber mills and construction sites of the Lower Mainland. The Khalsa Diwan Society formed on 22 July 1906, and on 19 January 1908 opened a gurdwara at 1866 West 2nd Avenue - the community's first anchor in the city. Six years later the Komagata Maru anchored in Burrard Inlet carrying 376 passengers, most of them Sikhs, all of them British subjects seeking entry to a country that had written rules specifically to keep them out. Canada allowed 24 ashore. The rest sat aboard the ship for two months while the community on land fought for them in the courts and fed them by launch, and on 23 July 1914 the vessel was forced back to sea. On its return to India, troops opened fire at Budge Budge and twenty passengers were killed. Canada apologised in 2008, and again formally in the House of Commons in 2016; Vancouver added its own apology in 2021.

## Hundreds of Vancouver Specials

Immigration policy loosened after the Second World War, and from the 1950s through the 1970s successive waves of South Asian - overwhelmingly Punjabi Sikh - families settled into the Sunset neighbourhood of South Vancouver. They arrived exactly as the area was finishing its last great build-out, and the house they bought or built was usually a Vancouver Special: boxy, low-pitched, double-fronted, cheap to construct and generous enough inside for three generations under one roof. Architecture critics loathed them. Families that needed room for grandparents, children and a rented suite found them close to perfect. Hundreds went up across South Vancouver, and the neighbourhood around Main and 49th became predominantly Indo-Canadian.

## The Spring of 1970

Two things happened that season, a few weeks and a few blocks apart. In April the Khalsa Diwan Society opened the Ross Street Gurdwara, designed by Arthur Erickson after he travelled to Agra and Amritsar - a concrete building meant to read as a lotus rising from water, and unlike any other gurdwara in the world. On 31 May the Claires opened their shop. A place of worship and a place of commerce, both new, both permanent. By 1993 the market's standing was such that the city installed Vancouver's first bilingual street signs, Punjabi and English, at Main and 49th. The jewellers came in numbers - the strip is said to hold the highest concentration of them anywhere in Canada - and families across the province came here to outfit weddings.

## Three Hundred Thousand in the Street

Since 1979 the Khalsa Diwan Society has held a public Vaisakhi celebration each April, marking the founding of the Khalsa. It has grown into the largest single-day parade in Vancouver, drawing crowds that have reached 300,000 - so large that in 2013 the city moved it into its Civic Parades category, reserved for events over 100,000. What makes it remarkable is not the size but the practice: restaurants and ordinary households along the route set out food and give it away, to anyone, all day, without payment or expectation. Sikh hospitality has a name and a theology behind it, and for one day a year the whole of Vancouver is invited to eat.

## The Long Leaving

The market is now in a long transition. Beginning in the 1980s, South Asian families started leaving South Vancouver for Surrey and Delta, where the same money bought far more land, and newer arrivals in the 1990s and 2000s largely skipped Vancouver altogether. Retail vacancies grew. The mix shifted - a licensed cannabis shop here, a Tim Hortons there, chain tenants in newly built commercial space in the middle of a district that small businesses made. The City of Vancouver began a planning study in 2016, held six community engagement events in October 2019, and brought forward eight recommended actions covering cultural grants, public realm improvements, business support and a long-term stewardship partnership. The market turned fifty on 31 May 2020 and had to celebrate it online, the pandemic having taken the street away in the one year it most deserved a crowd.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.226N, 123.102W, on Main Street around 49th Avenue in the Sunset neighbourhood of South Vancouver, roughly 30-40 m above sea level on the gentle slope down to the Fraser. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL. From the air the district is a short commercial strip on a long north-south arterial, set in the dense grid of flat-roofed Vancouver Specials that fills South Vancouver; the Fraser River's north arm lies about 2 km south, downtown's towers about 7 km north. The most useful nearby landmark is the Ross Street Gurdwara, a distinctive terraced concrete structure near Marine Drive about 1.5 km east-southeast, close to the Knight Street Bridge. Nearest airports: Vancouver International (CYVR) about 4 nm southwest - expect to be under the arrival and departure flow - Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CXH) about 4 nm north, and Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 10 nm south-southeast. Low cloud and rain dominate from November through March; summer afternoons are typically clear with good visibility to the North Shore mountains.
