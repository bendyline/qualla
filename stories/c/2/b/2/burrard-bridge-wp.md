# Burrard Bridge

An Art Deco crossing of False Creek whose railings were engineered as a moving-picture shutter, and which took Vancouver two decades of political brawling to share with bicycles.

**Location:** 49.2755, -123.1370

**Categories:** Bridges, Art Deco architecture, Vancouver, British Columbia, Engineering, Urban history

## Introduction

Look at the concrete railings on the Burrard Bridge and you are looking at a piece of optical engineering disguised as decoration. The gaps between the balusters were calculated so that a driver moving at fifty kilometres an hour would perceive not a picket fence but an uninterrupted view of the harbour, the eye stitching the flickering slots together the way it stitches together frames of film. The effect holds anywhere between roughly forty and sixty-four kilometres an hour. Outside that range, the illusion breaks and the railing becomes a railing again. Someone in 1930 sat down and worked out the arithmetic of that, for a road deck, in a city of a quarter million people.

## Concrete Towers Hiding a Truss

The bridge went up between 1930 and 1932 and opened on Dominion Day, July 1, 1932: 836 metres of steel and concrete on four piers, carrying Burrard Street from downtown Vancouver across False Creek into Kitsilano. Its structure is a quiet trick. The two approach spans are Warren trusses slung below the deck, out of sight; the central span, 89.6 metres long, is a Pratt truss riding above the deck to give ships the clearance they need. Rather than let drivers see that steelwork, the architect George Lister Thornton Sharp and the engineer John R. Grant extended the masonry piers upward into monumental concrete towers, linked them with overhead galleries, and dressed the whole assembly as a pair of torch-like pylons. Busts of two Georgian-era naval men project from ship prows on the superstructure: Captain George Vancouver, with a V cut beneath him, and Sir Harry Burrard-Neale, with a B beneath his.

## The Sidewalk Problem

Nobody designing a bridge in 1930 was thinking about bicycle commuting. Cyclists originally rode in traffic with everybody else, and as volumes climbed and the posted speed on the deck rose to sixty kilometres an hour, the city moved them onto the sidewalks instead. Those sidewalks are 2.6 metres wide. Packing pedestrians and cyclists into that strip produced exactly what you would expect: collisions, injuries, and eventually a successful lawsuit against the city. From the mid-1990s onward, Vancouver studied every conceivable fix. Take a vehicle lane. Bolt outrigger sidewalks onto the outside. Hang a second deck underneath. Build an entirely separate crossing for people not in cars. In March 1996 the city actually tried the first option, closing one commuter lane for what was meant to be a six-month trial. Motorist outrage collapsed it inside a week.

## Two Elections and Sixty-One Million Dollars

The fight then became municipal politics in its purest form. On May 31, 2005, council received a detailed engineering report, voted 10-1 to ignore its recommendations, and instead reallocated the two curb lanes to cyclists as part of a push to raise cycling ahead of the 2010 Winter Olympics. The issue promptly dominated the November 19 municipal election. On December 20 the newly elected council reversed course 6-4, cancelling the trial in favour of widening the sidewalks. Widening, however, kept getting more expensive: over forty million dollars in 2006, fifty-seven million by April 2008, with sixty-one million floated as the realistic number. Heritage advocates fought the outriggers as vandalism, and Heritage Vancouver put the bridge at the top of its endangered list for three consecutive years. Then in November 2008 that council was voted out too, replaced by one opposed to widening and in favour of lane reallocation. The argument had gone a full circle, taking two elections to do it.

## Busiest in North America

The decisive trial began on July 13, 2009. One southbound vehicle lane went to cyclists behind a physical barrier, the northbound sidewalk went to cyclists, the southbound sidewalk to pedestrians. Predictions of gridlock did not materialize. Two weeks in, city data showed daily bicycle trips up about thirty percent, pedestrian numbers essentially flat, a slight dip in motor vehicle trips, and no measurable change in driving times along Burrard between 12th Avenue and Georgia Street. Some nearby merchants reported sharp drops in sales that summer and said so loudly. The lanes stayed. The bridge that once carried six lanes of cars now carries four, and by 2019 the bike lane on it was the busiest in North America; the downtown business association that had opposed the conversion came around, on the straightforward grounds that a lot of its customers and staff arrive by bicycle.

## The Ground Underneath

One reason the widening scheme stalled had nothing to do with money or heritage. The land directly beneath the bridge's south end belongs to the Squamish Nation, whose consent any construction there would require, and who raised concerns about heavy machinery working on their property. In December 2009 the Nation put up an electronic billboard on that land, two screens each nine metres by three, one facing each direction of traffic, cycling a new advertisement every ten seconds. Neighbours objected that it blocked mountain views and distracted drivers. The billboards had federal approval, and municipal and regional governments acknowledged they had no authority over what happens on reserve land. The Nation's stated purpose was revenue, which is a rather ordinary reason to put up a sign. Meanwhile the structure itself keeps demanding attention: in 2012 council committed fourteen million dollars over two years to stripping PCB contamination from the Burrard and Granville bridges. Heritage consultants rank it in the top tier of Vancouver's historic structures, and in 2011 Canada Post put it on a stamp, one of five Art Deco landmarks chosen to represent the country.

## Flight Context

The Burrard Bridge crosses False Creek at 49.2755 N, 123.1370 W, linking downtown Vancouver to Kitsilano. Best appreciated low and slow, from 1,000 to 2,500 feet, where the Art Deco pylons and the raised central truss separate it clearly from its neighbours. Orientation is easy: Burrard is the westernmost of the three False Creek crossings, with the Granville Street Bridge only 0.5 km southeast and the Cambie Bridge about 2 km east; Vanier Park, the Vancouver Maritime Museum and Kitsilano Beach sit off the south end, Sunset Beach and the West End off the north. Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC), the downtown seaplane base, is barely 2 nm northeast on Burrard Inlet; Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 6 nm south-southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 12 nm southeast and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) roughly 16 nm east. This is dense controlled airspace under CYVR arrivals with constant floatplane and helicopter traffic over the inlet, so it demands clearance and vigilance. Morning fog off the Strait of Georgia is common in winter; clearest light is late afternoon, with the North Shore mountains behind the city.
