# 2010 Winter Olympics

Vancouver had to truck in its own winter, lost a young athlete before the flame was lit, and still ran one of the most warmly remembered Games in Olympic history.

**Location:** 49.2767, -123.1119

**Categories:** 2010 Winter Olympics, Olympic Games in Canada, Sports competitions in Vancouver, Winter Olympics by year, Sports history

## Introduction

They trucked the winter in. Cypress Mountain, the venue assigned every snowboard event and the freestyle skiing, rises out of a rainforest city barely above sea level, and the El Nino winter of 2009-10 left its runs green and soft. So organizers loaded snow onto trucks at Manning Park, about 250 kilometres east, and hauled it over the mountains to West Vancouver. It worked. Every event ran. That improvisation set the tone for seventeen days in which Greater Vancouver - the most populous metropolitan area ever to host a Winter Olympics - kept solving problems in public and somehow kept looking delighted about it.

## A Vote in Prague

Getting there took a decade and two separate elections. The first was domestic: in November 1998 the Canadian Olympic Association had to choose among Vancouver-Whistler, a Calgary that wanted to host again, and a Quebec City still smarting from losing the 2002 bid. The first ballot was agonizingly tight - Vancouver 26, Quebec City 25, Calgary 21 - and in the December run-off Vancouver took it 40 to 32. The second election came on 2 July 2003 at the 115th IOC Session in Prague, where Vancouver faced Pyeongchang and Salzburg. Pyeongchang actually led the opening round. Salzburg dropped out, and in the run-off all but two of Salzburg's supporters swung behind Vancouver, making it the narrowest IOC host vote since Sydney edged Beijing for the 2000 Summer Games. Vancouver had tried twice before, for 1976 and 1980, and been turned down both times.

## The Day the Flame Arrived

On 12 February 2010, hours before the opening ceremony, Nodar Kumaritashvili went out for a training run at the Whistler Sliding Centre. He was twenty-one, Georgian, from a family with generations in the sport, and he lost control in the last turn of the fastest track in the world. He was thrown from the sled and died. The International Luge Federation attributed the crash to driving errors; the coroner found an interaction of factors, among them the track's speed, its technical difficulty and the athlete's limited time on it. Officials raised the wall at the exit of curve sixteen, reprofiled the ice, padded exposed steel and moved the men's start down to the women's, cutting the speeds. That evening the Games opened - indoors, a first for any Olympics, Winter or Summer - with a moment of silence and a tribute to him. Georgia's president publicly thanked the hosts for how they handled it. The Georgian team marched.

## Fourteen

Canada took its first gold on the second day of competition and did not top the gold table until the second-to-last, which meant two weeks of a country checking standings over breakfast. When it finished, Canada had fourteen gold medals - a Winter Olympic record that broke the thirteen shared by the Soviet Union in 1976 and Norway in 2002, and stood until Norway won sixteen at Beijing in 2022. No host nation had led the gold count since Norway in 1952. The United States, meanwhile, won thirty-seven medals overall, more than any country had taken from a single Winter Games to that point. Slovakia and Belarus won their first Winter Olympic golds. Some 2,626 athletes from 82 national committees contested 86 events across fifteen disciplines, with the Cayman Islands, Colombia, Ghana, Montenegro, Pakistan, Peru and Serbia making winter debuts. More than 97 percent of the 1.54 million tickets sold, and for the first time Olympic hockey was played on an NHL-sized rink rather than the wider international sheet.

## Who Objected, and Why

Not everyone wanted the Games, and the objections were more specific than general grumbling. The Squamish, Musqueam, Lil'wat and Tsleil-Waututh nations, on whose traditional territories the events were held, signed a protocol in 2004 as the Four Host First Nations, but other Indigenous voices dissented - a St'at'imc group at Sutikalh feared the tourism and real estate pressure the Games would bring to their territory. Inuit leaders objected to the choice of an inukshuk as the emblem, noting that a figure with a head, arms and legs is properly an inunnguaq, an imitation of a person, and not the stone marker the design claimed to be; Coast Salish people asked, reasonably, why a symbol from the Arctic stood in for the culture of the host coast at all. Promised low-income housing at the athletes' village was scaled back. And the security bill, budgeted at C$200 million, came in near C$1 billion.

## What Vancouver Kept

The unglamorous decision that has aged best was made years before the torch arrived: build modest venues and design every one of them for the day after. The Richmond Olympic Oval, a speed-skating rink improbably located at sea level, became an indoor multi-sport centre. The Hillcrest Centre, built for curling, is now a community facility with an ice rink and a pool. Whistler Olympic Park still serves recreational skiers, athletes in training and competition. GM Place hosted the hockey under the temporary name Canada Hockey Place, since Olympic venues cannot carry corporate branding, and reverted afterwards to commercial life as Rogers Arena. Vancouver also hosted the first Pride House in Olympic history, a welcome space for LGBT athletes that has since been replicated at Games around the world. Jacques Rogge called the way the city embraced the Games extraordinary. Sixteen years on, the crowds in the downtown streets are still the thing people remember first.

## Flight Context

The coordinates 49.2767 N, 123.1119 W put you over BC Place Stadium on the north shore of False Creek in downtown Vancouver - the opening and closing ceremony venue, unmistakable from the air for its white cable-supported retractable roof, with Rogers Arena (the Games' Canada Hockey Place) immediately to the east and the geodesic dome of Science World at the head of the inlet. Best viewing is 2,000 to 4,000 feet, which holds the whole Olympic geography in one frame: the downtown peninsula and Stanley Park below, Cypress Mountain on the North Shore ridge to the northwest above West Vancouver, the Richmond Olympic Oval on the Fraser River to the south, and the Sea-to-Sky corridor running north toward Whistler, about 60 nm up the coast past Howe Sound. Downtown Vancouver sits inside busy controlled airspace: Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 8 nm southwest, and Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) operates floatplanes directly off the Coal Harbour waterfront with heavy traffic, so coordination is essential. Other options are Boundary Bay (CZBB) 15 nm south, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) 15 nm east and Abbotsford (CYXX) 35 nm east. February brings the conditions that defined the Games themselves - low cloud, rain at sea level, and a freezing level that often sits above the North Shore peaks.
