# X̱wáýx̱way

The road around Stanley Park was surfaced with the midden of the village it replaced — X̱wáýx̱way, a Coast Salish settlement occupied for more than three thousand years.

**Location:** 49.3026, -123.1310

**Categories:** Former populated places in British Columbia, History of Vancouver, Squamish villages, Stanley Park, Indigenous history

## Introduction

The road was built out of the village. When surveyors laid out the perimeter drive around Stanley Park in the 1880s, they needed fill and surfacing material, and the nearest available source was the midden at X̱wáýx̱way — the deep shell layer, metres thick in places, left by more than three thousand years of people living on that shore. It was crushed and spread. Visitors driving the loop today, past Lumberman's Arch and out toward Brockton Point, are travelling on the compacted remains of a settlement, and of the ancestors buried within it. Very few of them know.

## Three Thousand Years on the Inlet

X̱wáýx̱way stood on the eastern peninsula of what is now Stanley Park, on the shore where Lumberman's Arch sits today, looking out across Burrard Inlet. It was one of many villages ringing that inlet, part of a landscape whose resources — salmon, shellfish, cedar, waterfowl — were used by Squamish, Musqueam and Tsleil-Waututh peoples. Archaeological estimates put occupation at more than three millennia. That is a span worth pausing over: the site was already ancient when the Roman Empire existed, and people were still living there when the first steamships appeared in the inlet. By the late nineteenth century it was the largest settlement anywhere on the peninsula, a working village of substantial houses rather than a seasonal camp.

## The House of Eleven Families

The centrepiece was a longhouse of extraordinary scale — roughly 60 metres long and close to 20 metres wide, about 197 by 66 feet, framed in massive cedar posts and clad in cedar slabs. Eleven families lived inside it, something on the order of a hundred people sharing a single structure and a single roof, each family with its own hearth and its own section. In 1875 that house hosted a large potlatch, the ceremonial feast through which wealthy Coast Salish families distributed goods, confirmed rank and marked important events. It is an odd accident of the record that we know about the potlatch partly because it appears in Vancouver city council minutes, where the medical health officer cited a smallpox outbreak and recommended that the buildings be destroyed.

## A Name That Means a Dance

The name is not a description of geography. X̱wáýx̱way — rendered in the Squamish language and in Halkomelem, and written by English speakers as Xway xway or Whoi Whoi — refers to a masked dance ceremony. The best translation is something close to "masked dance performance." A place named for what happened there rather than for what it looked like tells you where the emphasis fell. This was a site of ceremony, and the masks and the dance belonged to specific families who held the right to perform them. When the village went, the name stayed attached to the ground, waiting.

## Removal

The peninsula was surveyed by the Royal Engineers and set aside as a military reserve while British Columbia was still a colony — a designation that treated the land as empty. The Indian Act of 1876 supplied the machinery, and the transcontinental railway's arrival in 1886 supplied the pressure, turning a small settlement on the inlet into a boom city. Residents of X̱wáýx̱way and of nearby Sen̓áḵw were classified as an obstacle to that growth and moved to reserves. The village was demolished in the 1880s for the perimeter road. The anthropologist Charles Hill-Tout, excavating nearby, recorded skeletons in the ground. In Sḵwx̱wú7mesh practice the dead were placed in carefully made bentwood boxes and raised high into the trees; people of high standing were sometimes laid in cedar dugout canoes. None of that was designed to be dug through by a road crew.

## For All Time

Lord Stanley dedicated the park on 27 September 1888, in words that have been quoted ever since: to the use and enjoyment of peoples of all colours, creeds and customs, for all time. The village that had stood there for three thousand years was being cleared as he said it. Some Indigenous families remained on the peninsula well into the twentieth century — most were evicted by the park board in 1931, and a few held on until 1958. Today the park covers 405 hectares and its seawall, begun in 1917, runs 22 kilometres, the longest uninterrupted waterfront walkway in the world. In 2010 the chief of the Squamish Nation proposed that the whole park be renamed Xwayxway Park. The proposal went nowhere. The name is still there in the ground, under the asphalt.

## Flight Context

The X̱wáýx̱way site lies at 49.3026° N, 123.1305° W, on the inner shore of Stanley Park's eastern peninsula facing Burrard Inlet, immediately inland of Lumberman's Arch and about 1 km west of Brockton Point lighthouse. This is some of the busiest low-level airspace in Canada — Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC), a water aerodrome, sits barely 2 km east across Coal Harbour, with floatplane traffic operating at very low altitude directly past the park, and the whole area lies under Vancouver's controlled terminal airspace. Do not freelance here: get the VFR terminal procedures and stay on frequency. Realistic sightseeing altitude in the published harbour routes runs low, 1,000 to 2,000 feet, which is exactly where the view is best. From the air the peninsula reads as a dark green wedge against downtown's towers, with the Lions Gate Bridge spanning First Narrows off its northwest corner, the seawall tracing the entire shoreline, and Deadman's Island tucked into Coal Harbour to the southeast. Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 9 nm south, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 16 nm east, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 15 nm southeast. Winter brings low ceilings and rain off the Strait of Georgia; clear summer days give unobstructed views north to the Coast Mountains.
