# RCAF Station Jericho Beach

Canada's first West Coast air station flew fire patrols and fisheries work off a Vancouver beach for half a century; today the hangars are gone and the sailboats have the water.

**Location:** 49.2716, -123.2000

**Categories:** Royal Canadian Air Force stations, Aviation history, Military installations closed in 1996, History of Vancouver, Seaplane bases

## Introduction

On September 24, 1920, a Curtiss HS-2L flying boat lifted off the water at Jericho Beach in Vancouver. It had been built for a war that was over, shipped across the continent by rail in pieces from the air station at Dartmouth, Nova Scotia, and bolted back together on a shoreline that had been logged, made into a golf course, and largely forgotten. Nothing about the scene suggested permanence. Yet that flight opened a military and civil aviation presence on this beach that would last three quarters of a century, and for its first decade the work had almost nothing to do with fighting.

## Jerry's Cove

The ground came with two possible names and a long prior occupancy. One account traces Jericho to the lumberman Jeremiah Rogers, whose operation here was shortened in local speech to Jerry's Cove, or Jerry and Company. Another holds that the name belongs to Jericho Charlie, Shenáwtsut, who kept a cedar longhouse on this shore. The Crown declared the land a naval reserve in 1859 and then let it go quiet; loggers took the timber, and from 1889 a golf course occupied the clearing. When the Canadian Air Board went looking in early 1920 for a West Coast base, it already owned the property. Authorization came on February 14, construction began in June, and by autumn there was an air station where the fairways had been, one of five the Air Board's Flying Operations Branch stood up in a single summer.

## Fire, Fish and Smugglers

In 1921, only High River in Alberta logged more flying hours than Vancouver. The station operated several HS-2L flying boats and a single Felixstowe F.3 on jobs that read like a catalogue of everything a young country needed done from the air: forest fire patrol, forestry survey, anti-smuggling patrol, fisheries patrol, and simple transportation into places with no roads. A permanent hangar and fuel tank went up in 1922. That same year brought the station's hardest loss. Major Clarence MacLaurin had helped choose this site and had run it since the day it opened, and on September 11, 1922, he was killed in the crash of an HS-2L. Fisheries work dominated the following seasons, with three aircraft detached to Casey Cove near Prince Rupert through the summer and one to Bamfield in the fall, until budget cuts curtailed the patrols in 1926.

## Five Names in Five Years

Institutional identity took longer to settle than the aircraft did. The station became CAF Unit Vancouver in November 1922, RCAF Unit Vancouver in March 1923, RCAF Station Vancouver that October, No. 1 (Operations) Squadron in July 1925, and RCAF Station Vancouver again on July 1, 1927. The machines changed just as fast. Avro 504N floatplanes replaced the tired HS-2Ls for seaplane training by 1928. No. 1 Photographic Detachment worked out of here in 1930 and 1931, mapping the British Columbia coast with Fairchild FC-2 floatplanes. By 1931 the line included de Havilland Gipsy Moths, Canadian Vickers Vedettes and Vancouvers, a Consolidated Courier and a Vickers Vista. In the spring of 1934 the unit was reorganized as No. 4 (Flying Boat) Squadron and began training in Royal Air Force coastal reconnaissance tactics, which was the first real hint of what was coming.

## Handed Over, Handed Down

War moved the flying elsewhere. By 1940 the seaplane squadrons had shifted to RCAF Station Sea Island, and Jericho took on the unglamorous half of the job: No. 3 Operational Training Unit teaching aircrew to handle flying boats, and No. 3 Repair Depot keeping the machines alive, both running until 1945. The army arrived in 1942, when Pacific Command Headquarters moved in, and took the station over outright in 1947 with only a small air force presence left behind. In 1969 most of the property passed to the City of Vancouver and became Jericho Park. The remaining detachment closed in 1996. Nearly all the buildings came down, the flying boat hangars among them. What holds the beach now is the Jericho Sailing Centre, a pond, picnickers, and an entrenched population of feral rabbits.

## Flight Context

The former station occupies the shoreline at roughly 49.272 N, 123.200 W, on the south side of English Bay in west Vancouver proper, between Kitsilano and the Point Grey headland. Approach along the bay at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL and the site is easy: a broad crescent of pale sand backed by open green parkland, conspicuously unbuilt in a city that has built on everything else, with the Jericho Sailing Centre and a scatter of small craft at the western end. Spanish Banks stretches west toward the wooded bluff of the University of British Columbia; Kitsilano's dense blocks begin immediately east. English Bay in front of the beach is usually dotted with anchored freighters waiting for berths, which makes a distinctive and reliable navigation cue. The Lions Gate Bridge and the entrance to Burrard Inlet lie to the northeast, with the North Shore Mountains behind them. This is congested airspace beneath the Vancouver terminal area and close to active floatplane traffic, so a clearance and current charts are essential. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is about 4 nm east, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 6 nm south-southeast, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 20 nm east, and Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 16 nm southeast. Sea fog and low stratus are common in early summer mornings; winter brings long spells of rain and ceilings below a thousand feet.
