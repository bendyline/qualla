# 1918 Vancouver General Strike

British Columbia's first general strike lasted twenty-four hours, began at the funeral of a shot coal miner, and ended with veterans storming a union hall on Dunsmuir Street.

**Location:** 49.2608, -123.1139

**Categories:** 1918 in British Columbia, General strikes in Canada, History of Vancouver, Labour disputes in British Columbia, Labour history

## Introduction

At noon on 2 August 1918, while thousands filled the streets of Cumberland on Vancouver Island to bury a red-haired coal miner, the workers of Vancouver put down their tools. Shipyards fell quiet. Longshoremen left the docks. Garment workers and electricians walked out with them — around 5,600 people in all, roughly a third of the city's unionized workforce. It lasted twenty-four hours, and it was the first general strike in the history of British Columbia. One thing had triggered it: six days earlier a labour organizer named Albert Goodwin had been shot dead in the hills above Comox Lake, and the labour movement of this province did not accept the account it was given. By the following evening, a union secretary would be halfway out a third-floor window.

## The Red-Haired Man from Cumberland

Albert Goodwin was born in Britain in 1887 and came to Canada to work coal. The nickname everybody used — Ginger — came from the colour of his hair. He rose quickly through the labour movement: vice-president of the British Columbia Federation of Labour, president of District 6 of the International Union of Mine, Mill and Smelter Workers, and secretary of the union's Trail chapter, where on 10 November 1917 he led a strike for the eight-hour day. Not long after, a draft board that had exempted him for poor health re-examined him and declared him fit to serve. His appeals failed. Goodwin, a conscientious objector, took to the bush above Comox Lake with other men avoiding conscription. On 27 July 1918 a Dominion Police constable named Dan Campbell found him there and shot him through the neck, severing his spinal cord. Campbell said Goodwin had raised his rifle. He was charged with manslaughter; a grand jury in Victoria threw the charge out, over the objections of police officers and two justices who thought the evidence warranted a trial.

## A Province Already at the Boil

The anger had been accumulating for years. An economic depression in 1912 threw people out of work, thinned union rolls and cut the wages of those still employed. Wartime inflation then ate into real incomes, sharply so in 1917, even as conscription drained the shipyards of the men they needed. Meanwhile Ottawa banned radical organizations, censored their newspapers, and outlawed strikes and lockouts outright — measures that suppressed the movement without doing anything about its grievances. Events abroad pushed the other way: the Bolshevik Revolution convinced many on the left that a socialist transformation was genuinely achievable in Canada too. Conscription was the flashpoint. The Military Service Act of August 1917 made every man between twenty and forty-five liable to serve, and the opposition that followed drew on pacifist traditions running through the Quakers, Mennonites, Hutterites and Doukhobors. Riots broke out in Quebec City. In British Columbia, the Federation of Labour issued a manifesto demanding not only repeal of the Act but the end of the capitalist system.

## Noon, and the City Stops

The Metal Trades Council and the Vancouver Trades and Labour Council called the walkout for noon on 2 August — the hour of Goodwin's funeral, deliberately chosen. A general strike had never been attempted in the province before, and nobody involved knew how much of the city would actually follow. Roughly 5,600 workers did. They were shipbuilders and longshoremen, garment workers and electrical workers, and their absence was enough to stop the waterfront. The gesture was symbolic rather than strategic; the demands, such as they were, concerned a dead man and a conscription law rather than wages or hours. Critics were immediate and loud, accusing the strikers of being Bolsheviks, or German sympathizers, or both. In a city that had spent four years sending its sons overseas, the charge landed hard.

## 411 Dunsmuir Street

The Great War Veterans Association had already threatened violence through its secretary, A. E. Lees, and that evening it made good. Three hundred men marched on the Labour Temple at 411 Dunsmuir Street and ransacked the VTLC offices. They seized the council's secretary, Victor Midgley, and tried to push him out of a window; a woman working in the office threw herself into the struggle to stop them and was badly bruised for it. Midgley and a longshoreman were then forced to kiss the Union Jack. Helena Gutteridge, the suffragette and VTLC member, was present through all of it. The next morning the veterans went after the Longshoremen's Hall, demanding that union leaders leave the province until the war ended; the longshoremen broke up chairs and fought back with the legs. Mayor Robert Henry Otley Gale brokered a mediation committee, and the men went back to work. The building still stands, now the 411 Seniors Centre.

## One Hundred and Seventeen to One

In the recriminations that followed, the strike's leaders were accused of having acted on their own, dragging an unwilling membership into a fight it had not chosen. Their answer was a number: the VTLC delegates had voted for the strike 117 to 1. To settle the question, Midgley and the others resigned their positions and stood again. They were re-elected almost unanimously. What happened in Vancouver that August turned out to be an opening move rather than a conclusion. The Canadian Labour Revolt built through the following months and crested in May 1919 with the Winnipeg General Strike, and Vancouver walked out again that year in sympathy with Winnipeg — a strike that remains the longest general strike in Canadian history. Goodwin lies in the Cumberland Municipal Cemetery, where thousands came the day the city stopped.

## Flight Context

The strike centred on downtown Vancouver, around 49.2608°N, 123.1139°W, with the Labour Temple at 411 Dunsmuir Street — today the 411 Seniors Centre — a few blocks inland from the Burrard Inlet waterfront where the struck docks lay. Fly it low and slow, 1,500 to 2,500 feet, to make sense of the geography: Coal Harbour and the working piers to the north, False Creek to the south, Stanley Park closing the peninsula to the west, and the North Shore mountains rising immediately across the inlet.
