# Hogan's Alley, Vancouver

For six decades an alley off Union Street held Vancouver's only concentrated Black neighbourhood, until a freeway that was never finished took it down anyway.

**Location:** 49.2772, -123.0973

**Categories:** History of Black people in British Columbia, Former neighbourhoods in Canada, Neighbourhoods in Vancouver, History of Vancouver, Urban renewal

## Introduction

At 209 Union Street, Vie's Chicken and Steak House served fried chicken until three in the morning to whoever turned up, and the people who turned up included Louis Armstrong and Nat King Cole. In the kitchen at various points worked a woman from Knoxville, Tennessee named Nora Hendrix, whose grandson Jimi would come up from Seattle to visit her. The restaurant sat at the edge of an alley officially called Park Lane and known to everyone as Hogan's Alley, running roughly between Main Street and Jackson Avenue. It has been described as the first and last neighbourhood in Vancouver with a substantial concentrated Black population. Almost none of it survives.

## The Letter From Victoria

The community's roots reach back to 1858 and a meeting at San Francisco's Zion Church. A sailor named Jeremiah Nagle arrived from Vancouver Island carrying news of gold and a letter from James Douglas inviting Black Californians to British Columbia, and a great many took the offer, leaving a city whose racial climate was turning against them. Money was short, so many settled on Salt Spring Island. Around the turn of the century their children and grandchildren began moving to Vancouver for the work. Others came from Alberta, families who had originally left Oklahoma, and still others were sleeping-car porters of the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters who settled in Strathcona because the Great Northern Railway ended nearby. By 1914 the neighbourhood numbered roughly three hundred people. The nickname came from Richard F. Outcault's comic strip The Yellow Kid, whose Hogan's Alley was a crowded, polyglot tenement block. Vancouver's version was genuinely mixed: Black, Italian, Chinese, Japanese, Jewish and Indigenous families on the same short street.

## Chapel, Chicken, and the Harlem Nocturne

The African Methodist Episcopal Fountain Chapel opened at 823 Jackson Avenue sometime between 1918 and 1923, the only Black church in the city, and Nora Hendrix was among its founders. Around it grew everything a neighbourhood needs and a few things a city would rather not notice: eateries, clubhouses, unlicensed bars and gambling rooms, tolerated through the 1920s and 1930s under a mayor who preferred his police chasing serious crime. Mother Alexander ran a tamale and chilli parlour on Union Street. Viva Moore, born on Salt Spring Island into a family that had come north in 1858, opened Vie's with her husband Robert in 1948. Just outside the alley at 343 East Hastings, the trombonist Ernie King ran the Harlem Nocturne, the only Black-owned nightclub in Vancouver. The day cook Dorothy Nealy remembered that practically every Black woman in Vancouver had worked in one of these kitchens at some point.

## The Word Was Slum

In 1950 the social scientist Leonard Marsh published Rebuilding a Neighbourhood, which described Strathcona as a slum requiring renewal. A 1957 redevelopment study repeated the diagnosis for the whole east side. Neither document asked the people living there. What followed was Project 200, a freeway meant to run straight through Hogan's Alley and on into Chinatown and Gastown, assembled largely out of public view and sprung on a city council hearing in late 1967. The reaction was ferocious. The Strathcona Property Owners and Tenants Association organized, fought, and won, and the freeway was never built; Chinatown, Gastown and most of Strathcona still stand because of them. But the victory came too late for the alley. By the time the plan collapsed, the houses of Hogan's Alley had already been levelled and the Georgia and Dunsmuir Viaducts were rising over the cleared ground.

## Putting the Names Back

The people are better documented than the place. Barbara Howard was the first Black woman to compete for Canada at an international sporting event, at the 1938 British Empire Games, and later the first visible-minority educator hired by the Vancouver School Board. Thelma Gibson-Towns performed and taught internationally and was honoured by the Black Historical Society of BC in 2005. In 2014 Canada Post issued a Black History Month stamp featuring Nora Hendrix and Fielding William Spotts, released alongside one for Africville in Halifax, two erased neighbourhoods acknowledged together. Stan Douglas rebuilt the alley digitally in Circa 1948. Anthony Joseph painted its residents onto the viaduct itself in 2020. Jamila Pomeroy's documentary Union Street followed in 2023. Nora Hendrix Place opened on Union Street in 2019, and in 2021 the city approved Nora Hendrix Way, the first Vancouver street officially named for a Black woman.

## Flight Context

The site lies at roughly 49.277 N, 123.097 W, at the southwestern edge of Strathcona in Vancouver, immediately east of the downtown core. There is no neighbourhood left to spot from the air, which is the point: what you are looking at is the absence. Overfly at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL and find the two long parallel concrete decks of the Georgia and Dunsmuir Viaducts sweeping east out of downtown toward Main Street. The former line of Hogan's Alley ran beneath and just south of them, between Union and Prior Streets, from about Main Street east to Jackson Avenue. Use the stadium roofs and the False Creek waterfront to the southwest as your anchor, with Chinatown's low brick blocks immediately northwest and the dense wooden houses of Strathcona filling the ground to the east. This is controlled airspace beneath the Vancouver terminal area, so carry current charts and a clearance. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) sits about 1.5 nm northwest on Burrard Inlet, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 8 nm southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm south-southeast, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 14 nm east. Expect low overcast and rain from November through March; the best light is late afternoon in summer, when the viaducts throw long shadows across the ground they cleared.
