# The Warehouse Studio

Vancouver's oldest brick building — an 1886 grocery warehouse that once served as the city's temporary hall — restored by Bryan Adams into a four-room studio built around a mixing console made for George Martin.

**Location:** 49.2831, -123.1020

**Categories:** Recording studios in Canada, Buildings and structures in Vancouver, Commercial buildings completed in 1886, Heritage buildings in Vancouver, Music of Vancouver, Bryan Adams

## Introduction

AC/DC recorded Rock or Bust in Vancouver's oldest brick building. So did R.E.M. with Reveal, and Shakira with Fijación Oral Vol. 1, and Slayer with God Hates Us All. The Oppenheimer Building has stood at the corner of Powell Street since 1886, among the first substantial structures to go up after fire levelled the young city that June. In the years since it has been a wholesale grocery, a temporary city hall, a glass warehouse and finally a ruin. Bryan Adams bought it in 1989. It now holds four recording rooms and a mixing console built for George Martin.

## Oppenheimer's Warehouse

The Oppenheimer Brothers put it up in 1886 and filled it with Vancouver's first and largest wholesale grocery business — the supply line for a settlement that had just burned to the ground and was rebuilding at speed. It is Victorian commercial architecture of the confident kind: three storeys of brick, heavy timber inside, built to carry weight and last.

David Oppenheimer became mayor in 1888, and for three years the building did double duty as Vancouver's temporary City Hall while the real one waited on money and time. A city government running out of a grocery warehouse is the sort of arrangement a two-year-old city makes. That it happened here, in the oldest brick structure still standing in Vancouver, gives the building a claim on the civic record that no plaque quite captures.

## Glass, Then Nothing

In 1902 Pilkington Brothers Limited, the British glass manufacturer, bought the place and turned it into a glass factory and storage warehouse. That is what it remained for decades — an unglamorous, useful building doing unglamorous, useful work on a waterfront street.

Then the wholesale trade moved, the neighbourhood changed, and the building fell into serious disrepair and was abandoned. This is the ordinary fate of nineteenth-century warehouses in every port city on the continent: they are too solid to fall down and too awkward to reuse, so they simply sit, deteriorating, waiting for either a wrecking ball or somebody with an unusual idea.

## Somebody With an Unusual Idea

In 1989 Bryan Adams bought 100–102 Powell Street. The plan was specific. The building at 102 was gutted and turned into parking and green space, hidden behind its retained street facade. The original Oppenheimer warehouse on the corner was restored and converted into a four-room recording facility, with Don Stuart Architects working to keep the brick and beam construction intact rather than boxing it in.

Ron Obvious designed and installed the studio complex itself. The work took four years, and the studio was fully operational in 1995. In 1998 the City of Vancouver gave it a Heritage Award for outstanding restoration of a historic building — which is a polite way of saying that the cheapest thing to do with the place would have been to knock it down, and somebody chose otherwise.

## George Martin's Console

The centrepiece sits on the second floor. The Neve A6630 in Studio 2 is one of only three consoles custom-ordered for Sir George Martin. Built in 1979, it went first into AIR Studios on Oxford Street in London, then to Atlantic Studios in New York, and in 1991 Adams bought it. Ron Obvious rebuilt and heavily modified it before installation.

It is a split design — 58 channels, 24 busses, a 32-channel tape monitor panel — and it does something most desks do not: the microphone preamps are not in the console at all. The 34427 modules live in rolling carts out on the tracking room floor, with the console's trim sending a control voltage down to them. Fifty-two of the channels carry 31106 EQ modules. The live room around it runs 32 feet by 58 with a 24-foot ceiling and three isolation booths.

## What Comes Out of It

Downstairs, Studio 1 is built around a 72-input Solid State Logic G Series desk with Total Recall and Black E Series EQ. Before it came here it sat in the basement of Adams' house on Mathers Avenue in West Vancouver, where, while he was away on tour, he let other bands record and mix — a stopgap that ran for years until the Powell Street plan came together.

The list of records cut in these rooms is long and genuinely strange in its range. AC/DC came repeatedly, for Stiff Upper Lip, Black Ice, Rock or Bust and Power Up. So did R.E.M., Shakira, Linkin Park, Slayer, Billy Talent, Matthew Good, The Tragically Hip, Elvis Costello and Chevelle. The producers and engineers who work here read like a roster of the loud end of the business: Bob Rock, Robert John "Mutt" Lange, Bob Clearmountain, Brendan O'Brien, Mike Fraser, Randy Staub. A grocery warehouse turns out to be very good at holding sound.

## Flight Context

The Warehouse Studio occupies the Oppenheimer Building at 100 Powell Street, 49.2831°N, 123.1020°W, on the eastern edge of Gastown in downtown Vancouver. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL, look for the Burrard Inlet waterfront and the long rail and port lands that run east from Canada Place: Powell Street parallels them one block inland, and the building sits at the Powell and Columbia corner where the downtown street grid changes angle. The white sails of Canada Place and the steam-clock blocks of Gastown lie immediately west; the Port of Vancouver container cranes are the dominant feature to the north and east.
