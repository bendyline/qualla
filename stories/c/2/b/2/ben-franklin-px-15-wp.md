# Ben Franklin (PX-15)

A 130-ton Swiss-built submarine designed not to steer - it drifted 1,444 miles inside the Gulf Stream with six men aboard while NASA watched to see what a month in a sealed tube does to people.

**Location:** 49.2777, -123.1480

**Categories:** Research submarines of Switzerland, Human analog missions, 1968 ships, Submarines of Switzerland, Ships built in Switzerland, Museum ships in Canada, Museum ships in British Columbia

## Introduction

Two days before Apollo 11 left for the Moon, six men climbed into a white steel cylinder off Palm Beach, Florida, sealed the hatch behind them, and deliberately surrendered all control over where they were going. The Ben Franklin was not built to travel anywhere in particular. It was built to be picked up by the Gulf Stream and carried, hanging weightless in the blue-black interior of a river that runs through the Atlantic Ocean, for as long as the current cared to take it. Thirty-one days later the hatch opened again roughly 300 miles south of Halifax, Nova Scotia, 1,444 miles from where it had gone down. The submarine now sits on dry land in Vancouver.

## A Submarine Designed Not to Steer

Jacques Piccard called the type a mesoscaphe - a vessel of the middle depths, distinct from his father's stratospheric balloons above and the bathyscaphes that crawl the abyssal floor below. The PX-15 was engineered to hover at neutral buoyancy somewhere between 600 and 2,000 feet, with a crush depth of 4,000, and its four external electric propulsion pods existed mainly to trim attitude rather than to go anywhere. It weighed 130 long tons and measured 48 feet 9 inches by 21 feet 6 inches by 20 feet high. Piccard and the Grumman Aircraft Engineering Corporation built it at the Giovanola works in Monthey, Switzerland, between 1966 and 1968 - the first submarine constructed to American Bureau of Shipping standards - then took it apart and shipped it to Florida. The most argued-over feature was glass. Piccard insisted on twenty-nine observation portholes. His engineers pointed out, correctly, that every porthole is a potential failure point in a pressure hull. He wanted to see, and he won.

## Six Men and an Audience

Piccard led the mission as senior scientist. Don Kazimir, a former United States Navy submarine officer, was chief pilot, with Piccard's associate Erwin Aebersold as co-pilot. Frank Busby came from the US Navy as an oceanographer, Ken Haigh from Britain's Royal Navy in the same role. The sixth man had a stranger brief: Chester May was a NASA scientist, and his subject was the other five. The agency had recognized the drift for what it structurally was - a small crew, sealed in a confined volume, unable to leave, unable to be resupplied, for a month - and treated it as a rehearsal for the psychology of long-duration spaceflight. While Apollo 11 flew, six men in the Atlantic were being studied for exactly the thing that would matter on a voyage to Mars. A young Woods Hole researcher named Robert Ballard made his first dive in a submersible aboard the Ben Franklin that same year, sixteen years before he found the Titanic.

## Thirty-One Days in the Stream

The drift began on 14 July 1969 and ended on 14 August. Surface vessels shadowed the submarine, but the boat itself had no say in the route; the Gulf Stream sets its own course, meandering, throwing off eddies, running faster in some layers than others. Through those twenty-nine portholes the crew watched a part of the ocean that almost nobody had ever simply sat still inside - the slow rain of marine snow, fish that arrived and stayed, the light failing upward into nothing. The Ben Franklin dove a few more times after 1969 and then, in 1971, ran aground on a reef. That was effectively the end of its working life.

## Three Decades on the North Shore

The submarine was sold to a Vancouver businessman, John Horton, and then it simply stopped being a story. For nearly thirty years the Ben Franklin sat on the North Shore of Burrard Inlet, weathering, unvisited, a 130-ton artefact of the deepest kind of obscurity - the kind that settles over an object too big to throw away and too odd to use. In December 1999 the choice finally narrowed to moving it or scrapping it, and it was offered to the Vancouver Maritime Museum. The museum took it, refurbished it, and set it outside on the grass. A vessel built to hang motionless in the middle depths of the Atlantic now sits motionless on a lawn in Vanier Park, which is either a comedown or a retirement, depending on your temperament.

## The Family That Went Up and Down

It is worth knowing whose boat this was. Jacques Piccard's father, Auguste, set altitude records by balloon in the 1930s. Jacques himself descended to the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench on 23 January 1960 aboard the bathyscaphe Trieste with US Navy Lieutenant Don Walsh, reaching roughly 35,800 feet - the deepest point in any ocean. His son Bertrand commanded the first non-stop balloon flight around the world in 1999. No other family has held records for both the highest flight and the deepest dive. The Ben Franklin is the middle term in that sequence, the vessel for the space between, and it is oddly fitting that it ended up parked where the mountains meet the sea.

## Flight Context

The Ben Franklin stands outdoors at the Vancouver Maritime Museum in Vanier Park, at 49.2777 N, 123.148 W, on the south shore of English Bay in Vancouver, British Columbia. From 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL the site is easy to pick out: Vanier Park is the wedge of green on Kitsilano Point immediately west of the Burrard Street Bridge, with the museum's distinctive peaked A-frame roof at its centre and the white cylinder of the submarine on the lawn beside it. Granville Island and False Creek lie just east under the bridges, Stanley Park is the dark forested headland directly north across English Bay, and the downtown Vancouver tower cluster sits northeast. This is congested airspace - Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 5 nautical miles south, Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre water aerodrome (CYHC) about 2 nautical miles east with constant floatplane traffic, and Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 12 nautical miles southeast - so check current control zone procedures before approaching. Low cloud and drizzle dominate from November through March; July and August afternoons routinely give unlimited visibility with the North Shore mountains sharp behind the city.
