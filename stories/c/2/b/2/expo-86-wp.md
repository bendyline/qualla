# Expo 86

The last world's fair held in North America turned a derelict rail yard into modern Vancouver — at a cost counted in dollars, and in the homes of more than a thousand people who were put out to make room for the visitors.

## Introduction

In October 1983 the Queen of Canada started a concrete mixer. Elizabeth II had come to the muddy edge of False Creek — until recently a Canadian Pacific rail yard and an industrial wasteland — to begin the Canada Pavilion and issue what the organisers called an invitation to the world. Thirty-one months later, on 2 May 1986, the Prince and Princess of Wales and Prime Minister Brian Mulroney opened Expo 86 on that ground. Over the following 165 days, 22,111,578 people passed through the gates of a fair originally conceived as a modest CA$78 million transportation show for Vancouver's hundredth birthday. Final expenditures came to $802 million.

## From Transpo to Expo

The idea belonged to Sam Bawlf, then British Columbia's Minister of Recreation and Conservation, who floated it in 1978 and submitted a proposal in June 1979 for something called Transpo 86. The legislature passed the Transpo 86 Corporation Act the following year, and in November 1980 the Bureau International des Expositions handed Vancouver the fair. Once it was clear this would be a genuine world exposition rather than a regional show, Commissioner General Patrick Reid renamed it Expo 86 in October 1981. Jim Pattison, the Vancouver business magnate, became chief executive and eventually president; Bruno Freschi was chief architect and Ron Woodall creative director. The theme — "Transportation and Communication: World in Motion, World in Touch" — suited a city built by rail and port, and the logo's three interlocking rings stood for land, air and water. Construction was disrupted by labour disputes for five months, but the preview centre opened on schedule in May 1985.

## A Fair That Was Also a Vehicle

Given the theme, the site had to move people as a form of exhibit. A 5.6-kilometre monorail glided over the crowds and touched every zone. A gondola hung above the water. A Japanese high-speed surface transport system ran alongside a French people mover, water taxis shuttled between four landings, and the brand-new SkyTrain delivered visitors to the gates. Fifty-four nations took part. Egypt sent treasures from the reign of Ramesses II; Norway sent its explorers. General Motors ran Spirit Lodge, a live show using holographic effects, developed with the Kwagu'ł community at Alert Bay. Canadian Pacific's pavilion screened Rainbow War, which went on to earn an Academy Award nomination for live-action short. And on the waterfront sat Highway 86, four lanes of concrete by S.I.T.E. Projects of New York carrying real cars, mopeds and bicycles, every one of them painted the same pale grey and fixed permanently in place. The mascot presiding over all of it was Expo Ernie, a life-sized robot.

## The People Who Lost Their Rooms

The fair needed beds for visitors, and the cheapest hotel rooms in Vancouver were already occupied. More than a thousand low-income residents of the Downtown Eastside were evicted from the single-room-occupancy hotels where many had lived for years, some of them given a single day's notice. The legal mechanism was an accident of category: because SRO tenants fell under British Columbia's Innkeeper's Act rather than ordinary landlord-and-tenant law, owners owed them no meaningful notice, and not necessarily anything in writing. Mayor Mike Harcourt asked the provincial government to change the statute. It declined. Among those forced out was Olaf Solheim, a Norwegian man who had lived at the Patricia Hotel; he died within a month of losing his room. The evictions are now a fixed part of how the fair is remembered in Vancouver, and they belong in any honest account of what the celebration cost.

## What the Fair Left Standing

The deficit reached $311 million, and almost nobody now argues it was wasted. SkyTrain, begun in the autumn of 1982 and carrying passengers from December 1985, became the spine of the region's transit system, joined later by the Millennium Line in 2001, the Canada Line in 2009 and the Evergreen Extension in 2016. The geodesic Expo Centre reopened in 1989 as Science World and still anchors the east end of False Creek. BC Place, which had opened in 1983 and hosted the fair's opening, traded its Teflon dome for a cable-supported retractable roof in 2011. The Canada Pavilion became Canada Place, a convention centre and cruise terminal with sails for a roof. The western lands went to the developer Concord Pacific, whose towers established the template of slim high-rises and public waterfront that the world now calls Vancouverism, while the southeast corner became the Olympic Village in 2010. Even the Von Roll gondola survived, reinstalled at the Alton Towers theme park in England in 1987.

## Flight Context

The Expo 86 site occupied the north shore of False Creek at roughly 49.2886° N, 123.111° W, in downtown Vancouver. From 2,000 to 3,500 feet the legacy is unmistakable: False Creek cuts east from English Bay as a narrow inlet, with the silver geodesic sphere of Science World marking its eastern head, the white retractable roof of BC Place just northwest of it, and the dense forest of glass condominium towers on the former fair grounds running along the north bank. On Burrard Inlet to the north, the white sails of Canada Place mark the old Canada Pavilion and the cruise terminal. The Coast Mountains rise immediately north of the harbour; Vancouver Island lies west across the Strait of Georgia. Nearby airports are CYVR (Vancouver International) about 7 NM southwest, CYHC (Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre, a water aerodrome in the inlet itself), CZBB (Boundary Bay) to the south, and CYPK (Pitt Meadows) east up the Fraser. Downtown Vancouver sits beneath controlled airspace — check current charts. Winter brings low cloud and rain; summer afternoons are often brilliantly clear with a sea breeze off the strait.
