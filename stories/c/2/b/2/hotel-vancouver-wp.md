# Fairmont Hotel Vancouver

A bare steel skeleton stood over West Georgia Street for most of the 1930s before the Depression let go and two rival railways rushed to finish a château in time for the King.

**Location:** 49.2838, -123.1210

**Categories:** Canadian National Railway hotels, Canadian Pacific Railway hotels, Hotels in Vancouver, Heritage buildings in Vancouver, Châteauesque architecture in Canada, Hotels established in 1939

## Introduction

For most of the 1930s it was a skeleton. Steelwork for a grand railway hotel went up on West Georgia Street starting in December 1928, and then the Depression arrived and the money stopped, and the frame simply stood there — a rusting grid over downtown Vancouver, visible from everywhere, in a city where a lot of people had no work and nowhere to be. It stayed that way for years. Construction only resumed in 1937, and what finally pushed it over the line was a royal visit: King George VI and Queen Elizabeth were touring Canada in 1939, and Vancouver was not going to receive them in a building site. The hotel opened in May, weeks before they arrived.

## A Deal Written in Mud

The hotel exists because of a swap involving tidal flats. In the 1920s the Canadian Northern Railway wanted a western terminus in Vancouver, and the city wanted the rail yards and the station that came with it. The arrangement they reached obliged Vancouver to prepare the tidal flats at the head of False Creek for railway yards and what became Pacific Central Station, and obliged the railway, in return, to build a large downtown hotel. By the time the digging started, Canadian Northern had been absorbed into Canadian National, and it was CN that broke ground in December 1928. When the Depression stranded the project, CN could not finish alone. In 1938 it did something that would have been unthinkable a decade earlier: it partnered with its bitter rival, Canadian Pacific, to get the building topped out. John Smith Archibald and John Schofield drew the plans.

## A Loire Château in the Rain

Canada's grand railway hotels borrowed their look from the châteaux of the Loire Valley, and this one wears the costume fully: a steep copper roof broken by dormers, carved stone hung on a steel frame, gargoyles, relief panels, and a run of Renaissance detailing layered over the Châteauesque bones. It is a French fantasy executed in a Pacific rainforest city, and the copper has long since weathered to the green that makes the roofline unmistakable from anywhere on Georgia Street. A group of artists was commissioned in 1939 for the decorative work — Olea Marion Davis, Charles Marega, Beatrice Lennie, Valentine Shabief and Lilias Farley. Marega, the sculptor who had made the lions guarding the Lions Gate Bridge and the statue of George Vancouver, died that March at sixty-seven, after teaching a class at the Vancouver School of Art. He did not see the hotel open.

## The Third of That Name

This is the third building in Vancouver to be called the Hotel Vancouver. The first two both stood a little southeast along West Georgia, and both belonged to Canadian Pacific. When the new hotel opened, CP — now a co-owner — shut down its hotel operations at the second Hotel Vancouver rather than compete with itself. That building sat idle, was sold to Eaton's in December 1948, and came down in 1949. Three hotels, one name, one surviving example. The habit of Vancouver replacing itself is not new.

## The Roof

On 1 May 1940 a young bandleader named Dal Richards opened at the Panorama Roof, the ballroom at the top of the hotel, with an eleven-piece band and a thirteen-year-old singer named Juliette who was not yet famous and would soon be. Richards stayed. The Roof became a CBC Radio programme broadcast live from the hotel, and for a generation of Vancouverites the sound of Saturday night came out of that room and through the radio. It is a peculiar kind of civic infrastructure — a ballroom that doubled as a broadcast studio, seventeen floors above a downtown that was still, in the 1940s, a fairly small place.

## Passing Through Hands

At 112.47 metres and seventeen floors, the hotel was the tallest building in Vancouver for thirty-three years, until the TD Tower went up in 1972. Inside are 557 rooms and suites, including a Lieutenant Governor's Suite panelled in black walnut veneer in full Art Deco manner. The ownership record reads like a corporate genealogy: CN took full control in 1962; Hilton managed the place from 1964 to 1984 without ever putting its name on it; Canadian Pacific Hotels bought it in 1988; the 2001 rebranding to Fairmont gave it the name it carries now; Fairmont sold the property to the Caisse de dépôt et placement du Québec in 2007 and kept managing it; and in 2015 Larco Enterprises bought it for C$180 million. A C$12-million renovation of the lobby, restaurant and rooms ran from 2014 to 2018, timed for the building's eightieth anniversary. The green roof has not moved.

## Flight Context

The Fairmont Hotel Vancouver stands at 49.2838° N, 123.1210° W, at 900 West Georgia Street in downtown Vancouver's Financial District, bounded by Burrard, West Georgia and Hornby streets. This is controlled terminal airspace over a dense downtown core, with Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) water aerodrome traffic operating low over Coal Harbour barely a kilometre north — get the VFR terminal chart and the published harbour routes before approaching, and stay on frequency. From the published low-level routes, roughly 1,000 to 2,000 feet, the hotel is genuinely easy to pick out despite being surrounded by far taller glass towers: look for the single large steep verdigris-green copper roof with dormers, the only roofline of its kind downtown. Christ Church Cathedral sits just north, the Vancouver Art Gallery and Robson Square immediately southeast. Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 8 nm southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm southeast, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 16 nm east. Winter means low ceilings and rain; on a clear day the North Shore Mountains rise directly behind the downtown skyline and the green roof reads instantly against the grey and glass.
