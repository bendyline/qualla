# Lions Gate Bridge

A brewing fortune built Vancouver's most beautiful bridge to sell mountainside lots - and the whole suspended deck was swapped out, section by section, while traffic kept crossing.

**Location:** 49.3153, -123.1383

**Categories:** Suspension bridges in Canada, Bridges in Greater Vancouver, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Bridges completed in 1938, Former toll bridges in Canada

## Introduction

Drive north across the First Narrows and you are aiming at two summits the Squamish call Ch'ích'iyúy Elx̱wíḵn - the Twin Sisters. In the story, twin sisters married Haida twins and brokered peace between two nations, and the Creator made them permanent by turning them into mountains. Around 1890 a British Columbia Supreme Court judge, John Hamilton Gray, suggested calling them the Lions instead, after heraldry and the bronze cats in Trafalgar Square. The name stuck to the peaks, then the gate, then the bridge. Underneath all of it, Vancouver's most photographed structure began as a real estate scheme funded by a brewing family.

## Guinness Money

The land speculator George Grant Mackay was writing in the papers as early as 1890 that somebody ought to bridge the First Narrows. A ferry did the job instead, running Ambleside to Gastown from 1909 to 1947, and Vancouver voted the bridge down in a 1927 plebiscite. What revived it was an engineer, Alfred James Towle Taylor, who had a land interest in the outcome and the persistence to chase investors. He persuaded Walter Guinness, of the Irish stout family, to back a syndicate called British Pacific Properties Ltd., which bought 4,700 acres of West Vancouver mountainside. A bridge would turn that hillside into building lots. A second plebiscite on 13 December 1933 carried with 70 percent in favour, and Ottawa eventually approved on condition that Vancouver materials and Vancouver workmen be used wherever possible - jobs being the currency of the Depression. The same 1933 bylaw carried an uglier clause, in the language of its time: 'no Asiatic person shall be employed in or upon any part of the undertaking or other works.' Vancouver's Chinese, Japanese and South Asian residents were written out of the city's signature project before the first pile went in.

## Eighteen Months and a Royal Ribbon

The Montreal firm Monsarrat and Pratley drew it, and later reused the design for Halifax's Angus L. Macdonald Bridge. Prospect Point in Stanley Park gave the south end a high rocky anchorage; the flat delta land on the north shore gave nothing, which is why the North Viaduct runs so long. The main span reaches 473 metres between towers that rise 111 metres, with 61 metres of clearance for shipping and a total length, viaduct included, of 1,823 metres. Work began on 31 March 1937 and the bridge opened to traffic on 14 November 1938 at a cost of CA$5,873,837. King George VI and Queen Elizabeth presided over the official opening the following May. Crossing cost 25 cents for a car - or a horse and carriage - and five cents on foot or by bicycle. The concrete lions guarding the south approach had been set in place that January by Charles Marega, an Austro-Hungarian-born sculptor who spent decades decorating Vancouver. He died that 27 March, after teaching a class at the Vancouver School of Art, two months before the King arrived.

## The Middle Lane

Two lanes were never going to be enough. On 19 May 1952 a third, reversible lane opened down the centre, controlled by signs at a cost of $18,000; traffic signals replaced the signs in 1965, and a redesigned counterflow system arrived in 1994. The Guinness interests sold the bridge to the province on 20 January 1955 for $5,873,837 - to the dollar, what it had cost to build. A parallel span was costed at $17 million and shelved in favour of the Second Narrows crossing further east. The toll came off on 1 April 1963 under a provincial scheme that cleared several bridges at once, and the toll plaza at the north end was eventually torn down. Today it carries Highways 99 and 1A and roughly 55,600 vehicles a day, with trucks over 13 tonnes and studded tires kept off entirely.

## Replaced While Running

By the late twentieth century the bridge had a problem: it had not been designed for durability. The North Viaduct's crumbling concrete deck came out in 1975, replaced by lighter, wider, stronger steel in a sequence of short closures - one section lowered away, one section lifted in. Then, between September 2000 and September 2001, engineers did the same thing to the entire suspended structure of the suspension bridge itself, without ever interrupting peak-hour traffic. It had never been done on a major suspension bridge in daily use. Forty-seven deck sections were swapped overnight and on weekends, each old one dropped onto a barge, each new one hoisted into place; the walkways were moved outboard of the cables and the three road lanes widened from 3 to 3.6 metres. A sixty-three-year-old bridge got a new lifespan without ever closing for more than a night.

## One Hundred and Seventy Lights

In 1986 the Guinness family made Vancouver one more gift: 170 decorative lights, designed and installed by the British engineer Ian Hayward, first switched on the evening of 19 February. A daytime landmark became a nighttime one, a curve of light between two dark shores. In July 2009 the 100-watt mercury vapour bulbs gave way to LEDs, cutting the bridge's lighting power draw by about 90 percent and saving the province some $30,000 a year. Canada designated the Lions Gate Bridge a National Historic Site on 24 March 2005. Work continues in the undramatic register that keeps old bridges standing: vessel-collision protection in 2020, LED lane-control signals in 2022, the northern approach repaved in late 2023. The sisters still stand above it, doing what the story says they do.

## Flight Context

The Lions Gate Bridge crosses the First Narrows of Burrard Inlet at 49.3153 N, 123.1383 W, linking Stanley Park's Prospect Point on the south to West Vancouver and the North Shore. It is one of the easiest landmarks in British Columbia to identify from the air: a green suspension span with 111-metre towers and 61 metres of clearance beneath, set exactly where the inlet pinches, with the dark forested block of Stanley Park to the south and the long North Viaduct curving down onto the delta flats to the north. View it from 1,500-3,000 ft. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC), the downtown water aerodrome, is under 2 nm southeast, and floatplanes transit this shoreline continuously at low level; Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 8 nm south, Squamish (CYSE) 28 nm north up Howe Sound, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 20 nm east. This is controlled airspace within CYVR's zone - clearance required. The two peaks of the Lions, 1,654 m and 1,606 m, sit on the skyline directly north and make the natural alignment cue; they are also the first thing to disappear in the low cloud that sits on the North Shore for much of the winter. The bridge's 170 lights make it unmistakable after dark.
