# Wreck Beach

Below the cliffs of Point Grey lies Canada's best-known clothing-optional beach - and, for fifty years, one of the most persistent grassroots preservation campaigns in Vancouver.

**Location:** 49.2611, -123.2614

**Categories:** Beaches of Vancouver, Nude beaches, Pacific Spirit Regional Park, Musqueam territory, Environmental activism

## Introduction

In 1970, police raided a beach below the University of British Columbia and came away with about a dozen arrests. The Georgia Straight's response was to organize a nude-in. Three thousand people came. The charges were dropped, the police settled into treating the place as a no-harm, no-foul zone, and Wreck Beach carried on essentially as it had - which is what it has kept doing through every subsequent attempt to change it. The beach runs 6.7 kilometres along the foot of the Point Grey cliffs, from Acadia Beach in the north down to Booming Grounds Creek on the north arm of the Fraser River, and reaching it still requires walking down a long flight of wooden stairs through the forest.

## Ulksen, the Nose

Long before any of this, the headland had a name. The Musqueam call it Ulksen - the nose - and the promontory has been hunting, fishing and logging ground and a place of spiritual significance for their people for a very long time. In Squamish the beach is Tsʼatʼlhm. The land is administered today by Metro Vancouver as part of Pacific Spirit Regional Park, more than 55 kilometres of trails wrapping the university endowment lands, but Indigenous claims to it are asserted repeatedly and particularly by the Musqueam Nation, whose reserve lies a short distance south along the river. The geography is unusual for a city beach: no seawall, no road access, no concession stands. Just sand, drift logs the Fraser delivers by the thousand, and a wall of eroding cliff at your back.

## Depression Economics

The convention of swimming without clothes here has a mundane origin. During the Great Depression, swimsuits cost money that people did not have, and men began gathering at Siwash Rock in Stanley Park to swim without them - away from the disapproval of neighbours whose standards were still essentially Victorian. From Siwash Rock the practice migrated west to Wreck Beach, which had the advantages of being farther from anywhere and much harder to reach. Through the following decades it stayed a quiet local habit. Then the 1960s arrived, the counterculture found the place, and the population of the beach on a summer afternoon stopped being quiet at all.

## Three Thousand People

The 1970 raid was the flashpoint, and the nude-in that followed was one of those protests that works precisely because it is unanswerable: what, exactly, does a police force do with three thousand people doing the thing it has just arrested twelve people for? The charges went away. So did the enforcement. For the next two decades the beach existed in a legal grey zone that everyone understood and nobody wrote down, until 1991, when it was formally recognized as a nudist beach. It remains clothing-optional rather than clothing-prohibited, and the boundaries of the section are marked with signs, which is a very Canadian resolution to a question that has caused enormous trouble elsewhere.

## Citizens Concerned for Coward's Cove

On January 25, 1977, a group of beach regulars incorporated under that splendid name. It became the Wreck Beach Committee, and in 1983 the Wreck Beach Preservation Society, and the list of things it has fought off since would exhaust a professional lobbying firm. A twenty-million-cubic-metre dredging operation behind the North Arm breakwater. A proposal to barge millions of gallons of jet fuel past the beach every week. Condominium towers running from the cliff toe to the cliff top, floated in 2004. A seawall twenty feet high and ten feet wide, built over the beach it was supposedly protecting. Viewing platforms. The clearing of forty acres of cliff-face forest and the shaving of the cliffs beneath it. An RCMP service road down to the sand. Permanent outhouses finally arrived in 2012 - one of the few additions the society did not oppose.

## Fifty-Three Metres

The current fight is about height. The University of British Columbia has proposed a seventy-metre tower on the land above, and the Preservation Society is campaigning to hold the line at the fifty-three-metre restriction it says already applies, gathering petition signatures through 2025 and into 2026. Read cynically, this is a squabble over a few storeys. Read the other way, it is the same argument the society has been making since 1977: that what makes Wreck Beach worth anything is the absence of the city above it. The cliff, the forest on the cliff, and the stairs that make you earn the descent are not obstacles to the beach. They are the beach. Take away the sense of having stepped out of Vancouver, and what is left is just sand under a skyline.

## Flight Context

Wreck Beach lies at 49.2611 N, 123.2614 W, along the western and southern foot of the Point Grey cliffs below the UBC campus. From 1,500 to 3,000 feet the geography is one of the cleanest reads on the whole coast: a blunt forested headland pushing into the Strait of Georgia, the pale grid of the university at its tip, a thin bright strip of sand at the base of a wooded bluff, and - just offshore - the sharp brown-and-green boundary where the Fraser River's north arm dumps silt into clear salt water. English Bay and the downtown towers lie northeast; Sea Island and the runways of Vancouver International sit directly across the river mouth to the southeast. Nearby airports: Vancouver International (CYVR) about 4 nm southeast; Vancouver Harbour (CYHC) water aerodrome roughly 7 nm northeast; Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm southeast; Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 22 nm east. This sits directly beneath CYVR's western arrival and departure paths, so the airspace is busy and controlled - get a clearance. Afternoon westerlies build chop on the strait, and low cloud is common from November through March.
