# Storyeum

Vancouver spent more than CA$22 million digging a 104,000-square-foot theatre under Gastown to perform the history of British Columbia — and closed it 28 months after opening night.

**Location:** 49.2839, -123.1075

**Categories:** Museums in Vancouver, Defunct museums in Canada, Former education in British Columbia, 2004 establishments in British Columbia, 2006 disestablishments in British Columbia, Gastown, Immersive theatre

## Introduction

More than CA$22 million bought eight underground sets, two of the largest passenger lifts ever built, a full-size replica steam locomotive, and roughly twenty-eight months of business. Storyeum opened beneath the cobblestones of Gastown in June 2004 and ran its last show on 17 October 2006. For that brief window, visitors at 142 Water Street stepped into an elevator, sank below one of the oldest streets in Vancouver, and were walked through the history of British Columbia by live actors working in a space larger than six NHL rinks. It was the most ambitious tourist attraction the city had built since Expo 86, and one of its fastest failures.

## Built in Six Months

Danny Guillaume conceived the idea in 2000 with architect Al Waisman, and the two founded Historical Xperiences Inc. to build and run it. Local businessmen and the City of Vancouver put in more than CA$22 million between them. Construction of the 104,000-square-foot complex took six months. The engineering was genuinely unusual: the lifts that carried audiences down at the start and back up at the end were among the biggest passenger elevators in the world, each rated for 200 people or 25,000 pounds, assembled from modular units of rotating horizontal and vertical plates. Nothing about the project was modest. The whole venture rested on a single bet: that enough of the tourist traffic already wandering Gastown's cobblestones would pay to go underneath them.

## Sixty-Five Minutes Underground

The show lasted about sixty-five minutes and refused to let anybody sit still. Rather than watching a stage, guests walked from set to set through eight environments. The first was an orientation film during the descent. Then came Ancient Trails, which retold a Tsleil-Waututh account of the creation of the first man and woman in a set dressed as coastal temperate rainforest, followed by The Big House and its story of their descendants. New Arrivals staged a first-contact encounter — the arrival of Europeans, the founding of the Hudson's Bay Company trade, the smallpox that came with the ships, and the sailors who left families an ocean away. Gold Seekers dropped visitors into Barkerville around 1862, fire and lawlessness included. National Dream took on the railway, the Chinese labourers who built it, and the head tax Canada charged them for the privilege of being here.

## The Arithmetic of Attendance

Last Stop closed the walking portion in a 1944 railway station, where a woman waited for a soldier returning from Europe and an exact replica of Canadian Pacific Locomotive 374 steamed in to deliver him. Then the second lift carried everyone back to daylight. It was well made, and it did not work. Not enough people came. Revenue fell, then profit vanished, and the October 2006 show was the last. Guillaume was left owing creditors more than CA$6 million. Those creditors spent the remainder of the year working through the roughly 70,000 square feet of basement looking for anything that could be auctioned. A proposal from Excalibur Entertainment of Surrey to convert the space into a bowling alley of more than forty lanes collapsed over a ground-floor liquor permit, made improbable by the restaurant and bar in the new Woodward's development across the street.

## What Lives Under Gastown Now

The City of Vancouver stayed on as landlord and rented the cavern out piecemeal — Vancouver Fashion Week, a Juno Awards after-party, film school shoots, assorted cultural celebrations. The complex was eventually split in two. A furniture retailer took roughly 12,000 square feet at 142 Water Street, while the 31,000-square-foot half at 151 West Cordova was run for a period by W2 Community Media Arts as a conference and festival venue called W2 Storyeum, complete with a community media lab, café, art studio and meeting rooms. Vancouver Film School worked in the basement, using the old sets for green screens and single-take live-action shoots. The irony is tidy. The most elaborate attempt Vancouver ever made to tell its own story now survives as a large, cheap, well-lit basement where other people go to make theirs.

## Flight Context

Storyeum occupied the block at 49.2839° N, 123.1075° W, beneath Water Street in Gastown, at the northeast edge of downtown Vancouver. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet the district is easy to pick out: Gastown sits between the downtown office towers and the working waterfront, with Waterfront Station and the white sails of Canada Place immediately north on Burrard Inlet, and the gantry cranes and container stacks of the Port of Vancouver running east along the shore. The Lions Gate Bridge spans the First Narrows to the northwest, linking Stanley Park to the North Shore, and the Coast Mountains wall off the horizon beyond. Nearby airports are CYVR (Vancouver International) roughly 8 NM southwest, CYHC (Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre) directly on the inlet a short distance north, CZBB (Boundary Bay) to the south and CYPK (Pitt Meadows) east up the Fraser Valley.
