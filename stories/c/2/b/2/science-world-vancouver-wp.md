# Science World

It began in 1977 with hands-on exhibits hauled around the Lower Mainland in a truck, and ended up inside the geodesic dome Expo 86 left behind at the head of False Creek.

**Location:** 49.2733, -123.1040

**Categories:** Museums in Vancouver, Science museums in Canada, Geodesic domes, World's fair architecture in Vancouver, Expo 86, 1989 establishments in British Columbia

## Introduction

Before there was a dome, there was a truck. In 1977 Barbara Brink was running a travelling set of hands-on exhibits called the Extended I around British Columbia's Lower Mainland — science you were allowed to touch, delivered to whoever would have it. Five years later the idea had grown solid enough to justify a building, and the temporary Arts, Sciences & Technology Centre opened in downtown Vancouver on 15 January 1982. More than 600,000 people came through it. Another 400,000 were reached by outreach programs that went out across the province. What that small operation did not have was a permanent home, and the thing that eventually gave it one was a world's fair.

## A Sphere at the End of the Creek

When Vancouver won the transportation-themed 1986 world's fair, Expo's chief architect Bruno Freschi designed a geodesic dome — Buckminster Fuller's structural idea, executed at the eastern end of False Creek — to serve as the Expo Centre, the fair's own introductory pavilion. Construction ran from 1984 into early 1985. Inside, a 3D IMAX theatre was built in 1986 for the fair's "Transitions" film series. From the moment it went up, the sphere did what good landmarks do: it gave a formless stretch of industrial waterfront an unmistakable shape, and it became shorthand for the whole east end of downtown. Expo shut its gates in October 1986, and the question of what happens to a fairground's buildings arrived on schedule.

## The Campaign

What followed was a lobbying campaign of real intensity: keep the dome, move the Arts, Sciences and Technology Centre into it, convert the Expo Centre into a science centre. Government backing came through, the province handed over the building, and then the money had to be found. Donations from the federal, provincial and municipal governments, the GVRD, private companies, foundations and individuals added up to $19.1 million — enough to build an addition, redesign the interior, and fabricate exhibits from scratch. A four-month preview in 1988 pulled in over 310,000 visitors, which settled any remaining doubt about demand. Science World opened properly on 6 May 1989. A year later, a 400-seat Omnimax theatre opened in the upper section of the dome, extending what the Expo IMAX had started.

## The Name Problem

Sponsorship money is useful and naming rights are the price, which produced one of the more instructive episodes in Vancouver's civic history. The Omnimax became the Alcan Omnimax Theatre in 1996, then quietly went back to being the Omnimax. In January 2005 the building itself was officially renamed the "Telusphere" in exchange for a $9-million donation. The name was, by all accounts, universally disliked — nobody used it, and by mid-2005 the institution had retreated to "Science World at Telus World of Science," a compromise that at least kept the words people actually said. When the agreement ended in 2020, the Telus half was dropped. Through the whole saga the SkyTrain station outside never budged from Main Street–Science World, which tells you which name had really taken.

## Beyond the Dome

Much of the organization's work happens nowhere near False Creek. Scientists and Innovators in the Schools sends volunteer researchers into classrooms. On the Road takes staff deep into the province to run workshops and shows in communities that have no science centre within reach. Super Science Club runs after-school programming in schools with the fewest resources, on the premise that a child's interest in how things work should not depend on their postal code. For a province the size of British Columbia — where the distance from Vancouver to the northern communities is greater than most Canadians realize — outreach is not a side project. It is the main argument for the place existing.

## Renovations and Occupations

The dome has been borrowed more than once. During the 2010 Winter Olympics it was handed over to "Sochi World," a hospitality pavilion for Russia, hosts of the next Winter Games; the centre closed in mid-January, transformed, and reopened that March. A round of indoor renovation followed, finishing in mid-2012, and the Ken Spencer Science Park opened outdoors late that year — a working demonstration of food, water and energy systems that you walk through rather than read about. In January 2022 Science World began a long-term partnership with Acuitas Therapeutics, the Vancouver company behind the lipid nanoparticle technology used in the Pfizer–BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine. That September, Teck Resources gave $650,000 to fund copper surfaces on exhibits, door handles and countertops, turning the building's own hardware into a demonstration of the metal's antimicrobial properties. The exhibit, in other words, is partly the thing you grab on the way in.

## Flight Context

Science World sits at 49.2733° N, 123.104° W, at the eastern head of False Creek in Vancouver, immediately south of the Main Street–Science World SkyTrain station. This is controlled terminal airspace over a dense urban core, with Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) water aerodrome traffic operating low about 2 km north — get the VFR terminal chart and published harbour routes, and stay on frequency. From the low-level routes, roughly 1,000 to 2,000 feet, the target is trivially easy: a single silver geodesic sphere on the waterfront, unlike anything else in the city, and lit at night. Use the surrounding cluster to orient — BC Place's white roof and Rogers Arena lie a few hundred metres northwest, the Cambie and Georgia viaducts run past to the north, and False Creek narrows westward toward Granville Island. Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 8 nm southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 13 nm southeast, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 15 nm east. Winter delivers low cloud and rain for weeks at a time; clear days give sweeping views west down False Creek and north to the Coast Mountains.
