# Granville Island

A tidal sandbar dredged into fourteen hectares of industrial land in 1915, abandoned by mid-century, then rebuilt without being demolished — a public market, seven theatres and a working concrete plant sharing one peninsula under the Granville Street Bridge.

**Location:** 49.2714, -123.1350

**Categories:** Granville Island, Neighbourhoods in Vancouver, Retail markets in Canada, Tourist attractions in Vancouver, Adaptive reuse of industrial structures in Canada, Former islands of Canada

## Introduction

Cement trucks still grind through the crowds. That is the thing visitors tend to miss about Granville Island: beneath the buskers and the berry stalls and the seven indoor theatres, this is a working industrial site, and one of its tenants has been batching concrete on the same ground for more than a century. It is not an island, either. It is a peninsula — and before that a sandbar, and before that open tidal water at the head of False Creek. Almost nothing here is what its name claims, which turns out to be the most interesting thing about it.

## Sen̓áḵw and the Sandbar

Long before the fill went in, this was a fishing ground for the Musqueam and Squamish peoples, and a village stood at the head of the inlet — sən̓aʔqʷ in hən̓q̓əmin̓əm̓, Sen̓áḵw in the Squamish language. False Creek then was more than twice the size it is now, a broad tidal basin of flats and channels with a large permanent sandbar in the middle of it.

Captain George Henry Richards charted that bar during the British Boundary Commission's naval survey of 1858–59, and what he drew is roughly the size and shape of Granville Island today; an Admiralty chart of 1893 matches the modern outline even more closely. The city above it was called Granville until it renamed itself Vancouver in 1886. The old name stuck to the street, the street's rickety wooden bridge crossed the bar, and eventually the bar inherited the name too.

## A Million Cubic Yards

The first attempt to turn the sandbar into property was freelance. Soon after the original Granville Street bridge went up in 1889, someone began driving piles around the perimeter to fence off some free real estate. Ottawa shut it down as a menace to navigation — but a photograph from 1891 shows the piles still standing.

In the meantime people lived there. "Shackers," as Vancouver called them, occupied shacks on the flats and floathouses on the water, and made a living by fishing and beachcombing, selling salmon, smelt and firewood door to door or at the public market on Main Street. Then, in 1915, the dredges arrived: roughly 760,000 cubic metres of fill pulled out of False Creek, much of the work done by a man named Alvin Kingston, at a cost of $342,000. When it was finished there were fourteen hectares of new industrial land where the tide used to run.

## Wire Rope and Green Heart

What went up on the new ground was not charming. Sawmills and factories went up almost immediately — a photograph from 1917 shows one already running — and the island filled with the unglamorous supply chain of a resource economy, packed into corrugated sheds within sight of downtown.

The biggest name was Wright's Canadian Ropes, which during the Second World War was Canada's largest maker of heavy-duty wire rope. Its Green Heart line went to the forestry and mining industries, which is to say the cable that hauled logs off British Columbia's mountainsides and lifted ore out of its shafts was spun here. A fire gutted the Granville Island plant in 1953, and by 1956 the company had moved to south Vancouver. Over the following years the island emptied out, and by the late 1960s it was mostly a derelict yard under a bridge.

## Kept, Not Cleared

In 1972 a federal order-in-council handed the fourteen hectares to Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation, and the decision that followed was the unusual one. The obvious move was demolition. Instead the redevelopment kept the sheds — the corrugated walls, the overhead cranes, the rail spurs set into the pavement — and filled them with something else.

The Public Market opened in 1979 inside a fifty-thousand-square-foot industrial building, with farmers and food vendors in place of machinery. Today the island holds more than three hundred businesses employing over three thousand people: upwards of fifty independent food stalls, eleven restaurants and bars, artisan studios, and theatre companies including the Arts Club, Arts Umbrella, Axis, Boca del Lupo, Carousel Theatre for Young People, Ruby Slippers and the Vancouver TheatreSports League. Granville Island Brewing has been making beer here since 1984 and still brews small batches on site.

## Giants on the Silos

The concrete plant never left. It arrived in the island's first industrial years as Ocean Concrete, operates now as Heidelberg Materials, and it is closed to the public — a genuine working facility in the middle of a tourist district, which is exactly why it matters.

In 2014 the Brazilian artists OSGEMEOS — twin brothers Gustavo and Otavio Pandolfo, whose name is Portuguese for "the twins" — painted enormous figures onto its silos. The Giants are the first thing you see coming across the water, and they are the whole argument of Granville Island in one image: industry not preserved behind glass but still running, wearing somebody else's artwork. In 2016 the federal government committed to a long-range Granville Island 2040 plan. The hardest question it faces is how to keep the place from becoming a tidy replica of itself.

## Flight Context

Granville Island sits at 49.2714°N, 123.1350°W on the south shore of False Creek, directly beneath the south end of the Granville Street Bridge in Vancouver. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL it is easy to pick out: a small rectangular peninsula of blue and grey industrial roofing pushed into the inlet, with the yellow drums and painted silos of the concrete plant at its western end and a marina basin on its eastern side. False Creek runs east–west as a narrow finger of water between the downtown towers to the north and the Fairview slopes to the south, crossed by three bridges — Burrard, Granville and Cambie — and the island tucks against the middle one. Nearby fields: CYVR (Vancouver International) about 6 nm southwest, CXH (Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre water aerodrome) 2 nm north, CZBB (Boundary Bay) to the southeast, CYPK (Pitt Meadows) to the east.
