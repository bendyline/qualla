# East Van Cross

Two words sharing a single letter A - an anonymous piece of Vancouver graffiti that became a monument, a beer label, and a goalie mask.

**Location:** 49.2654, -123.0777

**Categories:** Public art in Vancouver, Symbols of Canada, East Vancouver, Graffiti and street art

## Introduction

Write EAST downward. Write VAN across it. Let them share the A, and what you have drawn is a cross. Nobody signed the first one. It went up on walls and underpasses in the eastern half of Vancouver at some point before 1983, made by people who never claimed it, and it said a thing about that half of the city that took four letters and no sentence: we are from here, and here is not the nice part. Half a century later the same shape stands as a civic monument, sells on t-shirts, and has been painted onto an NHL goaltender's helmet. Symbols rarely stay where you leave them.

## A Line Called Ontario Street

Vancouver's addressing system splits east from west at Ontario Street, though anyone who lives there will tell you the real border is Main, a block over, because that is where the arterial runs. The distinction is not decorative. Since the 1880s the eastern side has been the first landing place for people arriving without British connections or money - Chinese families in Strathcona and Chinatown, Italians who made Commercial Drive into Little Italy through the 1950s, South Asian merchants who built the Punjabi Market along Main between 45th and 51st. Seven official neighbourhoods carry the East Vancouver label: Grandview-Woodland, Hastings-Sunrise, Kensington-Cedar Cottage, Killarney, Renfrew-Collingwood, Strathcona and Victoria-Fraserview. For most of the twentieth century, being from any of them meant being from the cheaper side, and everybody in the city understood the arithmetic.

## Written on a Pillbox

The cross has no author, no founding anecdote and no agreed-upon birthday - which is exactly the condition that let it belong to everyone who used it. Its earliest firmly dated appearance is a photograph taken in September 1983, showing the symbol sprayed on Second World War pillboxes at the Point Grey Foreshore. There is a joke buried in that location: the pillboxes sit on the far western edge of the city, on the wealthy side, as far from East Van as a person can get without swimming. Somebody carried the mark out there and left it. Whatever the writers meant individually, the symbol collectively expressed what one description calls the marginality and defiance of the east side - a way of naming a place before anyone else got to name it for you.

## Ken Lum Comes Home

Ken Lum was born in Vancouver in 1956 and grew up on that side of the line, in Strathcona and then Kensington-Cedar Cottage, in a Chinese Canadian family. He went on to an international career in conceptual art and, since 2012, a professorship at the University of Pennsylvania. In early 2010 his Monument for East Vancouver went up near the intersection of Clark Drive and East 6th Avenue, taking the anonymous scrawl and building it at monument scale, lit, permanent, official. The move was elegant and slightly perilous. A symbol that got its charge from being unsanctioned had just been sanctioned. Some read it as long-overdue recognition of a neighbourhood the city had spent a century treating as the back room; others noted that monuments tend to arrive right around the time the thing they commemorate is on its way out.

## The Cross Goes Commercial

What followed was the ordinary life of a successful symbol. The cross migrated onto clothing and jewellery, appeared on the packaging of Parallel 49 Brewing Company's Craft Lager, and on 22 December 2024 turned up on the right side of a new mask worn by Vancouver Canucks goaltender Thatcher Demko. Meanwhile the ground it referred to kept changing. Rising prices in East Vancouver have pushed out artists, seniors and young families - the same categories of people who made the neighbourhoods worth branding in the first place. So the symbol now does double duty. Worn one way it is a statement about where you come from; worn another it is a statement about where you moved to. Both readings are live at once, which may be the most East Van thing about it.

## Flight Context

Ken Lum's Monument for East Vancouver stands at 49.2654 N, 123.0777 W, at Clark Drive and East 6th Avenue, on the industrial edge of Mount Pleasant just south of False Creek. From the air at 1,500-2,500 ft the monument itself is too small to pick out, but the geography it names is unmistakable: the dense, uniform grid of East Vancouver running east from the Ontario/Main Street line to Boundary Road, the port and rail yards along Burrard Inlet to the north, and the abrupt change in tree canopy and lot size as you cross to the West Side. Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 6 nm southwest and its control zone covers this airspace - clearance is required; Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC), the downtown water aerodrome, is roughly 3 nm northwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 12 nm south, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) 15 nm east. The North Shore Mountains make the simplest orientation cue, and low cloud on their slopes is the usual autumn and winter limitation.
