# Japantown, Vancouver

Paueru-gai, the Japanese Canadian neighbourhood on Powell Street that was emptied by government order in 1942 and never allowed to come back - and the single building in Canada that was.

**Location:** 49.2831, -123.0970

**Categories:** Asian-Canadian culture in Vancouver, Ethnic enclaves in British Columbia, Japanese-Canadian culture, Japantowns, Neighbourhoods in Vancouver

## Introduction

On the evening of 7 September 1907, a crowd assembled by the Asiatic Exclusion League worked its way through Vancouver's Chinatown breaking windows, then turned east toward Powell Street. It did not arrive unannounced. Word had run ahead from Chinatown, and the residents of Japantown were waiting, armed. Four times the mob came at the blocks around Powell, and four times it was driven back, taking injuries as it went. More than fifty storefronts lost their glass before the night was over. It is worth holding onto that image - a neighbourhood standing its own ground - because of what happened to the same streets thirty-five years later, when the thing that emptied them was not a mob but a signature.

## Paueru-gai

The Japanese called it Paueru-gai, Powell Street, though outsiders often said Little Tokyo. By 1921 there were 578 Japanese-owned stores and businesses along it. Three Japanese-language dailies were printed in the district. Three Buddhist churches served it, the Vancouver Buddhist Church among them, founded in 1906. Four sento - public bathhouses - operated within a few blocks: the Chitose, the Tokiwa, the Kotobuki, the Matsunoyu. The Japanese language school, also founded in 1906, taught as many as a thousand children. Underpinning all of it were the kenjinkai, mutual-aid associations organised by the Japanese prefecture families had come from, which pooled money and looked after their own so effectively that the community came through the Great Depression with scarcely any recourse to public relief.

## Brainball at the Powell Street Grounds

In 1914 the neighbourhood put a baseball team on the open field at the Powell Street Grounds, now Oppenheimer Park, and called it the Asahi - morning sun. Outmatched physically by the larger players they faced, the Asahi built a game around it. Under manager Matsujiro Miyazaki they perfected what fans called brainball: bunts, stolen bases, cut-offs, speed and defence in place of power. It worked spectacularly. They took Terminal League titles in 1926, 1930 and 1933, and five straight Pacific Championships from 1937 to 1941. Crowds of every background came to watch them, which in a city that had voted for exclusion leagues was no small thing. They played their last season in 1941.

## March 1942

On 4 March 1942, order-in-council 1665 authorised the internment of all persons of Japanese racial origin and established a protected zone one hundred miles deep along the Pacific coast. Over 22,000 people were removed from it - more than ninety percent of Japanese Canadians, and roughly 14,000 of them born in Canada. Families were given days. The Custodian of Enemy Property took what they left behind, and from 1943 the government sold it: houses, farms, fishing boats, furniture, often far below value and without the owners' consent. Restrictions on where Japanese Canadians could live were not lifted until 1 April 1949. When the coast reopened, most had nothing on it to return to.

## One Building Came Back

At 475 and 487 Alexander Street, at Jackson, stand the Vancouver Japanese Language School and the Japanese Hall. They are the only property in Canada ever returned to Japanese Canadian owners after the war - one address, out of everything taken from twenty-two thousand people. Reopened in 1952 and back in its own building by 1953, the school was designated a National Historic Site on 20 June 2019 - recognised in the citation as a rare documented case of property returned to Japanese Canadians after internment. The Vancouver Buddhist Church also survives nearby. In 1988 Prime Minister Brian Mulroney apologised in the House of Commons and the government agreed to pay $21,000 to each surviving internee and to restore citizenship to those who had been deported to Japan. The money arrived forty-six years late, and for most of the people who had built Powell Street, too late entirely.

## Railtown, and the First Weekend of August

Developers now market these blocks as Railtown, a name with no memory in it, which is roughly the point of the name. But the neighbourhood is not entirely gone. Since 1977 the Powell Street Festival has filled Oppenheimer Park for two days every August - a celebration of Japanese Canadian heritage that has grown into something braided with the alternative and street culture of the Downtown Eastside around it. The Asahi were inducted into the Canadian Baseball Hall of Fame in 2003 and the BC Sports Hall of Fame in 2005, named an event of national historic significance in 2008, and put on a Canada Post stamp in 2019. On 7 September 2007, a century to the day after the riot, people walked the route the mob had taken through Chinatown and Japantown, this time on purpose.

## Flight Context

Historic Japantown centres on Powell Street around 49.2831 N, 123.0970 W, on the Vancouver waterfront flats immediately east of Gastown and north of Chinatown. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL the district reads as a band of low brick warehouses and flat roofs squeezed between the Burrard Inlet port terminals and the rail corridor to the south, with the green rectangle of Oppenheimer Park the only clear break in the grid. Use the Port of Vancouver's container cranes, the sulphur piles across the inlet at North Vancouver, and the Ironworkers Memorial Bridge to the east as references. This sits under Vancouver International's (CYVR) terminal airspace about 13 km northeast of that airport; Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is barely 1.5 km west along the waterfront and generates constant floatplane traffic at low level, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) lies 25 km east. Expect controlled airspace, harbour helicopter movements, and low stratus on winter mornings.
