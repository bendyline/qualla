# Greektown, Vancouver

A stretch of Kitsilano that Greek Vancouverites still call Ουέστ Μπροντουέι, where an immigrant neighbourhood's heart now sits on a street renamed in the Musqueam language.

**Location:** 49.2642, -123.1681

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods, Immigration and diaspora, Culture, Urban history, British Columbia

## Introduction

Ουέστ Μπροντουέι. Sound it out and you get "West Broadway" - the same street, the same shops, transliterated letter by letter into the Greek alphabet by the people who made this stretch of Kitsilano their own. The nickname is affectionate and a little wistful, the way people talk about a place that was more theirs a generation ago. Greektown has no legal boundary and never did. Ask around and you will be told it runs along West Broadway with Blenheim Street at one end and Macdonald at the other, and that its heart is the crossing at šxʷməθkʷəy̓əmasəm Street - a name that arrived only recently, and carries a story of its own.

## Who Came, and Why

The first wave arrived after the Second World War: Greek refugees displaced from Europe, along with families moving in from western Canada, settling into the low apartment blocks and wooden houses of Kitsilano. A second wave came as political exiles during the dictatorship that ruled Greece from the 1960s into the early 1970s. Between them they built the full infrastructure of a community that expected to stay - an Orthodox cathedral, social clubs, schools, businesses, a bank, newspapers, and eventually a television station. The 1971 census recorded the result plainly. In Kitsilano, Greek was the second-most common language and the second-most common ethnicity, behind only English and the British Isles.

## The Church That Outgrew Its Block

St. George's began at West Seventh and Vine, in a building that was adequate right up until it wasn't. Between 1954 and 1960 so many Greek immigrants arrived in Vancouver that the congregation no longer fit, and the lot was too small to rebuild on. So the community found new ground at Arbutus Street and Valley Drive and raised the cathedral that stands there today, completed in 1974, with the Hellenic Community Centre following in 1977. Inside are traditional Byzantine icons, gold-grounded and severe, the visual language of a church that has not changed its mind in a thousand years. The old property on Vine did not go to waste: it is now Kitsilano Neighbourhood House.

## Two Percent

Communities disperse, and this one did. By the 2001 census, only two percent of Kitsilano residents gave Greek as their mother tongue - fewer than reported English, Chinese, French or German. The markers that remain are specific and stubborn: the cathedral, the Athens Social Club, a Greek supermarket, a Greek Senior Citizen Society, and a density of Greek restaurants well above what a Vancouver neighbourhood would otherwise support. And there is the calendar. The city sanctioned an annual Greek Day in 1974; it lapsed, and in 2005 the City of Vancouver, the Province of British Columbia and the Government of Greece jointly brought it back. It has run every year since. On 28 June 2026, Broadway between Macdonald and Blenheim closed to traffic from eleven in the morning until nine at night, which is one way a neighbourhood insists on still existing. Each October the Hellenic community adds a food festival at the cathedral.

## The Street With an Older Name

For most of Greektown's life, its central cross street was called Trutch, after Joseph Trutch, British Columbia's first lieutenant-governor - a man who denied that Aboriginal title existed and cut back reserves that had already been established. Vancouver council voted to change it in 2021. In September 2022 the Musqueam Indian Band gifted a Halkomelem name, and on 20 June 2025 the new signs went up at a ceremony with Musqueam Chief Wayne Sparrow and Mayor Ken Sim: šxʷməθkʷəy̓əmasəm Street, rendered in English as Musqueamview Street. So the heart of a neighbourhood built by people who crossed an ocean now sits at a corner named in the language of the people who were here first. Both names are on the same pole, in a city that is still working out how to hold all of its arrivals at once.

## Flight Context

Greektown centres on 49.2642 N, 123.1681 W, along West Broadway in Kitsilano on the south side of English Bay. From the air the area is legible mostly as pattern rather than landmark: the long straight commercial ribbon of Broadway running east-west through a dense grid of low-rise apartments, with the water of English Bay and the Burrard Inlet beyond to the north. Fly it at 1,200 to 2,500 feet AGL for the neighbourhood texture; St. George's Greek Orthodox Cathedral at Arbutus and Valley Drive is the one distinct roofline. Vancouver International (CYVR) is about 10 km south-southwest on Sea Island, and the Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is the floatplane base at Coal Harbour across the water. This is busy controlled airspace over a dense residential city - approach it as scenery, not as a working waypoint. Clear days give the North Shore mountains as a backdrop; November through February, expect the whole grid under flat grey rain.
