# Gastown Steam Clock

Vancouver's most photographed antique was built in 1977 to cover a sidewalk vent, and an electric motor has been doing most of the work since 1986.

**Location:** 49.2844, -123.1089

**Categories:** Tourist attractions in Vancouver, Clocks, Steam power, Gastown

## Introduction

It looks like it has been standing on the corner of Water and Cambie since the gold rush. It has not. The Gastown steam clock went up in 1977, which makes it younger than the personal computer, and since 1986 the thing that actually drives its hands has been an electric motor. None of which stops the crowd. Every fifteen minutes the whistles let go with the Westminster Quarters, on the hour a plume climbs out of the top, and several dozen phones go up at once. It may be the most successful piece of deliberate fakery in western Canada - and, awkwardly, it was built to solve a problem about people sleeping on a warm grate.

## A Lid for a Steam Vent

Downtown Vancouver runs on district heat: a central plant pushes steam through pipes under the streets, and the system has to breathe. At Water and Cambie it breathed through a sidewalk grate, which was unsightly to the merchants trying to rebuild Gastown's reputation, and which was also warm - warm enough that people with nowhere else to go slept on it. The neighbourhood wanted the vent covered. The horologist Raymond Saunders offered a cover that would do something with the steam rather than merely hide it. Local merchants, property owners and private donors put up roughly $58,000, and in 1977 a clock appeared over the grate, styled to look a century older than the ground it stood on. The people who had been sleeping there simply went somewhere else, which is the part of the story that rarely makes the caption.

## Steel Balls and Whistles

The mechanism is genuinely clever and genuinely visible - Saunders glassed the sides so you can watch it work. Steam from the street network drives a small engine at the base. The engine turns a chain lift that carries steel balls upward; the balls roll back down and their weight drives a pendulum escapement, which in turn feeds the four faces. Eight brass whistles handle the sound, playing the Westminster Quarters every fifteen minutes, and on the hour the clock exhales a column of steam from its crown. There is no digital shortcut anywhere in that sequence. It is a Rube Goldberg machine built with real horology, wrapped in a Victorian costume, sitting on top of a municipal utility.

## The Quiet Substitution

Steam is a poor timekeeper. The clock was loud and it drifted, and by 1986 the electric motor installed as a backup had quietly become the primary drive. The steam remained for the whistles and the show. Purists grumble; everyone else keeps photographing it. In October 2014 the clock was lifted off the corner entirely for major repairs, carried out by the man who had built it, and returned to its post in January 2015. Saunders went on to build steam clocks elsewhere, most of them descended directly from this one: Otaru in Japan, Indianapolis, and the British Columbia towns of Whistler and Port Coquitlam. The Indianapolis clock stands seventeen feet tall outside the Indiana State Museum, its four neon-backlit dials two feet across, its eight whistles picking out 'Back Home Again in Indiana' on the quarter hour.

## Older Than It Looks, Newer Than It Pretends

Gastown's clock is usually credited with inventing the genre, and almost did - but it was beaten by more than a century. In 1859 the Birmingham engineer John Inshaw took over a public house at Morville and Sherborne Streets in Ladywood and, wanting a talking point, built a steam-driven clock above the door. A small boiler made steam; the steam condensed into droplets that fell on a plate at regular intervals; the falling water drove the mechanism. The pub became the Steam Clock Tavern and was doing well enough by the 1880s to turn into a music hall. Since 1977 the form has spread unevenly. Tim Hunkin built one for the Chelsea Farmers' Market in London in 1984; it still stands and no longer runs. At St Helier in Jersey, Smith of Derby delivered a full-scale replica of the midsection of the paddle steamer Ariadne in 1996, listed by Guinness as the world's largest steam clock, its steam workings since swapped for electrical fittings that fake the puffs on schedule. A half-scale prototype at Berwick, Australia, built by Peter Weare at his own expense, was dismantled in December 2010 after repeated vandalism.

## Water Street

The clock anchors a neighbourhood that has spent fifty years arguing about what it is. Gastown was saved from a planned freeway by citizen campaigning in the 1960s, designated a National Historic Site of Canada in 2009, and in the summer of 2024 saw Water Street turned into a pedestrian-priority zone from Richards to Carrall - which means the crowd that gathers on the quarter hour no longer has to negotiate with traffic. The clock itself has drifted into odd corners of popular culture: the cover of a Nickelback album, a scene in a 1991 Chuck Norris film, and a stretch of the 'Vancouver Velocity' course in Mario Kart. For a machine built to cap a steam vent, it has had a remarkable career.

## Flight Context

The steam clock stands at 49.2844 N, 123.1089 W, at Water and Cambie Streets in Gastown, on the downtown Vancouver peninsula about two blocks in from Burrard Inlet. At 1,500-2,500 ft the clock is invisible, but its setting is not: look for the low brick blocks of Gastown wedged between the glass towers of the financial district to the west and the Port of Vancouver's container terminals and grain elevators along the inlet to the north, with the sails of Canada Place immediately northwest. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC), the downtown water aerodrome, is less than a nautical mile northwest and its floatplane traffic uses this shoreline constantly; Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 6 nm southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) 13 nm south, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) 17 nm east. This is busy controlled airspace beneath and around CYVR's zone - clearance required, and expect heavy floatplane and helicopter movement at low level over the inlet. The North Shore Mountains rising immediately across the water are the orientation landmark in any weather that lets you see them.
