# Woodward's Building

Vancouver's grandest department store became its most contested ruin, and the fight over who the rebuilt block belonged to shaped everything that stands there now.

**Location:** 49.2826, -123.1080

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Vancouver, Department store buildings in Canada, Downtown Eastside, History of Vancouver, Public housing in Canada

## Introduction

Before the war, Vancouver navigated by a searchlight. A beacon on the roof of the Woodward's department store on Hastings Street threw a beam that people could see at night from as far away as Abbotsford and Mission, until the armed forces had it switched off in case it guided somebody else in. What replaced it in 1944 was stranger and more permanent: a twenty-five-metre steel replica of the Eiffel Tower, erected on top of a department store, carrying a single illuminated letter W. For half a century that letter was how the city found its own downtown after dark. It is still up there. Almost nothing beneath it is original.

## Everything Under One Roof

Charles A. Woodward put up the 1903 building as his store's second home, on a stretch of Cordova and Hastings that was then the centre of Vancouver retail. His idea was that a customer should never need to go anywhere else. The store sold clothing and household goods, cashed cheques, booked travel, and stocked things nobody else in the city carried, and its basement food floor was reckoned at one point the most prominent supermarket in North America. Children were brought to see the Christmas windows. Over twelve separate construction phases the building swelled to twelve storeys across roughly two-thirds of the block, and because it grew in fits, its bones changed as it went: heavy old-growth timber in the original 1903 to 1908 portion, concrete slabs and columns in everything after. A great deal of that floor area was never meant to be seen, a warren of stockrooms and offices behind the selling floors.

## The Long Slide

Shoppers left for the suburbs in the 1960s, and the district's anchor tenants followed. When Eaton's abandoned West Hastings for Pacific Centre uptown, the old retail core lost its gravity. Industry drained out of the surrounding blocks and took working-class residents with it, and the new office economy planted itself at Burrard and Georgia instead. In the 1980s Woodward's sold its celebrated food floor to Safeway, which had no real use for the location and ran it as an IGA until the doors shut for good. The company went bankrupt in 1993. Its suburban stores were bought by the Hudson's Bay Company and turned into Bay and Zellers outlets, which nobody mourned much, but the downtown building found no buyer at all. A dark twelve-storey hulk in the middle of the Downtown Eastside accelerated everything that was already going wrong around it.

## Woodsquat

Fama Holdings bought the building in 1995 and proposed filling it with private housing. Residents of the neighbourhood objected on a single point: any redevelopment here had to include social housing. The province offered to fund some, could not come to terms with the owner, and the plan collapsed. For years the building sat empty except when a film crew rented it. The province bought it outright for $22 million in 2001, and a new provincial government shelved the project in early 2002. That autumn a small group of activists walked in and occupied the building. Police evicted them after a week, so they pitched tents on the sidewalks around the block and stayed three more months. The Woodward's Squat has been credited with setting in motion the redevelopment of the landmark it was protesting about, which is not how these things usually end.

## What Stands There Now

In 2003 the City of Vancouver, pushed by councillor Jim Green, bought the building from the province for $5 million and then asked the neighbourhood what it wanted. A two-stage competition in 2004 went to Westbank and Peterson Investment Group with Gregory Henriquez of Henriquez Partners as architect. The result cost roughly $400 million and covers nearly a million square feet: 536 market apartments, 125 non-market units for single people run by PHS Community Services, 75 non-market family units under the Affordable Housing Society, a grocery, a drugstore, a bank, the National Film Board, civic offices, a daycare, and 130,000 square feet of Simon Fraser University's School for Contemporary Arts. On the morning of September 30, 2006, Pacific Blasting rolled everything but the 1903 portion into the ground. The W came down first, was rebuilt with LEDs, and went back up on January 9, 2010. In the atrium hangs Stan Douglas's vast glass photograph, Abbott and Cordova, 7 August 1971, restaging the night police broke up a marijuana smoke-in a few steps from here.

## Flight Context

The complex sits at roughly 49.283 N, 123.108 W, on the Hastings and Cordova block at the western edge of the Downtown Eastside, wedged between Gastown and the Chinatown grid. Fly the Burrard Inlet shoreline at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL. The site is on the inland side of the waterfront, one block back from the port's container cranes and the sawtooth pier sheds, and reads from above as a bright, recently built block of pale towers and courtyard set against the darker low-rise brick of Gastown around it. The two residential towers are noticeably taller than anything immediately adjacent and cast a distinctive shadow across the plaza in afternoon light. The rooftop W is not identifiable from cruise altitude but is unmistakable from the ground at night.
