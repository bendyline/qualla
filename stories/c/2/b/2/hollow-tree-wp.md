# Hollow Tree

A dead western red cedar in Stanley Park, big enough inside to hold an elephant, kept upright by a steel frame and the stubbornness of the people who refused to let the city take it down.

**Location:** 49.3069, -123.1536

**Categories:** Individual trees in British Columbia, Stanley Park, Tourist attractions in Vancouver, Heritage conservation

## Introduction

Somewhere in the photographic record of Vancouver between 1914 and 1931 there is a picture of an elephant standing inside a tree, lifting a woman off the ground with its trunk. The elephant fits. So does the woman, the photographer, and presumably whatever crowd had gathered to watch. The Hollow Tree in Stanley Park was for decades the most photographed thing in the park, and the reason is straightforward arithmetic: a western red cedar that once measured 18.3 metres around leaves an interior roomy enough to accommodate almost any idea a Vancouverite might have on a Sunday afternoon.

## What Is Actually Standing There

It is important to be clear about what the Hollow Tree is, because the name oversells it slightly. This is not a tree. It is a stump - the standing shell of a western red cedar that died a very long time ago and whose interior decayed away, leaving a ring of outer wood open to the sky. That shell sits in Stanley Park, 404.9 hectares of peninsula that Lord Stanley dedicated on September 27, 1888, on ground where the Squamish and Musqueam had villages long before: Xwayxway, 'place of masks,' near what is now Lumberman's Arch, and Chaythoos, 'high bank,' to the west. The cedar was already ancient and already hollow when the park was created around it. Its circumference has since dropped from 18.3 metres to 17.1. It is, in the most literal sense, slowly going away.

## The Souvenir

For generations the Hollow Tree was the picture you took to prove you had been to Vancouver, and it held that title through the eras of the glass plate, the box camera and the colour snapshot alike. The photograph that survives from Frank Gowen's collection - the elephant, the woman, the trunk - is the outer limit of a tradition that otherwise consisted of ordinary people grinning out of a hole in a dead cedar. The tree asked nothing of its visitors. It was simply large, obviously old, and open at the front, which turns out to be an unbeatable combination.

## December 15, 2006

The storm that meteorologists came to call the Hanukkah Eve windstorm crossed the region between December 13 and 15, 2006, and it did not treat Stanley Park gently. Roughly ten thousand trees came down inside the park - something close to forty percent of the forest was affected - and the cleanup ran to about $9 million. Across British Columbia, Washington and Oregon the storm killed eighteen people and cut power to something like 1.8 million homes and businesses. The Hollow Tree survived, but not intact. It had been leaning and deteriorating for years, and the wind finished the argument. By 2008 the Park Board had concluded the stump was unsafe and slated it for removal.

## Saved by Subscription

That decision did not go over well. In 2009 a group of Vancouverites incorporated the Stanley Park Hollow Tree Conservation Society and set about raising the money privately, donor by donor, to do what the city had declined to pay for. The engineering solution is invisible from the path: a metal frame was fitted inside the shell, taking the load that the rotted wood could no longer carry, so that the stump now stands on a skeleton it did not grow. A ceremony marked the restored tree in October 2011. There is something quietly odd about the whole enterprise - a city spending private money to keep a dead tree standing - and something entirely reasonable about it too. Landmarks do not have to be useful.

## Two Fires

In September 2014, firefighters were called to the Hollow Tree twice to put out blazes that investigators described as suspicious. The stump came through both. It is still there, off the Stanley Park Drive loop near Third Beach, still open at the front, still being photographed - though now by people holding phones rather than plate cameras, and now with a steel armature bracing the inside. The circumference keeps shrinking. Eventually the wood will lose the argument for good. Until then a very old cedar that stopped being alive before anyone now living was born continues to do the one job it has had for more than a century, which is to stand still and let people step inside.

## Flight Context

The Hollow Tree stands at 49.3069 N, 123.1536 W, in the western half of Stanley Park near the Stanley Park Drive loop, a short distance inland from Third Beach. The stump itself is under closed canopy and not visible from the air, so navigate by the peninsula: from 1,500 to 3,000 feet Stanley Park reads as a solid dark-green thumb of forest pushing northwest from the downtown towers, outlined by the pale ribbon of the seawall. Siwash Rock sits on the northwest shore, Prospect Point marks the northern tip beneath the Lions Gate Bridge, and Third Beach notches the western shoreline just below the tree's position. English Bay opens to the southwest with freighters at anchor. Nearby airports: Vancouver Harbour (CYHC) water aerodrome about 2 nm east in Coal Harbour; Vancouver International (CYVR) roughly 8 nm south; Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 19 nm east; Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 16 nm southeast. This is congested controlled airspace with heavy floatplane traffic along the Burrard Inlet shoreline; low cloud and rain are the rule from November through March.
