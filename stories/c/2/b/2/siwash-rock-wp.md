# Siwash Rock

A 32-million-year-old basalt stack off Stanley Park's western shore - to the Squamish, a man made permanent; to mariners, a bowling pin; and the subject of a long argument about what to call him.

**Location:** 49.3088, -123.1561

**Categories:** Rock formations of Canada, Stanley Park, Squamish people, Stacks of Canada, Sacred rocks, History of Vancouver

## Introduction

The hole near the base is where he kept his fishing tackle. That is not a geological explanation, though there is one of those too. It is the detail Squamish leader Andrew Paull offered about Slhx̱i7lsh, the figure who stands in the water off the western shore of Stanley Park - a man, in the Squamish account, transformed into stone by X̱ays the transformer and left there permanently. Vancouver mostly calls the outcrop Siwash Rock, mariners once called it Nine Pin Rock for its resemblance to a bowling pin, and geologists call it the only sea stack in the Vancouver area. All of these are true at once, which is why the small column of basalt has generated more argument than anything else its size in the city.

## The Man Who Would Not Move

The Squamish name is Slhx̱i7lsh, also recorded as Lhilhx̱í7elsh. Versions of his story differ, as versions of any story told for a very long time will. The plaque installed near the rock names him Skalsh the unselfish, transformed by Q'uas the transformer as a reward for his unselfishness. The poet Pauline Johnson, retelling the legend in her 1911 collection Legends of Vancouver, cast the transformation as a reward to a young father and called the result, in her phrasing, an indestructible monument to Clean Fatherhood. The Victorian capital letters are hers, and so is the framing; she was writing these stories in English, for a settler readership, at a moment when very little Squamish oral history was reaching print at all. What survives across every telling is the shape of the thing: a person who did the right thing so insistently that the world decided to keep him.

## Tekahionwake

Emily Pauline Johnson performed under the stage name Tekahionwake, which means double life - apt for the daughter of a Mohawk hereditary chief and an English immigrant, who made her living reciting on stages across Canada, the United States, and Britain. She retired to Vancouver in 1909. The pieces that became Legends of Vancouver ran first in the Daily Province, drawn from stories told to her by her friend Chief Joe Capilano of the Squamish, and the book turned into a fixture of the city's literature. She gave Lost Lagoon its name, noticing that the inlet seemed to vanish at low tide. She died in March 1913, and her ashes were placed in Stanley Park, a short walk from the rock she had written about. A large public procession followed her to the burial - the city she had adopted for four years turned out in force.

## Thirty-Two Million Years of Standing Still

The geology is straightforward and slightly humbling. Around 32 million years ago, magma pushed up through a fissure in the sandstone and mudstone that underlie the park, cooling into a narrow vertical dike of basalt. Basalt is hard. Sandstone is not. Waves have spent the intervening epochs grinding the softer rock backward while the basalt refused, and what is left stands between 15 and 18 metres - roughly 49 to 59 feet - offshore of a cliff that used to surround it. Every sea stack on Earth is the same story of differential erosion. This one just happens to be the only example in Vancouver, and to look uncannily like a figure standing with its back to the city, facing out toward the open water.

## Fort Siwash

The cliffs above the rock carry a lookout on the Siwash trail, and for two wars it was not a scenic viewpoint but a defensive position known as Fort Siwash. An artillery battery was mounted there during the First World War; searchlights went in during the Second, sweeping the approaches to the harbour. The park's stranger residents clustered here too. Someone lived in a cave nearby for seventeen years, a stretch broken only by his service overseas, and was arrested not long after he came home from the war - a man who had defended a country that then charged him with living in it improperly. And park board lore holds that a runaway mountain goat roamed free above these cliffs for nearly a year in the mid-1960s before being struck by a car elsewhere in the park.

## What to Call Him

Siwash comes from Chinook Jargon, the trade language of the coast, where it meant simply a person of First Nations descent and was used without evident insult by Indigenous speakers themselves. Its root, though, is the French sauvage - wild, undomesticated, savage - and roots have a way of resurfacing. In 2017 Vancouver Park Board commissioner Catherine Evans moved to change the rock's official name to Slhx̱í7lsh. Her argument was short: we should not have a name for any part of our land that is derogatory to the Indigenous people of this land. Both names are still in circulation, and the Squamish one turns up more often every year. It is worth noticing what is actually at stake. One name describes a category of person. The other is somebody's name.

## Flight Context

Siwash Rock stands at 49.3088°N, 123.1561°W, in the water just off the western shore of Stanley Park, between Third Beach to the south and Prospect Point to the north. It is small - 15 to 18 m of dark basalt - so fly it low and slow, 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, and look for a single dark column standing clear of the cliff line with a wake breaking around it. Orientation is simple: Lions Gate Bridge crosses First Narrows immediately north at Prospect Point, English Bay opens to the southwest with freighters at anchor, Point Grey and the UBC headland close the far side of the bay, and the seawall traces the whole park perimeter in a bright ribbon. Airspace demands care - CYHC Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre lies about 4 km east with seaplane traffic routing through First Narrows directly past this shoreline, and CYVR Vancouver International sits roughly 13 km south. CZBB Boundary Bay is about 28 km southeast, CYPK Pitt Meadows around 35 km east. Get clearance and respect park overflight guidance. Late-afternoon light from the west is the only time the rock reads as a figure rather than a lump; winter storms out of the southeast throw spray well up the cliff.
