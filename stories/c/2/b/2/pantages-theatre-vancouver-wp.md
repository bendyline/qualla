# Pantages Theatre (Vancouver)

Built in 1907 by a Greek runaway who made a fortune in the Klondike, the oldest surviving vaudeville house in Western Canada answered to six names, then to none - and came down in 2011.

**Location:** 49.2814, -123.1011

**Categories:** Theatres in Vancouver, Theatres completed in 1907, Heritage buildings in Vancouver, Former cinemas and movie theatres in Canada, 1907 establishments in British Columbia, Demolished buildings and structures in Canada

## Introduction

Royal. State. Queen. Avon. City Nights. Sun Sing. The building at 144 East Hastings Street answered to six different names across its working life, and by the end it answered to none of them - it was simply the empty one with water pooling on the roof. When it was finally pulled down in 2011 it was considered the oldest surviving vaudeville theatre in Western Canada, a distinction that turned out to be worth less than the half-block of Downtown Eastside real estate it stood on. Vancouver has demolished a great deal of its own history. This is the demolition people still argue about.

## The Boy Who Ran Away in Cairo

The man who built it was born Periklis Alexandros Pandazis on the Greek island of Andros in 1867, and he did not stay there. At nine he ran away in Cairo, worked two years as a deckhand, and reached the United States in the early 1880s by way of Panama and San Francisco. He renamed himself Alexander after hearing about Alexander the Great, which tells you most of what you need to know about his ambitions. The Klondike made him. Pantages was in Dawson City around the turn of the century, waiting tables and carrying bags, when the Opera House burned in January 1900; he helped build its replacement, the Orpheum, which opened seven weeks later. The performer Kathleen Rockwell - Klondike Kate - became his early business partner. From Dawson he went to Seattle, opened the Crystal Theater, and started assembling a circuit. By 1920 he owned more than thirty theatres outright and controlled some sixty more by contract, and at his peak he ran eighty-four.

## Vaudeville on East Hastings

The Vancouver Pantages opened in 1907, when East Hastings was not a byword for anything and the city itself was barely twenty years past the fire that erased it. Vaudeville was the mass entertainment of its moment - acrobats, singers, comedians, animal acts, a dozen turns a night, priced for mill workers and longshoremen. A Pantages house was a product, built to a formula and fed by a booking circuit that could move an act from Winnipeg to San Diego without it ever leaving the payroll. Six hundred and fifty seats. What survives of that world now is mostly photographs, because vaudeville did not so much decline as get replaced: sound film arrived, the circuits collapsed, and the theatres either adapted or closed.

## Sun Sing

This one adapted, repeatedly. In the 1920s it became a movie house, and thereafter it changed names the way a building changes owners. Its longest and most interesting life was its last. On the edge of Vancouver's Chinatown, the theatre became the Sun Sing, a Chinese-language cinema, and it ran that way for decades - Hong Kong and Cantonese pictures for an audience the rest of the city's screens were not serving. It closed in 1994. A building that had opened for vaudeville audiences in 1907 finished by showing films in a language its builder never spoke, which is either a decline or a remarkably long run, depending on how you count.

## Endangered

Then it sat. Vacant buildings in this part of Vancouver do not age gracefully, and this one had a flat roof and a wet climate. A restoration proposal came forward - the 650-seat theatre brought back, plus a black box studio, an art gallery and 136 units of housing - and on 30 September 2008 Vancouver City Council refused it, along with the housing attached. The entire half block went up for sale. In 2009 Heritage Canada put the Pantages on its Top Ten Most Endangered Places list, and it was on the Vancouver Heritage Register, and neither designation kept water off a roof. The roof collapsed under the accumulated weight of years of standing water. Demolition followed in 2011, taking the neighbouring buildings with it.

## Sequel 138

What stands there now is called Sequel 138: seventy-six condominium units and eighteen social-housing rentals, a compromise that satisfied approximately nobody in a neighbourhood where the argument over who gets to live there has never cooled. It is worth noting that Vancouver lost the other one too. A second Pantages went up at 20 West Hastings starting in 1914, delayed by the First World War and not finished until somewhere between 1917 and 1918, and it ran as the Beacon, then the Odeon Hastings, then the Majestic. It came down in 1967 and was replaced by a parking lot. Two theatres, one name, a few blocks apart - and on East Hastings today, nothing at all to indicate that either of them was ever there.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 49.2814 N, 123.1011 W, at 144 East Hastings Street in Vancouver's Downtown Eastside, roughly two blocks east of Main Street and one block north of the Chinatown gate. The building is gone, so navigate by district: from 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, find the elbow where Burrard Inlet's port terminals, rail yards and grain elevators meet the downtown street grid, then look immediately inland to the low-rise brick blocks between the port and Chinatown - a conspicuously short-built pocket surrounded by towers. The Sun Tower's green cupola at Beatty and Pender and the Woodward's W beacon a block west are the reliable fixes; Science World's geodesic dome marks False Creek to the south, Canada Place the waterfront to the northwest. Airspace is controlled and busy: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) lies about 1 nautical mile northwest with continuous floatplane movements, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 8 nautical miles southwest, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 14 nautical miles east. Winter brings persistent low overcast and rain; clear summer afternoons show the old street grid cleanly against the North Shore mountains.
