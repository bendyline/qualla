# Hastings Mill

The sawmill Vancouver grew around, and the little store that outlived it, survived the fire that levelled the city, and was floated seven kilometres west on a barge to become a museum.

**Location:** 49.2860, -123.0950

**Categories:** Industrial history, Museums, Vancouver, British Columbia, Heritage buildings, Maritime history

## Introduction

Vancouver's oldest building is not standing where it was built. In 1930, a service organization called the Native Daughters of British Columbia lifted a sixty-five-year-old wooden store off the waterfront where a sawmill had just gone quiet, loaded it onto a barge, and floated it some seven kilometres west along the shore of the inlet to the foot of Alma Street. It had already outlasted the company that put it up, the fire that destroyed the rest of the settlement, and the industry that made the city possible. Putting it on a boat and moving it across town was, by that point, one of the least dramatic things that had happened to it.

## Captain Stamp's Bad Bet

Edward Stamp formed his company in England in 1865, backed by a hundred thousand dollars of capital, and secured from the colonial government of British Columbia the right to take 16,000 acres of timber on the lower coast. His first choice of mill site was Brockton Point, which he abandoned after finding the currents difficult and a shoal in the way. He settled instead on a spot further east along Burrard Inlet's south shore, at the foot of what is now Dunlevy Avenue. Then the machinery he had ordered from England failed to turn up, and the whole enterprise sat idle. Cutting for export did not begin until June 1867, two years after the company was formed. Stamp ran it for less than two years, retired, and watched his firm go into liquidation back in England. The mill closed in 1870.

## A Company Town on the Inlet

It reopened that August under new owners: Dickson, DeWolf and Company of San Francisco, who renamed it the Hastings Sawmill Company. Around the mill, without anyone planning it, a town assembled. There was the Hastings Mill Store, where everyone bought everything, and the Hastings Mill School, which drew children not only from the mill village but from Moodyville on the far side of the inlet. Wages came from one employer, goods from one counter, schooling from one room. That is what a company town is, and for two decades it was among the largest concentrations of settlers on Burrard Inlet, the nucleus, as one historian put it, around which the city of Vancouver grew up in the 1880s.

## June 13, 1886

The city had been incorporated for less than three months. Two land-clearing fires were burning west of town, one opening ground for the railway roundhouse, the other clearing the way for the city to expand. The wind came up. What followed consumed somewhere between six hundred and a thousand buildings, the count never established because there was so little left to count, and killed at least twenty-one people, though that figure is also uncertain. Survivors ran east toward the water, toward the mill, which stood beyond the fire's reach. Explosives were hauled there for safekeeping. The store became a hospital and then a morgue. Vancouver began rebuilding almost at once: within two weeks businesses were trading again out of rough sheds along Cordova Street, and within six months five hundred buildings stood, many of them now in brick.

## Terminus

What changed the settlement permanently was not the fire but a decision made in a boardroom. When the Canadian Pacific Railway chose Vancouver as the western terminus of the transcontinental line, the centre of gravity shifted away from the mill gate and toward the railway's own land. The company town dissolved into a city. Lumber remained the backbone of the local economy regardless, and Hastings Mill kept cutting for another four decades, outliving the era it had created. It finally closed in 1928, sixty-one years after the first boards went out for export, by which time the shoreline it sat on was becoming the industrial port that still occupies it.

## The Building That Floated Away

Two years after the mill shut, the store made its voyage. Officially reopened in 1931 and dedicated the following year as the Museum of B.C. Historical Relics in Memory of the Pioneers, it is known to everyone as the Old Hastings Mill Store Museum, and the Native Daughters of British Columbia still run it. Inside is the kind of collection that accretes rather than gets curated: household objects, photographs, oddities, the physical residue of a settlement that burned down and started over, along with First Nations art. The building itself is the real exhibit. Put up in 1865 for a sawmill company that would be liquidated inside four years, it is now the oldest structure in a city of well over half a million, sitting in a small green park by the water, a long way from where it started and still standing.

## Flight Context

The Hastings Mill site lies at 49.2860 N, 123.0950 W on the south shore of Burrard Inlet, at the foot of Dunlevy Avenue just east of downtown Vancouver; nothing of the mill remains, and the ground is now container and bulk terminal. The surviving store building sits about 7 km west-southwest at Hastings Mill Park, by the water at the foot of Alma Street in Point Grey. Best viewed from 1,000 to 3,000 feet. Orientation is straightforward: follow Burrard Inlet east from the Lions Gate Bridge, past the white sails of Canada Place, and the mill site is the working waterfront immediately beyond, with the Ironworkers Memorial Second Narrows Crossing further east and the North Shore mountains rising directly across the water. Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) is barely 1 nm west on the same inlet, with heavy floatplane traffic; Vancouver International (CYVR) lies roughly 8 nm southwest, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 13 nm east and Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm southeast. Expect controlled airspace, harbour helicopter and seaplane movements, and frequent low marine cloud from October through March.
