# BowMac sign

Vancouver's biggest piece of 1950s neon survived the wrecking crew by agreeing to be hidden behind a toy company's logo.

**Location:** 49.2633, -123.1310

**Categories:** Signs and landmarks, Heritage preservation, Design, Urban history, British Columbia

## Introduction

In the 1950s you could find Vancouver from the air by its glow. The city had become the neon capital of North America - not just for the sheer number of signs, but for the craft in them, the benders and glassblowers and the electrical showmanship on display along its commercial streets. The largest single contributor to that glow went up in 1958 above a car dealership on West Broadway. Bowell McLean built a sign twenty-nine metres tall, orange, studded with hundreds of bulbs and tubes, and when it was lit there was nothing else like it. Only the BC Electric Building downtown burned brighter, and no freestanding sign in North America was bigger. Drivers claimed you could see it from eighteen miles out.

## A Style With an Expiry Date

The BowMac was not just large, it was of its exact moment: the font, the proportions, the confident geometry of a business that assumed the future belonged to automobiles. That moment passed quickly. Through the 1960s, stricter bylaws went after the signs one by one, and the neon that had defined the city's night began coming down. Vancouver spent two decades quietly deleting the thing it had been famous for. The BowMac survived mostly by being too big and too well built to bother with - the largest, the most central, the most recognisable of the survivors, standing over a stretch of Broadway that was steadily becoming something other than a car strip.

## The Demolition Permit

By the 1990s people wanted it gone. Urbanex Development Corporation was building a retail centre on the old dealership site and went to Vancouver's planning staff in the autumn of 1996 with a proposition: keep the non-conforming sign structure, but swap the lettering for a tenant's name. Planners and developer worked at it together and could not square the circle. Retaining enough of the sign's character while satisfying a corporate logo and a construction budget proved impossible, and a demolition permit was duly issued. That should have been the end of it. Instead the conversation restarted, and out of the second round came the odd solution that stands on Broadway today. In May 1997 the city formally designated the sign a landmark worthy of preservation and revitalisation.

## Preserved by Being Covered

The compromise was this: keep the sign, keep the letters, and put something else in front of them. The BOW MAC letters remain in place but are no longer self-illuminated. Over them sits a heavily perforated three-quarter-inch metal screen carrying the Toys "R" Us logo in its corporate colours, lit from within. The Heritage Commission accepted it knowing exactly what it was accepting - a conservation measure that obscures the artifact it conserves, while keeping it physically intact and partly visible through the perforations. Vancouverites promptly gave the hybrid its own names. It has been the "Toys 'R' BowMac" and the "Toymac" ever since, which is about as affectionate as a city gets toward a structure many of its residents consider oversized and frankly ugly.

## The Argument That Never Ends

Nearly thirty years on, the arrangement still provokes people. One camp sees a heritage object held hostage by a billboard and wants the screen stripped off. Another sees a billboard, full stop, and wonders why anything this large is protected at all. What both camps are really arguing about is what a city owes its own recent past. The BowMac is not a cathedral or a courthouse. It is commercial signage built to sell sedans, and preserving it means admitting that the ordinary noise of a boom era is also history - that the glow which once told pilots where Vancouver was is worth a little civic trouble. Look at it from the sidewalk on West Broadway and you can still read the ghost letters behind the mesh, a 1958 sign patiently waiting out its disguise.

## Flight Context

The sign stands at 49.2633 N, 123.131 W, on West Broadway in Vancouver's Fairview neighbourhood, about a kilometre south of False Creek and midway between the Granville Street and Cambie Street bridges. This is a street-scale object rather than a terrain feature: at 29 metres it is best appreciated from the ground or from a low circuit, and from typical approach altitudes into the city it reads only as a bright rectangle standing proud of the Broadway commercial strip - though at night, with the screen lit, it is easier to pick out than most buildings around it. Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 12 km southwest on Sea Island, and the Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is the floatplane base at Coal Harbour just north of downtown. Both sit under busy controlled airspace; treat the Broadway corridor as sightseeing rather than a navigation fix. Winter brings low cloud and rain that flattens the city's light; clear autumn evenings are the best time to understand what the neon era actually looked like.
