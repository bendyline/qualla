# Burrard Dry Dock

The North Vancouver yard that built more than 450 ships - including the little RCMP schooner that beat the Northwest Passage - is now a waterfront plaza with two cranes left standing over it.

**Location:** 49.3083, -123.0789

**Categories:** maritime-history, industry, world-war-ii, british-columbia, pacific-northwest

## Introduction

Two gantry cranes stand on the North Vancouver waterfront with nothing left to lift. They are the last working-sized objects on a site that once launched ships into Burrard Inlet at a rate no other yard in Canada could match, and they were kept deliberately, restored and left in place because somebody understood that a plaza with cranes over it tells a story that a plaza without them cannot. Below them, where 14,000 people once clocked in, families eat ice cream. The yard beneath the pavement built more than 450 vessels across nearly a century, and it began with one man making wooden fish boats on False Creek in 1894.

## Twice Burned

Alfred Wallace - Andy, to everyone - started with fishing boats, and in 1909 his boatworks burned down and was abandoned. He had already incorporated Wallace Shipyards in 1905 and moved the following year to a bigger site at the foot of Lonsdale Avenue in North Vancouver, directly across the inlet from the young city of Vancouver. On 11 July 1911 that yard burned too. It was rebuilt immediately, which tells you most of what you need to know about the temperament of the operation. Shipyards are flammable places - timber, pitch, oil, hot rivets - and the industry's early history is a long argument between fire and stubbornness. At Lonsdale, stubbornness won twice.

## The First Steel Hulls in Canada

The First World War transformed a regional boatyard into an industrial concern. Wallace Shipyards took a contract to manufacture shells for 18-pounder guns, then built six large cargo schooners and three freighters for the merchant marine - the first deep-sea steel-hulled ships ever built in Canada. To handle the wooden schooners the company opened Number 2 Yard west of the Squamish reserve, on ground now occupied by the North Shore Auto Mall; it was leased to the William Lyall Shipbuilding Company in 1917, which turned out 27 wooden ships there before closing in 1920. The company took the name Burrard Dry Dock in 1921 and installed Vancouver's first floating drydock four years later. Andy Wallace died in 1929, and his son Clarence took over a yard that no longer resembled the one his father had founded.

## A Small Ship with a Long Reach

In 1928 the yard launched something almost comically modest by its own standards: a 104-foot auxiliary schooner for the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, designed by Tom Halliday on the model of Roald Amundsen's Maud. She was built of Douglas fir and sheathed in Australian eucalyptus so that Arctic ice would grind at hardwood rather than at her frames. Named St. Roch, she spent the years from 1940 to 1942 becoming the first vessel to run the Northwest Passage from west to east, then in 1944 the first to make the passage in both directions. On 29 May 1950 she completed the first circumnavigation of North America. Since 1962 she has sat under a roof at the Vancouver Maritime Museum, a National Historic Site of Canada - a wooden boat from a steel yard, and the most famous thing it ever made.

## Fourteen Thousand People

Between 1940 and 1945 Burrard Dry Dock became the busiest shipyard in Canada. It built 109 Park and Fort class freighters - Canada's answer to the Liberty ship - along with corvettes, minesweepers, landing ships and Admiralty maintenance vessels of the Beachy Head class, and it converted and outfitted 19 escort carriers for the Royal Navy. Demand outran the North Shore site, so a South Yard opened at the foot of McLean Avenue across the water. Employment peaked at 14,000, of whom about 1,000 were women. They came into a trade that had never wanted them, learned welding and riveting and crane work in a matter of weeks, and were expected to leave again when the war ended. Most did. The ships they built carried the Allied war effort across two oceans.

## Polar 8

Consolidation followed victory. Burrard bought Yarrows of Esquimalt in 1946, the neighbouring North Vancouver Ship Repairs in 1951, and the assets of Victoria Machinery Depot in 1967, closing that yard at once. The Wallace family sold out in 1972 to Cornat Industries, part of the Canfor group; the merged operation became Burrard Yarrows and then, in 1985, Versatile Pacific Shipyards. A Panamax-class drydock and new cranes went in at North Vancouver in 1982. Then Ottawa cancelled the Polar 8 icebreaker, the enormous contract the yard had been built up to fulfil, and in 1992 Versatile Pacific went bankrupt. The North Vancouver yard closed and the last workers were laid off. A plan for a National Maritime Museum of the Pacific on the site was shelved in 2010 when provincial funding never arrived. What went up instead was housing, retail and public space - the restored Pipe Shop and Coppersmiths Shop, Shipyard Plaza, Craneway Plaza, and those two cranes with nothing to lift.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.308N, 123.079W on the north shore of Burrard Inlet at the foot of Lonsdale Avenue, North Vancouver, at sea level. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, or from any approach down the inlet. Identify it by Lonsdale Quay's covered market and the SeaBus terminal immediately west, the two preserved orange-red gantry cranes standing above the waterfront plazas, and the line of condominium towers stepping up the slope behind. The Lions Gate Bridge spans the First Narrows about 2.5 nm west and the Ironworkers Memorial (Second Narrows) Bridge about 2 nm east; downtown Vancouver's towers sit directly across the water to the south, with the twin peaks of the Lions on the skyline to the north-northwest. Nearest airports: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CXH) about 2 nm southwest for floatplanes, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 8 nm south-southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm south, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 16 nm east. Burrard Inlet holds low cloud and drizzle through much of the winter; summer mornings can begin with marine layer that burns off by midday.
