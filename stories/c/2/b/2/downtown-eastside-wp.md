# Downtown Eastside

Vancouver's oldest neighbourhood was once the centre of the city; a century of removals, closures and policy reversals left it carrying burdens the rest of the city set down.

**Location:** 49.2814, -123.0997

**Categories:** Downtown Eastside, History of Vancouver, Neighbourhoods in Vancouver, Urban history, Public health

## Introduction

The grey stone building on the corner of Main and Hastings was finished in 1903 with $50,000 from Andrew Carnegie, and for fifty-four years it held Vancouver's public library. When the books moved to Burrard Street in 1957, residents of the surrounding blocks organized and won the building back; since the 1980s it has been the Carnegie Community Centre - a place to eat cheaply, read the paper, and be indoors. Almost everything about the Downtown Eastside is in that sequence. This was the middle of the city once. Then the city moved, and the people who could not left behind a neighbourhood that has been asked to hold what nowhere else would.

## When This Was Downtown

After the Great Vancouver Fire of 1886 destroyed almost everything, the town rebuilt itself on the edge of Burrard Inlet between Cambie and Carrall - the townsite that became Gastown and the western part of today's Downtown Eastside. By the turn of the century the neighbourhood held city hall, the courthouse, the banks, the main shopping streets, and the Carnegie Library. Passengers moving between Pacific steamships and the end of the Canadian Pacific Railway filled hundreds of hotels and rooming houses. Japanese Canadians built Japantown along Powell Street; Chinese Canadians built Chinatown beside it. Hastings Street ran on beer parlours, theatres and the traffic of a working port. It was loud, crowded and central, which is exactly what it stopped being.

## A Century of Subtraction

In 1942, the Canadian government interned the neighbourhood's entire Japanese Canadian population - an estimated 8,000 to 10,000 people, most of them Canadian citizens, dispossessed of homes and businesses. Few came back after the war, and Japantown never recovered. In the 1950s the interurban depot at Carrall and Hastings closed, the shops and theatres drifted west to Granville, and the tourist hotels ran down and were carved into single-room occupancy units, a use that persists. In the 1980s the losses accelerated: between 800 and 1,000 tenants evicted from residential hotels before Expo 86 to make room for visitors; British Columbia's psychiatric hospitals emptying under a policy of de-institutionalization that promised community care and did not fund it; the federal government ending social housing funding in 1993; Woodward's department store, the anchor of West Hastings, closing the same year.

## The Emergency That Did Not End

By 1997 the local health authority declared a public health emergency. HIV was spreading through shared needles at rates unmatched anywhere outside sub-Saharan Africa, and more than a thousand people had already died of overdoses. The responses that followed were invented here and copied worldwide: a needle exchange in 1989; Insite, North America's first supervised injection facility, at 139 East Hastings in 2003; overdose prevention sites in supportive housing from 2017. Fentanyl arrived at the end of 2014 and changed the arithmetic completely, and in 2016 the province declared an overdose emergency that has never been rescinded. In January 2023 British Columbia began a three-year pilot decriminalizing possession of small amounts of certain drugs. The pilot expired on 31 January 2026 and was not renewed; since 1 February 2026, possession has been a criminal matter again.

## The Women of Low Track

Between 1980 and 2002, more than sixty women disappeared from these blocks. A great many were Indigenous. For years their families were told, in effect, that women who used drugs or sold sex simply move on - a response that says more about who was listening than about who was missing. Robert Pickton was charged with twenty-six of the murders and convicted in December 2007 on six counts of second-degree murder; twenty further charges were stayed in 2010, and he died in custody on 31 May 2024 after being attacked by another inmate. The National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls, struck in 2016, delivered its final report on 3 June 2019. The women were daughters, mothers, aunts and sisters, and every 14 February since 1992 their names have been read aloud at a memorial march that begins at Main and Hastings.

## The Part Outsiders Miss

Tourists are told to avoid the neighbourhood, though they are rarely the ones harmed in it. What they miss is a dense civic life: the Heart of the City Festival each autumn, the Powell Street Festival in and around Oppenheimer Park every August since 1977, the Firehall Arts Centre, the artist-run studios, the poets - Bud Osborn among them - who wrote the neighbourhood's own account of itself. "Behind the visible people who have a lot of troubles, there's a community," former mayor Sam Sullivan said in 2010. "Some very intelligent people say this is the city's cultural heart." Roughly 7,000 people live here. Condominium towers now press in from three sides, and the argument over whether that is revival or eviction is the neighbourhood's permanent weather.

## Flight Context

The Downtown Eastside centres on 49.2814 N, 123.0997 W, around Main and Hastings, a few blocks east of downtown Vancouver. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet AGL it reads as the low-rise gap between the glass towers to the west and the rail yards and port terminals along Burrard Inlet to the north, forty to fifty blocks of older brick and masonry. Landmarks: the container cranes at Centerm, the Sun Tower's copper dome near Pender and Beatty, the viaducts curving in from the east, and the Woodward's tower on West Hastings. Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 8 nm southwest; Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is a seaplane base 1 nm northwest in Coal Harbour. Downtown sits inside CYVR's control zone, with heavy floatplane and helicopter traffic below 2,000 feet.
