# Little Sister's Book and Art Emporium

A Davie Street bookshop survived two bombings and then spent seventeen years suing the Canadian government over the mail it was never allowed to receive — and rewrote Charter law doing it.

## Introduction

In December 1987 a bomb went off in the stairwell of a bookstore on Davie Street. Two months later a second device detonated in the restaurant below it. Nobody was seriously hurt either time, and Little Sister's Book and Art Emporium stayed exactly where it was. In the end the people who tried to close the shop with explosives proved far less effective than the Canadian government, which for years achieved something similar by simply opening the store's mail and keeping what was inside. That quieter campaign is the one that ended up in front of the Supreme Court of Canada.

## A Storefront in the West End

Jim Deva, Bruce Smyth and Barbara Thomas opened the shop in 1983 in Vancouver's West End, on the stretch of Davie Street that would become known as Davie Village. It sold books, and it sold a good deal else: Pride merchandise, sex toys, material that no chain retailer of the era would put on a shelf. The store catered deliberately to a gay and lesbian readership at a moment when few businesses in Canada admitted to having one, while remaining open to anybody who walked in. Janine Fuller ran it as manager for decades and became one of its public faces. For a certain generation of queer Vancouverites, the address at 1238 Davie was less a retailer than a civic institution — the place you could find the book nobody else would order for you.

## Everything Stopped at the Border

Most of what Little Sister's sold had to be imported, and that is where the trouble lay. Canada Customs held the power to bar "obscene" material at the frontier, and it exercised that power against the store's shipments with remarkable consistency. The seized titles dealt, almost without exception, with male–male or female–female sexuality. One consignment belonged to Kiss & Tell, a collective of Vancouver lesbian artists whose photographic series Drawing the Line examined queer desire; images travelling to the store by way of Deneuve magazine were stopped at the border, judged to depict bondage, and pronounced immoral. Glad Day Bookshop in Toronto was running into the same wall. The pattern was not subtle, and a trial court would eventually give it a name: systemic targeting of Little Sisters' importations in the Customs Mail Centre.

## Seventeen Years of Argument

The first ruling came in January 1996. The court accepted that the store's shipments had been wrongly delayed or withheld, but it declined to strike down the law itself: prohibiting the import of obscene material infringed section 2 of the Charter, the judge held, yet survived as a reasonable limit under section 1, and there was no breach of the equality guarantee in section 15. The British Columbia Court of Appeal agreed. Little Sister's pressed on to the Supreme Court of Canada, which ruled in 2000. That court, too, found the customs regime justified in principle and confirmed that the store had been unfairly singled out in practice. But it went further on one crucial point. The provisions of the Customs Act that required an importer to prove material was not obscene were unconstitutional. The burden belonged to the state. A bookseller now has a Charter right to import expressive material unless the government can prove it illegal.

## What the Fight Left Behind

The case escaped the courtroom and entered the culture. It was fictionalised as a subplot of the feature film Better Than Chocolate, documented by Aerlyn Weissman in Little Sister's vs. Big Brother in 2002, and rendered as comics in the anthology What Right?: Graphic Interpretations Against Censorship, whose contributors included Alison Bechdel and Marc Bell and whose proceeds went to the store's defence fund. Jim Deva died on 21 September 2014 at sixty-four; Bruce Smyth followed on 23 December 2019. In 2019 the BC and Yukon Book Prizes created the Jim Deva Prize for Writing that Provokes, funded by a donation from Smyth and given to challenging work by British Columbia writers. The shop itself is still trading on Davie Street, sold in 2026 to Parmjot Gill, a Vancouver entrepreneur who chairs Queer Business BC. During Pride Month that year Canada Post put Little Sister's on a stamp in its Places of Pride series, with portraits of Deva, Smyth and Fuller.

## Flight Context

Little Sister's sits at 49.2823° N, 123.1343° W, on Davie Street in Vancouver's West End — the dense peninsula of high-rise apartment towers between downtown and Stanley Park. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet the neighbourhood reads as a compact grid of closely spaced residential towers, bounded by the green mass of Stanley Park to the northwest, the curve of English Bay and Sunset Beach to the southwest, and False Creek with the Burrard Street Bridge to the southeast. Davie Street runs diagonally across the peninsula from Burrard Inlet to English Bay, one of the few streets in the grid that does. The Coast Mountains rise beyond the harbour to the north.
