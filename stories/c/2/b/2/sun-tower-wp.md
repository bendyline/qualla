# Sun Tower

Vancouver's 1912 newspaper tower spent a century under a steel dome painted to look like copper — until 2021, when it finally got the real thing.

**Location:** 49.2810, -123.1080

**Categories:** Newspaper headquarters in Canada, Commercial buildings completed in 1912, Heritage buildings in Vancouver, Beaux-Arts architecture in Canada, Skyscraper office buildings in Vancouver

## Introduction

On 31 October 1918 a crowd packed West Pender Street to watch a man in ordinary street clothes climb the outside of the tallest building in Vancouver using nothing but his hands. Harry Gardiner — the Human Fly, a name he was said to owe to a former American president — was doing it to sell Victory Loans, and by then he had scaled something like seven hundred buildings across Europe and North America without ropes or equipment of any kind. He worked ledges, projections and window sills, finding purchase in whatever the architecture offered. This building offered a great deal. The Sun Tower rises 82 metres above 128 West Pender Street, a Beaux-Arts stack of terracotta and rusticated brick under a domed hexagonal tower, and nine partly nude terracotta women hold its cornice up.

## A Newspaper Man's Tower

L. D. Taylor commissioned the building to house his newspaper, The Vancouver World, on a premise that was half publishing strategy and half swagger: make it the tallest thing in the city, and it would be visible across the paper's entire circulation area. John Coughland and Sons of Vancouver fabricated 1,250 tons of steel for the frame. When the World Building opened in 1912 it did indeed top everything else in town, including the Dominion Building just around the corner, whose record it had taken. The heritage plaque goes further and claims it was the tallest building in the British Empire. It was certainly the tallest in Canada, though only for a year — Toronto's Canadian Pacific Building went up in 1913 at 85 metres and took the title east.

## The Nine Maidens

William Tuff Whiteway, who also designed the original Woodward's store nearby, gave the tower an eight-storey L-shaped base topped by a nine-storey hexagonal shaft and finished with a Beaux-Arts dome and cupola. The detail is where it earns its reputation. Nine terracotta caryatids by the sculptor Charles Marega carry the cornice line, and their arrival caused a small scandal among respectable Vancouver at the opening: the figures are only partly clothed, bare-breasted, and were judged by some to be posed rather too sensuously for a downtown office block. All the terracotta, the maidens included, was made an ocean away in Tamworth, Staffordshire, by Gibbs and Canning Limited. The window sills, by contrast, are local — volcanic andesite quarried on Haddington Island, the same stone used in the carvings near the top, where animal skulls sit wreathed in garlands of fruit and flowers.

## The Dome That Was Pretending

For more than a century the tower's most recognisable feature was a lie, and a charming one. The dome looks like weathered copper — that particular soft blue-green of a roof gone to patina — but the dome is steel, and the colour was paint. Whether this was thrift or theatre depends on which account you believe. Restoration of the heritage building began in 2018, and in early February 2021 the scaffolding came off to reveal something new: a roof clad in real copper tiles, bright and penny-coloured, looking nothing at all like the dome Vancouver had always known. That is temporary. Copper oxidises, slowly and inevitably, and this roof will spend the coming decades turning into exactly the colour its predecessor spent a hundred years imitating.

## Newspaper, Movers, Newspaper, Geologists

The tenant list reads like a core sample of a downtown's twentieth century. The Vancouver World held it from 1912 to 1917. Bekins Moving and Storage occupied it from 1924 to 1937 — a moving company in the tallest building in the city, which is its own small comedy. Then The Vancouver Sun bought the place in 1937 and renamed it, and the name proved more durable than the tenancy: the paper left for South Granville and later Granville Square, but the Sun Tower is still the Sun Tower. From 1968 to 1996 the Geological Survey of Canada worked here, mapping the country's rock from a newspaper baron's folly. Software and law firms have followed since. The building changed hands in March 2008, when its assessed value stood at CA$6.16 million and the new owners promised to restore it — a promise the copper roof made good on thirteen years later.

## Playing the Watchtower

Television found the tower eventually. In The CW's Smallville it stands in for the Watchtower, the Justice League's operational headquarters, digitally reworked into a clock tower with six turret faces set into the dome and stretched taller so it can be the highest building in Metropolis. It also turns up in The Twilight Saga: Breaking Dawn – Part 2. In real life it is a good deal more modest and rather harder to find than it used to be, hemmed in by newer towers a block from Stadium–Chinatown SkyTrain station. Even its address has moved: it was 100 West Pender until 2011, when a new building filled the vacant lot at Pender and Abbott and the city's street-numbering bylaw quietly renumbered the old giant to 128.

## Flight Context

The Sun Tower stands at 49.2810°N, 123.1080°W, on the eastern edge of downtown Vancouver at West Pender and Beatty, overlooking Victory Square. At 82 metres it no longer dominates anything, so identify it by shape rather than height: an L-shaped masonry base under a narrow hexagonal shaft and a domed cupola, now capped in copper that will read bright orange-brown for some years yet before it greens. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet the surroundings orient you quickly — the white dome of BC Place and the dark bulk of Rogers Arena lie immediately south, Chinatown sits to the east, Burrard Inlet and the port are half a mile north, and the North Shore mountains close the view. Airspace is controlled and congested. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is less than a nautical mile northwest in Coal Harbour, with continuous floatplane movements; Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 6 nm southwest; Boundary Bay (CZBB) is roughly 13 nm south. Ceilings are frequently low from October through April, and the terracotta detail reads best in flat overcast light rather than hard sun.
