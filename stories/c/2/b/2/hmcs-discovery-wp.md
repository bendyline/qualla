# HMCS Discovery

A navy ship that has never left harbour, on an island the Squamish used as a burial ground, in the middle of Vancouver's Coal Harbour.

**Location:** 49.2952, -123.1228

**Categories:** military-history, maritime-history, indigenous-history, british-columbia, pacific-northwest

## Introduction

The Royal Canadian Navy keeps a ship in Vancouver that has never gone anywhere, because it is not a ship. HMCS Discovery is a stone frigate - a shore establishment commissioned and run as though it floated - and it occupies the entirety of a 3.8-hectare island in Coal Harbour, tucked against the eastern edge of Stanley Park. The navy has held the ground since 1944. The island itself has mattered to people for far longer than that. The Squamish called it skwtsa7s, which means, without embellishment, island. They laid their dead there in red cedar boxes lashed into the upper branches of the trees. Almost nothing about this place is what a visitor first assumes, beginning with the name on the map.

## skwtsa7s

Tree burial was the Squamish practice here: the dead raised into the canopy rather than put under the ground, in a cemetery that looked to settlers like an ordinary stand of cedar. The English name came from a different story. Chief Capilano is said to have told the early settler John Morton that the island was dead ground, the site of a long-ago battle in which some two hundred warriors died. Whether that account was history or warning, the settlers took the ominous half and called it Deadman's Island. What followed was harder still. Between 1888 and 1892 the island served as a quarantine station for smallpox victims during an epidemic. A logging dispute known as the Ludgate affair began in 1899, and by 1911 both the forest and the squatters who had settled among it were gone. The Musqueam First Nation includes the island in its land claim, and its long-term status remains unresolved.

## Eighty-Seven Yachtsmen

The unit's ancestry is oddly genteel. At the outbreak of the First World War, Number 2 (Vancouver) Company of the Royal Naval Canadian Volunteer Reserve was formed from 87 members of the Royal Vancouver Yacht Club - men who already knew boats and offered what they knew. They served, and at the armistice they were demobilized and the company dissolved. Nothing replaced it for six years. Then in May 1924 Lieutenant-Commander J.W. Hobbs received permission to raise the first Vancouver Half Company of the Naval Volunteer Reserve, and Vancouver had a naval reserve again. Cadets in the 1920s trained aboard the Casco, the schooner Robert Louis Stevenson had chartered for his South Seas voyaging - a literary ghost pressed into service teaching teenagers to hand, reef and steer.

## Commissioned 1 November 1941

War made the reserve essential, and on 1 November 1941 the Vancouver division was commissioned as HMCS Discovery, taking up quarters in the Stanley Park Barracks. In 1944 it was allowed onto Deadman Island and built its training establishment there. By any measure it was one of the most productive recruiting sources in the country: 372 officers, 6,974 ratings and 650 members of the Women's Royal Canadian Naval Service - the Wrens - passed through its enrolment. Sixty-one of them were killed in action. When the fighting stopped, Discovery became the discharge centre for the entire British Columbia mainland, and 8,378 men and women came back through the same gate they had walked in by, to be handed their papers and sent home.

## Disc Over Y

The establishment takes its name from HMS Discovery, the vessel George Vancouver used to chart most of the northwest coast of North America - which is to say the ship that produced the map this island appears on. Its badge is the work of Sir Arthur W. Cochran, Clarenceaux King of Arms, who proposed something a herald rarely gets to attempt: a rebus. The design shows a gold disc sitting above a shake-fork, a heraldic charge shaped like the letter Y. Disc over Y. It is a visual pun, sanctioned by the College of Arms and worn in earnest by people in uniform, and it may be the only naval badge in the Commonwealth that has to be read aloud to be understood.

## Still Standing Watch

Discovery has never become a museum piece, though it houses one - the Naval Museum of Vancouver shares the island, along with the Vancouver Naval Veterans Association, the Royal Canadian Sea Cadet Corps Captain Vancouver and the Navy League Cadet Corps Captain Rankin. Its reservists have deployed to Korea, the Persian Gulf, Egypt, Bosnia, Iraq, Japan, the Golan Heights and Afghanistan. It provided support during the 1997 APEC meeting in Vancouver and ran as the Olympic Maritime Operations Centre for the 2010 Winter Games, coordinating port security across the country. The Western Region Diving Centre works from here, directing the port inspection diving teams at naval reserve divisions throughout western Canada. And when foreign warships call at the Port of Vancouver, this is the establishment that receives them - a ship that cannot sail, playing host to ones that can.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.295N, 123.123W on Deadman Island in Coal Harbour, immediately southeast of Stanley Park and roughly a kilometre northwest of downtown Vancouver's waterfront. Best viewed at 1,000-2,000 ft AGL, or from the water; the island is small (3.8 hectares) and heavily treed, joined to the park shoreline by a short causeway, with white service buildings visible through the canopy. Use Stanley Park's dark green mass as the anchor: the island sits at its southeast corner, with the Coal Harbour marina basin and its dense rows of moored boats immediately east, the sails of Canada Place beyond, Brockton Point to the northeast and the Lions Gate Bridge spanning First Narrows about 1.5 nm northwest. Nearest airports: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CXH) is essentially adjacent, less than a nautical mile east across the harbour, with floatplane traffic on the water year-round; Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 7 nm south-southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm south-southeast and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 17 nm east. Expect busy harbour airspace, low ceilings and rain through the winter, and clear northwest visibility in settled summer weather.
