# Woodward's 43

Vancouver imploded a beloved department store and built, on the same block, an argument about who a city is for - with a neon W put back on top.

**Location:** 49.2829, -123.1081

**Categories:** architecture, urban-history, housing, british-columbia, pacific-northwest

## Introduction

The letter W burned over the Downtown Eastside for decades, spinning on a steel replica of the Eiffel Tower bolted to a department store roof. When Woodward's failed and the store went dark, the W stayed - a lit sign over an empty building in a neighbourhood that had lost more than a shop. It came down before the implosion in 2006. On 9 January 2010 a replica went back up, LED-lit now, over a 122.3-metre tower containing social housing, market condominiums, an art school, a grocery store, a daycare and an unresolved argument about whose neighbourhood this is.

## Food Floors and $1.49 Day

Charles Woodward opened his first store at Main and Georgia in 1892. Woodward Department Stores Ltd. was incorporated on 12 September 1902 and built a new store at Hastings and Abbott, which grew by accretion over the following decades into a city institution. Its Food Floors were enormous, better stocked than any competitor's, with conveyor systems to move parcels to waiting customers. Its $1.49 Day - the first Tuesday of each month, one price, everything from toys to hardware - arrived in April 1958 with a jingle that lodged permanently in the memory of British Columbia. Then came a decade of failing to keep pace with modern retail. Woodward's filed for bankruptcy protection on 11 December 1992, and in June 1993 the Hudson's Bay Company bought 21 of its 25 locations for $235 million. The Hastings Street store was not among the survivors.

## The Occupation

The building stood empty for roughly a decade, an enormous vacancy in the middle of one of the poorest neighbourhoods in the country, while the province that owned it and the city that surrounded it argued about what should happen. What broke the stalemate was not a proposal but an occupation: in 2002, housing activists and homeless residents moved into the derelict store and would not leave quietly. The squat put the question on the front page. In 2003 the City of Vancouver, pushed by councillor Jim Green, bought the building from the province for $5 million and did something developers rarely do first - it asked. A public consultation process went to the neighbourhood and asked what the neighbourhood wanted from the site.

## Three Developers and an Implosion

A two-stage competition among three developers concluded in September 2004 with the selection of Westbank Projects and Peterson Investment Group, working with Henriquez Partners Architects under Gregory Henriquez and structural engineers Glotman Simpson. The development permit was issued on 26 January 2007. In 2006 most of the old store came down - the implosion was filmed, watched and mourned - though the oldest portion, built between 1903 and 1908, was kept and restored as 31,500 square feet of non-profit office and community space. Substantial completion was scheduled for June 2010 and slipped to September. The finished project ran to nearly a million square feet and roughly $400 million.

## Everything on One Block

What went up is unusual less for its height than for its mixture. The complex holds 536 market housing units, 125 non-market units for single residents operated by PHS Community Services, and 75 non-market family units run by the Affordable Housing Society. It also holds a 130,000-square-foot downtown campus for Simon Fraser University's School for Contemporary Arts, the National Film Board of Canada, London Drugs, Nesters Market, a bank, civic offices, a daycare, a covered atrium and a public plaza. The tower borrows deliberately from its surroundings: its cornice nods to Daniel Burnham's 1902 Flatiron Building in New York, its form answers the neighbouring Dominion Building, and its exterior steel screens recall the way Vancouver built when the original store was rising. There is a small pool and a garden on the roof, which is about as far from the Food Floor as a block can travel in a century.

## Abbott and Cordova

Hung in the atrium is a photograph on glass, thirty feet by fifty, made by the Vancouver artist Stan Douglas in 2008. It reconstructs the Gastown riot of 1971, when police broke up a demonstration a block from here. A basketball hoop shares the space with it. Nothing about the pairing is accidental, and nothing about the project has settled the question it was built to answer. Long-time Downtown Eastside residents resisted the redevelopment throughout, arguing that a tower of market condominiums in the poorest part of the city would push out the people already there. Some were pushed out - among them sex workers who had worked these blocks for years, and who mostly moved a few streets over rather than away. The original neon W now sits indoors at ground level, retired, while its replica turns overhead. Both versions are honest about what they are.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.283N, 123.108W, at Hastings and Abbott in Vancouver's Downtown Eastside, at the eastern edge of Gastown and near sea level. Best viewed at 1,500-3,000 ft AGL. The tower rises 122.3 m (401 ft) over 37 floors, standing clear of the shorter historic blocks around it on the downtown peninsula's east side; look for the illuminated W at its crown and the distinctive flatiron wedge of the Dominion Building immediately adjacent. Victory Square is a block southwest, the Harbour Centre lookout tower and the sails of Canada Place lie to the northwest along the waterfront, and the Port of Vancouver's container terminals line the inlet to the north. Nearest airports: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CXH) less than 1 nm northwest on the Coal Harbour waterfront, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 6 nm south-southwest, and Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm south. Downtown Vancouver sits under busy harbour and approach traffic; expect low ceilings and rain from late autumn through early spring.
