# Playland (Vancouver)

Vancouver's amusement park runs a 1958 wooden roller coaster that was its designer's last work, on grounds that held Japanese Canadian families in livestock barns sixteen years earlier.

**Location:** 49.2827, -123.0370

**Categories:** Amusement parks in British Columbia, Hastings Park, Culture of Vancouver, History of Vancouver, Roller coasters

## Introduction

The lift chain hauls you seventy-five feet up, and then the track drops sixty-seven of them and the train hits seventy-two kilometres an hour on timber that was first bolted together in 1958. It cost two hundred thousand dollars to build. Carl Phare drew it with Walker LeRoy, and it was the last roller coaster Phare ever designed; of everything he built across a long career, this is the only one still standing anywhere. Riders in Vancouver have been screaming on it for more than six decades, which is long enough that the ride has outlived the amusement park it was built to anchor, twice over.

## Happyland, Then Playland

The ground is older than the rides. In 1888 the province granted this land in trust to the city of Vancouver, to be kept in perpetuity as public parkland. Horses began racing here in 1892. The Pacific National Exhibition, the late-summer fair that still defines the calendar for a good part of the city, was first held in 1910. An amusement park called Happyland opened on the site in 1929 and ran until 1957, when it was pulled down to make room for the Pacific Coliseum. Playland replaced it in 1958, next door to the fairgrounds but not quite of them; it did not formally become a division of the Pacific National Exhibition until January 1993. The city still owns the land underneath. Very little in Hastings Park has ever belonged entirely to one purpose.

## The Barns

Between Happyland and Playland, in 1942, the park was put to a use nobody advertises on the midway. After Canada declared war on Japan, Hastings Park became the place where Japanese Canadians from up and down the coast were gathered before being moved inland. Men were routinely separated from their families and sent on to road camps and labour projects. Women and children were housed in the livestock buildings, in stalls built for animals, without privacy and without adequate sanitation, waiting for transport to the interior or to prairie farms. More than 22,000 Japanese Canadians, over ninety percent of the country's Japanese Canadian population, were interned between 1942 and 1949. Most were Canadian citizens. On September 22, 1988, the federal government apologized and paid redress of $21,000 to each surviving internee. The livestock barns are still standing, a short walk from the roller coaster.

## Phare's Last Design

What Phare and LeRoy left behind is 2,840 feet of track, a twister layout with no inversions, a chain lift and an honest drop, running two trains of eight cars with riders sitting two abreast, sixteen at a time. When it opened it was the largest roller coaster in Canada. It has no loops, no launch system, no harness that pins you to the seat, and it does not need any of those things, because a wooden structure moves under a train in a way that steel does not, and the whole ride feels perpetually one degree less controlled than it is. American Coaster Enthusiasts gave it Classic status and then, in 2009, named it a Roller Coaster Landmark. It has appeared in the industry's annual rankings of the world's best wooden coasters for nearly thirty years running.

## Forty Rides and a Ghost

The park today runs roughly forty attractions from May through September, then reopens in October for Fright Nights. Four of them are roller coasters: the 1958 wooden one, the Kettle Creek Mine Coaster from 2004, the family-scaled Bug Whirled from 2017, and ThunderVolt, a nine-million-dollar launched coaster from Zamperla that opened in 2024. Atmosfear swings riders from sixty-six metres, and Hellevator drops them sixty. The Beast, a pendulum ride, arrived in 2015. Some old favourites are gone: the Corkscrew came out in 2018 after a long second career as a film prop, most famously standing in as Devil's Flight in the opening of Final Destination 3, and the Revelation followed in 2022. Between 2010 and 2018 the park drew an average of about 378,000 people a year, roughly 300,000 in the summer season and 78,000 in the dark.

## Flight Context

Playland occupies the northeast corner of Hastings Park at roughly 49.283 N, 123.037 W, in east Vancouver about half a kilometre inland from Burrard Inlet. Overfly at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL and the complex is one of the easiest targets in the city: look for the pale oval of Hastings Racecourse, the round saucer roof of the Pacific Coliseum beside it, and the dense tangle of ride structures and midway immediately north and east of them. The white lattice of the wooden roller coaster reads clearly from above as a rectangular wooden box of track, visually distinct from the thin steel of everything around it. The park sits directly under the approach corridor between the inlet and the residential grid of east Vancouver, with the container terminals and grain elevators of the port along the shoreline just to the north and the North Shore Mountains rising immediately across the water. This is class C airspace beneath the Vancouver terminal control area with substantial floatplane and helicopter traffic on the inlet; clearance and current charts are required. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is about 4 nm west, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 11 nm southwest, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 11 nm east, and Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 15 nm south. August and September afternoons give the clearest air and the busiest park; winter brings weeks of low overcast.
