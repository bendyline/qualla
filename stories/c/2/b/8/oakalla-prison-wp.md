# Oakalla Prison

For seventy-nine years a hillside above Deer Lake in Burnaby held a working dairy farm, a women's prison, British Columbia's only gallows, and an experiment in reforming men by changing their faces.

**Location:** 49.2321, -122.9855

**Categories:** Prisons, British Columbia, Burnaby, Social history, Defunct institutions, Capital punishment history

## Introduction

It had a dairy herd and a gallows. The Oakalla Prison Farm spread across 185 acres of hillside above Deer Lake in Burnaby, and when it opened in 1912 the plan was for a model institution holding 150 men and women who would spend their sentences doing agricultural work in the open air. Both halves of that idea were sincere. Inmates did milk cows and grow vegetables and tend livestock that fed the place. They also, for four decades, lived a short walk from the only room in British Columbia where the province carried out its death sentences. Oakalla is now a subdivision and a park, and almost nothing above ground records either function.

## A Model That Kept Growing

The prison-farm concept had a logic to it that made sense to reformers of the early twentieth century: keep people occupied, keep them outdoors, let productive labour do the correcting. What the design did not anticipate was scale. A facility built for 150 held well over a thousand by the 1950s, and the farm that was meant to be the point of the institution became a sideline to the business of simply housing people. The dairy kept running, the gardens kept producing, the hillside above the lake kept its pastoral appearance from a distance. Inside, Oakalla had become the Lower Mainland's main remand and short-sentence institution, and would eventually be renamed to say so: the Lower Mainland Regional Correctional Centre.

## The Women's Side

Women were held at Oakalla almost from the beginning, in a section that officially opened in 1916. In 1942 they were moved into a separate facility of their own on the grounds, a rare thing in Canada at the time, and in 1953 that unit was expanded into two cottage-style buildings deliberately designed to look and feel less like a prison. In 1979 the women's institution got a name that acknowledged its setting rather than its function: the Lakeside Correctional Centre for Women. The architecture was gentler than the main cell blocks. The sentences were not.

## The Only Place in the Province

From 1919 onward, every judicial execution in British Columbia took place at Oakalla. Forty-four people were hanged there between that year and 1959. In 1931, Shinkichi Sakurada and Tadao Hitomi were the first to die on the prison's rebuilt gallows, condemned for the murder of a Vancouver fisherman. The last was Leo Mantha, a thirty-three-year-old former sailor, executed in 1959. Nobody knew at the time that his would be the final execution in the province's history, but it was; Canada would formally abolish capital punishment in the decades that followed, and the room at Oakalla never saw another. A prison that raised its own milk also kept the machinery of the death penalty in working order, and the two facts sat on the same hillside for forty years without anyone finding the arrangement remarkable.

## Faces and Recidivism

In the late 1950s Oakalla became a site for one of the more unusual criminological experiments of the era. Dr. Edward Lewison, a plastic surgeon, operated on inmates to correct what the language of the day called deformities, working from the theory that a visibly disfigured man faced a hostile world that pushed him back toward crime. Around 450 men volunteered, and the programme ran into the mid-1960s. The numbers looked striking: 42 percent of those who had surgery reoffended, against 72 percent of the general prison population, and similar programmes were launched across Canada and the United States on the strength of that comparison. Lewison himself later punctured it. He had no control group of disfigured inmates who went without surgery, he acknowledged, and the way participants had been selected may itself have skewed everything. Men who volunteer for elective surgery may simply be men already inclined to change.

## Riot, Escape, Subdivision

The farm closed first, in 1979, its work programme obsolete and its land more valuable as open space; 64 acres went to the City of Burnaby and were folded into Deer Lake Park. The institution itself limped on through a deteriorating final decade, punctuated by a riot in 1983 and a mass escape in 1987 that made the case for closure impossible to argue with. Oakalla shut in 1991. Its population was dispersed across newer facilities around the province, among them the Surrey and Vancouver pretrial centres, Fraser Regional, and Alouette. The hillside was subdivided for housing, and the rest went to enlarge the park. People walk dogs there now, and jog past ground where forty-four people were executed, on a lakeshore that has recovered its scenery completely.

## Flight Context

The Oakalla site lies at 49.2321 N, 122.9855 W on the south-facing slope above Deer Lake in Burnaby, British Columbia, roughly 6 nm east-southeast of downtown Vancouver. Best viewed from 2,000 to 4,000 feet. Deer Lake itself is the anchor: a small dark oval of water ringed by green, with the Burnaby Village Museum and the Shadbolt Centre on its north shore and the former prison ground rising to the south, now residential streets running into parkland. The high-rise cluster of Metrotown stands about 2 km southwest and is impossible to miss; Burnaby Mountain and the Simon Fraser University campus occupy the ridge 6 km north. Nearby airports: Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) about 6 nm northwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) roughly 9 nm southwest, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 10 nm northeast, and Vancouver International (CYVR) some 10 nm west. This sits within Vancouver terminal airspace, so expect arrival traffic overhead. Winter brings persistent low overcast; late summer afternoons give the clearest look at the lake and the street pattern laid over the old institutional grounds.
