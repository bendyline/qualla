# Burnaby Village Museum

A 1920s village rebuilt on ten acres beside Deer Lake, anchored by a 1912 carousel that Vancouver saved from being auctioned off one horse at a time.

**Location:** 49.2391, -122.9660

**Categories:** Museums in British Columbia, Open-air museums in British Columbia, History of Burnaby, Tourist attractions in Burnaby, Relocated buildings and structures in Canada

## Introduction

In 1989 the carousel was going to be broken up. Antique Parker horses fetch serious money individually, and the machine that had spun children at Happyland and then Playland since the Depression was headed for auction, one animal at a time. A group calling itself the Friends of the Vancouver Carousel Society raised $350,000 and bought the whole thing instead. It is still whole and still turning - forty-one hand-carved horses under a pavilion at the entrance to the Burnaby Village Museum, older than nearly every building standing around it.

## The Long Way to Deer Lake

Charles Wallace Parker built the carousel in Leavenworth, Kansas, in 1912 - the 119th machine his company produced, which is how it got its name. It sold in 1913 for $5,886 and spent two years touring Texas with the Lone Star Circus before being shipped back to the factory. Where it went between 1915 and 1936 nobody knows. Then it surfaced at Vancouver's Happyland amusement park, ran there until 1957, moved to Playland, and turned outdoors in the rain until 1972. After the rescue, a new pavilion went up in 1993 and donors sponsored individual horses, many of which now carry their names. A carousel that crossed a continent, disappeared for two decades, and nearly ended in pieces is an unusually good fit for a museum built out of salvaged buildings.

## A Village Assembled from Real Parts

The museum opened on 19 November 1971 as Heritage Village, a British Columbia centennial project, with Governor General Roland Michener doing the honours and more than 15,000 people coming through in three days. It started on 4.3 acres. It now covers ten, with 31 full-scale buildings arranged as a 1920s village and costumed staff working the trades. The distinction that matters here is which is which: some structures are genuine heritage buildings lifted from elsewhere in Burnaby and restored on site, while others are careful replicas built to house collections. Elworth, the 1922 Bateman family house, never moved at all - the museum grew around it.

## The Tram That Came Back

Interurban 1223 was built in 1912 for the British Columbia Electric Railway, the network that once knitted the Fraser Valley together by rail. By the end of the twentieth century the car sat at the Edmonds Bus Loop, exposed and repeatedly vandalized. In 2001 it went into a warehouse for a five-year restoration by the Friends of Interurban 1223, and in 2007 it came back to a purpose-built tram barn at the museum. Vorce Station, an original 1911 stop from the Burnaby Lake interurban line, was restored beside it in 2008. Nearby stands Old Curly, the oldest surviving steam locomotive in British Columbia, which hauled material during construction of the Canadian Pacific Railway in the 1880s.

## Whose Village It Was

A reconstructed village can tell a narrow story if nobody watches it, and this one has widened over time. The Jesse Love Farmhouse of 1893 is among the oldest buildings surviving in Burnaby. Seaforth School opened in 1922 with twenty pupils. Bell's Dry Goods, built around 1922, housed Flora and William Bell's business until 1937. The Way Sang Yuen Wat Kee herbalist's shop is furnished with the actual contents of a store that operated in Victoria from about 1900 until 1971. A replica Japanese ofuro, a bathhouse, was built in 1977 to mark the centenary of the first Japanese immigrant's arrival in British Columbia. An Indigenous Learning House has since been added to the grounds.

## Free at the Gate

Admission costs nothing. That is unusual for a site of this size, and it changes who comes: the museum functions less as an attraction than as a neighbourhood park with a village in it, which is roughly what a 1920s village would have been. The season runs from May to September, with special events filling the darker months from September through March, the Christmas programme among them. Temporary exhibits rotate through the Stride Studios gallery; the current one, delecTABLE: Burnaby Food Journeys, traces how the city has eaten. The carousel still charges a small fee, which feels fair. Someone had to pay $350,000 for it.

## Flight Context

Burnaby Village Museum sits at the southwest corner of Deer Lake Park, 49.2391 N, 122.9660 W, about 7 nm east of downtown Vancouver. View it from 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL. Deer Lake itself is the landmark - a small dark oval in the middle of Burnaby's suburban grid, with the museum's cluster of small roofs and the carousel pavilion on its southwest shore, the Shadbolt Centre for the Arts adjacent. Burnaby Mountain and Simon Fraser University rise 4 nm north; Metrotown's tower cluster is 1.5 nm south. Vancouver International (CYVR) is roughly 10 nm west and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 12 nm east; Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is 7 nm northwest. The whole area lies beneath CYVR's terminal airspace, and low stratus commonly covers the Burrard lowland on winter mornings while Burnaby Mountain stays in sun.
