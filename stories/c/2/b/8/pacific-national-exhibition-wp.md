# Pacific National Exhibition

Vancouver's fair has run at Hastings Park since 1910 - through the year its livestock barns were used to hold Japanese Canadian families, and on into a century of building and unbuilding.

**Location:** 49.2828, -123.0366

**Categories:** festivals, urban-history, amusement-parks, british-columbia, pacific-northwest

## Introduction

Sir Wilfrid Laurier opened it. In August 1910 the sitting Prime Minister of Canada travelled to a muddy park on the eastern edge of Vancouver and declared the city's first exhibition open, three years after a group of local boosters had formed an association to argue for it and two years after they had bothered to incorporate. The city council had been unenthusiastic. The jockey club, which had been racing horses on that ground since 1892, was openly hostile. They lost. What they lost to has now run for more than a century at Hastings Park, and the fair that Laurier opened is only the most cheerful part of what has happened on those 105 acres.

## Boosters and a Jockey Club

The Vancouver Exhibition Association formed in 1907 and incorporated on 18 June 1908, and its case was the standard case of the era: a growing city needed a fair, and a fair needed land. Hastings Park had been granted in trust to the city by the province in 1888, and by the time the association came asking, a racetrack was already established there. Council hesitated. The horsemen objected. The exhibition went ahead anyway, and over the following decades it accumulated buildings at a startling rate - a grandstand, a forestry hall, a manufacturers' building, a poultry and pigeon building, an aquarium, a miniature railway, a streetcar station, a ferryboat wharf. The organisation took the name Pacific National Exhibition on 7 February 1946.

## The Barns, 1942

The reason the name change came in 1946 and the fair did not reopen until 1947 is that the exhibition had stopped four years earlier. Military use had been creeping across the grounds since the war began, but the fair itself was not cancelled until 1942, after Canada declared war on Japan. That same year the livestock barns at Hastings Park were pressed into a different service. Japanese Canadians brought in from communities all over British Columbia - families, elderly people, children, the great majority of them Canadian citizens - were held in those buildings, spaces built for animals and hastily fitted with bunks, while the government processed them for removal to internment camps in the interior. They lost homes, boats, farms and businesses in the same stroke. The Momiji Commemorative Garden now sits on the grounds as a memorial to them, a quiet piece of ground in a place otherwise devoted to noise.

## Concrete, Wood and a Saucer

The postwar decades filled the site with permanent structures. Empire Stadium went up for the British Empire Games that Vancouver hosted in 1954. The Pacific Coliseum and the PNE Agrodome followed, the Coliseum's broad saucer roof becoming the most recognisable object on the skyline east of the port. Playland, the amusement park that had grown up beside the fair, became a division of the PNE in 1993; its Wooden Roller Coaster, a white timber lattice that riders can hear working from the far side of the park, is now the oldest thing at Hastings Park that still moves. In August 2013 the City of Vancouver named both the Coliseum and the coaster heritage sites - one an arena, the other a ride, both judged worth keeping.

## Unbuilding

Just as striking as what was built is what has been removed. During 1997 and 1998 the Food Building, the Showmart and the Poultry Building all came down, and in their place went the Sanctuary - parkland with a pond that restored part of a stream which had once run out of the park to Burrard Inlet. The demolished list runs to dozens of names: Shoot the Chutes, Dip the Dips, the Giant Dipper, the vaudeville stage, the press bureau, the sheep stables. An Italian garden occupies ground where the Pure Foods Building and Showmart once stood. For a while it looked as though the whole enterprise would move - land was actually purchased in Surrey to become the fair's new home - but ownership passed instead from the province to the City of Vancouver, and the PNE stayed put. It operates as a registered charity, on roughly 42 hectares of a 62-hectare park.

## Fire, Riot, Timber

The last few years have been eventful even by the standards of a place that has been running since 1910. The fair went dark in 2020 with the pandemic, alongside the Calgary Stampede and every other major fair in the country, and came back in 2021 at reduced capacity. In the early hours of 20 February 2022, fire tore through part of the grounds, destroying vehicles, tools, equipment and buildings. That September, on the second night of a hip-hop festival at the PNE Amphitheatre, the crowd turned after an announcement that the headliner would not perform; fighting and vandalism followed, police were called in force, seven people were arrested that night and fifteen more were charged on 19 September 2023 after investigators worked through video the attendees themselves had posted. Damage ran to millions of dollars, but nobody was seriously hurt. A winter fair was added in December 2022. And in 2026 the Freedom Mobile Arch opened on the grounds - an amphitheatre described as the largest free-span timber frame in the world, which is a very Vancouver way to answer a bad year.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.283N, 123.037W at Hastings Park in the Hastings-Sunrise neighbourhood of East Vancouver, roughly 5 km east of downtown and a few hundred metres south of Burrard Inlet, near sea level. Best viewed at 1,500-3,000 ft AGL. The site is unmistakable from the air: the broad circular saucer roof of the Pacific Coliseum, the white timber lattice of the Wooden Roller Coaster and the bright rides of Playland, the green oval of Hastings Racecourse, Empire Field, and the pond and trees of the Sanctuary, all packed into a single block bounded by East Hastings Street, Renfrew Street and the port lands. The Ironworkers Memorial (Second Narrows) Bridge crosses the inlet about 2 nm north-northwest, with the North Shore mountains rising immediately beyond it. Nearest airports: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CXH) about 3 nm west, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 8 nm southwest, Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 13 nm south, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 13 nm east. Harbour traffic is heavy; expect low ceilings and rain from November into March and clear, smooth air on summer mornings.
