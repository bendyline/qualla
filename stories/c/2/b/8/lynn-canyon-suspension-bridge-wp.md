# Lynn Canyon Suspension Bridge

North Vancouver's other suspension bridge hangs fifty metres over Lynn Creek, sways more than the famous one down the road, and has never charged more than a dime.

**Location:** 49.3437, -123.0180

**Categories:** 1912 establishments in British Columbia, Bridges completed in 1912, Bridges in Greater Vancouver, Former toll bridges in Canada, North Vancouver (district municipality), Pedestrian bridges in British Columbia, Suspension bridges in Canada, Tourism in British Columbia, Parks in British Columbia

## Introduction

The bridge moves. That is the point of it, and a large part of why people who actually live on the North Shore prefer it to the celebrated one down the road. Lynn Canyon's span is shorter than Capilano's and hangs lower, but it is narrower, and it works on you — step onto the deck behind a group of teenagers and the whole thing sets up a slow lateral sway that does not settle until the last of them is off. Fifty metres below, Lynn Creek grinds through a slot in the bedrock, dark green and extremely loud. Walking across costs nothing, and never has cost more than a dime.

## Vogel's Span

The bridge was a private venture, put up by people who expected to make money from it. The civil engineer C.H. Vogel designed it, and it was finished in 1911 — a year before the park around it formally existed. Both were opened together on 14 September 1912, at the first Lynn Valley Days.

The business model was modest even by 1912 standards: ten cents to cross. That was later cut to five. When the bridge passed into public hands the toll disappeared entirely, and it has been free ever since — a distinction that has come to define the place far more than any engineering detail. There are not many world-class scenic attractions in a major North American city where the admission price is zero and nobody is trying to sell you a photograph at the far end.

## Twelve Acres to Six Hundred

The park assembled itself piece by piece. In 1910 the McTavish brothers gave five hectares of canyon land to the District of North Vancouver. The District added four more. Walter Draycott bought three lots in 1912, the year the gates opened on a park of twelve acres.

It has grown by a factor of fifty since — 617 acres, roughly 250 hectares of second-growth rainforest, creek and rock, with the suspension bridge as its front door and a network of trails, including a stretch of the Baden-Powell Trail, running out behind it. In 1971 the Ecology Centre opened near the bridgehead in a building designed to resemble a Pacific dogwood blossom, British Columbia's floral emblem, which is the kind of architectural gesture that could only have happened in exactly that decade.

## The Canyon Has a Temper

Lynn Creek is not a gentle watercourse. It has cut a narrow, steep-walled gorge with smooth rock chutes and deep green pools — the 30 Foot Pool is the best known — and on hot summer days the temptation to jump into them from the rocks above is considerable.

The canyon has taken a serious toll for it. Signage in the park has recorded 32 deaths between 1985 and 2016, and roughly twenty in the last twenty-five years, most in cliff-jumping incidents, alongside many more injuries. The water is colder than it looks, the pools are shallower in places than they appear from above, and the current beneath the surface is stronger than anyone expects. Almost all of those lost here were young. It is the one thing worth knowing before you come, and the park does not hide it.

## Free Versus Famous

The comparison with the Capilano Suspension Bridge is unavoidable, because Capilano advertises everywhere and Lynn Canyon does not advertise at all. Capilano is longer and higher. Lynn Canyon is narrower and livelier underfoot, and the canyon it crosses is arguably the more dramatic piece of geology.

What the two share is a visitor pattern. The bridge connects an extensive trail system on both sides of the gorge, yet a great many people who come here never set foot on those trails — they walk out, take the photograph, feel the deck move, walk back, and leave. The hikers head up the canyon into the second-growth. Everyone else stays within a hundred metres of the parking lot, which is its own kind of tradition.

## Syke's Bridge, and Auradon

Television found the canyon early and keeps coming back. The bridge appeared in an episode of MacGyver, "The Invisible Killer." It turns up again in Disney's Descendants as part of Auradon, with the 30 Foot Pool cast as the Enchanted Lake.

The strangest credit belongs to Beyond Belief: Fact or Fiction, which in a 2002 episode renamed it "Syke's Bridge," placed it on a fictional "Misty Loop Trail," relocated the whole thing to 1960s Texas, told the audience the story was true — and then had the bridge collapse. A hundred-and-some-year-old span that has never given way, playing a bridge that falls, in a state most of a continent away. The real one is still there, still free, still swaying.

## Flight Context

The bridge hangs at 49.344°N, 123.018°W, roughly 3 nm up Lynn Valley from the Burrard Inlet waterfront in the District of North Vancouver. This is mountain flying, not sightseeing: the North Shore wall rises fast behind the canyon toward Grouse Mountain and Mount Seymour, and the valleys generate mechanical turbulence and downdrafts that punish anyone flying low and slow up a drainage. Stay at or above 3,000 ft and look down the cut rather than into it - the canyon reads as a dark seam in unbroken second-growth forest, with the Ecology Centre roof and the parking area the only clear human marks. Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) lies about 5 nm southwest, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 12 nm southwest, and Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 11 nm east; the Second Narrows and Lions Gate crossings anchor the inlet below. Cloud frequently sits on the North Shore slopes when downtown is clear, so what looks like a VFR day from the harbour may not be one three miles inland.
