# Lynn Valley Tree

The tallest Douglas-fir in the credible record — 126.5 metres — was felled on a North Vancouver farm property in 1902, and no photograph of it is known to survive.

**Location:** 49.3407, -123.0386

**Categories:** Individual Douglas firs, Individual trees in British Columbia, Forestry in British Columbia, North Vancouver

## Introduction

No photograph of it is known to exist. That is the strange part. There are pictures of loggers all over British Columbia posed grinning in the undercuts of trees far smaller than this one — but when the Lynn Valley Tree came down in 1902, on a farm property on Vancouver's North Shore, nobody brought a camera. What survives is a number and a conversation. The number is 126.5 metres, four hundred and fifteen feet. The conversation happened ten years later, when the man who had owned the land sat down with a local historian and told him what he had measured on the ground.

## The Column on Argyle Road

Alfred John Nye owned the property. What first caught his attention, he told the historian Walter Mackay Draycott in 1912, was the bole — the massive, unbranched column of the trunk — and the way the tree stood clear above the canopy of the forest around it. That is worth pausing on. The forest around it was itself coastal old growth, a stand of Douglas-fir that would today be a national treasure in its own right. This tree looked down on it.

In 1902 the Tremblay Brothers felled it at Argyle Road in Lynn Valley. No account of the cutting survives in any detail. Two men with saws and axes spent part of a day on a job, and the tallest Douglas-fir in the credible record went over into the brush.

## What Nye Measured

With the tree on the ground, Nye ran a tape along it: 125 metres, 410 feet, lying in the slash. The stump it came off was 1.52 metres high — five feet — and at that cut the trunk measured 4.34 metres across the butt. Fourteen and a quarter feet, through the middle, of solid wood.

The detail that lands hardest is the bark. Thirty-four centimetres thick — 13.5 inches. You could not get a hand around to the wood. Old Douglas-firs grow bark like that as armour against fire, layer over layer, so that a ground fire sweeping the understory scorches the outside and leaves the tree standing. It is a defence built over centuries, and it is no defence at all against a crosscut saw.

## And the Rest of the Valley

The Lynn Valley Tree was not an isolated giant. It was the tallest member of a forest, and that forest went the same way — the entire old-growth stand in the lower valley was logged.

Among the trees that came down with it: a fir 4.24 metres in diameter whose stump carried 1,280 growth rings, meaning it had started life around the seventh century and was already ancient when Europeans reached the coast. Another fir felled in the same valley was said to have measured 107.3 metres. Any one of these, standing today, would draw visitors from around the world. All of them were cut within a few decades by people who were not doing anything unusual by the standards of their time, and who left almost no record of what they had removed.

## The Argument About Ceilings

There is a reason the 126.5-metre figure attracts scepticism. Water has to reach the top of a tree through its plumbing, and plant physiologists have long argued that the column can only be pulled so high before it fails. One Oregon State University study framed the limit for Douglas-fir bluntly: a 350-foot-long drinking straw is as long as it gets — roughly 107 metres, well below what Nye taped.

Yet felled-tree measurements keep exceeding the ceiling. The Nooksack Giant, cut in the nineteenth century just south of the border, was taped at 142 metres. A giant sequoia called the Father of the Forest, from the Calaveras grove, reportedly measured 133 metres after it fell centuries ago. Historical reports of firs between 110 and 120 metres are not rare. One proposed explanation is that in reliably foggy forests a tree can take water in through its crown, reversing part of the internal flow and supplying the upper reaches that the roots cannot. Nobody can settle it now. The specimens are gone.

## What the Number Means Today

The tallest living tree on Earth is Hyperion, a coast redwood in northern California, at 116.22 metres. The tallest Douglas-fir standing anywhere today is 99.8 metres, in Oregon.

So if Nye's tape was honest, the tree cut on his property in 1902 stood more than ten metres above anything alive on the planet, and nearly twenty-seven metres above the best surviving example of its own species. Lynn Valley is now a settled part of North Vancouver — houses, a shopping centre, second-growth forest on the slopes above. There is no stump to visit, no photograph to look at, no plaque at the spot. There is only a measurement written down by a historian in 1912, and the uncomfortable business of deciding whether to believe it.

## Flight Context

The Lynn Valley Tree stood near 49.3407°N, 123.0386°W, in the lower Lynn Valley on the North Shore of Vancouver, British Columbia — now a residential district of the District of North Vancouver. From 2,500–4,500 ft AGL the setting reads clearly even though the tree is long gone: Lynn Creek runs south out of a steep, forested canyon in the Coast Mountains and empties into Burrard Inlet, and the valley floor below the canyon mouth is the suburban ground where the old-growth stand once was. Dark second-growth timber climbs the slopes toward Lynn Headwaters Regional Park and Mount Seymour to the northeast; Grouse Mountain and the Capilano watershed lie west. Use the Second Narrows (Ironworkers Memorial) Bridge and Highway 1 along the inlet as your southern reference. Nearby fields: CXH (Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre water aerodrome) about 3 nm southwest, CYVR (Vancouver International) 11 nm southwest, CYPK (Pitt Meadows) east, CZBB (Boundary Bay) southeast. The North Shore mountains generate orographic cloud constantly — the valley mouth is often clear while the canyon above it is solid — and this is high-terrain airspace close to a major city; check airspace and NOTAMs.
