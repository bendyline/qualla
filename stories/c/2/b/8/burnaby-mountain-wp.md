# Burnaby Mountain

A 370-metre forested ridge above Burrard Inlet carrying a Brutalist university, a conservation area, black bears, and the pipeline that made it a place people were arrested for standing on.

**Location:** 49.2792, -122.9090

**Categories:** Mountains of the Lower Mainland, Tourist attractions in Burnaby, Parks in British Columbia, Simon Fraser University, Energy politics

## Introduction

In November 2014, more than a hundred people were arrested in a municipal park. The park was on Burnaby Mountain, a modest forested ridge in suburban Vancouver where a pipeline company wanted to run survey work, and the people camped in the trees to stop it included members of the Squamish and Tsleil-Waututh Nations, students from the university at the top of the hill, and residents of the neighbourhoods at the bottom. Burnaby Mountain is 370 metres high. It is not the sort of terrain that usually generates news. It has, nevertheless, managed to be a university campus, a wildlife corridor, a bike park, a memorial to a Japanese sister city, and one of the sharpest arguments in Canadian energy politics - all at once.

## An Instant University

Construction started on the ridge in the spring of 1964; eighteen months later, on September 9, 1965, Simon Fraser University opened its first semester with 2,500 students. Arthur Erickson and Geoffrey Massey had won the design competition, and what they built on the summit is Brutalist in the full sense - long concrete horizontals, covered walkways, an academic quadrangle laid out like a single continuous structure rather than a collection of buildings. Film crews love it; the campus has stood in for space stations, government complexes, and futures of various kinds. Around it now sit the Discovery Park research community, BC Hydro's System Control Tower, and UniverCity, a residential neighbourhood with shops that turned the mountaintop into an actual place to live. In November 1995 the province and the university agreed to hand 330 hectares of campus land to the City of Burnaby for the Burnaby Mountain Conservation Area - a decision that drew the line between what gets built and what stays forest.

## Who Else Lives Here

Condominium construction keeps nibbling at the edges, and yet the mountain remains genuinely wild in a way that surprises visitors who arrive expecting a campus. Deer browse the verges. Coyotes trot down residential streets as though they hold the deed. Black bears - sometimes a sow with cubs - turn up on the forest trails and occasionally in the backyards of Westridge, the neighbourhood on the north slope. Overhead the roster is excellent: several kinds of woodpecker, northern flickers, the electric blue of Steller's jays, great blue herons stalking the wet ground, barred owls and great horned owls after dark. Bald eagles over the eastern forest are common enough that people stop pointing them out.

## November on the Mountain

The pipeline has been under this hill for decades, running from Alberta down to the Westridge Marine Terminal on Burrard Inlet at the mountain's foot, where crude is loaded onto tankers. In 2007 a contractor's excavator struck the line during unrelated work and opened it. Between 224 and 234 cubic metres of crude came out - some of it spraying across a residential street, some reaching the inlet. Roughly 225 to 250 residents were evacuated, and the cleanup ran more than a year. When expansion of the pipeline was proposed, that memory was not abstract to anyone living below. The 2014 camp followed, and the arrests, and the years of litigation and consultation after them.

## Eight Hundred and Ninety Thousand Barrels

The expansion was finished anyway. Construction stretched across roughly twelve years and cost about C$34 billion, and the expanded system began operating on 1 May 2024, carrying some 890,000 barrels a day to the terminal below Burnaby Mountain. Whether that counts as a resolution depends entirely on which side of the argument you occupied, and the people who camped in the park in 2014 would not describe it as one. What is not in dispute is the geography: a great deal of Canadian crude now reaches tidewater through a narrow inlet beneath a suburban hill, past the houses of Westridge and the trailheads of a conservation area.

## What the Mountain Is For

Most days none of that is what the mountain is doing. Centennial Park faces west over the inlet toward downtown Vancouver, and every summer during the Celebration of Light fireworks the lawns fill with people who have worked out that the best seat in the city is the one furthest from it and 370 metres up. In the park stands a group of Ainu carvings, gifts marking the friendship between Burnaby and its sister city Kushiro, in Hokkaido - figures from the tradition of Japan's Indigenous northern people, standing in a forest on the far side of the same ocean. Mountain bikers have mapped the slopes thoroughly, from the gentle Sidebandit to the steep punishment of Nicoles and Gearjammer. And because the summit sits just high enough, snow often falls up here while rain falls on the cities below - which is how a suburb ends up with a toboggan hill.

## Flight Context

Burnaby Mountain rises to 370 m (1,214 ft) at 49.2792°N, 122.9090°W, about 15 km east of downtown Vancouver on the south shore of Burrard Inlet's upper arms. Fly it at 3,000-4,500 ft AGL. It is one of the easiest features to identify in the Lower Mainland: an isolated dark green dome in a sea of suburb, with the pale concrete slabs of the Simon Fraser University campus laid along the summit ridge and the UniverCity blocks beside them. Burrard Inlet wraps the north side, with the Westridge Marine Terminal's jetty and tank farm visible at the base and Indian Arm reaching north beyond it. The Ironworkers Memorial Second Narrows Crossing lies west; the North Shore Mountains rise steeply across the water; Mount Baker sits on the southeast horizon in clear air. Nearby airports: CYPK Pitt Meadows roughly 15 km east, CYVR Vancouver International about 20 km southwest, CYHC Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre some 14 km west with seaplane traffic on the inlet, and CZBB Boundary Bay around 25 km south. Watch for marine layer filling the inlet on autumn mornings while the summit stands clear above it - the same inversion that gives the mountain snow when the valley gets rain.
