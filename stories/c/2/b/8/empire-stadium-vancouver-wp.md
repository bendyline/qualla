# Empire Stadium

Built in nine months for the 1954 Commonwealth Games, the Hastings Park bowl staged the Miracle Mile, seven Grey Cups, Elvis and the Beatles — and is now a community running track.

## Introduction

Two men ran a mile in under four minutes on the same afternoon here, which had never happened anywhere before. In the final straight John Landy looked back over his shoulder for Roger Bannister, and Bannister was not where he expected. That half-second is cast in bronze. The statue of the two runners stood outside Empire Stadium until the building came down, and it now stands at the north entrance to the Pacific National Exhibition, a few metres from where the race was actually run. The stadium itself is gone. For four decades, though, nearly everything that gathered Vancouver into a single place happened inside it.

## Four Hundred Thousand Short

Hosting the 1954 British Empire and Commonwealth Games meant Vancouver needed a main venue for the opening ceremony and the athletics, and as the deadline closed in, city council found itself $400,000 short of what it wanted to build. The answer was compromise. Work began on 4 September 1953 on a 25,000-seat stadium costing $1.5 million. By the time the games arrived the bowl held 32,375, a figure trimmed to 30,229 after 1974. Nine months is not long to build a stadium, and the result was never architecturally admired. It did not need to be. What Empire Stadium had was a running track, a grass field, and sightlines that put thirty thousand people close enough to hear an athlete breathing on the far turn.

## The Mile and the Marathon

The games produced two moments that outlived everything else. The first was the mile, when Bannister and Landy both broke four minutes in the same race and the phrase "Miracle Mile" entered the language. The second was harder to watch. Jim Peters, one of the finest marathon runners in the world, entered the stadium for the final lap with a lead of nearly seventeen minutes and his body already failing him. He collapsed, got up, collapsed again, and went down repeatedly in front of a crowd that had come to cheer him. He never reached the line. Officials carried him off on a stretcher, and he never raced again. Two of the defining images of the 1954 games were made on the same track: one of triumph, one of a great athlete being destroyed in public by heat and his own courage.

## Lions, Whitecaps, and a Slingshot

Afterward the stadium settled into its real career as a football ground. The BC Lions played there from 1954 to 1982, and in 1955 Empire hosted the first Grey Cup ever staged west of Ontario — the first of seven, the others falling in 1958, 1960, 1963, 1966, 1971 and 1974. High school teams met there for the Shrine Bowl provincial championship. Soccer came too: the Vancouver Royals for their single season in 1968, then the Vancouver Whitecaps through the North American Soccer League years. Two innovations arrived here first. Just before the 1966 Grey Cup, the stadium put up the gooseneck or slingshot goalposts devised by Jim Trimble and Joel Rottman, the first championship game anywhere to use them, and within a few years the standard design in both the CFL and the NFL. In 1970 Empire became the first facility in Canada laid with artificial turf, 3M's Tartan Turf. Elvis Presley played here. So did the Beatles.

## The Ground It Stood On

By the late 1970s the concrete was aging, and PNE president Erwin Swangard argued for a new stadium and convention centre at Hastings Park. The city went elsewhere with both ideas — False Creek for the stadium, Coal Harbour for the convention centre — and those two decisions ran directly into Expo 86 and SkyTrain. Empire Stadium came down in the early 1990s. The site had one brief encore: a temporary ground called Empire Field stood there in 2010 and 2011 while BC Place received its retractable roof, then it too was taken away. What remains is Empire Fields, a community track and sports facility open to anyone who wants to run on it. The park itself carries older weight. In 1942 the livestock barns beside this ground were used to hold and process Japanese Canadians before they were sent inland to internment camps — a fact worth knowing about the soil the bronze runners now stand on.

## Flight Context

Empire Stadium stood at 49.2826° N, 123.0332° W, in Hastings Park in East Vancouver, on ground sloping north toward Burrard Inlet. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet the Pacific National Exhibition grounds are the obvious feature — a walled block of exhibition halls, the wooden roller coaster at Playland, and the open green oval of Empire Fields where the stadium used to be. The Ironworkers Memorial Second Narrows Crossing spans the inlet a short distance northeast, with the grain terminals and container berths of the Port of Vancouver lining the shore below. Downtown Vancouver and the towers of the West End lie about 4 NM west; the North Shore mountains rise directly across the water. Nearby airports are CYVR (Vancouver International) roughly 9 NM southwest, CYHC (Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre) on the inlet to the west, CYPK (Pitt Meadows) east up the Fraser Valley, and CZBB (Boundary Bay) to the south.
