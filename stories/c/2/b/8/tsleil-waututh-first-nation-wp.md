# Tsleil-Waututh First Nation

The People of the Inlet have governed the north shore of Burrard Inlet since long before Vancouver existed, and have spent the last half-century making the rest of the country notice.

**Location:** 49.3106, -122.9879

**Categories:** Tsleil-Waututh First Nation, Coast Salish governments, First Nations governments in the Lower Mainland, Indigenous history

## Introduction

In 1967 Canada threw itself a hundredth birthday party, and in Vancouver the organizers invited a local chief to speak. Dan George had led his nation from 1951 to 1963. What he delivered was not a greeting. The "Lament for Confederation" mourned what a century of Canada had cost the people who were here first, spoken plainly to a crowd expecting pageantry, and it is generally credited with helping ignite a generation of Indigenous political organizing in this country. The man who wrote it was səlilwətaɬ - Tsleil-Waututh, the People of the Inlet - and the water he was talking about is visible from the reserve where he was born.

## People of the Inlet

səlilwət is Burrard Inlet, the long saltwater arm that separates Vancouver from its north shore, and the Tsleil-Waututh have lived in and along it since, in the Nation's own phrasing, time out of mind. They are Coast Salish and speak hən̓q̓əmin̓əm̓, the Downriver dialect of Halkomelem - closely related to their Squamish and Musqueam neighbours, but politically and culturally distinct, with territories that overlap in places. Canada called them the Burrard Indian Band for most of the twentieth century. Their main community, Burrard Inlet 3, occupies 108.2 hectares on the inlet's north shore in the District of North Vancouver; two much smaller reserves, Inlailwatash 4 and 4A, sit upstream on the Indian River. As of 2024 the Nation counted 706 members, with 292 living on reserve.

## The Longshoreman Who Became Ol' Antoine

He was born Geswanouth Slahoot on 24 July 1899. The surname George was imposed when he entered residential school at five. He worked as a longshoreman, then in construction, then drove a school bus, and he did not begin acting until he was sixty. Within a decade he was nominated for an Academy Award for playing Old Lodge Skins in Little Big Man in 1970 - a performance that refused the flat, humourless "Indian" that Hollywood had been printing for fifty years. Canadian audiences already knew him as Ol' Antoine in the CBC series Cariboo Country. American audiences met him again as Lone Watie opposite Clint Eastwood in The Outlaw Josey Wales in 1976. He died in North Vancouver on 23 September 1981, aged 82.

## A Family, and a Movement

The Georges kept turning up where decisions were being made. Hereditary Chief John L. George, the Nation's longest-serving elected chief, was a founding member of the Union of BC Indian Chiefs, formed in 1969 in opposition to the federal White Paper that proposed abolishing Indian status altogether - a document that united First Nations across the province against it. Leonard George, Dan George's son, was an actor too, and as elected chief he founded Takaya Developments, built the partnerships that brought major real estate development onto Tsleil-Waututh land, and took the Nation into the British Columbia treaty process. The Nation's canoe culture runs on the same continuity: the Burrard Canoe Club races war canoes, and Takaya Tours takes visitors out into the inlet in ocean-going canoes.

## Sacred Trust

Tsleil-Waututh opposition to the Trans Mountain pipeline expansion was not a protest so much as a decade of litigation and science. Under the banner of the Sacred Trust Initiative, the Nation commissioned its own assessments of what a sharply increased tanker traffic would mean for the inlet. In August 2018 the Federal Court of Appeal quashed the project's approval, finding that Canada had failed its constitutional duty to consult. The government consulted again, approved again, and in July 2020 the Supreme Court of Canada declined to hear the Nation's further appeal. The expanded line began commercial operation on 1 May 2024, carrying close to 890,000 barrels a day. The stewardship work continues regardless - salmon habitat research in the Indian River watershed, herring and shellfish restoration in an inlet that industry spent a century treating as a drain.

## Hosts in Their Own Territory

In 2010 the Winter Olympics came to unceded Coast Salish land, and the Tsleil-Waututh joined the Musqueam, Squamish and Lil'wat as the Four Host First Nations - the first time the Olympic movement formally recognized Indigenous title holders as hosts of its Games rather than as decoration in a ceremony. Four years earlier, four Tsleil-Waututh youth had made a documentary for CBC about their own struggles inside the school system. The two events belong to the same argument: that the Nation intends to describe itself. From the water, the community reads as a quiet crescent of houses backed by forest, facing a harbour full of freighters. The freighters are the new part.

## Flight Context

The Tsleil-Waututh community at Burrard Inlet 3 lies at 49.3106 N, 122.9879 W, on the north shore of Burrard Inlet in the District of North Vancouver, near Dollarton. Fly it at 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL along the inlet. Landmarks: the Ironworkers Memorial Second Narrows Crossing 2 nm west, the north shore port terminals beyond it, Belcarra and the Indian Arm fjord opening north, and Mount Seymour rising sharply behind the community. Indian Arm runs nearly 20 km north toward the Indian River, where the Nation's smaller reserves sit. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is 6 nm west in Coal Harbour, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 14 nm southwest, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) roughly 13 nm east. Expect heavy floatplane traffic in the inlet below 2,000 feet and rapid cloud build on the Seymour and Indian Arm slopes; the fjord narrows quickly and offers few turnaround options.
