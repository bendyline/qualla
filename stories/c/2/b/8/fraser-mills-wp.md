# Fraser Mills

A sawmill on the north bank of the Fraser imported its workforce from Quebec and Punjab, became its own municipality, then a landfill, then a retail strip - and is now slated to be a waterfront town again.

**Location:** 49.2333, -122.8500

**Categories:** Former municipalities in British Columbia, Populated places on the Fraser River, Labour disputes in British Columbia, Coquitlam, Forestry in Canada

## Introduction

In 1909 the Canadian Western Lumber Company could not find enough hands on the Fraser, so it went four thousand kilometres east to look. A contingent of 110 French-Canadian mill workers was recruited out of Quebec and Eastern Ontario; roughly forty families arrived that year, and another group followed in June 1910. They settled the slope just north of the mill, gathered around a young Oblate priest from France named Edmond Maillard, and the settlement took his name. Maillardville is still there. The mill that summoned it is not.

## The Mill That Kept Restarting

Cutting began here in 1889, at an operation known as the Ross McLaren Mill. It did not last: the mill closed soon after co-founder James McLaren died, and the site sat idle on the riverbank for years. When it reopened as the Fraser River Sawmills, it grew into one of the great lumber operations of the coast, with the Canadian Western Lumber Company behind it and a workforce large enough to need a town of its own. In 1913, Fraser Mills was incorporated as a municipality - a company town with a council and a name on the provincial register, occupying the flats between the Fraser River and the hill. It stayed separate for decades before amalgamating into the City of Coquitlam.

## Two Communities on One Payroll

The French Canadians were not the only workers brought in to feed the saws. Through the early twentieth century, Punjabi Sikh men came to the mills of the lower Fraser and settled on Coquitlam's south slope. The timing is worth pausing on: they arrived in the years when Canada was actively building legal machinery to keep South Asians out - a head tax on Indian immigrants, and a continuous journey regulation that no available shipping route could satisfy. The men who made it through worked the same shifts as their Québécois neighbours, on the same riverbank, for the same company. Two communities, recruited or arrived from opposite ends of the world, shaped the sound of this stretch of the Fraser for a century.

## Ten Weeks

The Depression arrived at Fraser Mills the way it arrived everywhere in the resource economy: orders stopped, then jobs did. Many workers were laid off outright. Those who kept their jobs were handed wage reductions, and they refused. The strike that followed ran ten weeks. Consider what that meant in a company town - a place where the employer was also, in effect, the landlord and the municipal authority. Walking out was not merely a matter of lost pay for the duration; it was a confrontation with the institution that supplied nearly everything the community depended on. That the workers held for ten weeks says something about how little was left to lose by then.

## Garbage, Then Big Boxes

The saws fell silent for good in 2001. What followed is a sequence familiar to any post-industrial riverfront, run at speed. The abandoned grounds became the Terranova landfill, taking Greater Vancouver's waste on the site where the region's lumber had been cut. After that came retail: the big-box development strung along United Boulevard, which acquired a second life as a rat-run for drivers escaping the jams on the adjacent Trans-Canada and Lougheed highways. For a generation, the answer to what happens to a hundred and twelve years of industry was a dump and a parking lot.

## Waterfront Village

Coquitlam has since designated the ground as the Waterfront Village Centre and rezoned it for both housing and commerce. The masterplan advanced by the developer Beedie proposes 5,500 homes across fifteen multi-unit buildings, with a 16-acre park, sports and commercial amenities, and a bus exchange linking to Braid SkyTrain station - which would give Coquitlam the only waterfront community it has. Whether the built result matches the drawings is a question the next decade answers, not this one. Upslope, meanwhile, the settlement the mill created has outlasted it comfortably: Maillardville counted 15,837 residents in 2021, though by the 2016 language figures only 1.8 percent spoke French at home. The inheritance persists in street names, in French-language schooling and immersion programs, and in the Festival du Bois - a lumber town's memory kept by the descendants of the people it sent for.

## Flight Context

Fraser Mills sits at 49.2333°N, 122.8500°W, on the north bank of the Fraser River in southwestern Coquitlam, just east of the New Westminster boundary. Fly it at 2,000-3,500 ft AGL. The river is the navigation anchor - follow the Fraser's north arm upstream past Annacis Island and the site is on the left bank where the flats widen, with the Brunette River joining from the north just west of it. Look for the signature of the redevelopment ground: a broad, flat, largely open riverfront parcel with big-box retail and parking lots strung along United Boulevard, bounded on the north by the Trans-Canada and Lougheed highways and by the steep wooded rise up to Maillardville. The new stal̕əw̓asəm Bridge crosses to Surrey a short way downstream, with the decommissioned 1937 Pattullo span beside it, closed to traffic since February 2026. Nearby airports: CYPK Pitt Meadows roughly 10 km east, CZBB Boundary Bay about 20 km southwest, CYNJ Langley Regional some 22 km southeast, and CYVR Vancouver International around 25 km west. This is controlled airspace beneath Vancouver's terminal environment - get clearance. Fraser Valley fog and low stratus are persistent from autumn through early spring; cold, dry outflow days give the clearest view of the river corridor.
