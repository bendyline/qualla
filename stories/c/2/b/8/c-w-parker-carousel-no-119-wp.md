# C. W. Parker Carousel No. 119

A 1912 Kansas carousel that toured Texas with a circus, spent half a century on Vancouver midways, and was nearly auctioned off one horse at a time before its neighbours bought it back.

**Location:** 49.2407, -122.9680

**Categories:** 1912 establishments in Kansas, Amusement rides introduced in 1912, Buildings and structures in Burnaby, Carousels in Canada, Tourist attractions in Burnaby, Museums

## Introduction

In 1989 the plan was to take the carousel apart and sell it in pieces. Antique carousel horses had become collectibles, worth more individually on a New York auction floor than the intact machine was worth turning in the rain at Vancouver's Playland, and a sale was announced. What stopped it was a group of local people with no particular expertise and no money, who gave themselves a name - the Friends of the Vancouver Carousel Society - and set about raising $350,000. It is also when the machine got the nickname it still carries: Parker #119.

## One of a Thousand, Out of Leavenworth

Charles Wallace Parker called himself the Amusement King and let people call him Colonel, though he never served a day. From a factory in Leavenworth, Kansas - he moved the operation there in 1911 - his company turned out something like a thousand carousels, along with shooting galleries and Ferris wheels, and by 1905 he was running four full-sized travelling carnivals of his own. Parker's portable machines were marketed as Carry-Us-Alls, built to be broken down, loaded onto railcars and put back up in the next town. Number 119 came off the line in 1912. It sold the following year for $5,886 and went on the road with the Lone Star Circus, working Texas for two seasons before being shipped back to the factory in 1915.

## Twenty Lost Years

Where it went next, nobody knows. Between 1915 and 1936 the carousel's history simply disappears from the record. What survives is physical evidence: the factory appears to have rebuilt it, adding fancier horses and heavier rounding boards, and the horses themselves carry different dates - some carved in 1917, others between 1920 and 1922. The trail picks up again in 1936, when Happyland, an amusement park in Vancouver, bought the machine. It stayed there until Happyland was torn down in 1957, moved to a small pavilion at Playland, and stayed until that pavilion came down in 1972. For the next seventeen years it ran in the open air and was packed away every winter - a 1912 wooden machine wintering outdoors on the West Coast, which is precisely as bad an idea as it sounds.

## Bought Back

The Burnaby Village Museum agreed in May 1989 to give the carousel a home, which turned an emotional campaign into a workable plan. Don Wrigley led the Friends as president; the carousel expert Keith Jamieson was brought in to run the rebuild. The purchase closed in 1990, and more money was raised for restoration afterward. The fundraising mechanism was elegantly simple: donors sponsored a horse and got to name it. A new building went up to house the machine, opened in 1993 and named the Don Wrigley Pavilion, and the carousel itself was rechristened the Burnaby Centennial Parker Carousel. It still turns there, in Deer Lake Park, beside the museum entrance that everyone now calls the Carousel Entrance.

## Old Paint, and Other Horses That Are Not Horses

Horse number 37 was never restored. Sponsored by the museum itself, it was deliberately left in its original condition - the flaking, faded 'old paint' that gave it its name - and set in a display case beside the machine, so that anyone admiring the restored animals can see exactly what the restorers started with. Other numbers in the roster stretch the definition of horse considerably. Number 40 is the Wurlitzer band organ. Number 39 is four small metal ponies and a wheelchair position, added so that toddlers and riders who use wheelchairs can go round with everyone else. Number 41 is two more small ponies, displaced by that wheelchair and now standing sentry at each of the museum's entrances.

## The Chariot That Was Sawn in Half

Number 38, the Gold Heart Chariot, carries the clearest scar of the carousel's working life. Somewhere along the line, one of its amusement-park owners cut it in half, stripping off the backdrop and fitting extra seats in the space - more bodies per revolution, more money per hour. Its twin chariot vanished altogether under a theme park's ownership and has never been found; the small ponies now occupy the gap where it stood. During the museum restoration, number 38 got its backdrop back and became a chariot again. The rest of the roster reads like a small local census: Captain Julius, Royal Burnaby Belle, Mr. Ed, Phar Lap, Nipoti, Mignonette, Happy Marie, Royal George. Every one of those names was chosen by someone who paid to keep the machine whole.

## Flight Context

The carousel turns inside the Don Wrigley Pavilion at Burnaby Village Museum, Deer Lake Park, at 49.2407 N, 122.9680 W - about 12 km east-southeast of downtown Vancouver. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL the anchor is Deer Lake itself, a small dark oval of water in a wooded park, with Burnaby's Metrotown high-rise cluster 3 km southwest and the wooded dome of Burnaby Mountain and SFU's campus 5 km north. The Trans-Canada Highway runs east-west just north of the park. This is dense controlled airspace beneath the Vancouver terminal area: Vancouver International (CYVR) is 18 km west, Boundary Bay (CZBB) 18 km south, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) 17 km east, and Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) 11 km northwest with constant floatplane traffic.
