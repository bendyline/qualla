# D-Wave Systems

The company that has been selling quantum computers longer than anyone else works out of a Burnaby suburb, and has spent two decades arguing with physicists about whether what it sells counts.

**Location:** 49.2566, -122.9990

**Categories:** technology, british-columbia, science, quantum-computing, business, innovation

## Introduction

Almost every serious argument about whether quantum computers actually work has, sooner or later, been an argument about a company in Burnaby. D-Wave has been selling machines it calls quantum computers since 2011, longer than anybody, and has spent the same two decades being told by parts of the physics community that what it sells is not quite the thing. The company started with a business course at the University of British Columbia. Its machines now sit at NASA Ames and at a German supercomputing centre, its results have appeared in Nature and in Science, and its critics remain unconvinced - which is, arguably, the most interesting fact about it.

## A Business Course at UBC

Four people founded D-Wave in 1999: Haig Farris, Geordie Rose, Bob Wiens and Alexandre Zagoskin. The connection between them was a classroom. Farris taught a business course at the University of British Columbia, where Rose was finishing a physics PhD and Zagoskin was a postdoctoral fellow. The name came from the hardware itself - the first qubit designs used d-wave superconductors.

For years the company operated the way a university spinout does, working out of assorted Vancouver addresses and borrowed lab space at UBC before settling in Burnaby, the suburb immediately east of the city. It kept its academic footing deliberately, funding research and building a collaborative network that ran through UBC, the Universite de Sherbrooke, Toronto, Twente, Chalmers, Erlangen, IPHT Jena and NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

## Tunnelling Instead of Climbing

D-Wave does not build a universal quantum computer, and has never claimed to. Its machines do one thing: quantum annealing, a method for finding the lowest point in a mathematical landscape.

Picture that landscape as terrain full of valleys. You want the deepest one. A classical optimizer has to climb out of each shallow valley to check whether a deeper one lies beyond, and tall barriers stop it. A quantum system can tunnel straight through a barrier instead, and the odds of doing so depend on how thin the barrier is as much as how high - so walls that would trap a classical search can simply be passed. What this hardware cannot do is run gate-model algorithms like Shor's, which need precise operations D-Wave's architecture does not provide. That narrowness is exactly why the company had something to ship years before anyone else. The approach came out of experimental condensed matter physics rather than the quantum information field, and much of the argument that followed traces back to that difference in upbringing.

## Sudoku at the Computer History Museum

On 13 February 2007, D-Wave demonstrated a 16-qubit machine called Orion at the Computer History Museum in Mountain View, running three problems: a search for a molecule resembling a known drug, a seating plan for guests who did and did not get along, and a Sudoku puzzle. It was an odd trio for what was billed as the first public demonstration of a quantum computing service, and it set the tone - real output, immediately contested.

The commercial milestones came steadily after that. Lockheed Martin signed a multi-year contract in November 2010. On 11 May 2011 the company announced D-Wave One, a 128-qubit system quoted at roughly ten million US dollars, its chip fabricated at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory. In May 2013 NASA, Google and the Universities Space Research Association opened a Quantum Artificial Intelligence Lab at Ames Research Center around a 512-qubit D-Wave Two. The following February, the company was on the cover of Time.

## The Argument, Continued

The qubit counts kept climbing - 1,000-plus in the D-Wave 2X of 2015, the 2000Q in 2017, the Leap cloud service in 2018, then Advantage in 2020 with 5,760 qubits and a topology giving each one fifteen connections. Advantage2 followed, and as of 17 July 2025 the company listed a 4,400-plus qubit system as generally available, with twenty connections per qubit.

The disputes scaled with the hardware. In March 2025 D-Wave announced it had achieved quantum supremacy on a specialized optimization problem; the Wall Street Journal reported the performance gap but also the skeptics, who argued the problem had been chosen to flatter the machine. A 2025 paper in Science, describing a simulation of a magnetic material run far faster than a classical computer could manage, drew the same response from some physicists. Meanwhile an Advantage system sold to Forschungszentrum Julich went to work at the Julich Supercomputing Centre, where researchers used it to simulate false vacuum decay and published the result in Nature - a question about the stability of the universe, answered on a machine from a Burnaby office park.

## The Price of Being First

Being early is expensive. D-Wave reported revenue of $24.6 million for 2025 against a net loss of around $355 million, with roughly 220 employees on the books the year before. Very few companies can sustain that gap, and the ones that do are betting that the market arrives before the money runs out.

In January 2026 the company made its largest move yet, acquiring Quantum Circuits Inc. for $550 million - a reach beyond the annealing hardware that has defined it for twenty-five years. Whether that settles the long argument or simply relocates it is not yet clear. What is clear is that the whole of it has been conducted from a suburb between Vancouver and the Fraser Valley, by people who started out in a business class and decided to build the thing before the theory was finished agreeing with them.

## Flight Context

D-Wave's Burnaby offices sit at 49.26N, 123.00W in Burnaby, British Columbia, the suburb immediately east of Vancouver. There is nothing to identify from the air at building scale, so navigate by the surrounding geography: Burnaby Lake and the green corridor of Still Creek lie just north, the Trans-Canada Highway runs east-west across the municipality, and the cluster of Metrotown towers - the tallest thing in Burnaby - stands to the southwest. Burnaby Mountain, with the concrete terraces of Simon Fraser University on its summit, rises to the north and is the clearest landmark in the area. Downtown Vancouver's peninsula is about 12 km west, with the North Shore mountains behind it, and the Fraser River marks the southern boundary. Nearby airports: Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) 12 km west, Vancouver International (CYVR) 18 km southwest, Pitt Meadows Regional (CYPK) 20 km northeast, Boundary Bay (CZBB) 25 km south, and Abbotsford International (CYXX) 50 km east. Best viewing altitude is 2,000-4,000 feet, within Vancouver's Class C airspace. Low cloud and rain are the default from November into March.
