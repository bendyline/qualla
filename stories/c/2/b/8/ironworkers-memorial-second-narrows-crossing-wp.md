# Ironworkers Memorial Second Narrows Crossing

Seventy-nine men were standing on an unfinished span over Burrard Inlet when a miscalculated support gave way - and the bridge they were building now carries their name.

**Location:** 49.2958, -123.0264

**Categories:** Bridges in Greater Vancouver, Cantilever bridges in Canada, Bridges completed in 1960, Transport in North Vancouver (district municipality), Bridge disasters in Canada, Disasters in British Columbia, History of Vancouver, Labor monuments and memorials, Road bridges in British Columbia

## Introduction

On 17 June 1958, a crane reached out from the north side of the unfinished bridge to join the two chords of the arch, and the structure beneath it failed. Several spans went down together. Seventy-nine ironworkers fell thirty metres into Burrard Inlet. Eighteen of them died at once or soon after, some almost certainly pulled under by the weight of their own tool belts, the heavy leather rigs that hold a working man's hammers and wrenches and spud wrenches and that cannot be shed in a fall. A diver searching the water for bodies afterwards drowned as well. Nineteen dead, on a job that was supposed to be routine. The bridge was finished anyway, and thirty-six years later it was given their name.

## The Bridge Ships Kept Hitting

A crossing had stood at the Second Narrows since 1925, carrying road traffic first and trains a year later, and it had an unenviable relationship with shipping. The freighter Eurana, loaded with lumber, struck it on 10 March 1927. The Norwich City hit it in 1928. The Losmar rammed straight through a span on 24 April 1930. Then, on 19 September that same year, the barge Pacific Gatherer wedged itself under the 91-metre span; the span hung off to one side, broke free, and sank. Lawsuits followed, the bridge company went bankrupt, and the crossing sat closed for four years. Vancouver had learned, expensively, that it needed a bridge tall enough to ignore the traffic below it.

## A Number on a Drawing

Construction on the replacement began in November 1957, to a steel truss cantilever design by Swan Wooster Engineering. The Royal Commission that examined the collapse the following June found the cause in a calculation. A temporary steel box support at the base of the falsework - the scaffolding that holds a cantilever arm up until it reaches something to lean on - was carrying the weight of the newly built fifth anchor span, and it was not strong enough to do it. The falsework plans had been drawn by a junior engineer; the supervising engineer had not caught the error. That is the whole of it. No storm, no ship, no sabotage: a figure on a drawing that nobody checked hard enough, holding up seventy-nine men.

## Impracticable

The collapse was not the first death on the job. In 1957 a steelworker had already fallen from the structure and been killed. That December, a safety inspector from the British Columbia Workmen's Compensation Board considered whether nets should be strung beneath the work platforms and reported that the installation of a net was "impracticable" - a word that would be quoted for decades afterwards, because six months later seventy-nine men went into the water with nothing between them and it. Four more workers died during the construction that followed. High steel in the 1950s was understood to be lethal work, and the men who did it knew the odds better than anyone; what the record shows is how little was in place to change them.

## Twenty-Three Million Dollars

The bridge opened on 25 August 1960, having cost roughly $23 million. It runs 1,292 metres with a centre span of 335 metres, and it carries six lanes of the Trans-Canada Highway between Vancouver and the North Shore - the District and City of North Vancouver and, beyond them, West Vancouver. Drivers paid tolls until 1 April 1963 and have crossed free ever since. The 1925 bridge alongside it was stripped of its road deck and left to the railways, and in 1969 Canadian National replaced it outright with a lift bridge whose 150-metre moveable span was then the longest in Canada. The two structures still stand side by side at the narrows, the low one rising for ships, the high one indifferent to them.

## Seventeenth of June, 1994

Thirty-six years to the day after the collapse, on 17 June 1994, the Second Narrows Bridge was formally renamed the Ironworkers Memorial Second Narrows Crossing. The name covers the eighteen killed in the collapse, the diver who drowned looking for them, and the four other workers who died before the job was done - twenty-three men in total. Something in the order of 130,700 vehicles cross it every day, most of them at commuting speed, most of their drivers reading the sign as a mouthful of syllables on a highway exit list rather than a war memorial. It is worth knowing what it means. There is no plaque on a girder that a driver at 80 km/h can read, so the name has to do the remembering by itself.

## Flight Context

The Ironworkers Memorial Second Narrows Crossing spans 49.2958°N, 123.0264°W, at the Second (eastern) Narrows of Burrard Inlet, joining east Vancouver to the District of North Vancouver. From 1,500-2,500 ft AGL the pairing is unmistakable: a tall green steel cantilever truss carrying six lanes of Highway 1, with the low CN lift bridge and its towers immediately west of it, squeezed into the tightest point of the inlet. Look for the grain terminals and sulphur piles on the north shore, the Port of Vancouver's container cranes and rail yards along the south, and the deep-water anchorage where bulk carriers wait their turn through the narrows. The Lions Gate Bridge crosses First Narrows about 8 km west, with Stanley Park beside it. Mount Seymour and the Seymour valley rise steeply behind the north end of the bridge. Airspace is busy and controlled: CYHC Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre lies roughly 8 km west with continuous seaplane traffic along the inlet, CYVR Vancouver International about 17 km southwest, CYPK Pitt Meadows some 22 km east and CZBB Boundary Bay around 25 km southeast. Clearance is required before working the inlet. Clear northeast-outflow days in winter give the sharpest view of the truss against the mountains; autumn and spring bring low cloud that can hide the North Shore slopes entirely while the water stays open.
