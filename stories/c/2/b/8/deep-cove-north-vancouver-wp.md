# Deep Cove, North Vancouver

Whales were butchered on this beach; a century later the same shoreline launched Vancouver's first mountain bikes and rents kayaks by the hour.

**Location:** 49.3269, -122.9490

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in North Vancouver (district municipality), Designated places in British Columbia, Indian Arm, Outdoor recreation

## Introduction

By the middle of the nineteenth century, whales were being hauled onto the gravel here and flensed where the kayak racks now stand. It is a hard image to hold alongside the present-day version of Deep Cove - a one-street village at the foot of Mount Seymour, thirteen kilometres from downtown Vancouver, where the weekend problem is parking. Locals just call it the Cove. It faces due east onto Indian Arm, which means the sun arrives over the water and leaves early behind the mountain, and it has spent its entire recorded history being wanted by more people than it can comfortably hold.

## Deepwater

This is Tsleil-Waututh and Squamish territory, and for the Tsleil-Waututh the cove was a clamming and fishing ground long before it had an English name - the first one it got was Deepwater, which at least described the bathymetry honestly. British and Spanish naval expeditions poked into Indian Arm in the late eighteenth century and found a steep-sided fjord that went nowhere useful. What followed was extraction in sequence: whaling on the shore by the mid-1800s, then logging on the slopes, then granite quarrying out of the rock above the village. Each industry left something behind. The quarry left its name on the lookout that now draws more visitors than any other feature in the neighbourhood.

## Summer People

In the 1910s Vancouver discovered the Cove as a summer resort and started building cabins. For decades the social geometry of the place was simple - a yacht club, a dance hall and a general store, and that was the centre of town. Getting here was the constraint. When the Second Narrows Bridge opened in 1960 and the drive became straightforward, the population began to climb through the sixties and seventies, and yet the village stubbornly kept its rural texture; well into that period a large open horse paddock sat right beside Gallant Avenue, the main street. The surrounding neighbourhoods filled in around it - Cove Cliff, Dollarton, Parkgate, Woodlands - and the Cove became the place all of them walk down to.

## Where the Mountain Bike Landed

Deep Cove has an outsized claim on a global sport. The Deep Cove Bike Shop brought the first mountain bikes to Vancouver in the early 1980s, at the foot of exactly the kind of steep, wet, root-tangled terrain that would make the North Shore a reference point for riders everywhere. The water side is just as well served. The bay is one of very few in Indian Arm that combines a sheltering shape with a bottom shallow enough to anchor a pleasure boat overnight, which explains the marina, the rowing club and the kayak outfits that put beginners on the water within sight of the beach. Above the village, the Baden-Powell Trail climbs to Quarry Rock, a granite shelf with a long view down the Arm; check current trail conditions before you set out, since the route sees both heavy use and periodic maintenance closures.

## The Cove's Cast

For a village of this size, the roster of people who have lived here is unusually strange. The writer Malcolm Lowry squatted in a beach shack at nearby Dollarton through the 1940s; the shack is long gone, and a plaque in Cates Park marks roughly where it stood. Annora Brown, who spent a career painting the plants and landscapes of the Canadian prairies, retired here and died in the Cove in 1987. The artist Charles van Sandwyk spent his teenage years here. So, for many years, did the occultist Charles Stansfeld Jones and his family. And in August 1977 the actor Sebastian Cabot - Mr. French in Family Affair, and the voice of Bagheera in Disney's The Jungle Book and the narrator of the Winnie the Pooh films - suffered a stroke at his vacation home in Deep Cove and died in a Victoria hospital days later.

## A Village That Keeps a Theatre

The permanent institutions are modest and durable. The Cultural Centre holds the 130-seat Shaw Theatre, home to the First Impressions Theatre Company and the Deep Cove Stage Society, alongside the Seymour Art Gallery and the Deep Cove Heritage Society, which is the reason anybody can still tell you about the dance hall and the horse paddock. Three community schools serve the area - Cove Cliff Elementary, Sherwood Park Elementary and Seycove Secondary. None of that is what brings the crowds; the crowds come for the water and the trail. But it is what keeps the Cove a place where people live rather than a place people visit, which is the tension every beautiful village at the end of a road eventually has to manage.

## Flight Context

Deep Cove lies at 49.3269 N, 122.9490 W, on the west shore of Indian Arm at the northeastern corner of the District of North Vancouver. Fly it at 2,000-3,500 ft: Indian Arm is a narrow, steep-walled fjord running roughly north from Burrard Inlet, and Deep Cove is the obvious notch on its western side near the mouth, with Mount Seymour's forested bulk rising immediately west and the marina's docks making a clear pale mark on the water. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is about 7 nm west and its floatplane traffic uses Indian Arm regularly; Pitt Meadows (CYPK) sits roughly 12 nm southeast, Vancouver International (CYVR) 12 nm southwest, and Boundary Bay (CZBB) 15 nm south. Terrain is the real consideration here - the walls of the Arm rise steeply on both sides and the valley funnels wind, so expect turbulence and downdrafts on windy days and keep altitude in hand. Cloud frequently caps Mount Seymour while the water below stays clear.
