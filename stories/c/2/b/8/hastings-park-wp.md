# Hastings Park

Granted in 1888 to be kept forever as wilderness, Vancouver's Hastings Park became a racetrack, a fairground, a roller coaster — and in 1942, a holding place for Japanese Canadian families.

**Location:** 49.2852, -123.0401

**Categories:** Hastings Park, Parks in Vancouver, Fairgrounds in Canada, World War II internment camps in Canada, Parks established in the 1880s

## Introduction

In 1888 the Province of British Columbia handed 62 hectares on the shore of Burrard Inlet to the City of Vancouver in trust, under the terms of a dead man's will, on the condition that the land be kept in perpetuity as wilderness park for public use. Almost none of that survived the twentieth century. Hastings Park today holds a thoroughbred racetrack, an amusement park with a wooden roller coaster, a hockey arena, an agricultural showground, rows of livestock barns and a great deal of asphalt. In 1942 those same barns held Japanese Canadian families, waiting for the country they lived in to move them somewhere else.

## The Fair That Ate the Park

The wilderness clause did not last long. Horses were racing here by 1892. The Pacific National Exhibition held its first fair on the grounds in 1910, and an amusement park opened in 1926, and from then on the site filled in steadily: buildings, then more buildings, then parking lots for the people who came to the buildings. The first great hall was the Exhibition Building, a Mission-style pile crowned with ornate cupolas, which proved structurally inefficient and came down by the 1920s. Three large Art Deco concrete buildings and one smaller one rose in its place — the Showmart, the Food Building, the Forum and the Gardens. Each was built for crowds. None was built for what the Forum would be used for two decades later.

## Nineteen Forty-Two

After Canada went to war with Japan, the federal government forced people of Japanese descent off the British Columbia coast, most of them Canadian-born citizens, and Hastings Park became the funnel through which they passed. Families arrived from fishing villages up and down the coast and from Vancouver itself, and were put into the buildings the fair used for livestock. They lived in stables and barnyards without privacy, in conditions that were unsanitary from the first day. Men were often separated from their families and sent inland; women and children stayed at the park until they could be moved to camps in the interior or to sugar-beet farms on the prairies. A woman named Kimiko later described the intense cold of that winter, and the single pot-bellied stove inside a stable. The food was poor enough that the Red Cross shipped in supplies. A photograph taken on 13 May 1942 shows a kindergarten class on the grounds; another shows the men's dormitory in the Forum, cots in rows beneath a ceiling built for exhibitions.

## Empire and Coliseum

The post-war decades brought the park its biggest structures. Empire Stadium went up in time for the British Empire and Commonwealth Games that Vancouver hosted in 1954; it has since been demolished, as has Empire Field, the temporary ground that later stood on roughly the same patch. The BC Building arrived with the once-famous Challenger Relief Map of British Columbia inside it, a scale model of the entire province that visitors walked around for decades. The Agrodome followed. Playland kept expanding, most visibly upward — its wooden roller coaster, added to the city's heritage register in August 2013, is still the first thing you see coming into Vancouver from the east on Hastings Street or Highway 1. Largest of all is the Pacific Coliseum, for many years the biggest indoor sports and entertainment venue in the city.

## Daylighting a Creek

By the 1990s the neighbourhood wanted some of the original bargain honoured. A Restoration Plan was drawn up with Hastings-Sunrise residents and approved by the City in 1997, and between 1997 and 2001 a genuine piece of park came back. The Sanctuary occupies the old BC Pavilion site: a large pond ringed by native planting and walkways, drawing birds and small animals into ground that had been pavement. The pond is stocked with rainbow trout, and you can fish for them from a small dock at its northwest corner. Creekway Park extends the idea outward, daylighting a buried stream to link the Sanctuary with New Brighton Park on the inlet shore; its first stage was completed in September 2013. The work has continued in stages ever since.

## What Comes Next

In 2025 a soccer-specific stadium for the Vancouver Whitecaps was provisionally approved for the site, subject to the agreement clearing Vancouver City Council; the club continues to play at BC Place downtown. Whatever is decided, it will be the latest answer to a question the park has been arguing with since 1888, which is whether land given to a city forever as wilderness can be anything else. The honest answer is that it already has been, many times over, and that the record includes both a roller coaster and a set of barns that people were made to live in. Hastings Park is one of those places where a city's whole character — its appetite for spectacle, its capacity for cruelty, its slow attempts at repair — is legible in a single 62-hectare rectangle.

## Flight Context

Hastings Park occupies 49.2852°N, 123.0401°W in northeast Vancouver, a compact rectangle set back a few blocks from Burrard Inlet in the Hastings-Sunrise neighbourhood. From 1,500 to 3,000 feet it is one of the easiest landmarks in the city to identify: the oval of Hastings Racecourse anchors the northern half, the saddle-shaped roof of the Pacific Coliseum sits beside it, and the white timber lattice of Playland's wooden roller coaster reads clearly on the southern side. Navigation cues: Hastings Street runs along the southern edge, New Brighton Park and the inlet shoreline lie immediately north, the Ironworkers Memorial Second Narrows Crossing spans the inlet to the northeast, and downtown's towers stand about three miles west. Airspace is controlled and busy. Vancouver Harbour Flight Centre (CYHC) is roughly 3 nm west in Coal Harbour with constant floatplane traffic; Vancouver International (CYVR) lies about 8 nm southwest; Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 14 nm east-southeast and Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 14 nm south. Low cloud and rain prevail October through April; late-August afternoons, during the fair, put the whole site in motion below you.
