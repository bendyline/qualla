# Indian Arm

A twenty-kilometre glacial fjord with no bridges and almost no roads, running north out of Burrard Inlet into wilderness that begins within sight of a major city.

**Location:** 49.3667, -122.8833

**Categories:** Fjords of British Columbia, Coast of British Columbia, Landforms of Lower Mainland, Provincial parks of British Columbia, Hydroelectricity

## Introduction

Twenty kilometres of saltwater fjord run due north out of Burrard Inlet, and almost none of it can be reached by car. Indian Arm is narrow, with mountains rising directly out of the water on both sides; the slopes are so steep that most have never been built on at all, despite sitting on the doorstep of a major metropolitan region. There are no crossings. Ice carved the trench during the last glaciation and the sea filled it afterwards. Its Halkomelem name is səl̓ilw̓ət, and the Tsleil-Waututh - the people of the inlet - take their name from this water, rather than the water taking its name from them.

## Water Coming Off Rock

The walls are either dense coastal forest or bare granite, and in spring they leak. Dozens of unnamed seasonal falls appear on the cliffs during the runoff, run for weeks and vanish; in a cold winter some of them freeze in place. The named ones are permanent. Granite Falls, the largest, comes down the east side near the north end, at a spot that was once a working quarry cutting stone for Vancouver's buildings and breakwaters. Spray of Pearls Falls drops at Wigwam Creek in the northwest corner, and Silver Falls comes off Elsay Creek on the western wall. In odd-numbered years a large pink salmon run enters the inlet from July into October and pushes up the Indian River at the head of the fjord, which is where the bears go, and where the people who want to see bears go as well. Seals work the deep water. Bald eagles and herons work the shallows.

## Say Nuth Khaw Yum

In 1995, under the province's Lower Mainland Nature Legacy Program, 6,826 hectares of both shores - plus Racoon and Twin Islands - were protected as Say Nuth Khaw Yum Provincial Park, sitting squarely inside the core of Tsleil-Waututh traditional territory. Three years later the Nation and the Province signed a management agreement that put equal numbers of Tsleil-Waututh and provincial representatives on a board that co-manages every aspect of the park and heritage area, an arrangement that was well ahead of its time in British Columbia. Getting there still means a boat. Kayakers and small craft come up from Deep Cove, and charter day boats run from Granville Island, Port Moody and Coal Harbour to wilderness campsites at sea level at Bishop Creek, Granite Falls and Twin Islands. The exception is on foot: a rough perimeter trail around the arm was finally completed in 2003, cut over many years by the engineer and trailbuilder Don McPherson, the same man who carved the punishing Grouse Grind up the side of Grouse Mountain.

## Vancouver Power, Carved in Granite

On the eastern shore, two powerhouses have been turning since the beginning of the last century. The scheme is elegant and slightly improbable: water from Coquitlam Lake runs through a tunnel bored clean through Eagle Mountain into Buntzen Lake, which sits about 150 metres above the tideline, and steel penstocks then drop that water down the mountainside into the generators at the water's edge. Buntzen No. 1 opened in 1903 - Vancouver's first hydroelectric plant, built to run the city's streetcars - with four 1,500-kilowatt machines, and grew steadily: three 5,000-kilowatt units by 1912, then a rebuilt north end and a single 55,000-kilowatt unit in 1951. Its neighbour a thousand feet south, Buntzen No. 2, was designed by the architect Francis Rattenbury and has barely changed since it was finished, a single handsome building set against the rock. Look for the ornate granite addition on No. 1 with the words Vancouver Power cut into the stone. Both stations still generate; both are unmanned, run by remote control from a BC Hydro control room on Burnaby Mountain.

## The Biergarten at the End of the Fjord

At the head of the arm stands a building with a genuinely strange biography. The Wigwam Inn opened in 1910 as a luxury resort and fishing lodge built around a German biergarten, reached by a daily sternwheeler run on the Skeena. Its heyday belonged to the German financier Gustav Konstantin von Alvensleben, who ran it in the years before the First World War and drew guests including John D. Rockefeller and a great-grandson of the fur-trade millionaire John Jacob Astor. Operators came and went until 1963; afterwards the property changed hands repeatedly and spent a period as a gambling house, which ended the way gambling houses in remote inlets tend to, with a raid by the Royal Canadian Mounted Police. Rumours have attached themselves to the place ever since - that Al Capone hid there, that people were killed there, that it is haunted - none of which any record supports. The Royal Vancouver Yacht Club owns it now and keeps it as an outstation for members. There is no public moorage, and no reciprocal access for other clubs, so most visitors see it the way it is best seen anyway: from the water, at the end of twenty kilometres of mountains.

## Flight Context

Indian Arm centres on 49.3667 N, 122.8833 W and runs almost exactly north-south, a narrow dark trench of salt water branching north from the eastern end of Burrard Inlet. Enter from the south over Deep Cove and the arm opens ahead: Belcarra and the Buntzen powerhouses on the east shore, the flank of Mount Seymour on the west, Buntzen Lake as a bright green-blue pool perched above the eastern wall, and the Indian River delta closing the far end before the Coast Mountains take over. Fly it at 3,000 to 5,000 feet - well above the ridgelines, not down in the trench. The walls rise steeply from sea level on both sides, the fjord is narrow enough that turning room is limited at low level, there are no landing options anywhere along it, and strong outflow or inflow winds across the ridges produce serious turbulence and downdrafts against the lee walls. Vancouver Harbour Water Airport (CYHC) is about 12 nm southwest and its floatplanes also work these inlets; Pitt Meadows (CYPK) is about 12 nm southeast, Vancouver International (CYVR) about 20 nm southwest, and Abbotsford (CYXX) 35 nm southeast. Clear summer days give exceptional definition on the granite walls and waterfalls; from autumn through spring the arm fills with low cloud that can close the trench while the inlet mouth stays open.
