# Port Moody

For one spectacular year the head of Burrard Inlet was the western end of Canada. Then the railway laid twelve more miles of track, and Port Moody had to become something else.

**Location:** 49.2831, -122.8320

**Categories:** Port Moody, Cities in British Columbia, Populated places in Greater Vancouver, Canadian Pacific Railway, Railway towns

## Introduction

On July 4, 1886, the first scheduled transcontinental passenger train in Canadian history came to a stop at the head of Burrard Inlet, and Port Moody was, briefly, the place where the country ended. Land prices went vertical on the strength of a single fact: everyone and everything crossing Canada by rail would have to arrive right here. The fact held for roughly a year. In 1887 the Canadian Pacific Railway laid twelve more miles of track west along the shoreline to a townsite it had named Vancouver, and the western end of Canada moved down the inlet, taking most of the money with it. Port Moody has spent the 140 years since figuring out what else to be.

## Built Against an Invasion That Never Came

The city takes its name from Colonel Richard Clement Moody of the Royal Engineers, and its existence from a defensive worry. Moody's men cut a trail from New Westminster north to Burrard Inlet so that the colonial capital would have a saltwater escape route and second front if the United States ever came for it. A settlement grew at the trail's end, expanding after 1859 on land granted to the Royal Engineers themselves. The Americans never came. What arrived instead was a railway - and the reason a railway was coming at all is that British Columbia had made a transcontinental line the price of joining Confederation in 1871.

## Terminus

In 1879 the CPR named Port Moody its western terminus, and a village that nobody had thought much about suddenly mattered enormously. Surveying began in 1881, with John Murray Sr. and Jr. both working on it; Murray Jr. named a good many of the streets after his own relatives, which is one way to leave a mark. Andrew Onderdonk held the government contract for the westernmost stretch and built 227 miles of line from Port Moody through Hope, Yale and Savona, starting construction at the Port Moody end in 1883 and finishing in 1885. He put up a large wharf and receiving yard. The last spike went in at Craigellachie on November 7, 1885, and a train reached Port Moody the following day. Everyone assumed a major Pacific metropolis was now inevitable.

## Twelve Miles

Twelve miles is what it cost them. The CPR's 1887 branch line west along the inlet ended the speculation in a season, and the boom town at the head of the water emptied out - people who had bought at the top losing a great deal and moving on. Some stayed. The ship captain and real estate figure James A. Clarke did. So did several lumber mills, which had the advantage of caring about timber and deep water rather than about being the end of the line. Port Moody's council sat as a city for the first time on April 7, 1913. And the town kept the date: Golden Spike Days, held every July, still commemorates the arrival of that first passenger train, which is a genuinely graceful way of celebrating the best thing that ever happened to you and the thing that was taken away, at the same time.

## Forty-One Streams

Something over forty-one streams run down out of the mountains through Port Moody into Burrard Inlet, and the city has built a fair bit of its modern identity around not wrecking them. A municipal stream stewardship program manages the creeks, the streamside vegetation and the watersheds; the volunteer-run Port Moody Ecological Society operates a salmon and trout hatchery and a water quality lab alongside it. City Hall has been pesticide-free since 1988, and the city later banned their use more broadly. The payoff arrived in 2004, when Port Moody took a prize in the Planning for the Future category of the UN-backed International Awards for Liveable Communities, finishing third overall among cities its size. Bert Flinn Park is the clearest expression of the idea: 138 hectares of mostly undeveloped forest on former industrial land, laced with unmarked trails along old logging roadbeds.

## City of the Arts, Brewer's Row

On June 16, 2004, Port Moody trademarked itself as the City of the Arts - a slogan that turns out to be grounded in something real. Artists had been coming for years for the cheap rent, the water light and the scenery, and roughly six percent of local employment is now in arts and culture, one of the highest concentrations anywhere in the region. The Inlet Theatre seats two hundred. The PoMo Museum keeps a restored heritage rail car. The other thing the city is known for is a single street lined with craft breweries, universally called Brewer's Row. The old economy has not vanished - there is still a deep-sea bulk terminal, petrochemical distribution, a large wood products manufacturer - though the 950-megawatt Burrard Generating Station stopped producing electricity in 2016, four of its six units left behind as synchronous condensers feeding reactive power into the grid. Today 33,535 people live on 25.85 square kilometres at the head of the inlet, at the end of a line that turned out not to be the end of anything.

## Flight Context

Port Moody wraps the head of Burrard Inlet at 49.2831 N, 122.832 W, roughly 12 nm east of downtown Vancouver. From 2,500 to 5,000 feet the shape of the place is obvious: the inlet narrows sharply past the Second Narrows and terminates in a shallow mudflat basin, with the city filling the flats and climbing the slope to the south and east. Look for the big industrial waterfront on the north shore, the green rectangle of Rocky Point Park on the south shore, and the dark unbroken block of Bert Flinn Park on the ridge behind town. The Coast Mountains rise immediately north beyond Belcarra and Anmore; Burnaby Mountain with the Simon Fraser University campus on top sits due west. Nearby airports: Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 8 nm east; Vancouver Harbour (CYHC) water aerodrome roughly 10 nm west down the inlet; Vancouver International (CYVR) about 17 nm southwest; Boundary Bay (CZBB) about 15 nm south. The inlet funnels wind, and outflow conditions in winter can make the eastern basin turbulent; low stratus is common here in autumn and winter.
