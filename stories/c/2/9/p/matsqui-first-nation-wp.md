# Matsqui First Nation

A Sto:lo nation on the Fraser River whose name means "easy portage," whose founder was transformed into a beaver, and who speak two languages where most of their neighbours speak one.

**Location:** 49.1062, -122.3426

**Categories:** Sto:lo governments, First Nations governments in the Lower Mainland, Politics of Abbotsford, British Columbia, Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast

## Introduction

The name is a piece of navigational advice. Matsqui means something close to "easy portage" - the place where you can carry a canoe over the ground without much trouble - which tells you the two things that matter most about this stretch of the Fraser Valley. The river is the road, and here the river has a shortcut. The Matsqui are one of the Sto:lo, the People of the River, and their territory runs along the Fraser from Crescent Island east to Sumas Mountain and south across a border that was drawn straight through it in the middle of the nineteenth century without anyone asking. The reserve lands now total about 431 hectares. Roughly 266 people are members, fewer than a hundred of them living on reserve.

## Sk-Ele'yitl, Who Became a Beaver

Every Sto:lo community has its sxwoxwiya:m, the origin account that explains how the world took its current shape. In them, Xe:Xa:ls - three brothers and a sister, working together - moved through a world in which humans, animals and plants were not yet distinguishable from one another and fixed each into its recognizable form. They also transformed the founding ancestors of the Sto:lo communities. The Matsqui founder, Sk-Ele'yitl, was made into a beaver. It is not an incidental animal. The beaver is credited with bringing both fire and salmon to all the Sto:lo peoples, and because beavers range widely, a beaver seen anywhere in Sto:lo territory can recall the Matsqui to their own beginning. Because this history lives in speech rather than on paper, the telling carries an obligation: get a story wrong and you may lose the standing to tell stories at all. Accuracy is not a scholarly virtue here. It is a form of respect.

## Two Languages on One River

Most of the eleven other Sto:lo nations along the Fraser speak Halq'emeylem, the upriver dialect of the Halkomelem language. The Matsqui speak Halq'emeylem and Nooksack, making them one of only two bilingual communities in the Sto:lo family - the Chilliwack being the other. Nooksack is a Salish language of the country immediately south, in what is now Washington State, and the bilingualism is a record of relationships that predate the boundary: marriage, trade, visiting, shared fishing grounds. Oral history collected by the chronicler P.R. Jeffcott holds that members of the Ska-leih-hes migrated north into what is now Canada, which they understood as a return to where they had originally come from. The forty-ninth parallel, arriving in 1846, cut across all of that without noticing it was there.

## What the Gold Rush Brought

In 1858 miners came up the Fraser from San Francisco by the thousand, and they did not come to negotiate. Encroachment on Salish fishing sites and threats of violence against anyone who objected escalated into the Fraser Canyon War, which ended with Salish resistance broken. The far greater destruction was invisible. Smallpox moved through the Fraser Valley with the newcomers and killed somewhere between half and three-quarters of the Indigenous population of the region - not a statistic but a hollowing-out of families, of villages, of the people who held particular songs and particular fishing rocks and particular stories that no one else could tell. When British Columbia entered Confederation in 1871, the new province broke with practice everywhere else in Canada and largely declined to make treaties. It allotted reserves smaller than federal policy called for. The consequence is that most of British Columbia, including Matsqui territory, has never been surrendered by any agreement - the legal fact that underlies almost everything that has happened since.

## Investigations and Pipelines

In 1994 the Sto:lo communities merged their separate advocacy organizations - formed in response to the federal government's 1969 attempt to dissolve Indian status altogether - into the Sto:lo Nation Society. The work since has been the unglamorous kind: fishing rights, cranberry-picking rights, consultation files. Some of it is heavier. St. Mary's, the residential school at Mission that took children predominantly from Sto:lo communities, ran from 1863 until 1984, the last such school operating in British Columbia; survivors described physical and sexual abuse, and at least twenty-two students died or disappeared. The land has been Sto:lo reserve since 2005, and in 2021 the Sto:lo Nation began a three-year research project into unmarked graves at Fraser Valley residential school sites, including that cemetery. Meanwhile the Trans Mountain pipeline runs through Matsqui territory, and its expansion - C$34 billion, in service since 1 May 2024 - raised the line's throughput from 300,000 to 890,000 barrels a day.

## People of the River

Drive the Matsqui Prairie today and you see dairy barns, blueberry fields, dyke roads and the wide brown Fraser sliding past it all, and nothing obvious announces a nation. That is largely the point. The Matsqui position themselves plainly: others arrived from elsewhere, and the Matsqui have been, are, and will remain of this place. Governance runs through a custom electoral system, with Chief Alice McKay and councillor Brenda Morgan representing the community on the Sto:lo Nation Chiefs Council. Sto:lo territories are still defined by watersheds rather than survey lines, and Matsqui's begins on the Fraser below Sawmill Creek - the same organizing principle that named the easy portage in the first place.

## Flight Context

Matsqui sits at 49.1062 N, 122.3426 W, on the Matsqui Prairie in the northern part of Abbotsford, British Columbia, about 3 nautical miles north of the Canada-United States border. From 2,000 to 4,000 feet AGL the ground reads as a dead-flat alluvial floodplain of rectangular dairy and berry fields enclosed by dykes, bounded north by the Fraser River and east by the forested hump of Sumas Mountain. The Fraser itself is the primary reference - trace it upstream past Mission and watch for the broad south-bank plain between Crescent Island and Sumas Mountain. Mission's Westminster Abbey on the ridge across the river and the Mission railway bridge are useful visual fixes. Abbotsford International (CYXX) lies about 5 nautical miles south with an active control zone; Chilliwack (CYCW) is roughly 17 nautical miles east, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 15 nautical miles west, and Bellingham International (KBLI) about 22 nautical miles southwest across the border. Dense radiation fog fills this floodplain on calm autumn and winter mornings and can persist all day; late spring through early autumn afternoons give the clearest view, with Mount Baker standing to the southeast.
