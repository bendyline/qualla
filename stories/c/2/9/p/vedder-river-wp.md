# Vedder River

A river that changed its course twice - once by accident, once on purpose - and in doing so drained a lake, made a farming empire, and earned a name change halfway along.

**Location:** 49.1271, -122.0988

**Categories:** Tributaries of the Fraser River, Rivers of the Lower Mainland, Rivers of Washington (state), Canadian Cascades, North Cascades of Washington (state)

## Introduction

Most rivers stay where you left them. This one did not. In 1875 a wall of storm-driven logs jammed the channel below Vedder Crossing and shunted the Chilliwack River out of the bed it had used for centuries. Seven years later, someone built a jam on purpose and pushed it farther still. A flood in 1894 made the new arrangement permanent, and engineers then finished the job with dikes and a canal. The result is a river with two names and a manufactured mouth - the Chilliwack above the crossing, the Vedder below it, draining the same snow off the same peaks into a valley that has been rearranged around it.

## Born at Hannegan Pass

The water starts in Washington, high in the North Cascades, at Hannegan Pass inside North Cascades National Park. It runs north as the Chilliwack River, dropping almost immediately through Hells Gorge, a steep slot so difficult to reach that the waterfall inside it is more rumour than destination for most people who hike the valley. The Little Chilliwack River joins before the international boundary. Just across it, the river slows into Chilliwack Lake and builds a wide sandy beach at the inlet - a place generations of locals treated as a private discovery, now largely off limits, with the lake's south shore protected as an ecological reserve. Out the north end, the river turns west and follows the Chilliwack River Valley for the rest of its roughly 80-kilometre run to the lowland.

## Two Names, One River

At Vedder Crossing the Sweltzer River comes in, carrying the outflow of Cultus Lake, and the combined stream passes under the Vedder Bridge. On the upstream side of that bridge it is the Chilliwack River. On the downstream side it is the Vedder. Nothing about the water changes; only the label does, a quirk of settlement history now so ordinary to residents that the strangeness is invisible. The older name carries the older meaning: Chilliwack comes from the Halkomelem Tcil'Qe'uk, understood as "valley of many streams," which is exactly what a pilot sees looking down at the braided creeks feeding in from both walls of the valley.

## The River That Was Moved

Historically the Chilliwack ran north from Vedder Crossing across a broad alluvial fan into the Fraser. The 1875 log jam split it into two small creeks, Vedder Creek and Luckakuck Creek, which was a disaster for farmers who had settled the fan. In 1882 a new jam was deliberately created, throwing the water west toward Sumas Lake, and the great freshet of 1894 - part of the flood that devastated the whole Fraser basin - locked the new course in place. In the early twentieth century the diverted river was diked and channelized into the Vedder Canal, the central instrument in draining Sumas Lake between 1920 and 1924. Roughly 4,000 hectares of lakebed became farmland. For the Semá:th people, whose fishing, hunting, and seasonal life were built around that water, the drainage took away a world. The old distributaries below Vedder Crossing survive as Chilliwack Creek, meandering quietly past downtown to reach the Fraser.

## Salmon, Steelhead, and a Rowing Course

For a rearranged river, the Vedder is astonishingly alive. Chinook, coho, chum, pink, and sockeye return in the fall, and winter and spring bring steelhead anglers who line the bars in weather nobody would choose. Below the Vedder Bridge the gravel flats are a summer institution: swimming, campfires, sunbathing, tubing. The Fraser Valley Regional District runs a campground on the bank from 1 April to 31 October. The canal itself once hosted an international sporting event, when the rowing regatta of the 1954 British Empire and Commonwealth Games was staged here from 30 July to 7 August. Canada's University of British Columbia crew took gold in the eights. Bobby Williams earned bronze in single sculls, and Donald Guest and Larry Stephan took bronze in the doubles - medals won on a channel dug to drain a lake.

## November 2021

The lake has not conceded. In mid-November 2021, an atmospheric river parked over the Nooksack and Fraser watersheds, dikes were overtopped, and Sumas Prairie went back to being Sumas Lake. Highway 1 disappeared under water. Roughly 1,100 homes were evacuated, and farm families and agricultural workers across Abbotsford lost livestock, equipment, and in many cases the season. The Vedder Canal held, but the event made an old point in a new way: this valley's shape is a hundred-year-old engineering decision, maintained daily, and the river remembers the ground it used to cover.

## Flight Context

The Vedder River runs at 49.1271 N, 122.0988 W where it crosses the Fraser Lowland south of Chilliwack. Fly the valley at 3,000 to 5,000 feet AGL from Chilliwack Lake northwest - the lake, the tight forested trench of the Chilliwack River Valley, and the abrupt straight line of the Vedder Canal where the river leaves the mountains make an unmistakable sequence. Cultus Lake sits just south of Vedder Crossing. Downstream, the flat geometric fields of Sumas Prairie fill the old lakebed, with Sumas Mountain rising at its northeast corner. Chilliwack Airport (CYCW) is about 5 nm north; Abbotsford International (CYXX) roughly 15 nm west; Vancouver International (CYVR) about 50 nm west. Valley cloud and rapid ceiling drops are routine in autumn and winter, and the Chilliwack River Valley narrows quickly - do not press eastward toward the lake in deteriorating weather.
