# Sumas Mountain

A 910-metre island of forest rising from the Fraser floodplain, named for the lake beside it that engineers drained in 1924 — and that came back, briefly and catastrophically, in November 2021.

**Location:** 49.0892, -122.1710

**Categories:** Mountains of the Lower Mainland, Abbotsford, British Columbia, Fraser Valley, Indigenous history, Floods and water management

## Introduction

In November 2021 a lake that had been gone for ninety-seven years came back. Water off the Nooksack and the Fraser spilled across the flat ground south of Sumas Mountain and refilled the basin that used to be Sumas Lake, cutting the Trans-Canada Highway and forcing eleven hundred households in Abbotsford out of their homes. From the slopes of the mountain you could watch it happen: dairy barns and berry fields disappearing under grey water, the old shoreline reappearing exactly where the maps of a century earlier said it had been. The mountain is named for that flat place, not for itself. It has always been the thing that watches the valley flood.

## A Big Level Opening

The name is Halq̓eméylem and it means "a big level opening" — a description of the prairie, not the peak. Nineteenth-century writers often rendered it "Smess." Simon Fraser, coming downriver in 1808, wrote it in his journal as "shemotch." What all of them were pointing at was the extraordinary flatness on the mountain's south side, a basin that held a shallow lake of roughly 4,000 hectares reaching across what is now the international boundary. This was Semá:th territory, the homeland of the people known today as the Sumas First Nation, a Stó:lō community whose economy ran on what the lake produced: sturgeon, trout and salmon, waterfowl, and the seasonal abundance that comes with warm shallow water. The mountain rising 910 metres above it was the landmark by which everything else was located.

## Draining the Lake

A catastrophic Fraser basin flood in 1894 concentrated official minds on water management, and in the early 1920s a BC Electric engineer named Fred Sinclair produced the plan that would end the lake. Between 1920 and 1924 the Chilliwack River was redirected west into a new channel, the Vedder Canal, and the remaining water was pumped out through the Sumas Drainage Canal into the Fraser. Where the lake had been there was now some of the richest farmland in British Columbia. What there was not, any longer, was the fishery the Semá:th had lived on, and the lakebed was not returned to them; in 2013 the Sumas First Nation filed a specific claim over that ground. Eight years after the claim, the water came back on its own and demonstrated, expensively, that the basin had never actually agreed to stop being a lake.

## Clay, Brick and Stumps

The mountain itself was never farmland. It was raw material. Its clay seams proved good enough for brickmaking that an entire company town grew around the pits — Clayburn, on the western flank, now folded into suburban Abbotsford but still recognizable by its brick. Higher up in the mountain's middle ground sits Straiton, officially named in 1904 for Thomas Bell Straiton, who had homesteaded there in 1893 and run the store and post office. Loggers worked the slopes; ranchers kept livestock on the benches; gravel operations opened where the rock was right. Most of that is still going. Sumas Mountain is not a wilderness park with a town at the bottom so much as a working hill that happens to have wilderness on top of it.

## Three Kinds of Wet

Botanically the whole mountain sits inside the Coastal Western Hemlock zone, which sounds uniform until you climb it. Three subzones are mapped on these slopes. The south and west faces at low elevation are very dry maritime — sun-baked by Pacific Northwest standards, the kind of ground where Douglas-fir and arbutus feel comfortable. The great bulk of the mountain is dry maritime. Push toward the top and it turns very wet maritime, the dripping, moss-heavy, fog-fed forest the coast is famous for. Chadsey Lake sits in that upper country, and Sumas Mountain Provincial Park protects the northern heights, where the eastern reaches look down over Greendale and the Chilliwack side of the valley. Walking from the Abbotsford suburbs to the summit, you cross climate zones the way you would climbing a much bigger mountain.

## The Shadow That Keeps the Stars

Sumas Mountain works as a divider in more ways than the geographic one that splits the Fraser Valley into Upper and Lower. Abbotsford, Chilliwack and Mission all throw light into the night sky, and the mountain's bulk blocks enough of it that McDonald Park, in its shadow, functions as a dark-sky preserve — a piece of genuinely black sky wedged between three growing cities. There is a second Sumas Mountain across the border in Whatcom County, Washington, which is why this one is sometimes called Canadian Sumas. Two mountains, one name, one drained lake between them, and a boundary line that the floodwater in 2021 crossed without any difficulty at all.

## Flight Context

Sumas Peak stands at 49.0892° N, 122.171° W, on the south bank of the Fraser River between Abbotsford and Chilliwack. Summit elevation is 910 m (2,986 ft) with 875 m of prominence, so it stands clear of everything around it — plan on 4,500 to 6,000 feet MSL for a comfortable circuit with terrain clearance. The mountain is unmistakable from the air: an isolated forested dome surrounded on every side by dead-flat floodplain — Sumas Prairie to the south, Matsqui Prairie to the west, Nicomen Island and Hatzic Prairie across the river to the north. Look for the straight-ruled Vedder Canal and Sumas Drainage Canal cutting the southern prairie, and Chadsey Lake in the upper eastern bowl. Abbotsford International (CYXX) is about 8 nm west and is the obvious base; Chilliwack (CYCW) is roughly 10 nm east, Langley (CYNJ) about 20 nm west, Bellingham (KBLI) about 22 nm southwest across the border, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 40 nm west. The US border runs barely 3 nm south of the peak — know exactly where you are. Valley fog fills the floodplain on clear autumn and winter mornings and frequently leaves the summit in sunlight above a solid white sheet; in summer, afternoon visibility is usually excellent east toward the Cascades and Mount Baker.
