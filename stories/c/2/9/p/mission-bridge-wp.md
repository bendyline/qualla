# Mission Bridge

Sixty years of ferries, planks laid beside railway tracks and one collapsed span explain why the opening of a plain girder bridge drew brass bands and parachutists.

**Location:** 49.1233, -122.3060

**Categories:** Bridges, Engineering, History, Transport, British Columbia

## Introduction

Soldiers dropped out of low-flying aircraft and landed on the deck. There were brass bands, a parade of vintage cars and police cruisers, jets overhead, and fifteen hundred people standing on a new highway bridge in the rain-shadow flatness of the Fraser Valley while Premier Dave Barrett said the words. That was 7 July 1973, and the fuss was not really about a steel and concrete girder bridge. It was about the six decades that had come before it, in which getting a car, a horse team or a truckload of berries across the Fraser between Mission and the south bank had been an exercise in patience, improvisation and occasional disaster.

## The Ferry Named for a Roadbuilder

In May 1911 tenders went out for a ferry to run just downstream of the railway bridge, linking Mission with Elliotville, the south-shore settlement named for its founder William Elliott. The boat that arrived that November was a gasoline-powered double-decker able to carry six teams of horses and their passengers, and it was named the John H. Sprott after a pioneer roadbuilder of the district. Crossing was free between 7am and 6pm; after that you paid. The schedule tightened as the valley filled - hourly six days a week, then seven, then every forty minutes. Forty minutes was still forty minutes, and the lobbying for a bridge began. In 1920 a replacement arrived: the Eena, her steel hull built by Yarrows Ltd for $31,000 and her engine by Westminster Iron Works for $10,080. She ran until 1 July 1927, then went off to work the Agassiz-Rosedale crossing instead.

## Planks Beside the Rails

Canadian Pacific had refused, back in 1910, to let wagons use its new railway bridge. It relented on the day the ferry stopped. From 1 July 1927 the ties were lengthened, planks were laid alongside the rails, and the Fraser crossing became a single lane of motor traffic threaded between trains. It worked for twenty-eight years. Then, in July 1955, high water scoured out the foundations of a concrete pier and a span dropped into the river. Nobody was hurt, but the valley's commercial link was cut, and within days a 90-foot ferry barge with room for twenty cars was running from seven in the morning until midnight. A second tug and barge were added the next summer to move the harvest. The bridge came back in August 1956; fire weakened its centre span in 1970; floods closed it on their own schedule.

## Thirteen Pieces, Barged Upriver

The replacement took four and a half years and never quite kept a completion date, sliding from autumn 1971 to summer 1972 to May and then June of 1973. CBA Engineering designed it and oversaw the $15 million project: twenty spans in all, eleven piers on land and eight standing in the river. The distinctive work happened in Vancouver, where Canron prefabricated thirteen structural steel sections for the box girders of the three main spans - modules up to 100 feet long weighing as much as 200 tons - and barged them up the Fraser. A floating crane made the first lift in July 1972, raising a 150-ton section forty-four feet long, forty-two wide and twenty deep some seventy feet into the air before it settled onto the north centre pier.

## The Line Down the Middle

The original design called for posts along the median. They were never installed, and the omission had a long and costly afterlife. Head-on collisions on the four-lane deck killed three people and injured twenty-six in 1987 and 1988 alone. Even then nothing changed. It took four more deaths in head-on crashes over four years before a median barrier was finally fitted in 2005 - a strip of steel on a bridge that had been carrying valley traffic for thirty-two years without one. Bridges are easy to read as engineering. They are also a record of which risks a jurisdiction decided it could live with, and for how long.

## What the Bridge Carries Now

At 1,126 metres it is a long, low, unglamorous thing: a 16-metre roadway with two-metre sidewalks, carrying Highway 11 between Mission and Abbotsford. Nobody agrees on what to call it - Matsqui-Mission, Abbotsford-Mission, Mission-Abbotsford all appear in print, the older dual-purpose crossing having been the Mission-Matsqui Bridge. An eight-inch natural gas line has run beneath the deck since 1973; a 2006 application to replace it with a twelve-inch pipe laid under the riverbed was denied. Between 2010 and 2015 a $21 million six-phase seismic retrofit strengthened columns and beams, added restraints and new deck joints, stabilised the slopes and raised the cycling railings. In August 2021, three days of emergency work to restore a single bearing closed the bridge in part and then entirely, which was all it took to remind the valley what the ferry years felt like.

## Flight Context

The Mission Bridge crosses the Fraser River at 49.1233 N, 122.306 W, carrying Highway 11 between Mission on the north bank and Abbotsford on the south. From 2,000 to 4,000 feet AGL it reads as a long, pale, low girder deck set slightly downstream of the older railway bridge, with the two crossings close enough to photograph in one frame - a useful pairing for navigation, since almost nothing else on this reach of the river is vertical. The Abbotsford grid and its farmland sections fill the south side; the town of Mission climbs the slope on the north. Abbotsford International (CYXX) lies a short hop south-southwest and is the obvious base; Chilliwack (CYCW) is east up the valley, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) west, and Vancouver International (CYVR) downstream. Mount Baker anchors the view to the southeast. Expect valley fog on autumn and winter mornings and a brown, high river through the spring freshet - the same water that took out a pier here in 1955.
