# Leq'á:mel First Nation

A Stó:lō nation on Nicomen Slough whose name means "the level place where people meet" — now governing ten scattered reserves, two cemeteries of largely unmarked graves, and the ground where British Columbia's last residential school stood.

**Location:** 49.2040, -122.0343

**Categories:** Sto:lo governments, First Nations governments in the Lower Mainland, Fraser Valley

## Introduction

Leq'á:mel means "the level place where people meet." The name is a description of function, not scenery — flat ground on the Fraser where canoes could land and people from up and down the river could do business, and by all accounts one of the busiest trading stops in Stó:lō territory. It is also thought to be where Halq'eméylem, the upriver dialect of Halkomelem, began. The Leq'á:mel First Nation still governs from that stretch of the valley, with offices at Deroche on Nicomen Slough, east of Mission. What has changed is the shape of the land beneath the name.

## Ten Pieces of a Territory

The Nation holds ten reserves, and no two of them touch. They are scattered across Abbotsford, Chilliwack, Deroche and Nicomen Island, most of them small, each pinned to a specific feature of water. Holachten No. 8 sits on the north bank of Nicomen Slough, 102.1 hectares — the largest of them. Skweahm No. 10 lies on the south bank, 69.4 hectares. Aylechootlook No. 5 occupies the confluence of the Sumas River and the Vedder Canal; Lackaway No. 2 the mouth of Wilson Slough; Zaitscullachan No. 9 the bank of the slough that shares its name.

Lakahahmen No. 11, where Deroche Creek meets the Fraser, is the most populated. Three reserves are residential. Two are cemeteries. The remainder are held by members under certificates of possession, leased out for farming, or set aside for economic development — the fragments of a territory converted into a portfolio.

## Graves Without Names

The two cemetery reserves are run by the band, and dozens of the graves in them carry no marker. That is not unusual in the Fraser Valley, and the reasons are not mysterious: wooden markers rot, families were displaced, and record-keeping about Indigenous dead was, for a long stretch of the twentieth century, nobody's institutional priority.

The Nation maintains these grounds itself. It is quiet, unglamorous work — mowing, fencing, keeping the record that exists — and it is also an assertion. Caring for your own dead on your own ground is a form of jurisdiction that requires no one's signature.

## Pekw'xe:yles

Between downtown Mission and Hatzic sits a 10.3-hectare reserve called Pekw'xe:yles. It is the site of St. Mary's Indian Residential School, opened by the Missionary Oblates of Mary Immaculate in 1863 and, after the federal government took it over in 1961, the last residential school still operating in British Columbia when it finally closed in 1984. Generations of children from across the Fraser Valley were sent there.

The land came back to the Stó:lō as reserve in 2005, and it is shared — not by one nation but by twenty-one. In 2021 a memorial house post was raised on the site for the students who died, the same year the Stó:lō Nation announced a three-year study into the likelihood of unmarked graves at Fraser Valley schools including this one. The ground now holds a daycare.

## Sixty Hectares Back

Also in 2021, sixty hectares of Crown land next to Fraser River Heritage Park and the Pekw'xe:yles reserve were transferred to a society formed by three nations: Leq'á:mel, Matsqui (Máthxwi) and Sumas (Semá:th). The parcel contains pre-contact archaeological sites, which is to say it holds evidence of the people who were there before anyone drew a boundary on it.

The transfer came with an unusual wrinkle. Because the land is not reserve, it remains subject to provincial and municipal law — and most of it was promptly leased back to government to keep running as parkland. Ownership changed; the picnic tables stayed. It is a modest, lawyerly kind of restitution, and it is more than the century before it produced.

## A Language, Counted

Leq'á:mel was called Lakahahmen from 1962 until 2003, when the Nation took its own name back. It governs under a custom election code and sits with the Stó:lō Nation Chiefs Council. Roughly eight hundred people are Leq'á:mel Xwélmexw.

The number that stings is the language one. Between the 2006 and 2016 censuses the share of members with knowledge of Halq'eméylem halved, to 2.2 percent. Hundreds of members speak only English. Yet the Nation names its institutions in Halq'eméylem anyway — its childcare centre is Leq'á:mel Iwesáwtxw — which is how a language stays in a community's mouth even when the census has stopped finding it there. The level place where people meet is still meeting. It is deciding what it will be called while it does.

## Flight Context

Leq'á:mel's administrative centre is at Deroche, British Columbia, near 49.2040°N, 122.0343°W, on the north side of Nicomen Slough about 12 nm east of Mission and 14 nm north-northeast of Abbotsford International (CYXX). From 2,500–4,500 ft AGL the landscape reads clearly: the braided Fraser River runs west, Nicomen Island sits as a flat green plate inside the slough's loop, and the steep, forested wall of the Coast Mountains rises immediately north — the settled ground is the thin bench between them. Follow Highway 7 (the Lougheed) east from Mission along the north bank; the Canadian Pacific line parallels it. Nearby fields: CYXX (Abbotsford International) to the southwest, CYCW (Chilliwack) to the southeast, CYPK (Pitt Meadows) to the west, and CYHE (Hope) about 25 nm east. Valley fog forms readily over the slough and river flats in autumn and winter, and the mountain wall to the north generates rapid cloud build-up — expect the ridge tops in cloud far more often than the valley floor.
