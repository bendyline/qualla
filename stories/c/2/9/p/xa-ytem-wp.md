# X̱á:ytem

A boulder above the Fraser at Hatzic is, in Stó:lō account, three leaders turned to stone - and when archaeologists dug beneath it in 1990 they found a house floor five thousand years old.

**Location:** 49.1514, -122.2517

**Categories:** archaeology, indigenous-history, british-columbia, national-historic-site, sacred-site, heritage

## Introduction

In 1990 a developer started grading a hillside above the Fraser River at Hatzic, east of Mission, for a residential subdivision. On the property sat a large boulder. To the survey it was an obstacle; to the Stó:lō it had never been a rock at all. In their account it is three leaders, fused together and turned to stone by Xexá:ls, the transformers who moved through the world setting things in their proper order. Someone decided the ground deserved a look before the lots went in. The archaeologist Gordon Mohs came out, dug, and found beneath the boulder the floor of a house roughly five thousand years old.

## The Rock That Is Three People

Transformer stones are not monuments and not metaphors. In Stó:lō understanding the landscape of the lower Fraser is a record of things that actually happened - people and beings fixed in place by Xexá:ls, left where they fell as instruction and as reminder. X̱á:ytem is one of the clearest of them, which is part of why it matters so much: the story has an address.

The official heritage documents, written in the careful language of federal designation, end up saying something unexpectedly tender about it. Among the site's character-defining elements are the rock's visibility from several vantage points, its unobstructed sightline east toward Mount Cheam, and - listed as plainly as any architectural feature - "the accessibility of the transformer rock which ensures that people can touch it." That is not how most national historic sites are described. It is a fair measure of what this one is for.

## Five Thousand Years of Floor

The excavation that followed the 1990 discovery was carried out in consultation with the Stó:lō, and what it turned up was not a scatter of tools but a settlement. Archaeologists recorded rectangular pit houses - long structures, occupied over generations - preserved as the outlines of posts, the burned rings of hearths, and packed floors. Around and within them lay trade goods, food remains, storage features, and objects associated with spiritual practice.

That combination is rare. Sites of this age usually yield a fragment of a life; this one yielded the shape of a community, with evidence of where people cooked, what they traded for, and what they held sacred, all in the same ground. It is now counted among the oldest archaeological digs in Canada, and the date - roughly 5,000 years before present - means people were living in a built house on this terrace before anyone had raised a pyramid at Giza.

## Designated for Something Living

Canada recognized the place on 4 June 1992, naming it a national historic site. The wording of that designation is worth pausing over. X̱á:ytem was recognized not only as a habitation site of great antiquity but for its spiritual value to the Stó:lō people - present tense, ongoing, a claim about belief rather than about artifacts. A federal heritage system built largely to commemorate forts, railways and prime ministers was asked here to acknowledge that a rock is three people, and did.

The site was added to the Canadian Register of Historic Places on 25 February 2005. Its heritage statement describes the place as a testament to Stó:lō survival and as a means of teaching connection to land and to creation accounts. The Stó:lō established the X̱á:ytem Longhouse Interpretive Centre on the property to carry that teaching out, under their own direction rather than someone else's.

## S'ólh Téméxw

Stó:lō means river, and the Stó:lō are the river people - their territory, S'ólh Téméxw, runs through the Fraser Valley and up into the lower canyon. Their language is Halq'eméylem, the upriver dialect of Halkomelem, and it is very nearly gone. Fewer than five fluent speakers remain. That did not happen by accident or by drift; residential schools forbade children to speak it, and a language survives one generation of that, not several.

Work continues against the arithmetic. The Stó:lō Shxweli Halq'eméylem Language Program has archived 1,745 words and 667 phrases, building the reference material a revival would need. Which returns you to the boulder above Hatzic, sitting in the open where anyone can put a hand on it. Words can be legislated out of children. Records can be lost, sites paved. But three leaders turned to stone are still standing on the hillside they were left on, facing Mount Cheam, exactly as the account says they should be.

## Flight Context

Located at 49.15N, 122.25W on the north side of the Fraser River at Hatzic, immediately east of Mission, British Columbia - about 80 km east of Vancouver on Highway 7, the Lougheed Highway, and roughly 15 km north of the Washington State border. From the air, look for Hatzic Lake and the flat farmed prairie east of Mission, with the Fraser running broad and braided to the south and the Mission railway bridge crossing it. The site itself occupies a low terrace above the floodplain and is too small to identify from altitude; use Hatzic Lake and the Highway 7 corridor as the reference. Mount Cheam stands prominently to the east up the valley, and the Golden Ears rise to the northwest. Nearby airports: Abbotsford International (CYXX) 15 km south across the river, Chilliwack (CYCW) 35 km east, Pitt Meadows Regional (CYPK) 35 km west, and Vancouver International (CYVR) 70 km west. Best viewing altitude is 2,000-4,000 feet. The Fraser Valley fills with dense radiation fog on calm mornings from October into February, often below 1,000 feet, with the surrounding peaks standing clear above it.
