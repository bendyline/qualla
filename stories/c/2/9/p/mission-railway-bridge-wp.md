# Mission Railway Bridge

A 1909 swing bridge over the Fraser River that once made cars queue behind trains, still keeps a tender on duty around the clock, and quietly stitches two transcontinental railways into a single one-way system.

**Location:** 49.1258, -122.3000

**Categories:** Railway bridges in British Columbia, Bridges over the Fraser River, Swing bridges in Canada, Engineering history, Transport in British Columbia

## Introduction

A barge comes down the Fraser with a load of wood chips, too tall to slip beneath the fixed spans, and the skipper reaches for the VHF radio. On the north bank of the river at Mission, in an office that is staffed every hour of every day, a CPKC bridge tender takes the call, steps outside, and walks out onto the bridge to a control booth perched on the swing span itself. Then a 533-metre railway bridge built in 1909 pivots open on a single concrete pier, the barge slides through a 30-metre gap, and the bridge swings shut behind it. Nothing about the procedure has been automated away. Someone still walks out there.

## Taking Turns With the Trains

For decades this was also how you drove between Mission and the farms of Matsqui and Abbotsford. Before the Mission highway bridge opened, cars used the railway crossing as a one-way alternating route, with traffic lights at each end deciding whose turn it was. The arrangement worked exactly as well as you would expect. Trains outranked automobiles, and when a freight was coming the lights held everyone in place, sometimes for a very long time. Waiting at the bridge was simply part of the rhythm of life in the central Fraser Valley, a shared local grievance that everyone had a story about. The highway bridge ended it. What is left on the rail deck today is a speed limit of ten kilometres an hour, which tells you how carefully a hundred-and-sixteen-year-old structure is asked to be treated.

## The First Way Across

The Fraser is a serious river, and for most of its lower course the nineteenth century simply could not cross it. When the Canadian Pacific Railway put a bridge here in 1891, it was the only bridge over the Fraser anywhere below Siska in the Fraser Canyon, and it stayed the only one until the New Westminster rail bridge went up in 1904. Everything else was ferries. The CPR replaced that first crossing in 1909 with the structure standing today: thirteen concrete piers marching across the channel, roughly 533 metres end to end, with a swing span mounted on the tenth pier counting from the north shore. It is not a graceful bridge. It is a piece of working equipment that has been doing the same job, in the same place, for well over a century.

## Where the Tide Runs Out

Two quieter facts make this spot matter more than its looks suggest. Beneath the north abutment sits a river-level gauge, watched closely every spring during the freshet, when snowmelt from half of British Columbia comes down the Fraser at once and the valley learns again what the river is capable of. And the bridge marks a boundary you cannot see: downstream of here the Fraser is increasingly worked by tides pushing in from the Strait of Georgia, while upstream it is river and nothing but river. The swing span clears only 4.9 metres above the water when closed. Its pier is shielded by two 46-metre timber protection piers, tapered at both ends, reaching upstream and downstream. Log tows and gravel barges pass under the fixed span between piers five and six. At night, fixed white lights mark piers nine and eleven and both ends of the protection pier.

## Two Railways, One Direction Each

The strangest thing about this bridge is that it carries no main line at all, and yet the national rail network would seize up without it. It anchors the southern end of a 249-kilometre directional running zone through the Fraser Canyon, an arrangement in which CPKC and Canadian National stop behaving like competitors and start behaving like a single two-track railway. Their lines run up opposite banks of the Fraser and Thompson rivers, swapping sides at the Cisco Bridges near Siska. Loaded export trains bound for the Port of Vancouver take the flatter CN grade down the eastern bank. Empties, containers and automobiles climb back out on the hillier CPKC line up the western bank. The Mission Railway Bridge is where trains change companies' rails at the bottom of that loop, which is why a tender is on duty here at three in the morning.

## Flight Context

The bridge crosses the Fraser at roughly 49.126 N, 122.300 W, linking Mission on the north bank to the Matsqui flats of Abbotsford on the south. Overfly at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL for the best view of the swing span and pier line. From the air the pairing is unmistakable: two parallel crossings a short distance apart, the modern Highway 11 Mission Bridge and this longer, lower, dark steel rail bridge on its row of thirteen concrete piers. On the bluff above the north bank, the white bell tower of Westminster Abbey is the most visible landmark in the district and makes an excellent visual fix from ten miles out. South of the river the land goes dead flat and geometric, a grid of Matsqui Prairie farm sections; north of it the Coast Mountains climb immediately. Nearest airports are Abbotsford International (CYXX) about 7 nm south-southwest, Chilliwack (CYCW) about 14 nm east, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 17 nm west-northwest, Langley Regional (CYNJ) about 17 nm southwest, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 35 nm west. Expect dense radio-controlled traffic in the Abbotsford circuit close by. Morning valley fog along the river is frequent from September through February and can hide the bridge while the mountains above stay clear; the freshet months of late May and June make the river itself the most dramatic thing in the frame, wide, fast and heavy with silt.
