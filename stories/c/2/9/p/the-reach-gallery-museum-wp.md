# The Reach Gallery Museum

Abbotsford's public gallery and regional archive, holding the photographic record of a valley that drained a lake in 1924 and watched it come back in 2021.

**Location:** 49.0528, -122.3270

**Categories:** Art museums and galleries in British Columbia, Museums established in 2008, Buildings and structures in Abbotsford, British Columbia, Archives, Fraser Valley

## Introduction

In November 2021 a lake reappeared in Abbotsford that had not existed since 1924. Rain-swollen water from the Nooksack and the Fraser poured onto the Sumas Prairie, submerged a stretch of Highway 1, and forced 1,100 homes to evacuate. The farms out there sit on a lakebed. Everyone knew that in an abstract way; the flood made it literal. A few kilometres away, in a low building near the airport, sits the institution that holds the photographs of the lake being drained in the first place - The Reach Gallery Museum, which is a contemporary art gallery and the Fraser Valley's regional archive at the same time, and whose two halves keep turning out to be the same job.

## Semá:th Xhotsa

Before the engineers, there was a shallow lake of roughly 4,000 hectares that swelled and shrank with the seasons. In Halkomelem it was Semá:th Lake, and the Semá:th people - a community of the Stó:lō - lived by it, ate from it, and shaped their year around it. The catastrophic Fraser flood of 1894 convinced the province that the valley floor should be farmland instead. Between 1920 and 1924, BC Electric engineer Fred Sinclair ran the project that pumped and diked the lake out of existence, exposing some of the most fertile soil in Canada. What the Semá:th lost is not recoverable in a museum case, but the record of how it happened - the survey plans, the newspaper accounts, the photographs of dredges and dikes - is exactly the sort of thing a regional archive exists to keep.

## Under Budget, Which Never Happens

The Reach broke ground in June 2007 and opened its doors on September 22, 2008. The finished building runs 20,000 square feet, built to LEED standards, with 6,100 square feet of it given over to exhibition: a Great Hall plus two community galleries where local groups get wall space that would cost them dearly anywhere else. Behind the public rooms are a multi-purpose studio, a flexible programming space, and two climate-controlled vaults where the archive and the artifact collection actually live. Total cost came to about $10.5 million - roughly $345,000 under the original estimate. Anyone who has watched a civic building project knows how rare that last sentence is.

## Voices of the Valley

In 2015 The Reach opened a permanent history exhibition called Voices of the Valley, built together with the Matsqui-Sumas-Abbotsford Museum Society - now the Heritage Abbotsford Society, which also runs the Trethewey House Heritage Site. The approach was collaborative rather than authoritative: a valley telling its own story in its own voices, including the ones that institutional history had tended to leave out. It worked. In 2016 the exhibition won a Governor General's History Award for Excellence in Community Programming, a national honour that does not usually travel to a city of Abbotsford's size.

## A Small Gallery That Exports

Most regional galleries import their shows. The Reach does the opposite often enough to be worth noting. In 2017 it organized and circulated Ready Player Two, pairing Sonny Assu - a Ligwilda'xw Kwakwaka'wakw artist whose work runs Indigenous form through the grammar of pop culture and video games - with the ceramicist Brendan Tang. The exhibition toured Canadian venues through 2020. The following year the BC Museums Association gave The Reach its Award of Merit for Excellence in Exhibitions for Grand Theft Terra Firma. Shows conceived in Abbotsford, in other words, get seen in cities that think of themselves as the centre.

## Three Thousand Photographs and Counting

The archive went online in 2010 with an initial 3,000 images spanning the 1890s to the 1980s, and it has kept growing - a valley's worth of barn raisings, berry harvests, storefronts, and floods, searchable from a kitchen table. Alongside it runs Emerge, relaunched under that name in 2015, which gives Fraser Valley artists and arts workers between 16 and 30 the professional footing that usually requires moving to Vancouver: mentorship, exhibition opportunities, real practice. In 2018, for its tenth anniversary, the gallery pushed art out of the building entirely with Ramble: Art Outdoors. The Reach calls itself the cultural heart of the Fraser Valley. For a region that spent a century being described mainly as farmland between two cities, the claim is less boast than correction.

## Flight Context

The Reach sits at 49.0528°N, 122.3270°W on Veterans Way in central Abbotsford, British Columbia. The easiest approach reference in the province is directly beneath you: CYXX Abbotsford International lies about 3 km south of the gallery, home of the Abbotsford International Airshow, so expect circuit traffic and check NOTAMs before sightseeing overhead. Fly the area at 2,500-4,000 ft AGL. Mount Baker's snow cone rises 10,781 ft to the southeast and orients everything; the flat green rectangle of the Sumas Prairie - the drained lakebed - spreads east toward Sumas Mountain and the international border, and the Fraser River curls along the city's north edge. Other nearby fields: CYNJ Langley Regional roughly 20 km west, KBLI Bellingham International about 35 km southwest across the border, and CYPK Pitt Meadows some 35 km northwest. Fraser Valley fog is the local hazard, pooling on the prairie on calm autumn and winter mornings and often clearing by late morning; summer afternoons deliver long, clean visibility with Baker sharp on the horizon.
