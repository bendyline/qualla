# Abbotsford, British Columbia

British Columbia's largest city by area sits on a drained lakebed at the US border — and in 2021 and again in 2025, the lake came back.

**Location:** 49.0517, -122.3289

**Categories:** Abbotsford, British Columbia, Cities in British Columbia, Populated places in Greater Vancouver, Populated places established in 1892, Floods, Sikh heritage

## Introduction

On 16 November 2021 a lake that had been gone for ninety-seven years came back. An atmospheric river pushed the Nooksack and the Fraser past their banks, water spread across the flat farmland of the Sumas Prairie, and before it stopped the old outline of Sumas Lake had become legible again from the air: Highway 1 under water, 1,100 homes evacuated, the city calling the situation catastrophic. In December 2025 another atmospheric river did it again, and the Sumas flats emptied of people a second time. Abbotsford is a city of 153,524 on the Canada–United States border, the most populous municipality in British Columbia outside metropolitan Vancouver and the largest of any by area. It is also a city partly built where a lake used to be, and the lake has never entirely agreed to the arrangement.

## What Fred Sinclair Drained

Sumas Lake covered roughly 4,000 hectares and reached across the international boundary into Washington. It was shallow and warm, thick with sturgeon, trout and salmon, ringed by wetland that pulled migrating birds down in enormous numbers. For the Semá:th, a Stó:lō people whose territory this is, the lake was not scenery — it was the food supply, and the rhythm of the year was built around it. After a catastrophic Fraser basin flood in 1894, the case for removing the lake altogether gathered force, and between 1920 and 1924 a scheme designed by BC Electric engineer Fred Sinclair carried it out. The Chilliwack River was diverted west into a newly cut Vedder Canal; the lake itself was drained through the Sumas Drainage Canal into the Fraser near the northeastern flank of Sumas Mountain. The works ran massively over budget. They also stripped the Sumas First Nation of the resource their community had rested on, and a specific land claim for the former lakebed has been pursued for decades since.

## A Combination of Two Ideas

The Royal Engineers surveyed this country in 1858, pulled in by gold on the Fraser, and the road they laid out — Yale Road, now Old Yale Road — became the first route to link the valley end to end. Butter, milk and tobacco were coming off the land by the late 1860s. In 1889 a former Royal Engineer named John Cunningham Maclure applied for a Crown grant on the 160 acres that would become the town. Where the name came from has never been settled. The usual account credits Henry Braithwaite Abbott, western superintendent of the Canadian Pacific Railway and a family friend. Maclure's sons insisted the real source was Abbotsford House, Sir Walter Scott's home, and said their father put the stress on the second syllable. Maclure himself, late in life, said the naming had been a combination of two ideas.

## The Temple on South Fraser Way

The Gur Sikh Temple at 33089 South Fraser Way was built in 1908 and opened in 1911, and it is the oldest Sikh temple in North America — a distinction it holds partly by outlasting the competition. Vancouver's Second Avenue temple has been demolished. The Golden temple of 1905 burned. This one still stands, wooden and unshowy, a National Historic Site now well past its hundredth year. What it marks has since reshaped the city entirely. The 2021 census counted 43,190 Abbotsford residents born outside Canada, 28.6 percent of the population, and more than half of them — 23,830 people — came from India. Among Canadian census metropolitan areas, only the Greater Toronto Area and Greater Vancouver have a higher proportion of visible minorities than Abbotsford–Mission.

## Berry Country, Border Country

The city's coat of arms carries diagonal crosses, a visual pun on Abbotsford as a place where routes meet, with a strawberry blossom at the centre for the berry industry that has defined the valley floor for generations. The flag was blue until 1995, when the District of Abbotsford merged with the District of Matsqui to form the city as it now exists and the colour changed to green. That was the second amalgamation; the District of Sumas had already been folded in back in 1972. The result covers 375 square kilometres — more ground than any other city in the province — running from the slopes of Sumas Mountain down across the prairie to the international boundary itself. Sumas, Washington, sits directly across the line. Mount Baker fills the southeastern sky. For nine consecutive years Statistics Canada has named Abbotsford the most generous city in Canada by charitable donations.

## Two Hundred Thousand on the Tarmac

In September 1984, Pope John Paul II celebrated an open-air Mass at Abbotsford International Airport before a crowd of more than 200,000 — larger than the city's entire population today. That airfield is still where Abbotsford gathers; the Abbotsford International Airshow has run there for decades and remains the event most outsiders associate with the place. Rail came first, though. A CPR line built through here in 1891 connected Mission with the Seattle, Lake Shore and Eastern Railway at Sumas, Washington, and until 1904 it was the only rail link between Vancouver and Seattle. The Village of Abbotsford was incorporated in 1892. The BC Electric Railway's Interurban tram reached the town in 1910 and ran until 1950, and its successor, BC Hydro, still holds the right to put passenger service back on the line.

## Flight Context

Abbotsford sits at 49.0517°N, 122.3289°W in the eastern Fraser Valley, with the Canada–United States border as its southern limit. View it from 3,000 to 5,000 feet AGL, which is high enough to take in the whole sweep from Sumas Mountain to the boundary. Navigation landmarks: the glaciated cone of Mount Baker dominates the southeast in Washington, the Coast Mountains wall off the north, the Fraser River runs along the northern edge toward Mission, and the dead-flat rectangle of the Sumas Prairie — the drained lakebed — is unmistakable against the surrounding rolling ground, cut by the Vedder Canal and the Sumas Drainage Canal. The clear-cut border swath runs arrow-straight along the southern city limit. Abbotsford International (CYXX) lies inside the city and is controlled and busy; expect temporary restrictions during airshow periods. Langley Regional (CYNJ) is about 12 nm west, Chilliwack (CYCW) roughly 18 nm east-northeast, and Bellingham International (KBLI) about 18 nm southwest across the border. Valley fog is frequent from late autumn through winter; atmospheric-river events in November and December bring heavy rain and, on the prairie, standing water that can redraw the old lake within hours.
