# Clayburn, Abbotsford

British Columbia's first company town was paid half a cent a brick to take its own factory apart - and the village it left behind is now a heritage conservation area and a favourite film set.

**Location:** 49.0833, -122.2670

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in Abbotsford, British Columbia, Company towns, Heritage conservation areas, Industrial history, British Columbia

## Introduction

The company paid half a cent a brick. Through the 1930s the people of Clayburn dismantled the plant that had made their village possible, working through eleven kilns - six round, five rectangular - the tunnel dryer, the office, the storage buildings, and at last the massive smokestack with CLAYBURN and 1911 set into it. Each brick had to be knocked clean of mortar and stacked before it counted. By 1940 the twenty-acre plant site north of Clayburn Road held nothing but a few ruined foundations. Across the road, the village those same bricks had built was still standing. It is standing now.

## A Burn Full of Clay

Charles Maclure found the clay first - deep fireclay deposits in the flank of Sumas Mountain, the kind that could take furnace heat without cracking - and brought in the investors who turned the discovery into the Vancouver Fireclay Company in 1905. The company stamped its product with a brand name, Clayburn, and the brand name eventually swallowed everything around it. The word itself is a small piece of Scottish grammar dropped into the Fraser Valley: Kelly Creek runs down off Sumas Mountain, across Matsqui Prairie and into the Fraser, and burn is the Scots word for creek. A creek running through clay. In 1906 a post office opened inside Charles Purver's store, the place needed a name on the map, and Clayburn it became. The bricks went a good deal further than the name did. Firebrick and building brick from this valley were shipped worldwide and went into building after building in Vancouver, and by 1918, reorganized as Clayburn Company Limited, the operation dominated the brick trade in British Columbia. At its height the plant employed as many as 180 men.

## Seven Houses Facing the Mountain

The first row of brick houses was not thrown up the way company housing usually was. Samuel Maclure - Charles's older brother and one of the most accomplished architects in the province - is believed to have laid out both the townsite concept and the original seven dwellings: a house for the plant manager, a house for the accountant, and five foremen's cottages. The Arts and Crafts influence shows in the shingle work, the window detailing and the interiors, and the houses are set at angles that give each one the view east to Sumas Mountain. Nothing else in British Columbia looked quite like that row. The hierarchy was legible anyway. Behind Clayburn Road, on Armstrong Avenue, came housing the village knew as the six green houses and the six red shacks. Everyone rented from the company: three to five dollars a month for a brick house in 1909, eight dollars for a foreman's cottage after the postwar inflation of 1920. When four more cottages were added, two of them were built from clinker brick - overfired rejects, warped and blistered, which builders of the day prized for exactly that.

## The Year the Kilns Went Cold

In 1930 the Clayburn plant shut down and production moved to the company's sister works at Kilgard, which Charles Maclure had founded in 1913 and Clayburn had bought in 1918. No single reason explains it. The Depression was closing in and running two plants was expensive; Clayburn's infrastructure was ageing; Kilgard sat closer to the clay inside Sumas Mountain; and the train trestles hauling clay down from Straiton needed work nobody wanted to pay for. Charles Maclure himself had been pushed out of the company back in 1909, replaced as plant manager by John Browne Millar, whose arrival had touched off a building boom - about a dozen new structures in a single year. Now it ran the other way. Workers stayed in the company houses and rode out to Kilgard to work. During the Second World War the company sold the houses off, offering them first to Clayburn's own veterans at three hundred dollars apiece before opening the rest to the public. The soccer pitch, tennis court and small golf course the Clayburn Athletic Association had built in 1917 are long gone.

## Hallmark Comes to Clayburn Road

What survived is the part that is hardest to manufacture: an intact early-twentieth-century village. Clayburn is now a municipally designated Heritage Conservation Area, and the church the company gave the land and bricks for in 1912, along with the 1907 schoolhouse, carry provincial heritage designation. The Clayburn Village Community Society - descended, improbably, from that 1917 athletic association, reactivated in 1967 and renamed in 1992 - owns the church, the school and the park, and funds the upkeep by renting them out for weddings, events and film shoots. Location scouts adore the place. The schoolhouse and the storefront on Clayburn Road turn up regularly in Hallmark Christmas movies, snow trucked in as required. The store itself has been the hub of the village since the Cooper family opened a general store there in 1911 and ran it until 1972; it reopened as the Clayburn Village Tea House in 1984 and trades today on British candy and sweets from elsewhere. In the basement of the restored schoolhouse, volunteers keep a museum with a scale model of Clayburn as it looked around 1920, back when the smokestack still stood.

## Flight Context

Clayburn lies at 49.0833 N, 122.267 W, tucked against the western foot of Sumas Mountain inside the City of Abbotsford, British Columbia. Sumas Mountain is the navigation anchor - an isolated forested hump rising out of otherwise flat farmland - and the village sits on its lower western slope where Clayburn Creek comes down onto Matsqui Prairie. Fly it at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL: the heritage village reads as a short, unusually regular row of dark roofs along Clayburn Road with a white church and schoolhouse a block west, while the cleared ground on the north side of the road is the old twenty-acre plant site. The Fraser River runs east-west about 3 nm north; Mount Baker fills the southeastern horizon on clear days. Abbotsford International (CYXX) is roughly 5 nm south and is a busy controlled field with airline, cargo and training traffic, so get clearance before wandering in. Chilliwack (CYCW) is about 15 nm east, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 20 nm west, and Bellingham (KBLI) about 20 nm south across the border - mind the boundary, which runs just a few miles to the south. Valley fog is common on autumn and winter mornings, and strong easterly outflow winds can funnel down the Fraser Canyon in winter, making the lee of Sumas Mountain turbulent.
