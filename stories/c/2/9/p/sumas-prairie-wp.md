# Sumas Prairie

Some of the most productive farmland in British Columbia sits on the floor of a lake that was drained a century ago - and that keeps trying to come back.

**Location:** 49.0500, -122.1330

**Categories:** Landforms, Agriculture, Floods, Indigenous history, British Columbia, Washington (state)

## Introduction

In November 2021 the lake returned. It filled the same shallow basin it had occupied for thousands of years, to roughly the same outline, and for a few days the satellite images of Sumas Prairie looked like photographs of a place that had stopped existing in 1924. Barns stood in open water. Highway 1 disappeared under it. The water came, improbably, from the Nooksack River in Washington State, which normally runs west to Bellingham Bay and which had decided, in a week of atmospheric rivers, to run north instead. This is the central fact about Sumas Prairie: it is not really a prairie. It is a lakebed with the plug pulled, held dry by pumps.

## Sema:th Xhotsa

Before the canal, this was a shallow freshwater lake of about 4,000 hectares, ringed by wetlands, swelling far wider in flood season and shrinking in summer. Sturgeon, trout and salmon used it. Migrating birds bred in the marshes, and grizzlies came down to the shores. The Sema:th people, a Sto:lo community, had their centre here - their name comes from the thick grass and reeds standing in the shallows at the lake's edge. As Chief Dalton Silver has described it, people gathered at Sema:th in summer to fish and in winter for ceremony. It was the place everyone came back to. A lake that fills and empties with the seasons looks like an obstacle if you intend to plough it, and like a larder if you have lived beside it long enough to know when the sturgeon run.

## The Engineering of an Absence

The great Fraser flood of 1894 made the argument, and the engineer Fred Sinclair drew up the plan. Between 1920 and 1924 the Chilliwack River was diverted into a new channel - the Vedder Canal - and the lake was drained north through the Sumas Drainage Canal into the Fraser. It was a monumental piece of work and a financial disaster: costs ran far past projections, and the reclaimed ground turned out to be less fertile than the promoters had promised, selling to settlers in the 1930s for sixty to a hundred and twenty dollars an acre. The Sema:th were not compensated for the loss of the lake that had defined them. A century later the Sumas First Nation was pursuing a specific land claim over it. You cannot drain a lake halfway. The decision, once made, was permanent for everyone who had depended on it.

## Living Below the Waterline

What replaced the lake is farmland of real consequence - dairy, poultry, berries, corn - spread across a dead-flat basin between Abbotsford and Chilliwack, with Highway 1 running straight across the old lakebed. Administratively it was the District of Sumas until 1972, when it was folded into Abbotsford; the northeast corner around Yarrow went to Chilliwack. The land stays dry because water is lifted off it, continuously, by pumps at the north end. The record is instructive. Fraser freshets pushed water back over the prairie in 1894, 1948, 1972 and 2007. Extreme rainfall did it in November 1990, in November 2021, and again in December 2025. Between events the basin looks like ordinary agricultural country. That is the trick of it: a floodplain is only a floodplain on the days it decides to be one.

## The Week the Water Won

The November 2021 flood became the costliest natural disaster in British Columbia's history - at least $450 million in insured damage, with total regional losses estimated in the billions. Eleven hundred homes in Abbotsford were evacuated, and ninety more in Huntingdon Village on November 28 when the Nooksack overtopped again. The hardest number to sit with is the livestock: as many as 630,000 animals died, most of them poultry, drowned in barns that farmers could not reach. Dairy herds were driven out through chest-deep water by neighbours in boats. On November 19, with a dike broken and the Barrowtown pumps at risk, the mayor announced that the Canadian Army would build a levee across the gap - a decision that meant deliberately flooding about a dozen homes to keep the water off thousands. Those are the choices a place makes when it lives on the floor of a lake it has agreed not to have.

## Flight Context

Sumas Prairie centres on 49.05 N, 122.133 W, straddling the Canada-US border in the eastern Fraser Lowland. Fly it at 3,500-5,000 feet AGL, high enough to see the whole basin at once - and from that height the old lake is unmistakable: a flat, pale, rectangle-gridded oval of fields sitting several metres below everything around it, hemmed by Sumas Mountain to the north and Vedder Mountain to the southeast. The ruler-straight Vedder Canal marks the eastern edge, and Highway 1 runs dead across the middle. Mount Baker, about 30 nm southeast, dominates the horizon in clear weather and is the single best orientation cue in the valley. Nearby airports: Abbotsford International (CYXX) about 9 nm west, Chilliwack (CYCW) about 10 nm northeast, Bellingham International (KBLI) about 22 nm southwest, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 40 nm west. Note the international boundary along the 49th parallel just south of the prairie. Valley fog and low stratus are routine from October through February - the same season that produces the atmospheric rivers this basin is built to survive.
