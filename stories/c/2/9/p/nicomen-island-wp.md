# Nicomen Island

A Fraser River island with the mainland's first rural post office, a municipality that existed only on paper, and thirty-five kilometres of dike standing between its farms and the water.

**Location:** 49.1644, -122.1167

**Categories:** Islands of the Fraser River, Populated places in the Fraser Valley Regional District, Former municipalities in British Columbia, Flood management, Stó:lo territory

## Introduction

In 1865 a settler named James Codville began handling mail out of a hotel on a river island that went underwater most springs. It was the first rural post office on the British Columbia mainland, and it lasted about a year before the operation moved on to the mouth of the Sumas River. That is Nicomen Island compressed into a single anecdote - early, ambitious, and permanently in negotiation with the Fraser River, which has never fully agreed to the terms. The island sits in the valley between Mission and Agassiz, ringed now by thirty-five kilometres of dike, its farms laid out on silt the river deposited and periodically comes back to collect.

## Four Meanings for One Word

Nicomen - rendered in older sources as Nickcöhrn-men - comes from Halkomelem, the language of the Stó:lo peoples of the lower Fraser. Nobody agrees on what it means. The suggested translations include 'level part,' 'part people travel to,' 'place cut through by a water course,' and 'near a big creek,' and the striking thing is that all four describe the place accurately. The island is level. People travel to it. A watercourse - Nicomen Slough - cuts it away from the mainland on the north. Several First Nations reserves lie on the island and around it. To the north beyond the slough rises Nicomen Mountain in the Douglas Ranges; to the south, across the main channel of the Fraser, stands Chilliwack Mountain. Dewdney occupies the northwestern end, about 11 kilometres east of Mission by road. Deroche holds the northeastern end, some 29 kilometres west of Agassiz.

## Raiders and a Warning Shot

Spanish ships may have made contact along this coast in the 1790s; fur traders certainly did a decade later. But the pressure that mattered most to the Halkomelem communities of the middle Fraser came from the north. Haida war canoes travelled up the river to raid villages, plundering them and carrying people off into slavery - a practice widespread on the Northwest Coast and devastating for the communities on the receiving end of it. When the Hudson's Bay Company built the first Fort Langley in 1827, its officers discovered quickly that the local people's priority was not trapping beaver but defending their families. The fort eventually fired on a passing war party. The raids fell off afterward. In 1846 the Company opened a fish-curing plant at the mouth of the Chilliwack River, opposite the island's eastern end, where local families brought canoe loads of salmon; it ran for roughly a decade.

## Landings

Joseph Deroche pioneered the northeastern portion, known then as North Nicomen. The rest of the island was settled by Codville in the mid-1860s. His landing lay about 1.4 kilometres downstream of Deroche Landing, at the foot of what is now McDonald Road, and his business model was the riverbank itself: he sold feed to people passing on the water, wintered their livestock on his 480 acres, and ran a hotel alongside the post office. Samuel McDonald bought the property in 1869. A recognizable community followed. William Brown, a blacksmith and wheelwright living at the western end near Dewdney, served as the first official postmaster from 1890 to 1900. Thomas Patton had a store open by 1895. The island's first school opened mid-island in 1890, doubling as the community hall, and was replaced in 1916. That second building sat empty for more than a decade and was derelict by 2020.

## The Municipality That Existed Only on Paper

In 1892 the stretch from Norrish Creek east to the mouth of the Harrison River was incorporated as the municipality of Nicomen. Then nothing happened. No meetings of consequence, no works, no records worth keeping - the body was inactive from the start and quietly vanished within a few years, leaving a name on old maps and a curiosity in the provincial record. The census of the same year explains why there was so little to govern: only six percent of the island was under cultivation, while 72 percent remained woodland and 22 percent was swamp or rough pasture. Spring freshets came up over the fields often enough that growing crops was the secondary occupation. Livestock could be moved. Wheat could not.

## Thirty-Five Kilometres of Dike

The flood of 1894 was the benchmark against which everything since has been measured, and 1948 came close enough to be terrifying. That year residents drove their animals off much of the island ahead of the water, and then the dike burst - bridges and buildings destroyed, stragglers lifted off rooftops, poultry and livestock lost in numbers the farms felt for years. Thirty-five kilometres of dike now encircle Nicomen, and the honest assessment is that a repeat of 1894 would still come over the top. A 2015 report put the cost of bringing the whole system to modern standards at $65 million. In 2017 the province contributed $6 million toward widening the dikes and $4.5 million toward upgrading the pumps. A proposal floated in 2021 would run a 32-kilometre greenway around the island - a walking route, in effect, along the top of the thing that keeps the island an island.

## Flight Context

Nicomen Island lies at 49.1644 N, 122.1167 W, in the Fraser Valley roughly midway between Mission and Agassiz. From 2,500 to 4,500 feet the island is unmistakable: a long, flat green rectangle of fields and dike-top roads, separated from the north bank by the narrow dark ribbon of Nicomen Slough and from the south by the full width of the Fraser. Highway 7 runs the slough's north shore; Nicomen Mountain rises immediately behind it, and Chilliwack Mountain sits across the river to the south. Look for the perfectly straight grid of the Nicomen Island Trunk Road cutting the island lengthwise. Nearby airports: Chilliwack (CYCW) about 12 nm southeast; Abbotsford International (CYXX) about 14 nm southwest; Hope (CYHE) roughly 24 nm east; Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 25 nm west. Valley fog fills this reach of the Fraser on calm mornings from October through February, and outflow winds through the Fraser Canyon can make the eastern approach rough in winter.
