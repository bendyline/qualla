# Mission, British Columbia

A Fraser Valley city built on cedar and hydroelectricity, holding a five-thousand-year-old Stó:lo village site, a modernist Benedictine abbey, and a name it inherited from a residential school.

**Location:** 49.1337, -122.3112

**Categories:** Cities, British Columbia, Fraser Valley, Indigenous history, Architecture, Industrial history

## Introduction

On a bench of land above the Fraser at Hatzic sits a boulder that, in Stó:lo tradition, is not a boulder at all. It is three leaders, conjoined and turned to stone by the transformer X̱á:ls, and the Stó:lo know it as X̱á:ytem. For most of the twentieth century it was simply a rock in the way of a housing development. Then archaeologists working in consultation with the Stó:lo dug beside it and found the outlines of rectangular pit houses: post holes, hearths, floors, trade goods, the residue of long-term settled life roughly five thousand years old. In 1992 the site was designated a National Historic Site of Canada, and the government arranged to transfer the land to the Stó:lo, who run the longhouse interpretive centre there now. The oldest thing in Mission turned out to be the thing nobody had bothered to look under.

## The Name It Was Given

The city takes its name from Saint Mary's Mission, founded by Oblate priests in 1861 on the slope just east of the present downtown. What the mission became was an Indian residential school, one of the institutions to which Indigenous children across the Fraser Valley and far beyond were sent, separated from their families and their languages. The school is gone. The land it stood on is now the Peckquaylis reserve, held by Stó:lo communities, and the name of the town it produced is still in daily use on every road sign in the valley. Places carry their histories whether or not the people living in them chose those histories. Mission is unusual mainly in that its inheritance is written into the word itself.

## The Great Land Sale

The town proper begins with an auction. On May 19, 1891, a promoter named James Horne staged what he called the Great Land Sale, bringing buyers in by Canadian Pacific Railway from Vancouver and from eastern Canada to bid on commercial lots and residential parcels laid out on paper above the river. The railway made it possible, and the railway bridge that opened that same year did double duty for the next eight decades, a swing-span structure that carried alternating one-way road traffic across the Fraser until the Mission Bridge finally took over in 1973 with Highway 11. The municipal boundaries took nearly as long to settle: the District of Mission was incorporated in 1892, a separate Village of Mission was carved out of it in 1922, the two were folded back together in 1969, and it was not until March 29, 2021, that the whole thing was finally reclassified as a city.

## Cedar, Current, and Strawberries

Three things built the local economy, and two of them came out of the same forest. Red cedar shakes were the specialty, split and shipped out of mills along the river, and even now more than forty percent of the municipality is working forest managed under Tree Farm Licence 36, a rare arrangement for a Canadian city to hold in its own name. Upstream at Stave Falls, dams turned the river's drop into electricity. On the flats, growers put in strawberries. The tree farm has since acquired a second life: the trail network threaded through it has made Mission a mountain biking destination, which is a strange fate for a woodlot and a welcome one. In 1972 a federal medium-security penitentiary, Mission Institution, opened here too, an employer of a different kind. By the 2021 census the city counted 41,519 residents spread across 227.65 square kilometres, most of it green.

## A Norwegian Modernist on the Hill

Drive up the hill northeast of downtown and the skyline changes completely. A community of Benedictine monks moved from Burnaby to this ridge in 1954, and the abbey, church and seminary they commissioned came from the Norwegian architect Asbjørn Gåthe. It is not the stone-and-buttress monastery anyone expects. The abbey church, completed in 1982, is uncompromisingly modern, set on a hill with a long view up the Fraser valley, and the monks who live there operate the Seminary of Christ the King, whose high school division is the only Anglophone minor seminary in Canada and the only one anywhere run by Benedictines. Bells, a valley, plainchant, and hard geometric concrete: it should not work, and it does.

## Who Comes From Here

For a place of forty thousand people, Mission has sent an implausible number of them out into the wider world. The swimmer Brent Hayden grew up here and went on to four Olympic Games and a bronze medal in London in 2012, having held Canadian records in the 50, 100 and 200 metre freestyle and won the world championship in the 100 in 2007. The high jumper Debbie Brill came from Mission too. So did Carly Rae Jepsen, whose career began as a finalist on a national television singing contest. The town keeps the ordinary institutions of its own life running alongside all that: a soccer club founded in 1974 that marked fifty years in 2024, continuing a Mission side's four earlier Pakenham Cup wins (1924, 1925 and 1950-51), and a sister-city relationship with Oyama, in Shizuoka, Japan, since October 1996. It is a working valley town that happens to produce Olympians.

## Flight Context

Mission sits at 49.1337 N, 122.3112 W on the north bank of the Fraser River, about 65 km east-southeast of Vancouver. Best viewed from 3,000 to 6,000 feet. Identify it by the pair of bridges over the Fraser just downstream of the townsite, the 1891 swing-span railway crossing and the 1973 Mission Bridge carrying Highway 11 south to Abbotsford, and by the modernist bulk of Westminster Abbey standing alone on the ridge northeast of the centre. Further landmarks: Hatzic Lake and the X̱á:ytem site immediately east, the Stave Lake reservoir system stacked to the northwest, and Mount Baker's glaciated cone dominating the horizon 40 nm to the south across the American border. Nearest airports are Abbotsford International (CYXX) roughly 8 nm south, Chilliwack (CYCW) about 16 nm east, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) about 17 nm west, and Vancouver International (CYVR) some 33 nm west. Valley fog and low overcast are common October through March; the crispest views come on clear winter afternoons when Baker is out and the Coast Mountains stand sharp to the north.
