# Gur Sikh Temple

Abbotsford's 1911 gurdwara, built by mill hands who carried the lumber uphill on their backs, is the oldest Sikh temple still standing in North America.

**Location:** 49.0511, -122.3070

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Abbotsford, British Columbia, Gurdwaras in British Columbia, Sikhism in Canada, Sikhism in North America, Sikh places, National Historic Sites in British Columbia, Religious buildings and structures in British Columbia, Museums in British Columbia, Immigration history

## Introduction

They carried it up the hill on their backs. Board by board, from a mill down in the valley, the Sikh labourers of Abbotsford hauled the lumber for their gurdwara to a hillside lot they had bought themselves — men who spent their working days in sawmills and on Fraser Valley farms and their evenings building something meant to outlast them. What opened on 26 February 1911 looked, from the street, like any other two-storey wooden house in a British Columbia mill town: gabled roof, plain siding, no dome, no arches. That restraint was a kind of survival strategy. It is now the oldest gurdwara still standing anywhere in North America.

## Third Built, First to Survive

Sikh pioneers reached the Abbotsford area in 1905, taking work on farms and in the lumber industry — the jobs available to South Asian men in British Columbia at the time, and largely the only ones. By 1908 they were making plans for a gurdwara of their own.

Two had been built in Canada before theirs. The first went up in Golden in 1905, in the Columbia Valley, and burned down in 1926. The second was inaugurated on 19 January 1908 at 1866 West 2nd Avenue in Vancouver's Kitsilano, close to the False Creek sawmills where its congregation worked; it was demolished in 1970 when the Khalsa Diwan Society moved to a new temple on Ross Street. Abbotsford's was third to be built and is the only one of the three still on its foundations.

## A House That Is Not a House

The building reads as domestic because it was built by men with sawmill skills, using sawmill materials, in a town that had no vocabulary for anything else. The ground floor held the langar — the free community kitchen where anyone who comes is fed, without exception, one of Sikhism's defining practices. The prayer hall occupied the storey above.

It grew as the congregation did, with an expansion in 1932 and another during the 1960s. Nothing about it is monumental. Everything about it is deliberate: a place on a hill, paid for out of labourers' wages, put up at a time when the province was actively working to ensure there would be no more labourers like them.

## The Doors That Closed

The gurdwara's first decades ran alongside a determined campaign to shut South Asian migration down. In 1908 Ottawa introduced the continuous journey regulation, which barred anyone who had not travelled to Canada directly from their country of birth — a condition no steamship route from India could meet. It was written to be impossible, and it worked.

On 23 May 1914 the Komagata Maru arrived in Burrard Inlet carrying 376 passengers who intended to test the rule. They were held aboard within sight of the city for two months, while the courts weighed their case; the ruling went against them on 6 July, and on 23 July the ship was forced back out to sea; only a couple of dozen, all of them former Canadian residents, were permitted to land. For the families in Abbotsford, this was not distant news. Sons and brothers could not join them, and men already here could not vote: South Asians who served in the Second World War regained the franchise in British Columbia in 1945, and the rest in 1947. Through all of it, the building on the hill was where the community gathered, ate and held itself together.

## Two Temples, One Road

For its first sixty-four years the gurdwara belonged to the Khalsa Diwan Society in Vancouver, founded in 1906. In 1975 it was transferred to the Khalsa Diwan Society Abbotsford, which wanted the autonomy to run its own affairs.

By the 1980s the congregation had long outgrown the 1911 rooms. Rather than demolish or enlarge, they built across the street: a new temple opened in 1983, far larger and in a wholly different architectural idiom — the domes and arches the original had never had. The two stand facing each other now, separated by a road and seventy-two years, and the old one was kept precisely because it was small and plain and first.

## Museum on the Ground Floor

On 26 July 2002 the 1911 building was designated a National Historic Site of Canada in a ceremony led by Prime Minister Jean Chrétien. Restoration followed, and the temple reopened in 2007.

The centennial in 2011 brought Prime Minister Stephen Harper, who inaugurated the Sikh Heritage Museum installed on the ground floor — the old langar space turned over to telling the community's story in the room where that story largely happened. Prime Minister Justin Trudeau came on 19 May 2017 to see its Canada 150 exhibit.

Three prime ministers in fifteen years is a considerable amount of attention for a wooden building on a hill in Abbotsford. The men who carried the lumber up that hill would have found it hard to believe, which is roughly the point.
