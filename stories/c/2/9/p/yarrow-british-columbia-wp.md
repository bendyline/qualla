# Yarrow, British Columbia

A village built on the drained floor of Sumas Lake by Mennonite refugees from the Soviet Union - and a reminder, in November 2021, that the lake never really agreed to leave.

**Location:** 49.0799, -122.0470

**Categories:** Neighbourhoods in Chilliwack, Lower Mainland, Mennonitism in British Columbia, Fraser Valley

## Introduction

Yarrow's main street runs across the bottom of a lake. Not metaphorically: until 1924 the land west of the village was open water, roughly 4,000 hectares of shallow freshwater lake fringed with wetland, thick with fish and waterfowl. Engineers drained it, farmers moved onto the mud, and within four years the first Mennonite families arrived from the Soviet Union to buy lots on ground that had been lakebed inside living memory. For nearly a century the arrangement held. Then, in November 2021, an atmospheric river came over the Cascades, the pumps lost, and Sumas Lake reappeared - briefly, catastrophically, and exactly where it had always been.

## Level Place Lake

The Semá:th people, a Stó:lo nation whose territory this is, called the lake Semáts Xácho7 - Level Place Lake. It was shallow enough to wade far out into and rich enough to structure a year around: sturgeon and salmon, waterfowl by the cloud, wetland plants, and a summer shoreline that expanded and contracted with the Fraser's moods. This was not marginal country that happened to be wet. It was the centre of a food economy, and the seasonal rhythm of fishing and gathering it supported had been running for thousands of years before anyone proposed improving it. What the drainage engineers saw as 9,900 acres of wasted agricultural potential was, to the people who lived on its edges, the larder itself.

## Vedder's Ground

The first European claim here belonged to Volkert Vedder, who began pre-empting Crown land in 1862. With his sons Adam and Albert he built the holding to 960 acres through the 1860s, and a further Crown grant in 1878 pushed the Vedder Lands to about 1,200. The family name stuck to the landscape and never came off: Vedder Mountain rises just south of the village, the Vedder River runs past it, and the canal that would eventually carry that river to the Fraser took the name too. In 1910 the British Columbia Electric Railway ran an interurban line from Vancouver out to Chilliwack, skirting the edge of Sumas Lake. One of its stops was called Yarrow. The station existed before the town did.

## The Engineer's Solution

A Fraser basin flood in 1894 set the idea in motion, and BC Electric engineer Fred Sinclair drew up the scheme that finally executed it. Through the early 1920s the province built dykes, diverted the Chilliwack River into the new Vedder Canal, and pushed the lake itself out through the Sumas Drainage Canal into the Fraser. The work was complete in 1924. Twelve thousand acres west of Yarrow opened to the plough, and in the 1930s it sold for sixty to a hundred and twenty dollars an acre. The soil disappointed the people who expected grain and rewarded those who tried dairy and hops. The Semá:th lost their lake outright - the fishery, the waterfowl, the seasonal round - and received nothing for it. Nearly a century later the Sumas First Nation was still pursuing a specific land claim over the lakebed.

## Refugees on a Lakebed

By 1928 much of the reclaimed land belonged to one owner, Chauncey Eckert, and that year he began selling lots to a group of ethnic Dutch-German Mennonites who had fled persecution in the Soviet Union. They arrived with a religious tradition built for exactly this: start over, on hard ground, together. Yarrow became a genuinely Mennonite village - churches, schools, berry farms, a shared language - and it flourished from the late 1920s into the early 1960s. Then prosperity did what persecution had not. Younger Mennonites moved into the wider Canadian mainstream and out of a small rural village, and the people who bought their houses came from everywhere. The community that remains is mixed, about three thousand strong, and officially a semi-rural neighbourhood of Chilliwack.

## What Grows on a Lakebed

On the first weekend of June, Yarrow throws itself a party. The parade steps off at ten on Saturday morning from the community school on Wilson Road, runs the length of Yarrow Central Road, and ends at the Mennonite Brethren church; the park fills with craft booths, church bake sales and barbecues, and the evening moves to a dance at the Community Hall, with a pancake breakfast and an outdoor service to follow on Sunday. Meanwhile, on the edge of the village, the Yarrow Ecovillage has spent two decades building a different experiment in shared rural living, with a cohousing neighbourhood and a working farm. Both of them - the June parade and the ecovillage - are the same old Yarrow idea in different clothing: a small number of people, deliberately choosing to do things together, on ground that used to be under water and one bad November away from being under it again.

## Flight Context

Yarrow lies at 49.0799 N, 122.0470 W, on the eastern edge of Sumas Prairie in the Fraser Valley, about 90 km east of Vancouver. Fly it at 2,000-3,500 ft AGL: the village sits at the foot of Vedder Mountain on the south side, with Sumas Mountain to the northwest and the dead-flat, ruler-straight field pattern of the old lakebed spreading west - the clearest visual evidence anywhere in the valley of drained water, its drainage ditches running in parallel lines to the Vedder Canal. Chilliwack Airport (CYCW) is about 6 nm northeast; Abbotsford International (CYXX) roughly 13 nm west and the main controlled field in the area; Bellingham (KBLI) about 26 nm southwest across the border, and Vancouver International (CYVR) 45 nm west. The valley traps fog and low stratus from October through February, and the Cascade foothills immediately south and east generate rapid cloud build-up on summer afternoons.
