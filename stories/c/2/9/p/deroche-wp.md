# Deroche

A crossing on Nicomen Slough that has been a meeting place for far longer than it has been a village - and which now holds fewer people than it did in 1951.

**Location:** 49.1833, -122.0670

**Categories:** Populated places on the Fraser River, Populated places in the Fraser Valley Regional District, Unincorporated settlements in British Columbia, Designated places in British Columbia, 1860s establishments in British Columbia

## Introduction

Leqʼá꞉mel means the level place where people meet, and before there was a road or a rail line or a name on a map, this bend of the Fraser Valley was one of the busiest trading stops in Stó:lō territory. People came here because the ground was flat and the water was crossable. Everything that followed - the raft, the toll ferry, four generations of bridges, a highway - was a variation on the same idea. Today the place is called Deroche, a strip of houses and a general store on the north shore of Nicomen Slough, 21 km east of Mission at the foot of Nicomen Mountain. In 1951, 262 people lived here. In 2021, 141 did. The crossing is busier than ever; the village is quieter than it has been in a century.

## The Man Who Wintered Cattle

Joseph Deroche ran freight in the early 1860s, and somewhere along the way he noticed that Nicomen Island stayed mild enough to keep cattle through the winter. That observation gradually ate his business. He pre-empted 65 hectares in 1868, became the first resident on the northern part of the island, and let the freighting wind down as the farm grew. His main house stood about 1.8 km due south of where the bridge crosses now. The settlement on both banks was called North Nicomen until 1891, when it took his name instead. He lived to be nearly a hundred, dying in 1922 - the same year a westbound Canadian Pacific passenger train struck an automobile on a private crossing three kilometres west, killing all six people inside. Two of them were his grown children.

## Joe Kelly's Rope

The slough had to be crossed, and for a long stretch the crossing was a raft. By the early 1890s a steel cable guided a toll ferry over the water from a landing on the Leqʼá꞉mel reserve, where Joe Kelly hauled it back and forth by rope. The bridges that replaced him make a fair chronicle of Fraser Valley engineering: a wooden structure of three 13-metre spans in 1894, whose approaches needed rebuilding almost immediately after that year's flood; a raising and lengthening in 1897; then, in 1901, a proper piece of work - a 49-metre Howe truss, three 18-metre A trusses, and thirteen short spans behind them. Rebuilt in 1932. Paved through from the west by 1937. Replaced again in 1957, slightly to the west of its predecessor, which is roughly where the traffic crosses today.

## Forty-Four Years of Mail

From the late 1880s the new arrivals in eastern Nicomen were mostly French-Canadian, which is why the first postmaster, serving from 1891 to 1897, was Ernest DesRochers - a farmer and road worker who lived near the church. Norman McLeod taught the one-room school from 1893 to 1899; the building was replaced in 1914 and enlarged in 1932. Then came the Coopers. Three brothers - Ashley, Austin and Charles - arrived in 1900. Austin ran a general store that did not last and held the post office for a year. Charles J. Cooper took it over in 1903 and did not put it down until 1947, forty-four years of sorting a small community's mail. When fire gutted the store, the community hall and several houses in 1917, Louisa Cooper rebuilt and ran the store herself before leasing it on.

## Water Off the Hill

Charles Cooper also gave the village its plumbing. In 1913 he channelled a supply down from Burnbrae Creek; in the 1930s he switched the source to Deroche Creek and piped it to the houses, installing a small hydroelectric plant halfway down the drop because the water was already falling and he might as well get something out of it. That improvised system ran privately until the regional district took it over in 1993 - and then the mountain objected. Debris flows in the watershed fouled Deroche Creek repeatedly, cutting off supply and wrecking water quality. In 1999 and 2000 the system was rebuilt around a groundwater well instead, which finally settled the question of whether the village could count on its taps.

## Eighty-Five Students

Deroche Elementary is the smallest school in the Mission district, with about 85 children, roughly 65 per cent of them First Nations. That figure is the plainest statement anyone could make about where this place actually sits: the Leqʼá꞉mel First Nation's community and offices are next door, ten reserve parcels spread across Nicomen Island and the valley beyond, and the people who were here when Joseph Deroche's cattle arrived are still here. The village itself has kept only what a village of 141 needs - a hall, a store, a garage and pumps, a handful of small businesses. A neighbourhood plan was published in 2016. In 2015, a Carmelite formation house went up on the hill to the north, which is a strange and rather lovely thing to find above a slough. Highway 7 carries most people straight through without slowing. The level place where people meet still meets its own definition; it just does it at 80 kilometres an hour.

## Flight Context

Deroche sits at 49.1833°N, 122.067°W, on the north shore of Nicomen Slough at the foot of Nicomen Mountain, about 89 km east of Vancouver along the Fraser Valley. Fly the valley eastbound at 2,000-3,000 ft AGL and the geography does the navigating: the Fraser River runs wide to the south, and just inland of it Nicomen Slough splits off to enclose the long flat rectangle of Nicomen Island - the island is the landmark, not the village. Deroche is the cluster of roofs where Highway 7 and the Canadian Pacific main line squeeze between the slough and the rising ground. Look north for the steep green wall of Nicomen Mountain, east for the mouth of the Harrison River and Agassiz, west for Mission on its terrace. CYCW Chilliwack lies roughly 10 km southeast across the Fraser, CYXX Abbotsford about 28 km southwest with a busy control zone, and CYHE Hope some 46 km east where the valley narrows into the Fraser Canyon. CYPK Pitt Meadows is around 50 km west. Autumn and winter bring persistent valley fog and low ceilings that can seal the floor from Mission to Agassiz while the peaks stand clear; summer afternoons are reliably good, though outflow winds funnel hard down the canyon from the east.
