# Westminster Abbey (British Columbia)

A Benedictine monastery above the Fraser River at Mission, where two dozen monks keep the oldest daily routine in Western Christianity inside one of the most aggressively modern churches in Canada.

**Location:** 49.1536, -122.2710

**Categories:** Benedictine monasteries in Canada, Mission, British Columbia, Religious buildings and structures in British Columbia, Christian organizations established in 1939, Seminaries and theological colleges in Canada, Brutalist architecture in Canada, Christianity in British Columbia

## Introduction

Nothing about the building says monastery. The church that crowns the hill above Mission, British Columbia, is raw concrete and sharp angles, a 1982 composition by the Norwegian architect Asbjørn Gåthe that has more in common with a university lecture hall than with anything in a medieval print. Then the bells go, and two dozen men in black walk in to sing the Divine Office, as Benedictines have done at fixed hours since the sixth century. Westminster Abbey is that collision - a fifteen-hundred-year-old routine housed in a structure that looks like it arrived from the future and stayed.

## Five Monks From Oregon

The seminary came first. Archbishop William Mark Duke of Vancouver founded the Seminary of Christ the King in 1931, at Ladner on the Fraser delta, and eight years later he needed someone to run it. Mount Angel Abbey in Oregon sent five monks north in 1939 - among them Father Eugene Medved - to establish a priory and take the seminary in hand. The community took root quickly by monastic standards. Rome recognised the priory as independent in 1948 and raised it to the rank of abbey in 1953, with Medved as its first abbot. The seminary moved with them, Ladner to Burnaby to Mission. A Faculty of Theology opened in 1951, and in 1966 the school received its own university charter, letting it grant the Bachelor of Arts alongside degrees in theology.

## Building It Twice Over

Gåthe began work on the complex in 1953, and the monks moved onto the Mission site the following year. The church itself took nearly three decades to finish - it was completed in 1982 - which is a very Benedictine timescale, the order having never been in a hurry. The formal name is the Abbey of Saint Joseph of Westminster, Joseph being the patron. Inside, the concrete is not left to speak alone. Father Dunstan Massey, the abbey's resident artist, painted frescoes through the building and cast a series of bas-reliefs fixed to the pillars of the church, working the way monastic artists have always worked: slowly, in the gaps of the day, on walls he would pray in front of for the rest of his life. One fresco, The Celestial Banquet, was never finished.

## Ora et Labora

Pray and work. The Benedictine motto is not a slogan here but a timetable, and the work half of it is largely agricultural. The abbey's holdings run to more than seventy hectares of field and woodland on the bluff, and the monks farm it themselves. They keep up to forty head of cattle and a flock of chickens; most of the meat eaten at the abbey comes off its own ground, along with the eggs. It is a small operation by Fraser Valley standards, surrounded by berry farms and dairies running far more intensive businesses. That is rather the point. The farm is not there to turn a profit but to occupy hands between offices.

## The Only School of Its Kind

The Seminary of Christ the King still operates on the hill, in two parts: a minor seminary at high-school level and a major seminary for men in college and theology. The minor seminary is genuinely singular - the only English-language high school seminary in Canada, and the only minor seminary run by Benedictine monks anywhere in the world. Monks do much of the teaching, supported by volunteer instructors, while local diocesan priests take on the major seminarians. Ordinary school infrastructure arrived late and by unusual means. A Canadian Forces field unit helped build the athletic field in 1973 and 1974; the gymnasium-auditorium followed in 1977.

## Received as Christ

Chapter 53 of the Rule of St Benedict instructs that guests be received as Christ himself, and Westminster Abbey takes the instruction literally enough to run a guesthouse. Visitors come for silence, for retreats, or simply to stand on the terrace at the edge of the bluff and look south. The view is the valley's best argument: the Fraser River sliding west in a broad grey band, the flat farmland of Matsqui Prairie beyond it, and on a clear day the white cone of Mount Baker rising out of Washington State. Roughly twenty-four monks live here now. The bells ring several times a day over a valley of a few hundred thousand people, most of whom have no particular idea what the sound means.

## Flight Context

Westminster Abbey stands at 49.1536 N, 122.2710 W, on a bluff on the south edge of Mission, British Columbia, about 300 feet above the north bank of the Fraser River. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL the complex is unmistakable: pale concrete buildings and a tower on an isolated wooded knoll, with cleared pasture on the slopes below and the river immediately south. Use the Fraser itself as the navigation line - the abbey sits where the river bends east toward Dewdney, with the Mission railway bridge just downstream. Chilliwack (CYCW) lies about 30 km east, Abbotsford International (CYXX) 15 km south across the river with its associated controlled airspace, Pitt Meadows (CYPK) 30 km west, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 60 km west-southwest. Mount Baker dominates the southeast horizon on clear days. Valley fog is frequent on autumn and winter mornings, and the bluff can generate mechanical turbulence in a strong westerly.
