# Yarrow Ecovillage

An intentional community farming the drained bed of Sumas Lake, on land that has already been a Semá:th fishery, a Mennonite refuge, a dairy farm — and, for a few weeks in 2021, a lake again.

**Location:** 49.0810, -122.0530

**Categories:** Ecovillages, Rural community development, Neighbourhoods in Chilliwack, Intentional communities in Canada, Rural development in Canada, Utopian communities in Canada, Sustainable agriculture, Fraser Valley

## Introduction

The organic fields at the Yarrow Ecovillage grow on the bottom of a vanished lake. Sumas Lake covered 4,000 hectares of the eastern Fraser Valley and spilled across the international border until provincial engineers took it away between 1920 and 1924, diverting the Chilliwack River west into a freshly cut Vedder Canal and draining what remained toward the Fraser. The lake had been the centre of Semá:th life — sturgeon spawning on sandy banks, salmon, waterfowl by the sky-full, grizzlies on the margins. What replaced it was some of the most fertile farmland in Canada, and eventually a ten-hectare former dairy farm bought in 2002 by a handful of people who wanted to try living on it differently.

## What the Canal Took

The drainage scheme was the work of engineer Fred Sinclair, and it was audacious. Dykes redirected the Vedder River through a purpose-built canal into the Fraser; by 1924 the Chilliwack River had been pushed west into that same channel, and the lake had nowhere left to be. Roughly 12,000 acres opened for agriculture west of Yarrow, and the Barrowtown pump station was eventually built to keep the water out permanently. For the Semá:th people the arithmetic ran the other way. Their lifeways were intimately bound to the lake — it fed them, and its seasonal rhythms structured the year — and no canal could substitute for that. The province had converted a fishery into real estate. A century later the province's own emergency planners would still be reckoning with the fact that the prairie sits below the rivers around it.

## Refugees Buy the Bottom of the Lake

Volkert Vedder had claimed land here as early as 1862, accumulating 960 acres with his sons Adam and Albert and reaching 1,200 with a Crown grant in 1878. By 1928 much of the ground around the new village belonged to Chauncey Eckert, and that year a group of ethnic Dutch-German Mennonites who had fled persecution in the Soviet Union began buying lots from him. They arrived with almost nothing and built a village out of it. Their community flourished from the late 1920s into the early 1960s, after which families dispersed and assimilated into the wider Canadian culture around them. One of Eckert's parcels went to the Heinrichs family, who worked it as a dairy farm. That farm is the ground the ecovillage now occupies.

## Inventing a Zoning Category

The word itself was young. Robert and Diane Gilman coined the framework in a 1991 study for Gaia Trust called "Ecovillages and Sustainable Communities," which led to a conference of the same name at Findhorn, Scotland, in 1995. As Gaia Trust co-founder Ross Jackson put it, "somehow they had struck a chord that resonated far and wide." In the late 1990s a group in the Fraser Valley started asking whether they could build one, and in 2002 they bought the Yarrow dairy farm. The Yarrow Ecovillage Society Cooperative formed that August, growing quickly from six founding members. Their design process, begun in October, ran in a deliberate loop: brainstorming workshops inside the group, consultation with city officials, then public meetings with neighbours — repeated several times over. The patience paid off. In 2006 Chilliwack council approved the plans by granting the property something that had not previously existed: Ecovillage zoning.

## Stacking Functions

The master plan divided the ten hectares into distinct entities. Eight hectares would stay in organic production; a multigenerational cohousing community would occupy part of the rest; and a mixed-use parcel would hold just under 2,800 square metres of commercial space, a seventeen-unit senior cohousing project and a learning centre. The farm has been in operation since 2003, and in 2014 the original Osprey and Ohm Organic Farms gave way to three new commercial ventures — Ripple Creek Organic Farm, Chubby Roots Organic Farm and The Farmacy. The Stewart Creek Food Forest applies permaculture in earnest, layering plants and muscovy ducks so that each element does several jobs at once. The housing took shape through workshops led by Katie McCamant and Charles Durrett of the Cohousing Company from January 2010, and the resulting strata was named Groundswell Cohousing. Its thirty-three units held roughly 100 people as of January 2015, a third of them children. The Chilliwack Chamber of Commerce gave Groundswell its Sustainability Leadership Award in January 2014.

## The Lake Comes Back

In November 2021 the valley reminded everyone what it used to be. Overflow from the Nooksack and the Fraser poured onto the Sumas Prairie, sections of Highway 1 disappeared, and 1,100 homes were evacuated as Sumas Lake refilled its old basin for the first time in nearly a century. The water receded, the pumps caught up, and the fields came back into production. But the event settled an argument that the dykes had only ever postponed. Everything at Yarrow — the Mennonite village, the dairy barns, the food forest and the thirty-three units of Groundswell — sits inside a lakebed that has not agreed to stop being one.

## Flight Context

The Yarrow Ecovillage lies at 49.081° N, 122.053° W, in the Yarrow neighbourhood of Chilliwack near the eastern end of the Sumas Prairie. From 2,500 to 4,500 feet the drained lakebed is obvious: a perfectly flat floor of rectangular fields, ringed by hills that mark the old shoreline, with the arrow-straight Vedder Canal cutting northwest to the Fraser and Highway 1 crossing the prairie east to west. Vedder Mountain rises immediately south, Sumas Mountain closes the northwest corner, and the Fraser River runs along the north edge of the frame. Mount Baker stands roughly 25 nautical miles south-southeast and is the region's best single landmark on a clear day. Nearby airports are CYCW (Chilliwack) about 6 NM east, CYXX (Abbotsford International) about 10 NM west with full services, KBLI (Bellingham International) south across the border, and CYNJ (Langley Regional) further west. Morning fog pools on the prairie in autumn and winter; summer afternoons often bring strong easterly outflow winds funnelling down the Fraser Valley.
