# Matsqui Institution

A federal medium-security prison on the drained floodplain east of Vancouver, built in 1966 on the promise that men could be sent home better than they arrived.

**Location:** 49.0278, -122.3038

**Categories:** Prisons in British Columbia, Correctional Service of Canada institutions, Buildings and structures in Abbotsford, British Columbia, 1966 establishments in British Columbia, Federal government buildings in British Columbia

## Introduction

On the night of June 2, 1981, eight staff members were stranded on a roof at Matsqui Institution, and the building beneath them was on fire. Some three hundred men had taken control of the prison and set seven of its buildings alight; the damage would run into millions of dollars. Corporal Patrick Aloysius Kevin McBride went up and brought all eight down. The Governor General decorated him for it - the second time that year he had been honoured for bravery. Forty-odd years later the institution is still there, still medium security, still holding roughly four hundred men on a stretch of Fraser Valley farmland about seventy kilometres east of Vancouver.

## Ground the River Gave Back

The prison sits on Matsqui Prairie, a floodplain pinned between Sumas Mountain to the east and the Mount Lehman uplands to the west. This was never naturally dry land. The Fraser took it back in the great floods of 1894 and again in 1948, drowning Matsqui Village and the farms around it, and what makes the prairie farmable now is a century of dyking and pumping. The name belongs to the Matsqui people, Stó:lo - People of the River - whose reserves lie in what is now northern Abbotsford, and whose Halkomelem name for the place, Máthxwi, has been rendered both as 'easy passage' and as 'a stretch of higher ground.' The District of Matsqui itself no longer exists as a municipality; it merged with Abbotsford in 1995. The name survives mostly on a prison.

## Built on a Theory

Matsqui opened in 1966, and like most institutions built in that decade it reflects a particular conviction about what prisons are for. The general population lives in a three-storey unit organized around a central core of control posts - a design meant to let a small number of officers supervise a large number of men without the fortress architecture of an older penitentiary. Medium security means a perimeter fence rather than a wall, and movement within the grounds rather than confinement to a cell block. Around 313 people work here. Capacity is 437. A 96-bed living unit opened in 2013 to take some of the pressure off a population that had outgrown the original footprint.

## The Work of the Place

What actually happens at Matsqui, day to day, is school and shop work. The institution runs basic education programming and vocational training - the practical skills a man might carry out the gate with him - alongside the correctional programs that parole decisions turn on. It also operates the Aboriginal Basic Healing Program, built for First Nations men, who are represented in Canadian federal custody at rates far out of proportion to their share of the population. That the program exists at Matsqui, on Stó:lo land, named for a Stó:lo people, is a coincidence of geography with more weight than the Correctional Service's institutional profile lets on.

## What the Record Holds

The written history of a working prison is not a comfortable read. Matsqui's public record is largely a chronology of lockdowns: searches that turn up contraband tobacco and phones, packages thrown over the fence line, homemade alcohol, weapons, assaults, overdoses, men asking to be moved for their own safety. Institutions do not publish the other days. Behind each dated entry are people whose names mostly do not appear - the men inside, their families driving out the Trans-Canada on visiting days, and the officers who go home after a shift on a unit where a bad afternoon can turn into a very bad one. Prisons are among the few public buildings a society constructs and then agrees not to look at.

## Seen From the Air

From above, the place is oddly unremarkable. Long low roofs, a running track, a graded yard, everything set inside a double perimeter fence and surrounded by the green geometry of berry fields and dairy pasture. Matsqui is one of several federal institutions clustered in this corner of the Fraser Valley, close enough to Vancouver for families and lawyers to reach, far enough out that land was cheap when the government went looking for it in the early 1960s. Pilots flying the valley pass over it constantly without registering what it is. That is more or less how it was designed.

## Flight Context

Matsqui Institution lies at 49.0278 N, 122.3038 W, on the Matsqui Prairie in northwest Abbotsford, British Columbia, roughly 70 km east of Vancouver and about 4 km south of the Fraser River. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL the site reads as a compact cluster of long pale roofs and an oval track inside a double fence, isolated in a grid of berry fields, with Sumas Mountain rising to the east and Mount Baker's snow cone on the southeast horizon in clear weather. Abbotsford International (CYXX) is about 8 km southeast - expect its traffic and controlled airspace. Chilliwack (CYCW) is 25 km east, Langley Regional (CYNJ) 25 km west, and Vancouver International (CYVR) about 65 km west. The valley traps fog and low cloud in autumn and winter, and summer afternoons bring a reliable westerly up the river. Note that this is an active federal correctional facility; treat it as you would any restricted ground installation and do not loiter or descend over the site.
