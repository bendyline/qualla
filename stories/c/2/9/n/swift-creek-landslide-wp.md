# Swift Creek Landslide

For years Whatcom County dredged Swift Creek and gave the sediment away free for driveways and foundations - until testing showed what the mountain was shedding into it.

**Location:** 48.9080, -122.2470

**Categories:** Landslides in the United States, Whatcom County, Washington, Geology of Washington (state), Environmental health, Rivers of Washington (state)

## Introduction

For years the deal seemed like a good one for everybody. Swift Creek, on the west flank of Sumas Mountain east of Everson, Washington, fills with sediment faster than it can carry it away, so the county dredged the channel to keep the water off the roads and out of people's houses. The spoil had to go somewhere, and neighbours were welcome to it: free fill, useful stuff, good for a driveway or a house pad or levelling a low spot in the yard. Then, in 2006, the Environmental Protection Agency ran samples on the dredged material. It was up to four percent chrysotile asbestos, averaging 1.7 percent - above the threshold that, in a construction material, triggers worker-protection and disposal rules.

## The Mountain Is Made of the Wrong Rock

Nothing here is anybody's fault, which is part of what makes it so intractable. The bulk of the slide is serpentinite - rock from the Earth's mantle, chemically transformed by water in a process called serpentinization. One of the minerals that grows during that transformation is chrysotile, the fibrous form of asbestos. Sumas Mountain rose as the North American and Pacific plates ground against one another, and by roughly 45 million years ago the nearby Chuckanut Formation had stopped accumulating sediment while the volcanism of the North Cascades was still young. Pleistocene glaciers finished the job, dumping loose, unconsolidated material across the older bedrock. What sits on Sumas Mountain today is a mass of serpentinite resting on conglomerate bedrock with no real bond between them - a slab on a ramp, with the Pacific Northwest's rainfall doing the rest.

## A Slab That Never Stops

The slide runs roughly half a kilometre wide and a kilometre and a half long, and reaches something like a hundred metres deep. Its toe is the fastest-moving part, travelling upward of ten metres in a year. You can read the motion in the vegetation without any instruments. Nothing grows on the oversteepened toe, because nothing gets the chance. Along the flanks, trees lean at angles that no wind would produce. In the middle there is a strange island of undisturbed timber, standing upright and apparently unbothered, riding downhill as a single connected raft on top of the moving ground. At the head of the slide the earth has pulled open into pronounced scarps; along its sides, the ground shears like a fault line. All of it delivers more than 120,000 cubic yards - about 92,000 cubic metres - of sediment into Swift Creek every year.

## Free Fill

The people who took the dredge spoil were doing what their neighbours did and what the county allowed. It was clean-looking grey material, it was free, and there was no reason to think otherwise. In August 2006 the EPA carried out activity-based sampling: rather than testing a scoop of dirt in a lab, investigators measured what a person actually breathes while raking, digging, loading, running or riding a bike across the stuff. From those measurements they estimated cancer risk for adults and children doing the same things for typical durations - and in some scenarios the risk came out above one in ten thousand, a level most health agencies treat as grounds for action. Two years later, sampling outside homes where residents had built driveways and paths from the material found asbestos at up to six percent. In September 2008 the Whatcom County Health Department and the Washington Department of Health issued a joint health advisory. Old spoil piles along the creek banks were sprayed with a green-tinted tackifier to stop the fibres from blowing away.

## January 2009

Then the rain came, as it does. Heavy January rainfall in 2009 pushed the Sumas River over its banks, and when the water went down it left the sediment behind - in fields, along stream margins, wherever the flood had reached. The EPA sampled fourteen locations: upland flood deposits, streamside sediments, and the water itself. Some of those sediments were 27 percent asbestos. Measurements of asbestos in surface water came back above federal drinking-water standards. That July, the county and state extended their health advisory northward to cover the lower reaches of the Sumas River - which flows, as it happens, across the international border into British Columbia.

## No Cheap Way Out

This is the bind that has occupied local, state, regional and federal agencies ever since. Stop dredging, and the creek bed keeps rising until the water goes over the roads and into the houses along it. Keep dredging, and every cubic yard removed is a carcinogenic material that must be handled, transported and stored under rules written for asbestos abatement - at a cost far beyond what a rural county drainage budget was ever meant to carry. Naturally occurring asbestos is not unique to Whatcom County; several counties in California and Virginia fold it into their land-use planning and permitting as a matter of course. What makes Sumas Mountain unusual is the combination: a large, continuously moving slide, a creek that carries its product straight into an inhabited floodplain, and a fibre that travels in both water and air. The agencies keep working on funding and authority. The mountain, meanwhile, keeps moving ten metres a year.

## Flight Context

The Swift Creek Landslide scars the western flank of Sumas Mountain at 48.9080°N, 122.2470°W, in Whatcom County, Washington - about 8 km east of Everson and roughly 10 km south of the Canadian border. Fly it at 2,500-4,500 ft AGL. It is one of the easiest features to spot in the whole valley: an unvegetated grey-tan gash on an otherwise densely forested hillside, half a kilometre wide and running downslope toward the farmland. Use the flat Nooksack Valley as your approach - the checkerboard of berry fields and dairy pasture around Everson and Lynden ends abruptly where Sumas Mountain rises, and the slide sits on that first face. The Sumas River drains north from here into British Columbia; the Nooksack loops west toward Bellingham Bay; Mount Baker stands about 25 km east-southeast and makes an unmistakable heading reference. Nearby airports: CYXX Abbotsford International roughly 15 km northwest, KBLI Bellingham International about 25 km southwest, and the small strip at Lynden a similar distance west. Watch the international boundary and Abbotsford's control zone, both close by. Winter and spring show the slide at its rawest after rain; summer haze and valley fog in the shoulder seasons can hide the whole hillside.
