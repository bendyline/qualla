# Nooksack Giant

A Douglas-fir felled near Maple Falls in 1896 taped out at 465 feet - eighty-five feet taller than the tallest tree alive today, and the measurement has been argued over ever since.

**Location:** 48.9150, -122.1130

**Categories:** natural-history, washington, forestry, history, trees, record-holders

## Introduction

The tallest living tree on Earth is a coast redwood in California called Hyperion, measured in 2019 at 380.8 feet. In the winter of 1896, in a clearing above the North Fork of the Nooksack River in Whatcom County, Washington, a crew stretched a tape along a Douglas-fir they had just brought down and read 465 feet. If that figure is honest, the tallest tree ever recorded anywhere grew beside a homestead outside Maple Falls - and a handful of men with a crosscut saw took it down before anybody thought to measure it standing.

## Four Hundred and Eighty Rings

The tree stood on the Alfred Bruce Loop homestead, a property known locally as Loop's Ranch and now farmed as Alpenglow Farm. Measured on the ground, it ran 465 feet from cut to tip. Its circumference was 33 feet 11 inches - close to eleven feet through at the base. The first branch was 220 feet up, which is to say that the trunk rose the height of a twenty-storey building before it bothered to produce a limb.

Someone counted the rings: 480 of them. That puts the seed in the ground around 1416, generations before any European had seen this coast. It was already an old tree when Vancouver's ships worked these waters, and it kept growing through the whole of the nineteenth century until a winter morning in 1896, when the saw went in.

## A Slice of It on a Street Corner

A cross-section was hauled into town and set up at the corner of Railroad Avenue and Holly Street in New Whatcom - the city that would become Bellingham - with a wooden placard nailed to it listing the particulars. The placard recorded 96,345 board feet of what it called the finest quality lumber, from a single tree. The slab stayed on that corner for years, and people came to photograph it. Those photographs and engravings survive in the Whatcom County Museum and in digitized newspapers.

The press could not decide whether to boast or grieve. The Morning Times of 28 February 1897 worked out that the wood, sawn into one-inch strips, would stretch from Whatcom to China. A week later, on 7 March, The New York Times called it "the most magnificent fir tree ever beheld by human eyes" and described its destruction as "a truly pitiable tale" and a "crime." The regret, in other words, was not a modern invention. It was in print within a year.

## The Argument Over the Tape

Tree researchers have never fully accepted the number, and their reasons are good ones. At 465 feet, the Nooksack tree would sit right at the theoretical ceiling for its species - the height beyond which a conifer cannot lift water to its own crown. The record is littered with nineteenth-century giants that shrink under scrutiny: the 435-foot Ferguson Tree, a mountain ash reported in Australia's Watts River country in 1872; the 415-foot Lynn Valley Tree, a Douglas-fir felled in 1902 whose measurements are still disputed. The better-documented Mineral Tree, at 393 feet, is about as high as the credible record goes.

But there is one argument the skeptics have to answer, and it is made of volume rather than length. A 350-foot Douglas-fir typically yielded around 60,000 board feet. This one yielded more than 96,000. Height can be exaggerated by a tape held carelessly. A sawmill's tally is harder to inflate.

## What Was Still Standing in 1891

Five years before the felling, two Sumas lumbermen, John M. Saar and S. H. Soule, ran a timber cruise down the Nooksack some fifteen miles west of Loop's Ranch and wrote up what they found. Douglas-firs nine to fourteen feet in diameter. Heights they put at 350 to 400 feet. One grove in particular, within 656 feet of the railroad tracks south of what is now Everson, they called "in all reasonable probability, the finest group of fir trees west of the Cascade Mountains."

They described a single tree beside that grove rising "perfectly straight from the ground, close barked and without a blemish, full two hundred feet or more to the first limb." A single acre, they reckoned, held half a million feet of fir and half again as much cedar. Read now, the cruise report is not an inventory. It is a description of a forest that no longer exists anywhere on Earth.

## Rediscovered in an Archive

The tree was forgotten for most of a century. It came back in 2009, when Micah Ewers, an amateur big-tree researcher, went digging through digitized newspapers and lumber journals and found not only the Whatcom County fir but references to roughly a hundred other Puget Sound Douglas-firs reported at over 350 to 400 feet between the 1890s and the 1920s. The Seattle Times columnist Ron C. Judd wrote the story up on 4 September 2011, interviewing Ewers along with the man who then owned the old Loop homestead, and gave the tree the name it now carries: the Nooksack Giant.

Nothing like it stands in Washington today. The tallest known living Douglas-fir, in Oregon's Siuslaw National Forest, reaches about 326 feet - impressive, and 139 feet short. Whatever the tape really said in 1896, the forest it was measuring is gone.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 48.91N, 122.11W near Maple Falls, Whatcom County, Washington, on the North Fork of the Nooksack River about 25 miles east of Bellingham along State Route 542, the Mount Baker Highway. From the air the valley is a clear corridor of second-growth forest and small farm clearings running east toward the Cascades, with the river braiding white through gravel bars on the valley floor. Mount Baker, glaciated and unmistakable, rises to the southeast and is the primary visual reference for the whole drainage; the Canadian border lies roughly seven miles north. Nearby airports: Bellingham International (KBLI) 25 miles west, Abbotsford International (CYXX) 20 miles northwest across the border in British Columbia, Skagit Regional (KBVS) 45 miles southwest, and Vancouver International (CYVR) 55 miles northwest. Best viewing altitude is 3,000-5,000 feet, high enough to read the valley but low enough to see the scale of the standing timber. Low cloud and valley fog are routine here from October through spring, and the Cascade foothills generate rapid orographic buildup on warm afternoons.
