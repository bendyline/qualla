# Berkeley Pit

For two dollars you can stand on a wooden deck above one of America's largest Superfund sites and look down at an acidic lake the color of weak tea, with a critical waterline that the EPA monitors every week.

**Location:** 46.0200, -112.5100

**Categories:** montana, superfund, mining, environmental, dark-tourism, history, industrial

## Introduction

There is a viewing platform. You pay two dollars at a gift shop, walk through a short tunnel, and emerge on a wooden deck looking down at a body of water roughly a mile long and half a mile wide. It does not look toxic. In good light it can look almost pretty - a rusty turquoise, sometimes wine red where the iron concentrates near the rim. A recorded narration plays from a speaker. The water in front of you is around pH 2.5, more acidic than vinegar, holding heavy metals in solution that include copper, arsenic, cadmium, and zinc. Roughly 50 billion gallons of it. This was the open-pit copper mine that helped wire the United States in the twentieth century, and it is now one of the most carefully monitored holes in North America. Welcome to the Berkeley Pit, Butte, Montana - a city that grew up calling itself the Richest Hill on Earth.

## The Hill That Wired America

Underground copper mining in Butte began in the 1870s and turned the city, briefly, into one of the most important industrial centres in the West. By the early twentieth century the Anaconda Copper Mining Company was supplying a major share of the copper that ran through American power grids, telegraph wires, and electric motors. After decades of tunnel mining, the easy ore at depth was running out. In 1955 the Anaconda Company switched tactics: they began strip-mining from the surface, scraping the top off the existing tunnel network in giant terraced steps. The pit grew through the 1960s and 1970s, swallowing the neighbourhood of Meaderville and slowly eating into the eastern edge of Butte itself. At its operational peak the pit measured roughly a mile by half a mile and reached about 1,780 feet deep. Operations continued under Atlantic Richfield, which bought Anaconda in 1977, until April 22, 1982, when ARCO turned off the dewatering pumps and let the mine begin to do what mines underground do when no one stops them. It started to fill with water.

## What the Water Does

Groundwater seeped back into the pit and into the thousands of miles of abandoned shafts and stopes that honeycomb the hill beneath it. As the water touched air-exposed pyrite, sulfuric acid formed, and the acid in turn pulled metals out of the rock. The result is a lake holding more than twenty metals and minerals in solution, with a pH that has been measured as low as 2.5 in some years and tends to sit between 2.5 and 4.5 depending on depth and treatment. The water is heavy: a litre of it carries enough dissolved iron, copper, and aluminum to coat a sample tube. It also turns out to be a unique habitat. Researchers from Montana Tech have found extremophile microbes thriving in the pit - including yeast strains and protozoans like Euglena mutabilis - that produce novel compounds, some of which have shown promise in laboratory cancer-cell screens. The lake is a poison, and it is also a strange new ecosystem that did not exist anywhere before 1982.

## The Geese

On a November afternoon in 1995, a flock of migrating snow geese found themselves caught in a winter storm over the high plains and put down on the only open water they could see. Three hundred and forty-two were recovered dead in the days that followed. Necropsies showed burns on their throats, gizzards, and esophagi from the acidic water. Anaconda's successor settled for a fine, mining companies installed shotguns and air cannons and falconers on the rim, and for the next twenty years the bird kills mostly stopped. Then came late November 2016, when a much larger storm pushed a flock estimated in the tens of thousands onto the pit. The hazing systems were overwhelmed. About three to four thousand birds died over the following weeks. The images that came out of Butte that fall - white feathers on rust-coloured water, frozen geese collected from the shoreline - travelled around the world. The Berkeley Pit became, briefly, a national news story about what the long bill for industrial prosperity looks like.

## 5,410 Feet

The Environmental Protection Agency has set a target water level for the pit at 5,410 feet above sea level. Above that elevation, the contaminated water would begin to flow outward through the surrounding fractured bedrock rather than draining inward toward the centre of the pit, which would risk reaching Silver Bow Creek and Butte's groundwater. The Horseshoe Bend Water Treatment Plant, opened originally in 2003 and expanded since, pumps water from the pit, dosed with hydrated lime to neutralize the acid and precipitate the metals, and treats up to several million gallons a day. In October 2019, after years of testing, treated water from the pit began to be released into Silver Bow Creek for the first time - a milestone for the long Superfund cleanup, but also a permanent obligation. The water level inside the pit is reported weekly. Someone in Butte will be measuring it for as long as Butte exists.

## Dark Tourism, Honest Light

The viewing platform reopens every spring. School groups come, retirees come, road-trippers chasing the strangest sights in the West come. Butte itself is a working town - copper still moves through smelters elsewhere in Montana, and the city's downtown of Victorian commercial blocks survives more or less intact, listed as a National Historic Landmark District for its mining-era architecture. The Pit sits east of downtown like a quiet, terrible relative no one quite knows how to introduce. The honesty of the place is unusual. There is no attempt to soften it. The narration tells you what is in the water and how it got there. The lake gets a little deeper every year, and someone at the EPA writes down the number.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 46.02N, 112.51W. The Berkeley Pit lies on the eastern edge of Butte, Montana, immediately northeast of the historic downtown and visible as a roughly 1.6 km long oval lake at the foot of the East Ridge. From the air the pit is hard to miss - the water alternates copper-red, turquoise, and dark green depending on light angle, and the terraced benches of the old strip mine form concentric steps around the rim. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 ft AGL to keep the pit, the Anselmo and Mountain Con head frames, and the uptown street grid in frame together. Bert Mooney Airport (KBTM) sits on the valley floor at 5,550 ft elevation, just south of town. Field elevation is one of the highest in the lower 48 and density altitude on hot summer afternoons can be punishing - flight planning should account for it. Butte is in a north-south valley flanked by the Highland and East Ridge mountains; expect strong afternoon winds in summer and severe icing risk in winter.
